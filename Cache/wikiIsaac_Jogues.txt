<p><b>Isaac Jogues</b>, <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">S.J.</a> (10 January 1607 – 18 October 1646) was a French <a href="/wiki/Missionary" title="Missionary">missionary</a> and <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a> who traveled and worked among the <a href="/wiki/Iroquois" title="Iroquois">Iroquois</a>, <a href="/wiki/Wyandot_people" title="Wyandot people">Huron</a>, and other Native populations in North America. He was the first European to name <a href="/wiki/Lake_George_(lake),_New_York" title="Lake George (lake), New York">Lake George</a>, calling it <i>Lac du Saint Sacrement</i> (Lake of the Blessed Sacrament). In 1646, Jogues was martyred by the <a href="/wiki/Mohawk_people" title="Mohawk people">Mohawk</a> at their village of <a href="/wiki/Ossernenon" class="mw-redirect" title="Ossernenon">Ossernenon</a>, near the <a href="/wiki/Mohawk_River" title="Mohawk River">Mohawk River</a>.
</p><p>Jogues, <a href="/wiki/Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf" title="Jean de Brébeuf">Jean de Brébeuf</a> and six other martyred missionaries, all Jesuit priests or laypeople associated with them, were canonized by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> in 1930;<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> they are known as the <a href="/wiki/Canadian_Martyrs" title="Canadian Martyrs">Canadian Martyrs</a>, or the North American Martyrs. A shrine was built in their honor at <a href="/wiki/Auriesville,_New_York" title="Auriesville, New York">Auriesville, New York</a>, formerly believed to be that of the Mohawk village. Their feast day is celebrated on 19 October in the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> and 26 September in Canada.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_education">Early life and education</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Isaac_Jogues&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life and education">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Isaac Jogues was born to Laurent Jogues and Françoise de Sainte-Mesmin on 10 January 1607. He was born in <a href="/wiki/Orl%C3%A9ans" title="Orléans">Orléans</a>, France, into a bourgeois family, where he was the fifth of nine children.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was educated at home until the age of ten, at which point he began attending Jesuit schools. In 1624, at the age of seventeen, he entered the Jesuit <a href="/wiki/Novitiate" title="Novitiate">novitiate</a> at <a href="/wiki/Rouen" title="Rouen">Rouen</a> in Northern France. Here, his <a href="/wiki/Master_of_novices" class="mw-redirect" title="Master of novices">Master of Novices</a> was <a href="/wiki/Louis_Lallemant" title="Louis Lallemant">Louis Lallemant</a>. The Jesuit community had a strong missionary spirit, beginning in 1625 with their first mission to New France, including missionary pioneers, <a href="/wiki/%C3%89nemond_Mass%C3%A9" title="Énemond Massé">Énemond Massé</a>, and later, <a href="/wiki/Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf" title="Jean de Brébeuf">Jean de Brébeuf</a>. Lallement had two brothers and a nephew serving as missionaries in the colony of <a href="/wiki/New_France" title="New France">New France</a>. These Jesuit missionaries inspired Jogues, and he aspired to follow in their footsteps.<sup id="cite_ref-crawley_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-crawley-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Jogues professed simple vows in 1626, and went to study philosophy at the royal college of <a href="/wiki/La_Fl%C3%A8che" title="La Flèche">La Flèche</a>. In 1629, he taught humanities to boys in Rouen. In 1633, Jogues was sent to the <a href="/wiki/Lyc%C3%A9e_Louis-le-Grand" title="Lycée Louis-le-Grand">Collège de Clermont</a> in <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a> to pursue his studies in <a href="/wiki/Theology" title="Theology">Theology</a>. In 1636, he was ordained a priest at Clermont.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_missions">Early missions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Isaac_Jogues&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early missions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1636 missionary fathers Brébeuf, <a href="/wiki/Charles_Lallemant" title="Charles Lallemant">Charles Lallemant</a> and Massé returned from New France. They told Jogues of the hardships, treacheries, and tortures which ordinarily awaited missionaries in New France. Their accounts however, increased Jogues' desire to "devote himself to labour there for the conversion and welfare of the natives".<sup id="cite_ref-:4_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:4-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Soon after Jogues was ordained, he accepted service in the missions and embarked to New France with several other missionaries, among them Charles Garnier. Jogues was assigned as a missionary to the Huron and Algonquian peoples; both were allies of the French in Quebec.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Jogues sailed from France on 8 April 1636, and eight weeks later, his ship dropped anchor in the <a href="/wiki/Chaleur_Bay" title="Chaleur Bay">Baie des Chaleurs</a>. Jogues arrived in Quebec only several weeks later, on 2 July. On arrival, Jogues wrote to his mother: "I do not know it is to enter Heaven, but this I know—that it would be difficult to experience in this world a joy more excessive and more overflowing than I felt in setting foot in the New World, and celebrating my first Mass on the day of Visitation." <sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEScott192752_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEScott192752-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Jogues joined Jean de Brébeuf, the Superior of the Jesuit Mission, at their settlement on Lake Huron, the village of St-Joseph (Ihonatiria), on 11 September. Upon his arrival, Jogues was stricken by fever. Soon after that, a similar epidemic broke out among other Jesuits and the native people. Due to recurring epidemics, the Huron blamed the Black Coats, as they called the Jesuits, threatening to kill them all.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEScott192761_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEScott192761-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Father Brébeuf conciliated them, and by the following year, relations had improved as evidenced by one of his reports: "We are gladly heard, and there is scarcely a village that has not invited us to go to it... And at last, it is understood from our whole conduct that we have not come to buy skins or to carry on any traffic, but solely to teach them and to procure them their souls' health."<sup id="cite_ref-:2_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>For six years, Jogues lived in the village of St-Joseph and learned the Hurons' ways and language. The missionaries "accommodated themselves to the customs and food of the savages" as much as possible to show the Indians that they intended to share their life. Gradually, the native people began to accept Jogues. This did not last long, however, as there were some Indians who had been "among the English and Dutch settlers to the south" who spread reports that the missionaries brought "calamity wherever they went and that they had in consequence been driven out of Europe."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEScott192764_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEScott192764-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Jogues traveled with Garnier to the Petun, a first nations band located in modern-day southern Ontario, who were also known as the Tobacco Nation for their chief commodity crop. The natives of the village were so uninviting to the missionaries that the Fathers thought it would be impossible to do any missionary work among them. The rumors that had encircled them spread to the village and quickly discovered that their cause was just as hopeless as in the former place.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEScott192752_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEScott192752-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> They traveled from village to village, until after a couple of months, they decided that they could not continue to do their missionary work. Their luck changed, however, when in 1639, the new superior of the Jesuit Mission, Father <a href="/wiki/J%C3%A9r%C3%B4me_Lalemant" title="Jérôme Lalemant">Jérôme Lalemant</a>, entrusted the building of Fort Sainte-Marie to Jogues.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In September 1641, Jogues and Charles Raymbaut went into the territory of the Sauteurs (Oijbwe). Some two thousand Indians welcomed them upon their arrival. Jogues settled down to the duties of a resident missioner at St. Mary's for some time.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Capture_by_the_Iroquois">Capture by the Iroquois</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Isaac_Jogues&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Capture by the Iroquois">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Mosaic_in_the_Cathedral_Basilica_of_St._Louis.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Mosaic_in_the_Cathedral_Basilica_of_St._Louis.JPG/200px-Mosaic_in_the_Cathedral_Basilica_of_St._Louis.JPG" decoding="async" width="200" height="160" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Mosaic_in_the_Cathedral_Basilica_of_St._Louis.JPG/300px-Mosaic_in_the_Cathedral_Basilica_of_St._Louis.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Mosaic_in_the_Cathedral_Basilica_of_St._Louis.JPG/400px-Mosaic_in_the_Cathedral_Basilica_of_St._Louis.JPG 2x" data-file-width="2158" data-file-height="1726" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Mosaic_in_the_Cathedral_Basilica_of_St._Louis.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Mosaic of St. Isaac Jogues in the <a href="/wiki/Cathedral_Basilica_of_St._Louis" class="mw-redirect" title="Cathedral Basilica of St. Louis">Cathedral Basilica of St. Louis</a></div></div></div>
<p>On 3 August 1642, Jogues, <a href="/wiki/Guillaume_Couture" title="Guillaume Couture">Guillaume Couture</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Goupil" title="René Goupil">René Goupil</a>, and a group of Christian Hurons were heading back from Quebec City when they were waylaid by a war party of the <a href="/wiki/Mohawk_Nation" class="mw-redirect" title="Mohawk Nation">Mohawk Nation</a>, part of the Iroquois Confederacy. Jogues allegedly hid in reeds and bushes but decided to leave his hiding place to join the prisoners so that he could comfort them and ensure that their faith in Christianity remained strong. Shortly after that, and in retaliation for comforting a tortured <a href="/wiki/Guillaume_Couture" title="Guillaume Couture">Guillaume Couture</a>, the Mohawk beat Jogues with sticks, tore out his fingernails, then gnawed the ends of his fingers until finger bones were visible.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The war party then took their captives on a journey to a Mohawk village. The villagers marched them through a gauntlet, consisting of rows of Iroquois armed with rods and sticks, beating the prisoners walking in single-file. Afterward, the Iroquois forced Jogues and the prisoners onto an elevated platform where they were mocked. A captive Algonquin woman then cut off Jogues' thumb. At night, the prisoners were tied spread-eagled in a cabin. Children threw burning coals onto their bodies. Three days later, Jogues and the prisoners were marched from one village to another, where the Iroquois flogged them in gauntlets and jabbed sticks into their wounds and sores. At the third village, Jogues was hung from a wooden plank and nearly lost consciousness until an Iroquois had pity on him and cut him free. Throughout his captivity, Jogues comforted, baptized, heard confession from, and absolved the other prisoners.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hearing of their capture, <a href="/wiki/Arent_van_Curler" title="Arent van Curler">Arent van Curler</a>, <i>commis</i> of <a href="/wiki/Manor_of_Rensselaerswyck" title="Manor of Rensselaerswyck">Rensselaerswyck</a>, visited the "first castle" and attempted to ransom them, but without success as the Mohawk were not inclined to release them at that time. Van Curler was able to elicit a promise not to kill the captives. Instead of being put to death or integrated into a Mohawk family, Jogues remained a captive at large. Perpetually malnourished and inadequately dressed for the harsh winters, he spent his days gathering wood, praying, and proselytizing his captors.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEScott192752_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEScott192752-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Seeking solace in his faith, Jogues prayed so intensely that he had visions: in one, he suddenly appeared in a bookstore covered in crosses and bought a book that reminded him that, to enter into Heaven, it was necessary to experience many tribulations.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> His captivity dragged on, lasting about a year, during which he experienced severe malnourishment and exposure to the cold. During this period, some noteworthy incidents were when he saved the life of a pregnant woman that had fallen into a deep, fast-flowing creek during the winter and when he baptized the Iroquois man who had freed him from the wooden torture device.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_9-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:252px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Isaac_Jogues_NYC.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/Isaac_Jogues_NYC.jpg/250px-Isaac_Jogues_NYC.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="172" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/Isaac_Jogues_NYC.jpg/375px-Isaac_Jogues_NYC.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/Isaac_Jogues_NYC.jpg/500px-Isaac_Jogues_NYC.jpg 2x" data-file-width="587" data-file-height="403" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Isaac_Jogues_NYC.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>On a door to <a href="/wiki/St._Patrick%27s_Cathedral_(Manhattan)" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Patrick&#39;s Cathedral (Manhattan)">St. Patrick's Cathedral</a> in New York City</div></div></div>
<p>In the autumn of 1643, the Mohawk were persuaded to bring the priest with them when they came to <a href="/wiki/Beverwijck" title="Beverwijck">Beverwijck</a> to trade. Once there, Van Curler helped Jogues escape, hiding him in his barn until a deal could be reached. The Frenchman put on a ship to take him downriver.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Reformed minister <a href="/wiki/Johannes_Megapolensis" title="Johannes Megapolensis">Johannes Megapolensis</a> accompanied him to <a href="/wiki/New_Amsterdam" title="New Amsterdam">New Amsterdam</a>, where Jogues stayed with the minister while waiting for a ship to take him to France. Jogues was the first Catholic priest to visit Manhattan Island.<sup id="cite_ref-crawley_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-crawley-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> From there, he sailed back to France.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Return_to_France">Return to France</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Isaac_Jogues&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Return to France">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_VIII" title="Pope Urban VIII">Pope Urban VIII</a> considered Jogues a "living martyr" and gave him dispensation to say Mass with his mutilated hand. Under Catholic Church law of the time, the Blessed Sacrament could not be touched with any fingers but the thumb and forefinger. Jogues was unable to follow this law after losing two fingers while in Iroquois captivity, resulting in the requirement for dispensation by the pope. Jogues visited his mother in <a href="/wiki/Orl%C3%A9ans" title="Orléans">Orléans</a>, but was eager to return to the missions. Jogues experienced regret over his time in captivity, and a longing for martyrdom that motivated his return to New France in 1644 after only a year and a half in France, first to Quebec, followed by a trip to Wendake.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Return_to_New_France_and_death">Return to New France and death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Isaac_Jogues&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Return to New France and death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In the spring of 1646, Jogues returned to Iroquois territory, along with <a href="/wiki/Jean_de_Lalande" title="Jean de Lalande">Jean de Lalande</a>, to act as the French ambassador to the Mohawk. His ambassadorship was intended to maintain the tentative peace reached in 1645 between the Iroquois and the French, the Huron and the Algonquin. This was done to ensure a safe passage for trade and travel.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Jogues and Lalande were met with hesitation upon arrival, as some Mohawk regarded missionaries as evil practitioners of foreign magic. The Europeans transmitted European diseases, such as <a href="/wiki/Smallpox" title="Smallpox">smallpox</a> and <a href="/wiki/Measles" title="Measles">measles</a>, that spread among Native Americans. These diseases resulted in high fatality rates among the Mohawk, who lacked immunity to the new diseases. When the Mohawk suffered yet another outbreak of infectious disease and crop failure at <a href="/wiki/Ossernenon" class="mw-redirect" title="Ossernenon">Ossernenon</a>, they blamed these unfortunate events on Catholic paraphernalia left behind by the Jesuits, which the Mohawks perceived as magically harmful. Additionally, as a result of his previous experience on the territory, Jogues demonstrated an uncanny knowledge of the territory, which the Mohawks perceived as threatening.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_12-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 18 October 1646, the Mohawks killed Jogues with a <a href="/wiki/Tomahawk" title="Tomahawk">tomahawk</a>; they killed LaLande the next day. They threw the missionaries' bodies into the <a href="/wiki/Mohawk_River" title="Mohawk River">Mohawk River</a>. The killing seems to have been the work of an anti-French faction within the Mohawk community.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_12-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The story holds a curious double martyrdom of Jogues. Aboriginal allies of the French captured Jogues' killer in 1647 and condemned him to death. While awaiting his execution, this man was baptized and renamed with the Christian name of Father Isaac Jogues. His death represented a secondary martyring of Isaac Jogues.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Attitudes_towards_martyrdom">Attitudes towards martyrdom</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Isaac_Jogues&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Attitudes towards martyrdom">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Jogues' refusal to escape and how he embraced torture demonstrate selflessness that, like many other Jesuits in New France, he believed that being martyred would mean partaking in the torment that Jesus had endured on the cross. This would indicate his acceptance "into the pantheon of heroes whose physical and spiritual strength had been equal to the cruel persecutions inflicted on the primitive church."<sup id="cite_ref-:1_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Jogues is quoted as saying: "He [Jesus] was making us share his sufferings, and admitting us to participate in his crosses."<sup id="cite_ref-:2_9-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At another point, Jogues speaks of, "The procession [of torture victims] beginning to enter this narrow way of Paradise . . . it was indeed then that I could say with my Lord and master, <i>Supra dorsum meum fabricaverunt peccatores</i>,—'Sinners have built and left monuments and marks of their rage upon my back.'"<sup id="cite_ref-:2_9-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Jogues regarded his torture, and the death he thought would follow, as allowing him to imitate, and thus participate in, the passion of Jesus.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration_and_legacy">Veneration and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Isaac_Jogues&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Veneration and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Jogues was canonized on 29 June 1930 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XI" title="Pope Pius XI">Pope Pius XI</a> along with seven other Canadian Martyrs.<sup id="cite_ref-think_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-think-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is celebrated on 19 October in the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a>, and on 26 September in Canada. Jogues and companions are patron saints of North America.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Interior_of_Martyrs_Shrine_Coliseum.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/58/Interior_of_Martyrs_Shrine_Coliseum.jpg/220px-Interior_of_Martyrs_Shrine_Coliseum.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/58/Interior_of_Martyrs_Shrine_Coliseum.jpg/330px-Interior_of_Martyrs_Shrine_Coliseum.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/58/Interior_of_Martyrs_Shrine_Coliseum.jpg/440px-Interior_of_Martyrs_Shrine_Coliseum.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2816" data-file-height="2112" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Interior_of_Martyrs_Shrine_Coliseum.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Interior of North American Martyrs Shrine</div></div></div>
<p>There are several buildings and monuments dedicated to Jogues. The largest of these monuments is the Shrine of the <a href="/wiki/Canadian_Martyrs" title="Canadian Martyrs">North American Martyrs</a>, built in <a href="/wiki/Auriesville,_New_York" title="Auriesville, New York">Auriesville, New York</a> in 1930. It honors Jogues, René Goupil, Louis Lalande, and <a href="/wiki/Kateri_Tekakwitha" title="Kateri Tekakwitha">Kateri Tekakwitha</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>  It was completed in 1930. The shrine also honors Jean de Brébeuf and five of his companions killed in Canada in 1648 and 1649.
</p><p>There is also the Martyr's Shrine located in Midland, Ontario, Canada, which honors the <a href="/wiki/Canadian_Martyrs" title="Canadian Martyrs">Canadian Martyrs</a> (another term for North American Martyrs).<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A seasonal chapel on the east shore of <a href="/wiki/Saratoga_Lake" title="Saratoga Lake">Saratoga Lake</a>, New York is named after Jogues. A statue of Jogues stands in front of the main entrance to the chapel that faces the lake.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> While he was being taken into captivity, Jogues is said to have been the first European to see this lake.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Fordham_University" title="Fordham University">Fordham University</a>, a Jesuit university in New York, has a dormitory building at its Rose Hill Campus named Martyrs' Court.  The three wings of the building are named after Jogues, René Goupil, and Jean de Lalande.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Another statue of Jogues was erected in 1939, in the village of Lake George, in the Battlefield Park by the lake.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Camp_Ondessonk" title="Camp Ondessonk">Camp Ondessonk</a>, a Roman Catholic youth camp located in <a href="/wiki/Ozark,_Illinois" title="Ozark, Illinois">Ozark, Illinois</a>, is named after Jogues' Mohawk name.  The living quarters for campers are each named for one of the North American Martyrs, including Kateri Tekakwitha, Jean de Brébeuf, <a href="/wiki/No%C3%ABl_Chabanel" title="Noël Chabanel">Noël Chabanel</a>, <a href="/wiki/Antoine_Daniel" title="Antoine Daniel">Antoine Daniel</a>, <a href="/wiki/Charles_Garnier_(missionary)" title="Charles Garnier (missionary)">Charles Garnier</a>, René Goupil, Jean de Lalande, and <a href="/wiki/Gabriel_Lalemant" title="Gabriel Lalemant">Gabriel Lalemant</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
