<p><b>Bridget of Sweden</b>, <a href="/wiki/Bridgettines" title="Bridgettines">OSsS</a> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1303</span> – 23 July 1373) born as <i>Birgitta Birgersdotter</i>, also <i>Birgitta of Vadstena</i> (<a href="/wiki/Swedish_language" title="Swedish language">Swedish</a>: <i lang="sv">heliga Birgitta</i>), was a Swedish <a href="/wiki/Widow" title="Widow">widow</a>, <a href="/wiki/Mysticism" title="Mysticism">mystic</a>, <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>, and the founder of the <a href="/wiki/Bridgettines" title="Bridgettines">Bridgettines</a>. Outside Sweden, she was also known as the <i>Princess of <a href="/wiki/N%C3%A4rke" title="Närke">Nericia</a></i><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and was the mother of <a href="/wiki/Catherine_of_Vadstena" title="Catherine of Vadstena">Catherine of Vadstena</a>.
</p><p>Bridget is one of the six <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saints</a> of Europe, together with <a href="/wiki/Benedict_of_Nursia" title="Benedict of Nursia">Benedict of Nursia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Cyril_and_Methodius" title="Cyril and Methodius">Cyril and Methodius</a>, <a href="/wiki/Catherine_of_Siena" title="Catherine of Siena">Catherine of Siena</a> and <a href="/wiki/Edith_Stein" title="Edith Stein">Teresa Benedicta of the Cross</a>.
</p>

<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Brigitta_1476.jpeg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ed/St_Brigitta_1476.jpeg/220px-St_Brigitta_1476.jpeg" decoding="async" width="220" height="315" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ed/St_Brigitta_1476.jpeg 1.5x" data-file-width="277" data-file-height="396" /></a><figcaption>Saint Bridget in the <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">religious habit</a> of a Bridgettine nun, in a 1476 <a href="/wiki/Breviary" title="Breviary">breviary</a> of the form of the <a href="/wiki/Liturgy_of_the_Hours" title="Liturgy of the Hours">Divine Office</a> unique to her order</figcaption></figure>
<p>The most celebrated saint of Sweden was the daughter of the knight <a href="/wiki/Birger_Persson" title="Birger Persson">Birger Persson</a><sup id="cite_ref-kirsch_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> of the <a href="/wiki/Finstaatten" class="mw-redirect" title="Finstaatten">family</a> of <a href="/wiki/Finsta" title="Finsta">Finsta</a>, governor and <a href="/wiki/Lawspeaker" title="Lawspeaker">lawspeaker</a> of <a href="/wiki/Uppland" title="Uppland">Uppland</a>, and one of the richest <a href="/wiki/Land_tenure" title="Land tenure">landowners</a> of the country, and his wife Ingeborg Bengtsdotter, a member of the so-called Lawspeaker branch of the <a href="/wiki/Folkunga" class="mw-redirect" title="Folkunga">Folkunga</a> family. Through her mother, Ingeborg, Birgitta was related to the Swedish kings of her era.
</p><p>She was born in 1303. The exact date of her birth is not recorded. In 1316, at the age of 13<sup id="cite_ref-kirsch_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> she married Ulf Gudmarsson of the family of <a href="/wiki/Ulv%C3%A5sa" title="Ulvåsa">Ulvåsa</a>, Lord of <a href="/wiki/N%C3%A4rke" title="Närke">Närke</a>, to whom she bore eight children, four daughters and four sons. Six of her children survived infancy, which was rare at that time. Her eldest daughter was <a href="/wiki/M%C3%A4rta_Ulfsdotter" title="Märta Ulfsdotter">Märta Ulfsdotter</a>. Her second daughter is now honored as <a href="/wiki/Catherine_of_Vadstena" title="Catherine of Vadstena">St. Catherine of Sweden</a>. Her youngest daughter was <a href="/wiki/Cecilia_Ulvsdotter" title="Cecilia Ulvsdotter">Cecilia Ulvsdotter</a>. Bridget became known for her works of <a href="/wiki/Charity_(practice)" title="Charity (practice)">charity</a>, particularly toward Östergötland's unwed mothers and their children. When she was in her early thirties, she was summoned to be principal lady-in-waiting to the new Queen of Sweden, <a href="/wiki/Blanche_of_Namur" title="Blanche of Namur">Blanche of Namur</a>. In 1341, she and her husband went on <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> to <a href="/wiki/Santiago_de_Compostela" title="Santiago de Compostela">Santiago de Compostela</a>.
</p><p>In 1344, shortly after their return, Ulf died at the <a href="/wiki/Cistercians" title="Cistercians">Cistercian</a> <a href="/wiki/Alvastra_Abbey" title="Alvastra Abbey">Alvastra Abbey</a> in <a href="/wiki/%C3%96sterg%C3%B6tland" title="Östergötland">Östergötland</a>. After this loss, Birgitta became a member of the <a href="/wiki/Third_Order_of_Saint_Francis" title="Third Order of Saint Francis">Third Order of Saint Francis</a> and devoted herself to a life of prayer and caring for the poor and the sick.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It was at this time that she developed the idea of establishing the religious community which was to become the <a href="/wiki/Religious_order" title="Religious order">religious order</a> of the Most Holy Saviour, or the <a href="/wiki/Bridgettines" title="Bridgettines">Bridgettines</a>, whose principal house at <a href="/wiki/Vadstena_Abbey" title="Vadstena Abbey">Vadstena</a> was later richly endowed by King <a href="/wiki/Magnus_IV_of_Sweden" title="Magnus IV of Sweden">Magnus IV of Sweden</a> and his queen. One distinctive feature of the houses of the Order was that they were <a href="/wiki/Double_monastery" title="Double monastery">double monasteries</a>, with men and women both forming a joint community, but they lived in separate cloisters. They were required to live in poor convents and they were also required to give all of their surplus income to the poor. However, they were allowed to have as many books as they pleased.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1350, a <a href="/wiki/Jubilee_in_the_Catholic_Church" title="Jubilee in the Catholic Church">Jubilee Year</a>, Bridget braved a plague-stricken Europe to make a pilgrimage to Rome accompanied by her daughter, Catherine, and a small party of <a href="/wiki/Catholic_priest" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic priest">priests</a> and disciples. This was partly done to obtain authorization to found the new order from the Pope and it was also partly done in pursuance of her self-imposed mission to elevate the moral tone of the age. This was during the period of the <a href="/wiki/Avignon_Papacy" title="Avignon Papacy">Avignon Papacy</a> within the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>, however, so she had to wait for the return of the papacy from the French city of Avignon to Rome, a move for which she agitated for many years.
</p><p>It was not until 1370 that <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_V" title="Pope Urban V">Pope Urban V</a>, during his brief attempt to re-establish the papacy in Rome, confirmed the <a href="/wiki/Monastic_rule" class="mw-redirect" title="Monastic rule">Rule</a> of the order, but meanwhile Birgitta had made herself universally beloved in Rome by her kindness and good works. Save for occasional pilgrimages, including one to <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a> in 1373, she remained in Rome until her death on 23 July 1373, urging ecclesiastical reform.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In her pilgrimages to Rome, Jerusalem and Bethlehem, she sent "back precise instructions for the construction of the monastery" now known as the Blue Church, insisting that an "abbess, signifying the Virgin Mary, should preside over both nuns and monks."<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bridget went to confession every day, and she had a constant smiling, glowing face.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Although she never returned to Sweden, her years in Rome were far from happy, she was hounded by debts and opposition to her work against Church abuses. She was originally buried at <a href="/wiki/San_Lorenzo_in_Panisperna" title="San Lorenzo in Panisperna">San Lorenzo in Panisperna</a> before her remains were returned to Sweden. She was <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> in the year 1391 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_IX" title="Pope Boniface IX">Pope Boniface IX</a>, which was confirmed by the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Constance" title="Council of Constance">Council of Constance</a> in 1415. Because of new discussions about her works, the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Basel" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Basel">Council of Basel</a> confirmed the orthodoxy of her <a href="/wiki/Revelation" title="Revelation">revelations</a> in 1436.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Visions">Visions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bridget_of_Sweden&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Visions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:StBridgetSyonIndenture.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/d/d3/StBridgetSyonIndenture.jpg/220px-StBridgetSyonIndenture.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="231" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/d/d3/StBridgetSyonIndenture.jpg/330px-StBridgetSyonIndenture.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/d/d3/StBridgetSyonIndenture.jpg/440px-StBridgetSyonIndenture.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1720" data-file-height="1808" /></a><figcaption><i>The Vision of St Bridget.</i> The Risen Christ, displaying his wound from <a href="/wiki/Saint_Longinus" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Longinus">Longinus</a>, inspires the writing of Saint Bridget. (BL Harley MS 4640, f.15)</figcaption></figure>
<p>At the age of ten, Bridget had a vision of Jesus hanging upon the cross. When she asked who had treated him like this, he answered:<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>They who despise me, and spurn my love for them.</p></blockquote>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Passion_of_Jesus" title="Passion of Jesus">Passion of Christ</a> became the center of her spiritual life from that moment on.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Revelations_of_St._Bridget" class="extiw" title="s:Revelations of St. Bridget">revelations</a> which she had received since her childhood now became more frequent, and the records of these <i>Revelationes coelestes</i> ("Celestial revelations") which were translated into <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> by Matthias, canon of <a href="/wiki/Link%C3%B6ping" title="Linköping">Linköping</a>, and her confessor, Peter Olafsson, prior of Alvastra, acquired a great vogue during the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-kirsch_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> These revelations made Bridget something of a celebrity to some and a controversial figure to others.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Vision_of_the_birth_of_Christ_with_kneeling_Virgin">Vision of the birth of Christ with kneeling Virgin</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bridget_of_Sweden&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Vision of the birth of Christ with kneeling Virgin">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Her visions of the <a href="/wiki/Nativity_of_Jesus" title="Nativity of Jesus">Nativity of Jesus</a> would influence later depictions of the <a href="/wiki/Nativity_of_Jesus_in_art" title="Nativity of Jesus in art">Nativity of Jesus in art</a>. Shortly before her death, she described a vision which included the infant Jesus lying on (not in) clean swaddling clothes on the ground, and emitting light himself, and she described the Virgin as blond-haired and kneeling in prayer exactly as she was moments before the spontaneous birth, with her womb shrunken and her virginity intact.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Many depictions followed this scene, they included the popular ox and donkey and they reduced other light sources in the scene in order to emphasize the "child of light" effect, and the Nativity was treated with <a href="/wiki/Chiaroscuro" title="Chiaroscuro">chiaroscuro</a> through the <a href="/wiki/Baroque" title="Baroque">Baroque</a>. Other details which are frequently seen, such as Joseph carrying a single candle that he "attached to the wall," and the presence of <a href="/wiki/God_the_Father" title="God the Father">God the Father</a> above, also originated in Bridget's vision.
</p><p>The pose of the Virgin kneeling to pray to her child, to be joined by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Joseph" title="Saint Joseph">Saint Joseph</a>, technically known as the "Adoration of the Child", became one of the most common depictions in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, largely replacing the reclining Virgin in the West. A few earlier depictions of the Virgin which show her with an ox and a donkey (scenes which are not described in the gospels) were produced as early as 1300, before Bridget was born, have a <a href="/wiki/Franciscan" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan">Franciscan</a> origin, by which she may have been influenced, because she was a member of the Franciscan order.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<p>Her visions of <a href="/wiki/Purgatory" title="Purgatory">Purgatory</a> were also well-known.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Prophecy">Prophecy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bridget_of_Sweden&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Prophecy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In addition, "she even predicted an eventual Vatican State, foretelling almost the exact boundaries delineated by <a href="/wiki/Benito_Mussolini" title="Benito Mussolini">Mussolini</a> for <a href="/wiki/Vatican_City" title="Vatican City">Vatican City</a> in 1921."<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a> spoke of Bridget in a general audience on 27 October 2010, saying that the value of Saint Bridget's Revelations, sometimes the object of doubt, was specified by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> in the letter <i>Spes Aedificandi</i>: "Yet there is no doubt that the Church," wrote my beloved predecessor, "which recognized Bridget's <a href="/wiki/Catechesis" title="Catechesis">holiness</a> without ever pronouncing on her individual revelations, has accepted the overall authenticity of her interior experience."<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Fifteen_.27Our_Father_and_Hail_Mary_prayers.27"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Fifteen_'Our_Father_and_Hail_Mary_prayers'">Fifteen 'Our Father and Hail Mary prayers'</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bridget_of_Sweden&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Fifteen &#039;Our Father and Hail Mary prayers&#039;">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_Bridget%27s_Reliquary_(cropped).JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Saint_Bridget%27s_Reliquary_%28cropped%29.JPG/220px-Saint_Bridget%27s_Reliquary_%28cropped%29.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="196" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Saint_Bridget%27s_Reliquary_%28cropped%29.JPG/330px-Saint_Bridget%27s_Reliquary_%28cropped%29.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Saint_Bridget%27s_Reliquary_%28cropped%29.JPG/440px-Saint_Bridget%27s_Reliquary_%28cropped%29.JPG 2x" data-file-width="787" data-file-height="701" /></a><figcaption>Saint Bridget's reliquary, holding a piece of her bone.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Saint Bridget prayed for a long time to know how many blows Jesus Christ suffered during His terrible Passion. Rewarding her patience, one day He appeared to her and said, "I received 5480 blows upon My Body. If you wish to honor them in some way, recite fifteen Our Fathers and fifteen Hail Marys with the following Prayers, which I Myself shall teach you, for an entire year. When the year is finished, you will have honored each of My Wounds."<sup id="cite_ref-Puskorius_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Puskorius-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
<a href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Secret_of_Happiness" class="extiw" title="s:The Secret of Happiness">The prayers</a> became known as the "Fifteen O's" because in the original Latin, each prayer began with the words <i>O Jesu</i>, <i>O Rex</i>, or <i>O Domine Jesu Christe</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Some have questioned whether Saint Bridget is in fact their author; <a href="/wiki/Eamon_Duffy" title="Eamon Duffy">Eamon Duffy</a> reports that the prayers probably originated in England, in the devotional circles that surrounded <a href="/wiki/Richard_Rolle" title="Richard Rolle">Richard Rolle</a> or the English Brigittines.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Whatever their origin, the prayers were widely circulated in the late Middle Ages, and they became regular features in <a href="/wiki/Book_of_hours" title="Book of hours">Books of Hours</a> and other devotional literature. They were translated into various languages; an early English language version of them was printed in a primer by <a href="/wiki/William_Caxton" title="William Caxton">William Caxton</a>. The prayers themselves reflect the late medieval tradition of meditation on the passion of Christ, and are structured around the <a href="/wiki/Sayings_of_Jesus_on_the_cross" title="Sayings of Jesus on the cross">seven last words of Christ</a>. They borrow from <a href="/wiki/Church_Fathers" title="Church Fathers">patristic</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">Scriptural</a> sources as well as the tradition of devotion to the wounds of Christ.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During the Middle Ages, the prayers were circulated with various promises of <a href="/wiki/Indulgence" title="Indulgence">indulgence</a> and other assurances of 21 supernatural graces supposed to attend the daily recitation of the 15 orations at least for a year.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> These indulgences were repeated in the manuscript tradition of the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_hours" title="Book of hours">Books of Hours</a>, and may constitute one major source of the prayers' popularity in the late Middle Ages. They promise, among other things, the release from <a href="/wiki/Purgatory" title="Purgatory">Purgatory</a> of fifteen of the devotee's family members, and that they would keep fifteen living family members in a state of <a href="/wiki/Divine_grace" title="Divine grace">grace</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Duffy,_p._255_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Duffy,_p._255-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The extravagance of the promises which were made in these rubrics—one widely circulated version promised that the devotee would receive "his heart's desire, if it be for the salvation of his soul"<sup id="cite_ref-Duffy,_p._255_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Duffy,_p._255-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>—attracted critics early and late. In 1538, <a href="/wiki/William_Marshall_(translator)" title="William Marshall (translator)">William Marshall</a> enjoined his readers to "henseforth ... forget suche prayers as seynt Brigittes &amp; other lyke, whyche greate promyses and perdons haue falsly auaunced."<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<p><a href="/wiki/Martin_Luther" title="Martin Luther">Martin Luther</a> strongly rejected the Roman Catholic belief in the 21 promises and nicknamed St Bridget <i>Die tolle Brigit</i> (The foolish Bridget).<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
In the following decades, Protestantism sought to eradicate the devotion to similar angelic and spiritual entities claiming they were a 'popish' and 'pagan' legacy.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
Lutheranism and Calvinism were characterized by a lower degree of Marian devotion than that pertaining to the Roman Catholic Church, particularly with reference to the Marian title of <a href="/wiki/Titles_of_Mary#Devotional_titles" title="Titles of Mary">Queen of Angels</a>.
</p><p>The Vatican and the Lutheran Church jointly conceived a modern devotion to St Bridget which had remained a relevant factor of disagreement between the two churches till then.<sup id="cite_ref-ricerca.repubblica.it_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ricerca.repubblica.it-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> 
In 1954, the <a href="/wiki/Supreme_Sacred_Congregation_of_the_Holy_Office" class="mw-redirect" title="Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Holy Office">Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Holy Office</a> ruled that the alleged promises (though not the prayers themselves) are unreliable, and it directed local <a href="/wiki/Ordinary_(church_officer)" title="Ordinary (church officer)">ordinaries</a> not to permit the circulation of pamphlets which contain the promises.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Puskorius_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Puskorius-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The ecumenical process of reconciliation culminated on 8 October 1991 during the sexcentennial of St Bridget's canonization, when Pope John Paul II and two Lutheran bishops met and prayed in front of the burial place of St Peter Apostle, in Rome. It was the first time in which a joint prayer was said by members of the two communities.<sup id="cite_ref-ricerca.repubblica.it_24-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ricerca.repubblica.it-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bridget_of_Sweden&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Heliga_Birgitta-den_portrattlika.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Heliga_Birgitta-den_portrattlika.jpg/245px-Heliga_Birgitta-den_portrattlika.jpg" decoding="async" width="245" height="419" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Heliga_Birgitta-den_portrattlika.jpg/368px-Heliga_Birgitta-den_portrattlika.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Heliga_Birgitta-den_portrattlika.jpg/490px-Heliga_Birgitta-den_portrattlika.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1928" data-file-height="3299" /></a><figcaption>Statue of Bridget of Sweden in Vadstena Abbey. Work by sculptor <a href="/w/index.php?title=Johannes_Junge&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Johannes Junge (page does not exist)">Johannes Junge</a> in 1425.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The Brigitta Chapel was erected in 1651 in <a href="/wiki/Vienna" title="Vienna">Vienna</a>, and in 1900 the new district <a href="/wiki/Brigittenau" title="Brigittenau">Brigittenau</a> was founded. In Sweden, adjacent to <a href="/wiki/Skederid_Church" title="Skederid Church">Skederid Church</a>, built by Bridget's father on the family's land, a memorial stone was erected in 1930.
</p><p>On 1 October 1999, <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> named Saint Bridget a <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Her <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is celebrated on 23 July, the day of her death. Her feast was not in the <a href="/wiki/Tridentine_calendar" title="Tridentine calendar">Tridentine calendar</a>, but was inserted in the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> in 1623 for celebration on 7 October, the day of her 1391 canonization by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_IX" title="Pope Boniface IX">Pope Boniface IX</a>. Five years later, her feast day was moved to 8 October (but the Church in Sweden celebrates it on the 7th), a date which was not changed until the revision of the General Roman Calendar in 1969, when it was set on 8 October, the date which it is currently celebrated on.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> Some continue to use the earlier <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar_of_1954" title="General Roman Calendar of 1954">General Roman Calendar of 1954</a>, the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar_of_Pope_Pius_XII" title="General Roman Calendar of Pope Pius XII">General Roman Calendar of Pope Pius XII</a>, or the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar_of_1960" title="General Roman Calendar of 1960">General Roman Calendar of 1960</a>.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Third_Order_of_Saint_Francis" title="Third Order of Saint Francis">Third Order of Saint Francis</a> includes her feast day on its <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Calendar of saints</a> on the same day as the general Church, honoring her as a member of the order.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Bj%C3%A4rka-S%C3%A4by_Monastery" class="mw-redirect" title="Bjärka-Säby Monastery">Bjärka-Säby Monastery</a> contains a portrait of Bridget of Sweden which is venerated by Christians who are members of several <a href="/wiki/Christian_denomination" title="Christian denomination">denominations</a>. An hour away from this monastery, the <a href="/wiki/Vadstena_Abbey" title="Vadstena Abbey">Vadstena Abbey</a>, also known as the Blue Church, contains relics of the saint, and her body is venerated by Lutheran and Catholic believers.<sup id="cite_ref-Milliner1_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Milliner1-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Milliner2_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Milliner2-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bridget is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a>, which holds a <a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(Anglicanism)" title="Commemoration (Anglicanism)">commemoration</a> on 23 July<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> and on the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church liturgical calendar</a> on 7 October.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<span class="mw-image-border noviewer" typeof="mw:File"><a href="/wiki/File:046CupolaSPietro.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="icon" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/046CupolaSPietro.jpg/16px-046CupolaSPietro.jpg" decoding="async" width="16" height="12" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/046CupolaSPietro.jpg/24px-046CupolaSPietro.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/046CupolaSPietro.jpg/32px-046CupolaSPietro.jpg 2x" data-file-width="800" data-file-height="600" /></a></span>&#160;<a href="/wiki/Portal:Catholicism" class="mw-redirect" title="Portal:Catholicism">Catholicism&#32;portal</a></td></tr><tr><td class="sidebar-navbar"><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1129693374"><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1063604349"><div class="navbar plainlinks hlist navbar-mini"><ul><li class="nv-view"><a href="/wiki/Template:Sacred_Heart_of_Jesus" title="Template:Sacred Heart of Jesus"><abbr title="View this template">v</abbr></a></li><li class="nv-talk"><a href="/wiki/Template_talk:Sacred_Heart_of_Jesus" title="Template talk:Sacred Heart of Jesus"><abbr title="Discuss this template">t</abbr></a></li><li class="nv-edit"><a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Template:Sacred_Heart_of_Jesus&amp;action=edit"><abbr title="Edit this template">e</abbr></a></li></ul></div></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Although he was initially interested in Bridget's <i>Revelations</i>, <a href="/wiki/Martin_Luther" title="Martin Luther">Martin Luther</a> would later conclude that her visions were mere ravings.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Some 19th-century writers presented her as a forerunner of the <a href="/wiki/Protestant_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant Reformation">Protestant Reformation</a> due to her criticism of popes, bishops and other members of the clergy who did not live in accordance with the teachings of their religion.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> However, she never criticized that teaching or the church as such.
</p><p>Of her as depicted in his play <i>Folkungasagan</i> <a href="/wiki/August_Strindberg" title="August Strindberg">August Strindberg</a> explained Bridget as "a power-hungry, vainglorious woman who intentionally vied for sainthood", adding "of this unpleasant woman and according to the historical documents I made the uncontrollable ninny now in my drama, although in her honor I let her awaken to clarity about her silliness and her arrogance."<sup id="cite_ref-:2_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><i><a href="/wiki/Throne_of_a_Thousand_Years#2020_edition" title="Throne of a Thousand Years">Centuries of Selfies</a></i> (2020) describes how Bridget damaged <a href="/wiki/Magnus_IV_of_Sweden" title="Magnus IV of Sweden">King Magnus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Blanche_of_Namur" title="Blanche of Namur">Queen Blanche</a> by accusing them of "erotic deviations, extravagance and murderous plots",<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> a description particularly noted by <i><a href="/wiki/Dala-Demokraten" title="Dala-Demokraten">Dala-Demokraten</a></i> as likely to upset Swedish nuns.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> With the eventual translation of her Latin works into Swedish, more understanding and appreciation of her evolved in some Swedish circles.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Editions">Editions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bridget_of_Sweden&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Editions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Saint Birgitta's <i>Revelaciones</i>, that is, her <i>Revelations</i> written in Latin, appeared in critical editions during the years 1956 to 2002 under the aegis of the Royal Academy of Letters, History and Antiquities, Stockholm.
</p>
<ul><li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Revelaciones</i> Lib. I. Ed. by C.-G. Undhagen. Stockholm 1978.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Revelaciones</i> Lib. II. Ed. by C.-G. Undhagen† and B. Bergh. Stockholm 2001.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Revelaciones</i> Lib. III. Ed. by A.-M. Jönsson. Stockholm 1998.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Revelaciones</i> Lib. IV. Ed. by H. Aili. Stockholm 1992.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Revelaciones</i> Lib. V. Ed. by B. Bergh. Uppsala 1971.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Revelaciones</i> Lib. VI. Ed. by B. Bergh. Stockholm 1991.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Revelaciones</i> Lib. VII. Ed. by B. Bergh. Uppsala 1967.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Revelaciones</i> Lib. VIII. Ed. by H. Aili. Stockholm 2002.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Revelaciones extravagantes</i> Ed. by L. Hollman. Uppsala 1956.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Opera minora</i> Vol. I. Regula Salvatoris Ed. by. S. Eklund. Stockholm 1975.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Opera minora</i> Vol. II. Sermo angelicus Ed. by. S. Eklund. Uppsala 1972.</li>
<li><i>Sancta Birgitta. Opera minora</i> Vol. III. Quattuor oraciones Ed. by. S. Eklund. Stockholm 1991.</li></ul>

</p>

<li>Birgitta of Sweden, <i>Life and selected revelations</i>, edited, with a preface by Marguerite Tjader Harris; translation and notes by Albert Ryle Kezel; introduction by Tore Nyberg, (New York: Paulist Press, 1990) [Includes translations of <i>The life of Blessed Birgitta by Prior Peter and Master Peter</i>, and Books 5 and 7 of <i>Revelationes</i>, and the Four prayers from the <i>Revelationes</i>.]</li>
