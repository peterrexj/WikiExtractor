<p><b>Oliver Plunkett</b> (or <b>Oliver Plunket</b>; <a href="/wiki/Irish_language" title="Irish language">Irish</a>: <i lang="ga">Oilibhéar Pluincéid</i>; 1 November 1625&#160;– 1 July 1681) was the Catholic <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Armagh" title="Archbishop of Armagh">Archbishop of Armagh</a> and <a href="/wiki/Primate_of_All_Ireland" class="mw-redirect" title="Primate of All Ireland">Primate of All Ireland</a> and the last victim of the <a href="/wiki/Popish_Plot" title="Popish Plot">Popish Plot</a>. He was <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> in 1920 and <a href="/wiki/Canonised" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonised">canonised</a> in 1975, thus becoming the first new Irish saint in almost seven hundred years.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGlinert200980_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGlinert200980-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Oliver_Plunkett&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Oliver Plunkett was born on 1 November 1625 (earlier biographers gave his date of birth as 1 November 1629, but 1625 has been the consensus since the 1930s)<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> in <a href="/wiki/Loughcrew" title="Loughcrew">Loughcrew</a>, <a href="/wiki/County_Meath" title="County Meath">County Meath</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ireland" title="Ireland">Ireland</a>, to well-to-do parents with <a href="/wiki/Hiberno-Norman" class="mw-redirect" title="Hiberno-Norman">Hiberno-Norman</a> ancestors. A grandson of <a href="/wiki/Baron_Killeen" class="mw-redirect" title="Baron Killeen">James Plunkett, 7th Baron Killeen</a> (died 1595), he was related by birth to a number of <a href="/wiki/Landed_gentry" title="Landed gentry">landed</a> families, such as the recently ennobled <a href="/wiki/Earls_of_Roscommon" class="mw-redirect" title="Earls of Roscommon">Earls of Roscommon</a>, as well as the long-established <a href="/wiki/Earls_of_Fingall" class="mw-redirect" title="Earls of Fingall">Earls of Fingall</a>, <a href="/wiki/Baron_Louth" title="Baron Louth">Lords Louth</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Baron_Dunsany" class="mw-redirect" title="Baron Dunsany">Lords Dunsany</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEComyn19814_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEComyn19814-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Until his sixteenth year, the boy's education was entrusted to his cousin Patrick Plunkett, Abbot of St Mary's, Dublin and brother of <a href="/wiki/Luke_Plunkett,_1st_Earl_of_Fingall" class="mw-redirect" title="Luke Plunkett, 1st Earl of Fingall">Luke Plunkett, the first Earl of Fingall</a>, who later became successively <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Ardagh" title="Bishop of Ardagh">Bishop of Ardagh</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Meath" title="Bishop of Meath">of Meath</a>. As an aspirant to the priesthood, he set out for Rome in 1647, under the care of Father <a href="/wiki/Pierfrancesco_Scarampi" title="Pierfrancesco Scarampi">Pierfrancesco Scarampi</a> of the Roman Oratory. At this time the <a href="/wiki/Irish_Confederate_Wars" title="Irish Confederate Wars">Irish Confederate Wars</a> were raging in Ireland; these were essentially conflicts between native Irish <a href="/wiki/Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic">Catholics</a>, English and Irish <a href="/wiki/Anglicans" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglicans">Anglicans</a> and <a href="/wiki/Nonconformist_(Protestantism)" title="Nonconformist (Protestantism)">Nonconformists</a>. Scarampi was the Papal envoy to the Catholic movement known as the <a href="/wiki/Confederate_Ireland" title="Confederate Ireland">Confederation of Ireland</a>. Many of Plunkett's relatives were involved in this organisation.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_Oliver_Plunkett.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b3/Saint_Oliver_Plunkett.jpg" decoding="async" width="174" height="300" class="mw-file-element" data-file-width="174" data-file-height="300" /></a><figcaption>Portrait of Oliver Plunkett</figcaption></figure>
<p>He was admitted to the <a href="/wiki/Irish_College_in_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Irish College in Rome">Irish College in Rome</a> and proved to be an able pupil.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEComyn19814_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEComyn19814-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was ordained a priest in 1654, and deputed by the Irish bishops to act as their representative in Rome. Meanwhile, the <a href="/wiki/Cromwellian_conquest_of_Ireland" title="Cromwellian conquest of Ireland">Cromwellian conquest of Ireland</a> (1649–53) had defeated the Catholic cause in Ireland; in the aftermath, the public practice of Catholicism was banned and Catholic clergy were executed. As a result, it was impossible for Plunkett to return to Ireland for many years. He petitioned to remain in Rome and, in 1657, became a professor of theology.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEComyn19814_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEComyn19814-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Throughout the period of the <a href="/wiki/English_Commonwealth" class="mw-redirect" title="English Commonwealth">Commonwealth</a> and the first years of <a href="/wiki/Charles_II_of_England" title="Charles II of England">Charles II</a>'s reign, he successfully pleaded the cause of the Irish Catholic Church, and also served as theological professor at the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Colleges" title="Roman Colleges">College of Propaganda Fide</a>. At the Congregation of Propaganda Fide on 9 July 1669 he was appointed <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Armagh" title="Archbishop of Armagh">Archbishop of Armagh</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEComyn19814_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEComyn19814-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> the Irish primatial see, and was consecrated on 30 November at <a href="/wiki/Ghent" title="Ghent">Ghent</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Ghent" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Ghent">Bishop of Ghent</a>, Eugeen-Albert, count d'Allamont. He eventually set foot on Irish soil again on 7 March 1670, as the <a href="/wiki/English_Restoration" class="mw-redirect" title="English Restoration">English Restoration</a> of 1660 had begun on a basis of toleration. The <a href="/wiki/Pallium" title="Pallium">pallium</a> was granted him in the <a href="/wiki/Papal_consistory" title="Papal consistory">Consistory</a> of 28 July 1670.
</p><p>After arriving back in Ireland, he tackled drunkenness among the clergy, writing: "Let us remove this defect from an Irish priest, and he will be a saint". The <a href="/wiki/Penal_Laws_against_Irish_Catholics" class="mw-redirect" title="Penal Laws against Irish Catholics">Penal Laws</a> had been relaxed in line with the <a href="/wiki/Declaration_of_Breda" title="Declaration of Breda">Declaration of Breda</a> in 1660<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEComyn19815_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEComyn19815-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and he was able to establish a <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a> College in <a href="/wiki/Drogheda" title="Drogheda">Drogheda</a> in 1670. A year later 150 students attended the college, no fewer than 40 of whom were Protestant, making this college the first integrated school in Ireland. His ministry was a successful one and he is said to have confirmed 48,000 Catholics over a 4-year period. The <a href="/wiki/History_of_Ireland_(1536%E2%80%931691)" title="History of Ireland (1536–1691)">government in Dublin</a>, especially under the <a href="/wiki/Lord_Lieutenant_of_Ireland" title="Lord Lieutenant of Ireland">Lord Lieutenant of Ireland</a>, the <a href="/wiki/James_Butler,_1st_Duke_of_Ormonde" class="mw-redirect" title="James Butler, 1st Duke of Ormonde">Duke of Ormonde</a> (the Protestant son of Catholic parents) extended a generous measure of toleration to the Catholic hierarchy until the mid-1670s. Plunkett was executed in Tyburn, England on 1 July 1681. He was hanged, drawn, and quartered for treason and "promoting the Roman faith."
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Popish_Plot">Popish Plot</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Oliver_Plunkett&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Popish Plot">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Popish_Plot" title="Popish Plot">Popish Plot</a></div>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Titus_Oates.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/Titus_Oates.jpg/200px-Titus_Oates.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="250" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/Titus_Oates.jpg/300px-Titus_Oates.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/Titus_Oates.jpg/400px-Titus_Oates.jpg 2x" data-file-width="800" data-file-height="1001" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Titus_Oates" title="Titus Oates">Titus Oates</a>, perpetrator of the "<a href="/wiki/Popish_Plot" title="Popish Plot">Popish Plot</a>"</figcaption></figure>
<p>On the enactment of the <a href="/wiki/Test_Act" class="mw-redirect" title="Test Act">Test Act</a> in 1673, to which Plunkett would not agree for doctrinal reasons, the college was closed and demolished. Plunkett went into hiding, travelling only in disguise, and refused a government edict to register at a seaport to await passage into exile. For the next few years he was largely left in peace since the Dublin government, except when put under pressure from the English government in <a href="/wiki/London" title="London">London</a>, preferred to leave the Catholic bishops alone.
</p><p>In 1678 the so-called <a href="/wiki/Popish_Plot" title="Popish Plot">Popish Plot</a>, concocted in England by clergyman <a href="/wiki/Titus_Oates" title="Titus Oates">Titus Oates</a>, led to further anti-Catholic action. <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_Peter_Talbot" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop Peter Talbot">Archbishop Peter Talbot</a> of <a href="/wiki/Dublin" title="Dublin">Dublin</a> was arrested, and Plunkett again went into hiding. The <a href="/wiki/Privy_Council_of_England" title="Privy Council of England">Privy Council of England</a>, in Westminster, was told that Plunkett had plotted a French invasion. The moving spirit behind the campaign is said to have been <a href="/wiki/Arthur_Capell,_1st_Earl_of_Essex" title="Arthur Capell, 1st Earl of Essex">Arthur Capell, the first Earl of Essex</a>, who had been Lord Lieutenant of Ireland in 1672-77 and hoped to resume the office by discrediting the Duke of Ormonde. However Essex was not normally a ruthless or unprincipled man, and his later plea for mercy suggests that he had never intended that Plunkett should actually die.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000225_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000225-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Trial">Trial</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Oliver_Plunkett&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Trial">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Despite being on the run and with a price on his head, Plunkett refused to leave his flock. At some point before his final <a href="/wiki/Incarceration" class="mw-redirect" title="Incarceration">incarceration</a>, he took refuge in a church that once stood in the townland of Killartry, in the parish of <a href="/wiki/Clogherhead" title="Clogherhead">Clogherhead</a> in <a href="/wiki/County_Louth" title="County Louth">County Louth</a>, seven miles outside Drogheda. He was arrested in Dublin on 6 December 1679 and imprisoned in <a href="/wiki/Dublin_Castle" title="Dublin Castle">Dublin Castle</a>, where he gave absolution to the dying Talbot. Plunkett was tried at <a href="/wiki/Dundalk" title="Dundalk">Dundalk</a> for conspiring against the state by allegedly plotting to bring 20,000 French soldiers into the country, and for levying a tax on his clergy to support 70,000 men for rebellion. Though this was unproven, some in government circles were worried about the possibility that a repetition of the <a href="/wiki/Irish_rebellion_of_1641" class="mw-redirect" title="Irish rebellion of 1641">Irish rebellion of 1641</a> was being planned and in any case, this was a convenient excuse for proceeding against Plunkett. The Duke of Ormonde, aware that Lord Essex was using the crisis to undermine him, did not defend Plunkett in public. In private however, he made clear his belief in Plunkett's innocence and his contempt for the informers against him: "silly drunken vagabonds... whom no schoolboy would trust to rob an orchard".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000233_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000233-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Microcosm_of_London_Plate_094_-_Westminster_Hall_(tone).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Microcosm_of_London_Plate_094_-_Westminster_Hall_%28tone%29.jpg/200px-Microcosm_of_London_Plate_094_-_Westminster_Hall_%28tone%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="266" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Microcosm_of_London_Plate_094_-_Westminster_Hall_%28tone%29.jpg/300px-Microcosm_of_London_Plate_094_-_Westminster_Hall_%28tone%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Microcosm_of_London_Plate_094_-_Westminster_Hall_%28tone%29.jpg/400px-Microcosm_of_London_Plate_094_-_Westminster_Hall_%28tone%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2108" data-file-height="2804" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Westminster_Hall" class="mw-redirect" title="Westminster Hall">Westminster Hall</a>, where Plunkett was tried</figcaption></figure>
<p>Plunkett did not object to facing an all-Protestant jury, but the trial soon collapsed as the prosecution witnesses were themselves wanted men and afraid to turn up in court. <a href="/wiki/Anthony_Ashley-Cooper,_1st_Earl_of_Shaftesbury" class="mw-redirect" title="Anthony Ashley-Cooper, 1st Earl of Shaftesbury">Lord Shaftesbury</a> knew Plunkett would never be convicted in Ireland, irrespective of the jury's composition, and so had Plunkett moved to <a href="/wiki/Newgate_Prison" title="Newgate Prison">Newgate Prison</a> in London in order to face trial at <a href="/wiki/Westminster_Hall" class="mw-redirect" title="Westminster Hall">Westminster Hall</a>. The first grand jury found no <a href="/wiki/True_bill" class="mw-redirect" title="True bill">true bill</a>, but he was not released. The second trial has generally been regarded as a serious miscarriage of justice; Plunkett was denied defending counsel (although <a href="/wiki/Hugh_Reily" title="Hugh Reily">Hugh Reily</a> acted as his legal advisor) and time to assemble his defence witnesses, and he was also frustrated in his attempts to obtain the criminal records of those who were to give evidence against him. His servant James McKenna, and a relative, John Plunkett, had travelled back to Ireland and failed within the time available to bring back witnesses and evidence for the defence. During the trial, Archbishop Plunkett had disputed the right of the court to try him in England and he also drew attention to the criminal past of the witnesses, but to no avail. <a href="/wiki/Lord_Chief_Justice_of_England_and_Wales" title="Lord Chief Justice of England and Wales">Lord Chief Justice</a> Sir <a href="/wiki/Francis_Pemberton" title="Francis Pemberton">Francis Pemberton</a> addressing these complaints said to Plunkett: "Look you, Mr. Plunket, it is in vain for you to talk and make this discourse here now..."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECobbett1810450_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECobbett1810450-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and later on again: "Look you, Mr. Plunket, don't mis-spend your own time; for the more you trifle in these things, the less time you will have for your defence".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECobbett1810481_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECobbett1810481-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Scottish_people" title="Scottish people">Scottish</a> clergyman and future <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Salisbury" title="Bishop of Salisbury">Bishop of Salisbury</a>, <a href="/wiki/Gilbert_Burnet" title="Gilbert Burnet">Gilbert Burnet</a>, an eyewitness to the Plot trials, had no doubt of the innocence of Plunkett, whom he praised as a wise and sober man who wished only to live peacefully and tend to his congregation.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Writing in the 19th century, <a href="/wiki/John_Campbell,_1st_Baron_Campbell" title="John Campbell, 1st Baron Campbell">Lord Campbell</a> said of the judge, Pemberton, that the trial was a disgrace to himself and his country. More recently the <a href="/wiki/High_Court_of_Justice" title="High Court of Justice">High Court</a> judge <a href="/wiki/James_Comyn" title="James Comyn">Sir James Comyn</a> called it a grave mistake: while Plunkett, by virtue of his office, was clearly guilty of "promoting the Catholic faith", and may possibly have had some dealings with the French, there was never the slightest evidence that he had conspired against the King's life.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEComyn19819_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEComyn19819-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Execution">Execution</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Oliver_Plunkett&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Execution">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Tyburn_gallows_1746.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a6/Tyburn_gallows_1746.jpg/200px-Tyburn_gallows_1746.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="198" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a6/Tyburn_gallows_1746.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="250" data-file-height="247" /></a><figcaption>Map of Tyburn <a href="/wiki/Gallows" title="Gallows">gallows</a> and immediate surroundings</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/wiki/Archbishop" title="Archbishop">Archbishop</a> Plunkett was found guilty of high treason in June 1681 "for promoting the Roman faith", and was condemned to death.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000233_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000233-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> In passing judgement, the Chief Justice said: "You have done as much as you could to dishonour God in this case; for the bottom of your treason was your setting up your false religion, than which there is not any thing more displeasing to God, or more pernicious to mankind in the world".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECobbett1810492_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECobbett1810492-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The jury returned within fifteen minutes with a guilty verdict and Archbishop Plunkett replied: "<i>Deo Gratias</i>" (Latin for "Thanks be to God").
</p><p>Numerous pleas for mercy were made but Charles II, although himself a reputed crypto-Catholic,<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> thought it too politically dangerous to spare Plunkett.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000234_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000234-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The French ambassador to England, <a href="/wiki/Paul_Barillon" title="Paul Barillon">Paul Barillon</a>, conveyed a plea for mercy from his king, <a href="/wiki/Louis_XIV_of_France" class="mw-redirect" title="Louis XIV of France">Louis XIV</a>. Charles told him frankly that he knew Plunkett to be innocent, but that the time was not right to take so bold a step as to pardon him.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000234_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000234-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Lord Essex, apparently realising too late that his intrigues had led to the condemnation of an innocent man, made a similar plea for mercy. The King, normally the most self-controlled of men, turned on Essex in fury, saying: "his blood be on your head – you could have saved him but would not, I would save him and dare not".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000234_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000234-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Plunkett was <a href="/wiki/Hanged,_drawn_and_quartered" title="Hanged, drawn and quartered">hanged, drawn and quartered</a> at <a href="/wiki/Tyburn,_London" class="mw-redirect" title="Tyburn, London">Tyburn</a> on 1 July 1681 (11 July <a href="/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates" title="Old Style and New Style dates">NS</a>), aged 55, the last Catholic <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a> to die in England.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000234_13-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKenyon2000234-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> His body was initially buried in two tin boxes, next to five <a href="/wiki/Jesuits" title="Jesuits">Jesuits</a> who had died previously, in the courtyard of <a href="/wiki/St_Giles_in_the_Fields" title="St Giles in the Fields">St Giles in the Fields church</a>. The remains were exhumed in 1683 and moved to the Benedictine monastery at <a href="/wiki/Lamspringe" title="Lamspringe">Lamspringe</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Hildesheim" title="Hildesheim">Hildesheim</a> in <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a>. The head was brought to Rome, and from there to <a href="/wiki/Armagh" title="Armagh">Armagh</a>, and eventually to Drogheda where since 29 June 1921 it has rested in <a href="/wiki/St._Peter%27s_Roman_Catholic_Church,_Drogheda" title="St. Peter&#39;s Roman Catholic Church, Drogheda">Saint Peter's Church</a>. Most of the body was brought to <a href="/wiki/Downside_Abbey" title="Downside Abbey">Downside Abbey</a>, England, where the major part is located today, with some parts remaining at Lamspringe. On the occasion of his canonization in 1975 his casket was opened and some parts of his body were given to St Peter's Church in Drogheda Ireland and also to the new <a href="/wiki/Worth_Abbey" title="Worth Abbey">Worth Abbey</a> Church consecrated 13 July 1975.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Oliver_Plunkett&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Oliver_Plunkett_Shrine,_St_Peter%27s_Church,_Drogheda,_Ireland_-_Diliff.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Oliver_Plunkett_Shrine%2C_St_Peter%27s_Church%2C_Drogheda%2C_Ireland_-_Diliff.jpg/250px-Oliver_Plunkett_Shrine%2C_St_Peter%27s_Church%2C_Drogheda%2C_Ireland_-_Diliff.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="392" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Oliver_Plunkett_Shrine%2C_St_Peter%27s_Church%2C_Drogheda%2C_Ireland_-_Diliff.jpg/375px-Oliver_Plunkett_Shrine%2C_St_Peter%27s_Church%2C_Drogheda%2C_Ireland_-_Diliff.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Oliver_Plunkett_Shrine%2C_St_Peter%27s_Church%2C_Drogheda%2C_Ireland_-_Diliff.jpg/500px-Oliver_Plunkett_Shrine%2C_St_Peter%27s_Church%2C_Drogheda%2C_Ireland_-_Diliff.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2743" data-file-height="4302" /></a><figcaption>St Oliver Plunkett's head</figcaption></figure>
<p>Oliver Plunkett was beatified in 1920 and canonized in 1975, the first new Irish saint for almost seven hundred years,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGlinert200980_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGlinert200980-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> and the first of the <a href="/wiki/Irish_martyrs" class="mw-redirect" title="Irish martyrs">Irish martyrs</a> to be beatified. For the canonisation, the customary second miracle was waived. He has since been followed by 17 other Irish martyrs who were <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> in 1992. Among them were Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Dermot_O%27Hurley" title="Dermot O&#39;Hurley">Dermot O'Hurley</a>, <a href="/wiki/Margaret_Ball" title="Margaret Ball">Margaret Ball</a>, and the <a href="/wiki/Wexford_Martyrs" title="Wexford Martyrs">Wexford Martyrs</a>.
</p><p>In 1920 a mosaic of him at <a href="/wiki/Westminster_Cathedral" title="Westminster Cathedral">Westminster Cathedral</a>, London, was created by <a href="/wiki/Boris_Anrep" title="Boris Anrep">Boris Anrep</a>.
</p><p>As a spectacle alone, a rally and Mass for St Oliver Plunkett on London's <a href="/wiki/Clapham_Common" title="Clapham Common">Clapham Common</a> was a remarkable triumph. The Common was virtually taken over for a celebration of the 300th anniversary of Plunkett's martyrdom. Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Tom%C3%A1s_%C3%93_Fiaich" title="Tomás Ó Fiaich">Tomás Ó Fiaich</a>, twenty enrobed bishops and a number of abbots mounted a stage beneath a scaffolding shelter on 1 July 1981. Ó Fiaich had flown there in a helicopter with Plunkett's head. The occasion attracted thousands of pilgrims to the park.
</p><p>In 1997 Plunkett was made a patron saint for peace and reconciliation in Ireland, adopted by the prayer group campaigning for peace in Ireland, "St. Oliver Plunkett for Peace and Reconciliation".
</p>
