<p><b>Columba of Sens</b> (probably born <b>Eporita,</b> d. 273),<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> was a virgin and nun who was born to a noble pagan family in northwestern Spain. She left Spain for France as a child to avoid being denounced as a Christian and received the baptismal name Columba, meaning "dove" in French. She settled in <a href="/wiki/Sens" title="Sens">Sens, France</a>, where the Roman <a href="/wiki/Emperor_Aurelian" class="mw-redirect" title="Emperor Aurelian">emperor Aurelian</a> noticed her and tried to force her to marry his son. When she refused, he imprisoned her. She was protected from being burned alive by a female bear and a miraculous rain shower, but was finally beheaded. Columba was venerated throughout France; a chapel was later built over her relics and the Abbey of Sens, which at one time was a pilgrimage site in her honor, was eventually built there.
</p><p>Columba is portrayed "as a crowned maiden in chains", with a dog or bear on a chain, holding a book and a peacock's feather, with an angel on a funeral pyre, or beheaded. Her principal attribute is a she-bear. Her feast day is December 31. Her cult was probably a combination of two virgin martyrs, <a href="/wiki/Columba_of_Spain" title="Columba of Spain">Columba of Spain</a> and Columba of Sens. In 1595, Italian poet and writer <a href="/wiki/Lucrezia_Marinella" title="Lucrezia Marinella">Lucrezia Marinella</a> wrote an allegorical Christian epic poem about Columba.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Columba_of_Sens&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Columba was born to a noble pagan family in <a href="/wiki/Zaragoza" title="Zaragoza">Zaragoza</a>, the northwestern region of Spain.<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> According to Catholic historian Florence Capes, Columba's history "is somewhat legendary" <sup id="cite_ref-cathencyclopedia_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathencyclopedia-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> writer Fernando Lanzi called it "hardly historical".<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Columba fled as a child to <a href="/wiki/Vienne" title="Vienne">Vienne, France</a> and was baptized, receiving the name Columba (meaning "dove" in French).<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-cox-28_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cox-28-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographer</a> Omer Englebert, she left Spain for France because she had been told it was where "a more beautiful religion flourished" and because she "had an insurmountable horror of idols".<sup id="cite_ref-englebert_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-englebert-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Historian Katharine Rabenstein stated that according to legend, Columba left Spain with other believers to avoid being denounced as a Christian.<sup id="cite_ref-rabenstein_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rabenstein-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Columba continued to <a href="/wiki/Sens" title="Sens">Sens</a>, near Paris in north-central France, where she was martyred in 273.<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Aurelian" title="Aurelian">Aurelian</a>, the Roman emperor (270-275), passed through Sens and put all the Christians there to death, but as Englebert reported, "Alone, Columba found favour in his eyes, such was the nobility and the beauty of her features revealing her high origin".<sup id="cite_ref-englebert_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-englebert-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He wanted her to marry his son, but she refused, so he locked her up in a brothel in the town <a href="/wiki/Amphitheatre" title="Amphitheatre">amphitheater</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-englebert_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-englebert-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> She was protected from harm by a female bear; Aurelian tried to burn both Columba and the bear alive, but the bear escaped and a "provincial rain put out the fire".<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Columba was condemned to death and beheaded, perhaps near a fountain named d'Azon.<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Her body was left on the ground after she was killed, but a man named Aubertus, who had prayed to her for the restoration of his sight, took care of her burial.<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-cathencyclopedia_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathencyclopedia-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Columba was at one time venerated throughout France; as Rabenstein reported, "the historical monuments of Sens still testify to this devotion".<sup id="cite_ref-rabenstein_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rabenstein-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> A chapel was later built over her relics, and the Abbey of Sens, which at one time was a pilgrimage site in her honor, was eventually built there. She was the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Andorra" title="Andorra">Andorra</a> and might have been the patron of Chevilly Church in Paris.<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-cathencyclopedia_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathencyclopedia-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> She is invoked to bring about rain or avoid drought.<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> She is portrayed "as a crowned maiden in chains",<sup id="cite_ref-rabenstein_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rabenstein-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> with a dog or bear on a chain, holding a book and a peacock's feather, with an angel on a funeral pyre, or beheaded. Her principal attribute is a she-bear.<sup id="cite_ref-rabenstein_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rabenstein-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Her feast day is December 31.<sup id="cite_ref-lanzi2_1-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lanzi2-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Columba_of_Sens&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Giovanni_Baronzio_-_St_Colomba_Saved_by_a_Bear_(detail)_-_WGA01304.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Giovanni_Baronzio_-_St_Colomba_Saved_by_a_Bear_%28detail%29_-_WGA01304.jpg/220px-Giovanni_Baronzio_-_St_Colomba_Saved_by_a_Bear_%28detail%29_-_WGA01304.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="400" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Giovanni_Baronzio_-_St_Colomba_Saved_by_a_Bear_%28detail%29_-_WGA01304.jpg/330px-Giovanni_Baronzio_-_St_Colomba_Saved_by_a_Bear_%28detail%29_-_WGA01304.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Giovanni_Baronzio_-_St_Colomba_Saved_by_a_Bear_%28detail%29_-_WGA01304.jpg/440px-Giovanni_Baronzio_-_St_Colomba_Saved_by_a_Bear_%28detail%29_-_WGA01304.jpg 2x" data-file-width="700" data-file-height="1273" /></a><figcaption>Part of the work "Stories from the life of St. Columba" by <a href="/wiki/Giovanni_Baronzio" title="Giovanni Baronzio">Giovanni Baronzio</a> (c. 1345–1350)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Historian Allyson M. Poska stated that Columba's cult was "probably a combination of two virgin martyrs":<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Columba_of_Spain" title="Columba of Spain">Columba of Spain</a> (d. 853) and Columba of Sens.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Poska speculated that a <a href="/wiki/Visigoths" title="Visigoths">Visigothic</a> church dedicated to Columba in <a href="/wiki/Bande,_Ourense" title="Bande, Ourense">Bande</a> (near the Portuguese border) could have indicated the spread of her devotion before the Muslims invaded Spain. The legend probably became conflated with Columba of Spain when settlers repopulated <a href="/wiki/Galicia_(Spain)" title="Galicia (Spain)">Galicia</a> after the Muslims were expelled from the region.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1595, Italian poet and writer <a href="/wiki/Lucrezia_Marinella" title="Lucrezia Marinella">Lucrezia Marinella</a> wrote a poem about Columba, "La Columba sacre".<sup id="cite_ref-cox-28_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cox-28-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-cox-144_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cox-144-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Writer Virginia Cox, who compared this poem with another poem and a prose piece Marinella wrote about <a href="/wiki/Francis_of_Assisi" title="Francis of Assisi">Francis of Assisi</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-cox-28_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cox-28-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> stated that Marinella's appeal to Columba's story was not devotional; rather, it was "in its intrinsic narrative interest and in the allegorical potential suggested by the heroine's name, 'Dove,' which recalls the traditional imagery of the Holy Spirit and makes her story not simply one of a single, heroic maiden but one of the triumph of Christianity as a whole".<sup id="cite_ref-cox-144_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cox-144-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> "La Columba sacre" was structured around the conflict between her and Aurelian and describes Aurelian's "initial, non-violent attempts to persuade Columba to renounce her faith"<sup id="cite_ref-cox-144_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cox-144-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> at the beginning of the poem, her beheading at the end, and her protection from rape by a "she-bear" and from being burned alive "by a heaven-sent downpour".<sup id="cite_ref-cox-144_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cox-144-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Cox said the poem, while it closely resembled the secular epic poem, reworked the figure of the epic hero through Marinella's depiction of a role reversal between Columba, who is described as a warrior, and Aurelian.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Cox stated that the poem Columba's defeat of Aurelian signified the defeat of pagan values by Christian values, as symbolized by the dove.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
