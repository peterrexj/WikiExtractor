<p><b>Cedd</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Cedda, Ceddus</i>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 620 – 26 October 664) was an <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxon</a> monk and bishop from the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of Northumbria">Kingdom of Northumbria</a>. He was an <a href="/wiki/Evangelism" title="Evangelism">evangelist</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Angles" title="Middle Angles">Middle Angles</a> and <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Essex" title="Kingdom of Essex">East Saxons</a> in <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a> and a significant participant in the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Whitby" title="Synod of Whitby">Synod of Whitby</a>, a meeting which resolved important differences within the Church in England. He is <a href="/wiki/Veneration" title="Veneration">venerated</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Anglicanism" title="Anglicanism">Anglicanism</a>, and the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Orthodox Church</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background">Background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cedd&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The little that is known about Cedd comes to us mainly from the writing of <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> in his <i><a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People" title="Ecclesiastical History of the English People">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i>. The following account is based entirely on Book 3 of Bede's History.
</p><p>Cedd was born in the kingdom of Northumbria and brought up on the island of <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a> by <a href="/wiki/Aidan_of_Lindisfarne" title="Aidan of Lindisfarne">Aidan</a> of <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">the Irish Church</a>. He had three brothers: <a href="/wiki/Chad_of_Mercia" title="Chad of Mercia">Chad of Mercia</a> (transcribed into Bede's Latin text as Ceadda), <a href="/wiki/Cynibil" title="Cynibil">Cynibil</a> and <a href="/wiki/C%C3%A6lin" title="Cælin">Cælin</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-Bede_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bede-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> All four were priests and both Cedd and Chad became bishops. Despite being of apparent Northumbrian birth, the names of all four brothers are <a href="/wiki/Brittonic_languages" title="Brittonic languages">British Celtic</a> in origin, rather than Anglo-Saxon.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The first datable reference to Cedd by Bede makes clear that he was a priest by the year 653.<sup id="cite_ref-ReferenceA_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ReferenceA-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> This probably pushes his birth date back to the early 620s. It is likely that Cedd was oldest of the brothers and was acknowledged the head of the family. He seems to have taken the lead, while Chad was his chosen successor.
</p><p>Aidan had come to Northumbria from <a href="/wiki/Iona" title="Iona">Iona</a>, bringing with him a set of practices that are known as the <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Rite" title="Celtic Rite">Celtic Rite</a>. As well as superficial differences over the <a href="/wiki/Computus" class="mw-redirect" title="Computus">Computus</a> (calculation of the date of <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Easter</a>), and the cut of the <a href="/wiki/Tonsure" title="Tonsure">tonsure</a>, these involved a pattern of Church organization fundamentally different from the <a href="/wiki/Diocese" title="Diocese">diocesan structure</a> that was evolving on the continent of Europe. Activity was based in monasteries, which supported peripatetic missionary bishops. There was a strong emphasis on personal <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a>, on Biblical <a href="/wiki/Exegesis" title="Exegesis">exegesis</a>, and on <a href="/wiki/Eschatology" title="Eschatology">eschatology</a>. Aidan was well known for his personal austerity and disregard for the trappings of wealth and power. Bede several times stresses that Cedd and Chad absorbed his example and traditions. Bede tells us that Chad and many other Northumbrians went to study with the Irish after the death of Aidan<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> (651).
</p><p>Cedd is not mentioned as one of the wandering scholars. He is portrayed by Bede as very close to Aidan's successor, Finan. So it is highly likely that he owed his entire formation as a priest and scholar to Aidan and to <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mission_to_Mercia">Mission to Mercia</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cedd&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Mission to Mercia">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 653, Cedd was sent by <a href="/wiki/Oswiu" title="Oswiu">Oswiu</a> of Northumberland with three other priests to evangelise the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Angles" title="Middle Angles">Middle Angles</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-ReferenceA_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ReferenceA-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> who were one of the core ethnic groups of <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercia</a>, based on the mid-<a href="/wiki/River_Trent" title="River Trent">Trent Valley</a>. <a href="/wiki/Peada_of_Mercia" title="Peada of Mercia">Peada of Mercia</a>, son of <a href="/wiki/Penda_of_Mercia" title="Penda of Mercia">Penda</a>, was sub-king of the Middle Angles. Peada had agreed to become a <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> in return for the hand of Oswiu's daughter, Alchflaed (c.635-c.714) in marriage. This was a time of growing Northumbrian power, as Oswiu reunited and consolidated the Northumbrian kingdom after its earlier (641/2) defeat by Penda. Peada travelled to Northumbria to negotiate his marriage and baptism.
</p><p>Cedd, together with the priests, Adda, Betti and <a href="/wiki/Diuma" title="Diuma">Diuma</a>, accompanied Peada back to Middle Anglia, where they won numerous converts of all classes. Bede relates that the pagan Penda did not obstruct preaching even among his subjects in Mercia proper, and portrays him as generally sympathetic to Christianity at this point – a very different view from the general estimate of Penda as a devoted pagan. But, the mission apparently made little headway in the wider Mercian polity. Bede credits Cedd's brother Chad with the effective evangelization of Mercia more than a decade later. To make progress among the general population, Christianity appeared to need positive royal backing, including grants of land for monasteries, rather than a benign attitude from leaders.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop_of_the_East_Saxons">Bishop of the East Saxons</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cedd&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Bishop of the East Saxons">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Cedd was soon recalled from the mission to Mercia by Oswiu, who sent him on a mission with one other priest to the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Essex" title="Kingdom of Essex">East Saxon kingdom</a>. The priests had been requested by <a href="/wiki/Sigeberht_the_Good" title="Sigeberht the Good">Sigeberht the Good</a> to reconvert his people.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The East Saxon kingdom was originally converted by missionaries from <a href="/wiki/Canterbury" title="Canterbury">Canterbury</a>, where <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury" title="Augustine of Canterbury">Augustine of Canterbury</a> had established a Roman mission in 597. The first bishop of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Rite" title="Roman Rite">Roman Rite</a> was <a href="/wiki/Mellitus" title="Mellitus">Mellitus</a>, who arrived in Essex in 604. After a decade, he was driven out of the area. The religious destiny of the kingdom was constantly in the balance, with the royal family itself divided among Christians, pagans, and some wanting to tolerate both.
</p><p>Bede tells us that Sigeberht's decision to be baptized and to reconvert his kingdom was at the initiative of Oswiu. Sigeberht travelled to Northumbria to accept <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptism</a> from Bishop <a href="/wiki/Finan_of_Lindisfarne" title="Finan of Lindisfarne">Finan of Lindisfarne</a>. Cedd went to the East Saxons partly as an emissary of the Northumbrian monarchy. Certainly his prospects were helped by the continuing military and political success of Northumbria, especially the final defeat of Penda in 655. Practically, Northumbria gained hegemony among the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms.
</p><p>After making some conversions, Cedd returned to Lindisfarne to report to Finan. In recognition of his success, Finan ordained him bishop, calling in two other Irish bishops to assist at the rite. Cedd was appointed <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a> of the East Saxons. As a result, he is generally listed among the bishops of London, a part of the East Saxon kingdom. Bede, however, generally uses ethnic descriptions for episcopal responsibilities when dealing with the generation of Cedd and Chad.
</p><p>Bede's record makes clear that Cedd demanded personal commitment and that he was unafraid to confront the powerful. He <a href="/wiki/Excommunication" title="Excommunication">excommunicated</a> a <a href="/wiki/Thegn" title="Thegn">thegn</a> who was in an unlawful marriage and forbade Christians to accept the man's hospitality. According to Bede, when Sigeberht continued to visit the man's home, Cedd went to the house to denounce the king, foretelling that he would die in that house. Bede asserts that the King's subsequent murder (660) was his penance for defying Cedd's injunction.
</p><p>After the death of Sigeberht, there were signs that Cedd had a more precarious position. The new king, <a href="/wiki/Swithhelm_of_Essex" title="Swithhelm of Essex">Swithhelm of Essex</a>, who had assassinated Sigeberht, was a pagan. He had long been a client of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwold_of_East_Anglia" title="Æthelwold of East Anglia">Æthelwold of East Anglia</a>, who was increasingly dependent on <a href="/wiki/Wulfhere_of_Mercia" title="Wulfhere of Mercia">Wulfhere of Mercia</a>, the Christian king of a newly resurgent Mercia. After some persuasion from Ethelwald, Swithelm accepted baptism from Cedd. The bishop traveled into East Anglia to baptize the king at Ethelwald's home. For a time, the East Saxon kingdom remained Christian.
</p><p>Bede presents Cedd's work as decisive in the conversion of the East Saxons, although it was preceded by other missionaries, and eventually followed by a revival of paganism. Despite the substantial work, the future suggested that all could be undone.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastic_foundations">Monastic foundations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cedd&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Monastic foundations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Cedd founded many churches. He also founded monasteries at Tilaburg (probably <a href="/wiki/East_Tilbury" title="East Tilbury">East Tilbury</a>, but possibly <a href="/wiki/West_Tilbury" title="West Tilbury">West Tilbury</a>) and Ithancester (almost certainly <a href="/wiki/Bradwell-on-Sea" title="Bradwell-on-Sea">Bradwell-on-Sea</a>).
</p><p>Cedd was appointed as abbot of the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Lastingham" title="Lastingham">Lastingham</a> in his native Northumbria at the request of the sub-king <a href="/wiki/%C5%92thelwald_of_Deira" title="Œthelwald of Deira">Œthelwald of Deira</a>. Bede records the foundation of this monastery in some detail,<sup id="cite_ref-Bede_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bede-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> showing that Ethelwald was put in contact with Cedd through Caelin, one of the bishop's brothers, who was on the king's staff. Cedd undertook a 40-day fast to purify the site, although urgent royal business took him away after 30 days, and Cynibil took over the fast for him.
</p><p>Cedd occupied the position of abbot of Lastingham to the end of his life, while maintaining his position as missionary bishop and diplomat. He often traveled far from the monastery in fulfillment of these other duties. His brother Chad, who succeeded him as abbot, did the same. Cedd and his brothers regarded Lastingham as a monastic base,<sup id="cite_ref-Mayr-Harting2010_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mayr-Harting2010-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> providing intellectual and spiritual support, and a place of <a href="/wiki/Retreat_(spiritual)" title="Retreat (spiritual)">retreat</a>. Cedd delegated daily care of Lastingham to other priests, and it is likely that Chad operated similarly.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Final_years">Final years</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cedd&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Final years">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Cedd had been brought up in the <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Rite" title="Celtic Rite">Celtic Rite</a>, which differed from the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Rite" title="Roman Rite">Roman Rite</a> in the dating of the religious calendar and other practices, including the <a href="/wiki/Tonsure" title="Tonsure">tonsure</a> of monks. Supporters of each rite met at a council within the Northumbrian kingdom known as the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Whitby" title="Synod of Whitby">Synod of Whitby</a>. The proceedings of the council were hampered by the participants' mutual incomprehension of each other's languages, which probably included <a href="/wiki/Old_Irish" title="Old Irish">Old Irish</a>, <a href="/wiki/Old_English" title="Old English">Old English</a>, <a href="/wiki/Frankish_language" title="Frankish language">Frankish</a> and <a href="/wiki/Old_Welsh" title="Old Welsh">Old Welsh</a>, as well as <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a>. Bede recounted that Cedd interpreted for both sides.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Cedd's facility with the languages, together with his status as a trusted royal emissary, likely made him a key figure in the negotiations. His skills were seen as an eschatological sign of the presence of the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Spirit" title="Holy Spirit">Holy Spirit</a>, in contrast to the Biblical account of the <a href="/wiki/Tower_of_Babel" title="Tower of Babel">Tower of Babel</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> When the council ended, Cedd returned to Essex.
</p><p>According to Bede, Cedd accepted the Roman dating of the observance of Easter.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> He returned to his work as bishop, abandoning the practices of the Irish of <a href="/wiki/D%C3%A1l_Riata" title="Dál Riata">Dál Riata</a>.
</p><p>A short time later, he returned to Northumbria and the monastery at Lastingham. He fell ill with the <a href="/wiki/Plague_(disease)" title="Plague (disease)">plague</a> and died on 26 October 664.<sup id="cite_ref-Bede_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bede-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Powicke238_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Powicke238-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Bede records that immediately after Cedd's death a party of thirty monks travelled up from Essex to Lastingham to do homage.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> All but one small boy died there, also of the plague. Cedd was initially buried at Lastingham in a grave. Later, when a stone church was built, his body was moved and re-interred in a shrine inside the church of the monastery. Chad succeeded his brother as abbot at Lastingham.
</p><p>King Swithhelm of Essex died at about the same time as Cedd. He was succeeded by the joint kings <a href="/wiki/Sighere_of_Essex" title="Sighere of Essex">Sighere</a> and <a href="/wiki/S%C3%A6bbi_of_Essex" title="Sæbbi of Essex">Sæbbi</a>. Some people reverted to paganism, which Bede said was due to the effects of the plague. Mercia under King <a href="/wiki/Wulfhere_of_Mercia" title="Wulfhere of Mercia">Wulfhere</a> was the dominant force south of the Humber, so it fell to Wulfhere to take prompt action. He dispatched Bishop <a href="/wiki/Jaruman" title="Jaruman">Jaruman</a> to take over Cedd's work among the East Saxons. Jaruman, working (according to Bede) with great discretion, toured Essex, negotiated with local magnates, and soon restored Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Commemorations">Commemorations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cedd&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Commemorations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Cedd is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(Anglicanism)" title="Commemoration (Anglicanism)">commemoration</a> on <a href="/wiki/October_26" title="October 26">26 October</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> the anniversary of his death. <i>St Cedd's Day</i> is also known as <i>Essex Day</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Chelmsford" title="Diocese of Chelmsford">Diocese of Chelmsford</a> celebrated 1954, the 13th centenary of Cedd's mission to Essex, as <i>St Cedd's Year</i>. In that year, <a href="/wiki/Chelmsford_Cathedral" title="Chelmsford Cathedral">Chelmsford Cathedral</a>, already dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Mary_(mother_of_Jesus)" class="mw-redirect" title="Mary (mother of Jesus)">St Mary the Virgin</a> was additionally dedicated to St Cedd and St Peter (to whom <a href="/wiki/Chapel_of_St_Peter-on-the-Wall" title="Chapel of St Peter-on-the-Wall">Cedd's chapel at Bradwell</a> is dedicated<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>) while events in his honour included a rally at <a href="/wiki/West_Ham_United_F.C." title="West Ham United F.C.">West Ham United's</a> <a href="/wiki/Boleyn_Ground" title="Boleyn Ground">Boleyn Ground</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The site of an ancient tree in <a href="/wiki/Polstead" title="Polstead">Polstead</a>, <a href="/wiki/Suffolk" title="Suffolk">Suffolk</a>, known as the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_Oak,_Polstead" title="Gospel Oak, Polstead">Gospel Oak</a>, is one of a number of sites where Cedd is traditionally supposed to have preached. The original tree collapsed in 1953, but its remains can still be seen among its successor trees, and a church service is held there on the first Sunday of every August.
</p>
