<p>Saint <b>Paraskeva of the Balkans</b> (also known as: Света Петка Българска, Petka of Bulgaria, Petka of Serbia, Paraskeva of Serbia, Paraskeva the Serbian, Paraskeva of Belgrade, Parascheva the New, Parascheva the Young, <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Greek language">Ancient Greek</a>: <span lang="grc">Ὁσία Παρασκευὴ ἡ Ἐπιβατινή</span>, <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Οσία Παρασκευή η Επιβατινή ή Νέα</span>, <a href="/wiki/Romanian_language" title="Romanian language">Romanian</a>: <i lang="ro">Sfânta Cuvioasă Parascheva</i>,  <a href="/wiki/Serbian_language" title="Serbian language">Serbian</a>: <span lang="sr">Света Петка</span> / Sveta Petka or Петка Параскева / Света Петка Македонка / Petka Paraskeva, Paraskeva Pyatnitsa, Parascheva of Tirnovo) was an <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a> female <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> of the 10th century.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paraskeva_of_the_Balkans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Vita_icon_of_St_Paraskeve_of_Trnovo_(Patriarchate_of_Pe%C4%87,_1719-20).png" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/Vita_icon_of_St_Paraskeve_of_Trnovo_%28Patriarchate_of_Pe%C4%87%2C_1719-20%29.png/200px-Vita_icon_of_St_Paraskeve_of_Trnovo_%28Patriarchate_of_Pe%C4%87%2C_1719-20%29.png" decoding="async" width="200" height="310" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/Vita_icon_of_St_Paraskeve_of_Trnovo_%28Patriarchate_of_Pe%C4%87%2C_1719-20%29.png/300px-Vita_icon_of_St_Paraskeve_of_Trnovo_%28Patriarchate_of_Pe%C4%87%2C_1719-20%29.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/Vita_icon_of_St_Paraskeve_of_Trnovo_%28Patriarchate_of_Pe%C4%87%2C_1719-20%29.png/400px-Vita_icon_of_St_Paraskeve_of_Trnovo_%28Patriarchate_of_Pe%C4%87%2C_1719-20%29.png 2x" data-file-width="2715" data-file-height="4205" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Vita_icon_of_St_Paraskeve_of_Trnovo_(Patriarchate_of_Pe%C4%87,_1719-20).png" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Visual <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> of St Paraskeva (<a href="/wiki/Serbian_Patriarchate_of_Pe%C4%87" title="Serbian Patriarchate of Peć">Patriarchate of Peć</a>, 1719-20).</div></div></div>
<p>Paraskeva was born in the town of <a href="/wiki/Selimpa%C5%9Fa" title="Selimpaşa">Epivates</a> (close to present-day <a href="/wiki/Istanbul" title="Istanbul">Istanbul</a>) on the shore of the <a href="/wiki/Sea_of_Marmara" title="Sea of Marmara">Sea of Marmara</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Her parents were wealthy landowners.<sup id="cite_ref-patron_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patron-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Legend says that as a child, Paraskeva heard in a church <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">the Lord</a>'s words: "Whoever wants to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow Me." (Mark 8, 34). These words would determine her to give her rich clothes away to the poor and to flee to <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-patron_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patron-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  Her parents, who did not support her decision to follow an ascetic, religious life, looked for her in various cities.  Paraskeva fled to <a href="/wiki/Chalcedon" title="Chalcedon">Chalcedon</a> in Asia Minor, and afterwards lived at the church of the Most Holy Theotokos in <a href="/wiki/Heraclea_Pontica" title="Heraclea Pontica">Heraclea Pontica</a> in <a href="/wiki/Bithynia" title="Bithynia">Bithynia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-patron_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patron-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  She led an austere life, experiencing visions of the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a>. Her travels took her to <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>; she wished to spend the rest of her life there. After seeing Jerusalem, she settled in a convent in the desert near the <a href="/wiki/River_Jordan" class="mw-redirect" title="River Jordan">River Jordan</a>.
</p><p>When she was 25, an <a href="/wiki/Angel" title="Angel">angel</a> appeared, telling her to return to her homeland.  She returned to Constantinople, and then, aged 25, lived in the village of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Mimarsinan&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Mimarsinan (page does not exist)">Kallikrateia</a>, in the church of the Holy Apostles. She died at the age of 27.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Local_church-building">Local church-building</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paraskeva_of_the_Balkans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Local church-building">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>A church dedicated to her was built in Epivates (present-day <a href="/wiki/Selimpa%C5%9Fa" title="Selimpaşa">Selimpaşa</a>) on the spot where her house of birth once stood. The oldest testimony regarding the church - written by the Russian traveller <a href="/wiki/Anthony_of_Novgorod" title="Anthony of Novgorod">Anthony of Novgorod</a> (who later became <a href="/wiki/List_of_bishops_and_archbishops_of_Novgorod" title="List of bishops and archbishops of Novgorod">Archbishop of Novgorod</a>) - dates back to the early-13th century. In August 1817 a great fire completely destroyed the church; it was rebuilt in 1820 with the financial support of the citizens of Constantinople and of the former <a href="/wiki/List_of_rulers_of_Moldavia" title="List of rulers of Moldavia">Prince of Moldo-Wallahia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Alexander_Callimachi" title="Alexander Callimachi">Alexander Kallimachi</a>. In 1885 the Community<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Avoid_weasel_words" class="mw-redirect" title="Wikipedia:Avoid weasel words"><span title="The material near this tag possibly uses too vague attribution or weasel words. (January 2022)">which?</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> demolished the old church in order to construct a much bigger one on the same site. The building, completed after 6 years, re-used parts of the 1327–1341 Byzantine tower of <a href="/wiki/Megas_doux" title="Megas doux">Duke</a> <a href="/wiki/Alexios_Apokaukos" title="Alexios Apokaukos">Alexis Apokaukos</a> as building material. It was the biggest church in the whole of Eastern Thrace (16 m in height, 26 m in width and 30 m in length), a real jewel that could be seen from kilometres away.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> It was completely demolished in the spring of 1979; a park occupies the site.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paraskeva_of_the_Balkans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Christian tradition states that after an old sinner was buried near Paraskeva’s grave, the saint protested by appearing in a dream to a local monk.  The vision informed the monk where the saint had been buried; when the body was unearthed, it was found to be <a href="/wiki/Incorruptibility" title="Incorruptibility">incorrupt</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-patron_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patron-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  The relics were <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relics)" class="mw-redirect" title="Translation (relics)">translated</a> to the church of the Holy Apostles in Kallikrateia.<sup id="cite_ref-patron_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patron-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The cult of Saint Parascheva spread in the 14th century from Bulgaria northwards into the Romanian principalities, Wallachia and Moldavia. In this period, Bishop Evtimiy of Tarnovo (1332-1402) wrote  the biography of Saint Parascheva - "Hagiography of Saint Petka of Tarnovo”.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The bishop's work was inspired from the Greek <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">Bios</a> of deacon Basilikos, written in the year 1150 by request of Constantinople Patriarch Nicholas IV Mouzelon.
</p><p>Sometimes, Saint Parascheva of Thrace(St.Petka) is named The New. There are two other saints with her name, Saint Paraskevi of Rome (2nd century) and Saint <a href="/wiki/Paraskevi_of_Iconium" title="Paraskevi of Iconium">Paraskevi of Iconium</a>.
</p><p>Some scholars wrote that there could be According to some scholars, siome overlappings between these three saints. Also, confusion might have occurred with some folk tales characters.    
Paraskeva’s cult and attributes became confused with that of other <a href="/wiki/Saint_Paraskevi_(disambiguation)" class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" title="Saint Paraskevi (disambiguation)">saints with the same name</a> as well as pre-Christian deities of the <a href="/wiki/Slavs" title="Slavs">Slavs</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Slavic_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Slavic-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>This confusion was made because the Greek name of St Parascheva was “paraskevi”, meaning “Friday”.  The translation in languages as Romanian or Serbian was “Sfânta Vineri” or “Sveta Petka” meaning Saint Friday. 
The translation from Greek language to Romanian, Serbian or Bulgarian language was sometimes misunderstood by some scholars who connected the translated name of Saint Parascheva, Saint Friday, with a certain character from folk tales having a similar name.
</p><p>As one scholar asks:
</p>
<blockquote><p>Was Parasceve, or Paraskeva, an early Christian maiden named in honor of the day of the <a href="/wiki/Crucifixion" title="Crucifixion">Crucifixion</a>?  Or was she a personification of that day, pictured cross in hand to assist the fervor of the faithful?  And was the Paraskeva of the South Slavs the same who made her appearance in northern <a href="/wiki/Russia" title="Russia">Russia</a>?<sup id="cite_ref-Slavic_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Slavic-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>The answer is that there is a complete separation between the 10th-century Christian Saint Parascheva The New (called "of the Balkans") and the folk character mentioned above, derived perhaps from  pre-Christian mystical beliefs. 
The separation is made by rich biography and iconography transferred from the 10th century to 21st, all this information and studies being connected to a real person who lived in that period.
</p><p>Hagiographies of Saint Parascheva (Petka) were written by: deacon Basilikos in 1150, bishop Evtimiy of Tarnovo in ca. 1385, metropolitan Matei of Mira in 1605, metropolitan Varlaam of Moldova in 1643, Saint Nikodimos the Athonite (19th century), Romanian Bishop Melchisedec of Roman in 1889.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The cults of <a href="/wiki/Paraskevi_of_Iconium" title="Paraskevi of Iconium">Paraskevi of Iconium</a> (Paraskeva-Pyatnitsa) and Paraskeva of the Balkans were conflated with that of a <a href="/wiki/Slavic_mythology" class="mw-redirect" title="Slavic mythology">Slavic</a> deity associated with Friday, alternatively known as Petka, Pyatnitsa, or Zhiva.<sup id="cite_ref-Hubbs_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hubbs-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-rybakov_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rybakov-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  
Attributes, such as the association with spinning, were also merged into the cult of these saints.<sup id="cite_ref-Hubbs_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hubbs-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Any confusion was clarified after Romanian Orthodox Church decided on 28 February 1950 to generalise the cult of Saint Parascheva The New.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>  
The generalisation of the cult was celebrated on 14 October 1955 in Iasi Cathedral with the presence of high rank clerics from Bulgaria and Russia.
</p><p>Some modern Romanian theologians published studies about Saint Parascheva: Pr. Gh. Păvăloiu (1935), Arhim. Varahil Jitaru (1942), D. Stănescu (1938), Pr. M. Țesan (1955), Pr. Scarlat Porcescu, Pr. Prof. Mircea Păcurariu.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Relics">Relics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paraskeva_of_the_Balkans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Relics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In subsequent years, Paraskevi’s relics were transferred to various churches in the region.<sup id="cite_ref-patron_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patron-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1238, the relics were transferred from Kallikrateia to <a href="/wiki/Veliko_Tarnovo" title="Veliko Tarnovo">Veliko Tarnovo</a>, capital of the <a href="/wiki/Second_Bulgarian_Empire" title="Second Bulgarian Empire">Second Bulgarian Empire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-roca_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-roca-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1393, they were transferred to <a href="/wiki/Belgrade" title="Belgrade">Belgrade</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-roca_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-roca-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> specifically the <a href="/wiki/Ru%C5%BEica_Church" title="Ružica Church">Ružica Church</a>.  When Belgrade fell to <a href="/wiki/Ottoman_Empire" title="Ottoman Empire">Ottoman</a> forces in 1521, the relics were transferred to Constantinople.  In 1641, the relics were transferred to <a href="/wiki/Trei_Ierarhi_Monastery" title="Trei Ierarhi Monastery">Trei Ierarhi Monastery</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Ia%C5%9Fi" class="mw-redirect" title="Iaşi">Iaşi</a>, <a href="/wiki/Moldavia" title="Moldavia">Moldavia</a> (nowadays, eastern part of <a href="/wiki/Romania" title="Romania">Romania</a>).  In 1888, they were transferred to the <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_Cathedral,_Ia%C5%9Fi" class="mw-redirect" title="Metropolitan Cathedral, Iaşi">Metropolitan Cathedral of Iaşi</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-patron_2-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patron-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A severe drought in 1946-47 affected Moldavia, adding to the misery left by the <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">war</a>. Metropolitan <a href="/wiki/Justinian_Marina" class="mw-redirect" title="Justinian Marina">Justinian Marina</a> permitted the first procession featuring the coffin containing the relics of Saint Paraskevi, kept at Iaşi since then. The relics wended their way through the drought-deserted villages of Iaşi, Vaslui, <a href="/wiki/Roman,_Romania" title="Roman, Romania">Roman</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bac%C4%83u" title="Bacău">Bacău</a>, <a href="/wiki/Putna,_Suceava" title="Putna, Suceava">Putna</a>, Neamţ, Baia and Botoşani Counties. The offerings collected on this occasion were distributed, based on Metropolitan Justinian's decisions, to orphans, widows, invalids, school cafeterias, churches under construction, and to monasteries in order to feed the sick, and old or feeble monks.<sup id="cite_ref-dobrogea_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dobrogea-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Ia.C8.99i_Pilgrimage"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Iași_Pilgrimage">Iași Pilgrimage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paraskeva_of_the_Balkans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Iași Pilgrimage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Pilgrimage at the shrines located in the <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_Cathedral,_Ia%C8%99i" title="Metropolitan Cathedral, Iași">Metropolitan Cathedral of Iași</a> has become one of the major religious events in Romania. Hundreds of thousands of pilgrims gather each year in <a href="/wiki/Ia%C8%99i" title="Iași">Iași</a> in the second weekend of October to commemorate St. Parascheva, while the city itself established its Celebration Days at the same time.
</p>
