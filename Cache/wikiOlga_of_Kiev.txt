<p><b>Olga</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_East_Slavic_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old East Slavic language">Old East Slavic</a>: <span lang="orv">Вольга</span>, <small>romanized:&#160;</small><span title="Old East Slavic-language romanization"><i lang="orv-Latn">Volĭga</i></span>;<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup>  <a href="/wiki/Old_Norse" title="Old Norse">Old Norse</a>: <i><span title="Old Norse-language text"><i lang="non">Helga</i></span></i>; <a href="/wiki/Lithuanian_language" title="Lithuanian language">Lith</a>: <i>Alge</i>; Christian name: <i>Elena</i>; c. 890–925 – 969) was a <a href="/wiki/Regent" title="Regent">regent</a> of <a href="/wiki/Kievan_Rus%27" title="Kievan Rus&#39;">Kievan Rus'</a> for her son <a href="/wiki/Sviatoslav_I_of_Kiev" class="mw-redirect" title="Sviatoslav I of Kiev">Sviatoslav</a> from 945 until 960. Following her baptism, Olga took the name <b>Elenа</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_East_Slavic_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old East Slavic language">Old East Slavic</a>: <span lang="orv">Ѡлена</span>, <small>romanized:&#160;</small><span title="Old East Slavic-language romanization"><i lang="orv-Latn">Olena</i></span>).<sup id="cite_ref-:5_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:5-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> She is known for her subjugation of the <a href="/wiki/Drevlians" title="Drevlians">Drevlians</a>, a tribe that had killed her husband <a href="/wiki/Igor_of_Kiev" title="Igor of Kiev">Igor of Kiev</a>. Even though it was her grandson <a href="/wiki/Vladimir_the_Great" title="Vladimir the Great">Vladimir</a> who converted the entire nation to Christianity, because of her efforts to spread <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a> through Rus', Olga is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> with the epithet "<a href="/wiki/Equal-to-apostles" title="Equal-to-apostles">Equal to the Apostles</a>". Her <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a> is 11 July.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span></h3>
<p>While Olga's birthdate is unknown, it could be as early as 890 AD and as late as 925 AD.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> According to the <i><a href="/wiki/Primary_Chronicle" title="Primary Chronicle">Primary Chronicle</a>,</i> Olga was of <a href="/wiki/Varangians" title="Varangians">Varangian</a> (<a href="/wiki/Vikings" title="Vikings">Viking</a>) origin and was born in <a href="/wiki/Pskov" title="Pskov">Pleskov</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEVernadsky194839_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVernadsky194839-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Little is known about her life before her marriage to Prince Igor I of Kiev and the birth of their son, Sviatoslav.<sup id="cite_ref-ozy_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ozy-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/wiki/Alexey_Karpov" title="Alexey Karpov">Alexey Karpov</a>, a specialist in the history of ancient Russia, Olga was no more than 15 years old at the time of her marriage. Igor was the son and heir of <a href="/wiki/Rurik" title="Rurik">Rurik</a>, founder of the <a href="/wiki/Rurik_dynasty" title="Rurik dynasty">Rurik dynasty</a>. After his father's death, Igor was under the guardianship of <a href="/wiki/Oleg_of_Novgorod" title="Oleg of Novgorod">Oleg</a>, who had consolidated power in the region, conquering neighboring tribes and establishing a capital in Kiev.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> This loose tribal federation became known as Kievan Rus', a territory covering what are now parts of <a href="/wiki/Russia" title="Russia">Russia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ukraine" title="Ukraine">Ukraine</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Belarus" title="Belarus">Belarus</a>.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Drevlians" title="Drevlians">Drevlians</a> were a neighboring tribe with which the growing Kievan Rus' empire had a complex relationship. The Drevlians had joined Kievan Rus' in military campaigns against the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine Empire</a> and paid <a href="/wiki/Tribute" title="Tribute">tribute</a> to Igor's predecessors. They stopped paying tribute upon Oleg's death and instead gave money to a local warlord. In 945, Igor set out to the Drevlian capital, <a href="/wiki/Korosten" title="Korosten">Iskorosten</a>, to force the tribe to pay tribute to Kievan Rus'.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Confronted by Igor's larger army, the Drevlians backed down and paid him. As Igor and his army rode home, however, he decided the payment was not enough and returned, with only a small escort, seeking more tribute.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Upon his arrival in their territory, the Drevlians murdered Igor. According to the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine</a> chronicler <a href="/wiki/Leo_the_Deacon" title="Leo the Deacon">Leo the Deacon</a>, Igor's death was caused by a gruesome act of torture in which he was "captured by them, tied to tree trunks, and torn in two."<sup id="cite_ref-Leo_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Leo-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> D. Sullivan has suggested that Leo may have invented this sensationalist version of Igor's death, taking inspiration from <a href="/wiki/Diodorus_Siculus" title="Diodorus Siculus">Diodorus Siculus</a>' account of a similar killing method used by the robber <a href="/wiki/Sinis_(mythology)" title="Sinis (mythology)">Sinis</a>, who lived near the <a href="/wiki/Isthmus_of_Corinth" title="Isthmus of Corinth">Isthmus of Corinth</a> and was killed by <a href="/wiki/Theseus" title="Theseus">Theseus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Leo_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Leo-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

A sketch by <a href="/wiki/Vasily_Surikov" title="Vasily Surikov">Vasily Surikov</a>.</div></div></div>
<p>After Igor's death in 945, Olga ruled <a href="/wiki/Kievan_Rus%27" title="Kievan Rus&#39;">Kievan Rus'</a> as regent on behalf of their son <a href="/wiki/Sviatoslav_I" title="Sviatoslav I">Sviatoslav</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> She was the first woman to rule Kievan Rus'.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
Little is known about Olga's tenure as ruler of Kiev, but the <i>Primary Chronicle</i> does give an account of her accession to the throne and her bloody revenge on the Drevlians for the murder of her husband as well as some insight into her role as civil leader of the Kievan people.
</p><p>According to archeologist Sergei Beletsky, <a href="/wiki/Knyaginya" class="mw-redirect" title="Knyaginya">Knyaginya</a> Olga, like all the other rulers before <a href="/wiki/Vladimir_the_Great" title="Vladimir the Great">Vladimir the Great</a>, was also using the <a href="/wiki/Bident" title="Bident">bident</a> as her <a href="/wiki/Symbols_of_the_Rurikids" title="Symbols of the Rurikids">personal symbol</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Drevlian_Uprising">Drevlian Uprising</span></h4>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Olga,_1895_(priv._coll).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/St._Olga%2C_1895_%28priv._coll%29.jpg/200px-St._Olga%2C_1895_%28priv._coll%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="185" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/St._Olga%2C_1895_%28priv._coll%29.jpg/300px-St._Olga%2C_1895_%28priv._coll%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/St._Olga%2C_1895_%28priv._coll%29.jpg/400px-St._Olga%2C_1895_%28priv._coll%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="600" data-file-height="556" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Olga,_1895_(priv._coll).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Romanov" class="mw-redirect" title="Romanov">Romanov</a> Imperial icon created in 1895 of Saint Olga. Silver, gold, color enamel, tempera. Collection V.Logvinenko</div></div></div><p>
After Igor's death at the hands of the Drevlians, Olga assumed the throne because her three-year-old son Sviatoslav was too young to rule. The Drevlians, emboldened by their success in ambushing and killing the king, sent a messenger to Olga proposing that she marry his murderer, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Mal_(prince)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Mal (prince) (page does not exist)">Prince Mal</a>. Twenty Drevlian negotiators boated to Kiev to pass along their king's message and to ensure Olga's compliance. They arrived in her court and told the queen why they were in Kiev: "to report that they had slain her husband<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>... and that Olga should come and marry their Prince Mal."<sup id="cite_ref-:3_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Olga responded:</p><blockquote><p>Your proposal is pleasing to me, indeed, my husband cannot rise again from the dead. But I desire to honor you tomorrow in the presence of my people. Return now to your boat, and remain there with an aspect of arrogance. I shall send for you on the morrow, and you shall say, "We will not ride on horses nor go on foot, carry us in our boat." And you shall be carried in your boat.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote><p>When the Drevlians returned the next day, they waited outside Olga's court to receive the honor she had promised. When they repeated the words she had told them to say, the people of Kiev rose up, carrying the Drevlians in their boat. The ambassadors believed this was a great honor as if they were being carried by <a href="/wiki/Palanquin" class="mw-redirect" title="Palanquin">palanquin</a>. The people brought them into the court where they were dropped into a trench that had been dug the day before under Olga's orders where the ambassadors were buried alive. It is written that Olga bent down to watch them as they were buried and "inquired whether they found the honor to their taste."<sup id="cite_ref-:3_15-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Olga then sent a message to the Drevlians that they should send "their distinguished men to her in Kiev, so that she might go to their Prince with due honor."<sup id="cite_ref-:3_15-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> The Drevlians, unaware of the fate of the first diplomatic party, gathered another party of men to send "the best men who governed the land of Dereva."<sup id="cite_ref-:3_15-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> When they arrived, Olga commanded her people to draw them a bath and invited the men to appear before her after they had bathed. When the Drevlians entered the bathhouse, Olga had it set on fire from the doors, so that all the Drevlians within burned to death.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_15-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Olga sent another message to the Drevlians, this time ordering them to "prepare great quantities of mead in the city where you killed my husband, that I may weep over his grave and hold a funeral feast for him."<sup id="cite_ref-:3_15-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> When Olga and a small group of attendants arrived at Igor's tomb, she did indeed weep and hold a funeral feast. The Drevlians sat down to join them and began to drink heavily. When the Drevlians were drunk, she ordered her followers to kill them, "and went about herself egging on her retinue to the massacre of the Drevlians."<sup id="cite_ref-:3_15-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> According to the <i>Primary Chronicle</i>, five thousand Drevlians were killed on this night, but Olga returned to Kiev to prepare an army to finish off the survivors.
</p><p>The initial conflict between the armies of the two nations went very well for the forces of Kievan Rus', who won the battle handily and drove the survivors back into their cities. Olga then led her army to Iskorosten (what is today <a href="/wiki/Korosten" title="Korosten">Korosten</a>), the city where her husband had been slain, and laid siege to the city. The siege lasted for a year without success when Olga thought of a plan to trick the Drevlians. She sent them a message: "Why do you persist in holding out? All your cities have surrendered to me and submitted to tribute, so that the inhabitants now cultivate their fields and their lands in peace. But you had rather die of hunger, without submitting to tribute."<sup id="cite_ref-:4_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:4-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The Drevlians responded that they would submit to tribute, but that they were afraid she was still intent on avenging her husband. Olga answered that the murder of the messengers sent to Kiev, as well as the events of the feast night, had been enough for her. She then asked them for a small request: "Give me three pigeons<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>... and three sparrows from each house."<sup id="cite_ref-:4_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:4-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The Drevlians rejoiced at the prospect of the siege ending for so small a price, and did as she asked.
</p><p>Olga then instructed her army to attach a piece of sulphur bound with small pieces of cloth to each bird. At nightfall, Olga told her soldiers to set the pieces aflame and release the birds. They returned to their nests within the city, which subsequently set the city ablaze. As the <i>Primary Chronicle</i> tells it: "There was not a house that was not consumed, and it was impossible to extinguish the flames, because all the houses caught fire at once."<sup id="cite_ref-:4_16-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:4-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> As the people fled the burning city, Olga ordered her soldiers to catch them, killing some of them and giving the others as slaves to her followers. She left the remnant to pay tribute.
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Governance">Governance</span></h4>
<p>Olga remained <a href="/wiki/Regent" title="Regent">regent</a> ruler of Kievan Rus' with the support of the army and her people. She changed the system of tribute gathering (<a href="/wiki/Poliudie" title="Poliudie">poliudie</a>) in the first legal reform recorded in Eastern Europe. She continued to evade proposals of marriage, defended the city during the <a href="/wiki/Siege_of_Kiev_(968)" title="Siege of Kiev (968)">Siege of Kiev</a> in 968, and saved the power of the throne for her son.
</p><p>After her dramatic subjugation of the Drevlians, the <i>Primary Chronicle</i> recounts how Olga "passed through the land of Dereva, accompanied by her son and her retinue, establishing laws and tribute. Her trading posts and hunting-reserves are there still."<sup id="cite_ref-:4_16-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:4-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> As queen, Olga established trading-posts and collected tribute along the Msta and the Luga rivers. She established hunting grounds, boundary posts, towns, and trading-posts across the empire. Olga's work helped to centralize state rule with these trade centers, called <a href="/wiki/Pogost" title="Pogost"><i>pogosti</i></a>, which served as administrative centers in addition to their mercantile roles. Olga's network of <i>pogosti</i> would prove important in the ethnic and cultural unification of the Rus' people, and her border posts began the establishment of national boundaries for the kingdom.
</p><p>During her son's prolonged military campaigns, she remained in charge of Kiev, residing in the castle of <a href="/wiki/Vyshhorod" title="Vyshhorod">Vyshgorod</a> with her grandsons.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Christianity">Christianity</span></h3>
<p>The <i>Primary Chronicle</i> does not go into additional detail about Olga's time as regent, but does tell the story of her conversion to Christianity and subsequent effect on the acceptance of Christianity in Eastern Europe.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Lemko_Church_of_Saints_Vladimir_and_Olga.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Lemko_Church_of_Saints_Vladimir_and_Olga.jpg/200px-Lemko_Church_of_Saints_Vladimir_and_Olga.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="267" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Lemko_Church_of_Saints_Vladimir_and_Olga.jpg/301px-Lemko_Church_of_Saints_Vladimir_and_Olga.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Lemko_Church_of_Saints_Vladimir_and_Olga.jpg/400px-Lemko_Church_of_Saints_Vladimir_and_Olga.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2976" data-file-height="3968" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Lemko_Church_of_Saints_Vladimir_and_Olga.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>The <a href="/wiki/Lemko" class="mw-redirect" title="Lemko">Lemko</a> church of Saints Vladimir and Olga, now located at the Museum of National Folk Architecture and Rural Life in <a href="/wiki/Lviv" title="Lviv">Lviv</a></div></div></div>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Conversion">Conversion</span></h4><p>
In the 950s, Olga traveled to <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>, the capital of the Byzantine Empire, to visit Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantine_VII" title="Constantine VII">Constantine VII</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Once in Constantinople, Olga converted to Christianity with the assistance of the Emperor and the <a href="/wiki/Patriarch" title="Patriarch">Patriarch</a>. While the <i>Primary Chronicle</i> does not divulge Olga's motivation for her visit or conversion, it does go into great detail on the conversion process, in which she was baptized and instructed in the ways of Christianity:</p><blockquote><p>The reigning Emperor was named Constantine, son of Leo. Olga came before him, and when he saw that she was very fair of countenance and wise as well, the Emperor wondered at her intellect. He conversed with her and remarked that she was worthy to reign with him in his city. When Olga heard his words, she replied that she was still a pagan, and that if he desired to baptize her, he should perform this function himself; otherwise, she was unwilling to accept baptism. The Emperor, with the assistance of the Patriarch, accordingly baptized her. When Olga was enlightened, she rejoiced in soul and body. The Patriarch, who instructed her in the faith, said to her, "Blessed art thou among the women of Rus', for thou hast loved the light, and quit the darkness. The sons of Rus' shall bless thee to the last generation of thy descendants." He taught her the doctrine of the church, and instructed her in prayer and fasting, in almsgiving, and in the maintenance of chastity. She bowed her head, and like a sponge absorbing water, she eagerly drank in his teachings. The Princess bowed before the Patriarch, saying, "Through thy prayers, Holy Father, may I be preserved from the crafts and assaults of the devil!" At her baptism she was christened Helena, after the ancient Empress, mother of Constantine the Great. The Patriarch then blessed her and dismissed her.<sup id="cite_ref-:5_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:5-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote><p>While the <i>Primary Chronicle</i> notes that Olga was christened with the name "Helena" after the ancient <a href="/wiki/Helena_(empress)" class="mw-redirect" title="Helena (empress)">Saint Helena</a> (the mother of <a href="/wiki/Constantine_the_Great" title="Constantine the Great">Constantine the Great</a>), Jonathan Shepard argues that Olga's baptismal name comes from the contemporary emperor's wife, Helena.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> The observation that Olga was "worthy to reign with him in his city" suggests that the emperor was interested in marrying her. While the <i>Chronicle</i> explains Constantine's desire to take Olga as his wife as stemming from the fact that she was "fair of countenance and wise as well," marrying Olga could certainly have helped him gain power over Rus'.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
The <i>Chronicle</i> recounts that Olga asked the emperor to baptize her knowing that his baptismal sponsorship, by the rules of spiritual kinship, would make marriage between them a kind of spiritual incest.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Though her desire to become Christian may have been genuine, this request was also a way for her to maintain political independence.  After the baptism, when Constantine repeated his marriage proposal, Olga answered that she could not marry him since church law forbade a goddaughter to marry her godfather:</p><blockquote><p>After her baptism, the Emperor summoned Olga and made known to her that he wished her to become his wife. But she replied, "How can you marry me, after yourself baptizing me and calling me your daughter? For among Christians that is unlawful, as you yourself must know." Then the Emperor said, "Olga, you have outwitted me." He gave her many gifts of gold, silver, silks, and various vases, and dismissed her, still calling her his daughter.<sup id="cite_ref-:5_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:5-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Francis Butler argues that the story of the proposal was a literary embellishment, describing an event that is highly unlikely to have ever actually occurred.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> In fact, at the time of her baptism, Constantine already had an empress. In addition to uncertainty over the truth of the <i>Chronicle</i><span class="nowrap" style="padding-left:0.1em;">&#39;s</span> telling of events in Constantinople, there is controversy over the details of her conversion to Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> According to Russian sources, she was baptized in Constantinople in 957. Byzantine sources, however, indicate that she was a Christian prior to her 957 visit.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It seems likely that she was baptized in Kiev around 955 and, following a second christening in Constantinople, took the Christian name Helen. Olga was not the first person from Rus' to convert from her pagan ways-- there were Christians in Igor's court who had taken oaths at the St. Elias Church in Kiev for the <a href="/wiki/Rus%27%E2%80%93Byzantine_Treaty_(945)" class="mw-redirect" title="Rus&#39;–Byzantine Treaty (945)">Rus'–Byzantine Treaty</a> in 945--but she was the most powerful Rus' individual to undergo baptism during her life.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span id="Efforts_to_Christianize_Kievan_Rus.27"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Efforts_to_Christianize_Kievan_Rus'">Efforts to Christianize Kievan Rus'</span></h4>
<p><i>The Primary</i> <i>Chronicle</i> reports that Olga received the Patriarch's blessing for her journey home, and that once she arrived, she unsuccessfully attempted to convert her son to Christianity:</p><blockquote><p>Now Olga dwelt with her son Sviatoslav, and she urged him to be baptized, but he would not listen to her suggestion, though when any man wished to be baptized, he was not hindered, but only mocked. For to the infidels, the Christian faith is foolishness. They do not comprehend it, because they walk in darkness and do not see the glory of God. Their hearts are hardened, and they can neither hear with their ears nor see with their eyes. For Solomon has said, "The deeds of the unrighteous are far from wisdom. Inasmuch as I have called you, and ye heard me not, I sharpened my words, and ye understood not. But ye have set at nought all my counsel, and would have none of my reproach. For they have hated knowledge, and the fear of Jehovah they have not chosen. They would none of my counsel, but despised all my reproof."<sup id="cite_ref-:5_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:5-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote><p>This passage highlights the hostility towards Christianity in Kievan Rus' in the tenth century. In the <i>Chronicle,</i> Sviatoslav declares that his followers would "laugh" if he were to accept Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-:5_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:5-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> While Olga tried to convince her son that his followers would follow his example if he converted, her efforts were in vain. However, her son agreed not to persecute those in his kingdom who did convert, which marked a crucial turning point for Christianity in the area.<sup id="cite_ref-:7_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:7-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Despite the resistance of her people to Christianity, Olga built churches in Kiev, Pskov, and elsewhere.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Relations_with_the_Holy_Roman_Emperor">Relations with the Holy Roman Emperor</span></h4>
<p>Seven Latin sources document Olga's embassy to Holy Roman Emperor <a href="/wiki/Otto_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Otto I, Holy Roman Emperor">Otto I</a> in 959. The continuation of <a href="/wiki/Regino_of_Pr%C3%BCm" title="Regino of Prüm">Regino of Prüm</a> mentions that the envoys requested the emperor to appoint a bishop and priests for their nation. The chronicler accuses the envoys of lies, commenting that their trick was not exposed until later. <a href="/wiki/Thietmar_of_Merseburg" title="Thietmar of Merseburg">Thietmar of Merseburg</a> says that the first <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Magdeburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Magdeburg">archbishop of Magdeburg</a>, <a href="/wiki/Adalbert_(Archbishop_of_Magdeburg)" class="mw-redirect" title="Adalbert (Archbishop of Magdeburg)">Adalbert of Magdeburg</a>, before being promoted to this high rank, was sent by Emperor Otto to the country of the Rus' (<i>Rusciae</i>) as a simple bishop but was expelled by pagan allies of <a href="/wiki/Sviatoslav_I,_Prince_of_Kiev" class="mw-redirect" title="Sviatoslav I, Prince of Kiev">Sviatoslav I</a>. The same data is repeated in the annals of <a href="/wiki/Quedlinburg" title="Quedlinburg">Quedlinburg</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hildesheim" title="Hildesheim">Hildesheim</a>. 
</p><p>In 2018, Russian historian and writer <a href="/wiki/Boris_Akunin" title="Boris Akunin">Boris Akunin</a> pointed out the importance of a 2-year gap between invitation and arrival of bishops: "The failure of Olga's Byzantine trip has inflicted a severe blow to her party. The Grand Knyaginya made a second attempt to find a Christian patron, now in the West. But it seems, in the period between the sending of the embassy to Emperor Otto in 959 and the arrival of Adalbert in Kiev in 961, a bloodless coup took place. Pagan party prevailed, the young Sviatoslav pushed  his mother into the background, and that's why the German bishops had to return empty-handed."<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Russian historian <a href="/wiki/Vladimir_Petrukhin" title="Vladimir Petrukhin">Vladimir Petrukhin</a>, Olga invited the Roman rite bishops because she wanted to motivate Byzantine priests to <a href="/wiki/Catechesis" title="Catechesis">catechize</a> the Rus' people more enthusiastically, by introducing competition.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span></h2><p>
According to the <i>Primary Chronicle</i>, Olga died from illness in 969, soon after the <a href="/wiki/Pechenegs" title="Pechenegs">Pechenegs'</a> siege of the city.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> When Sviatoslav announced plans to move his throne to the Danube region, the ailing Olga convinced him to stay with her during her final days. Only three days later, she died and her family and larger parts of Kievan Rus’ mourned:</p><blockquote><p>Sviatoslav announced to his mother and his boyars, "I do not care to remain in Kiev, but should prefer to live in <a href="/wiki/Pereyaslavets" title="Pereyaslavets">Peryaslavets on the Danube</a>, since that is the centre of my realm, where all riches are concentrated; gold, silks, wine, and various fruits from Greece, silver and horses from Hungary and Bohemia, and from Rus' furs, wax, honey, and slaves." But Olga made reply, "You behold me in my weakness. Why do you desire to depart from me?" For she was already in precarious health. She thus remonstrated with him and begged him first to bury her and then to go wheresoever he would. Three days later Olga died. Her son wept for her with great mourning, as did likewise her grandsons and all the people. They thus carried her out, and buried her in her tomb. Olga had given command not to hold a funeral feast for her, for she had a priest who performed the last rites over the sainted Princess.<sup id="cite_ref-:8_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:8-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote><p>Although he disapproved of his mother's Christian tradition, Sviatoslav heeded Olga's request that her priest, Gregory, conduct a Christian funeral without the ritual pagan burial feast.<sup id="cite_ref-:6_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:6-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> Her tomb remained in Kiev for over two centuries, but was destroyed by the Mongolian-Tatar armies of <a href="/wiki/Batu_Khan" title="Batu Khan">Batu Khan</a> in 1240.<sup id="cite_ref-:6_31-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:6-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Sainthood">Sainthood</span></h3><p>
At the time of her death, it seemed that Olga's attempt to make Kievan Rus' a Christian territory had been a failure. Nonetheless, Olga's Christianizing mission would be brought to fruition by her grandson, <a href="/wiki/Vladimir_the_Great" title="Vladimir the Great">Vladimir</a>, who officially adopted <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a> in 988.<sup id="cite_ref-:6_31-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:6-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Primary Chronicle</i> highlights Olga's holiness in contrast to the pagans around her during her life as well as the significance of her decision to convert to Christianity:</p><blockquote><p>Olga was the precursor of the Christian land, even as the day-spring precedes the sun and as the dawn precedes the day. For she shone like the moon by night, and she was radiant among the infidels like a pearl in the mire, since the people were soiled, and not yet purified of their sin by holy baptism. But she herself was cleansed by this sacred purification<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>.... She was the first from Rus' to enter the kingdom of God, and the sons of Rus' thus praise her as their leader, for since her death she has interceded with God in their behalf.<sup id="cite_ref-:8_30-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:8-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote><p>In 1547, nearly 600 years after her 969 death, the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodox Church</a> named Olga a saint.<sup id="cite_ref-:7_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:7-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Because of her proselytizing influence, the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Ruthenian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Ruthenian Greek Catholic Church">Ruthenian Greek Catholic Church</a>, and the <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church">Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church</a> call Olga by the honorific Isapóstolos, "Equal to the Apostles". Olga's feast day is July 11, the date of her death.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> In keeping with her own biography, she is the patron of widows and converts.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-33">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Honorific title "Equal to the Apostles" was first given to her on the initiative of Archimandrite Innokenty (Prosvirnin) in the 80s of the 20th century.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Olga is venerated as saint in East Slavic-speaking countries where churches uses the Byzantine Rite: <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> (especially in <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodox Church</a>), <a href="/wiki/Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Greek Catholic Church">Greek Catholic Church</a> (especially in the <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church">Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church</a>), in churches with <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Rite_Lutheranism" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine Rite Lutheranism">Byzantine Rite Lutheranism</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> and among <a href="/wiki/Latin_Church" title="Latin Church">Western Catholics</a> in Russia.<sup id="cite_ref-Roman_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Roman-36">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Modern_reception">Modern reception</span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:152px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Coat_of_Arms_of_Lach%C4%8Dycy,_Belarus.png" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Coat_of_Arms_of_Lach%C4%8Dycy%2C_Belarus.png/150px-Coat_of_Arms_of_Lach%C4%8Dycy%2C_Belarus.png" decoding="async" width="150" height="201" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8f/Coat_of_Arms_of_Lach%C4%8Dycy%2C_Belarus.png 1.5x" data-file-width="195" data-file-height="261" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Coat_of_Arms_of_Lach%C4%8Dycy,_Belarus.png" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Image of Saint Olga on a seal of <a href="/wiki/Lyachchyzy" title="Lyachchyzy">Lyachchyzy</a> village in <a href="/wiki/Belarus" title="Belarus">Belarus</a>. The sword is commonly included in Olga's modern iconography linking her to the <a href="/wiki/Bogatyr#Female_bogatyr" title="Bogatyr">female bogatyr</a> image.<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup></div></div></div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:152px;"><a href="/wiki/File:1000_Olga.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/1000_Olga.jpg/150px-1000_Olga.jpg" decoding="async" width="150" height="383" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/1000_Olga.jpg/225px-1000_Olga.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/1000_Olga.jpg/300px-1000_Olga.jpg 2x" data-file-width="397" data-file-height="1014" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:1000_Olga.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Olga on the <i><a href="/wiki/Millennium_of_Russia" title="Millennium of Russia">Millennium of Russia</a></i> monument (1862) by M. Mikeshin</div></div></div>
<p>Scholarship has traditionally focused on Olga's role in the spread of Christianity to Eastern Europe and Russia as well as her role in advising her son against persecution of Christians in the Kievan Rus'.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (February 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> 
</p><p>Modern publications, on the other hand, reflect a broader interest in Olga beyond her role in expanding <a href="/wiki/Christendom" title="Christendom">Christendom</a>. Detailing her story, a 2018 article claimed she showed her countrymen how "a woman could rule with strength and decision." <i>The Russian Primary Chronicle's</i> claim that Olga was of <a href="/wiki/Vikings" title="Vikings">Viking</a> descent also received attention for its possible contribution to her "warrior spirit".<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-ozy_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ozy-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Thomas Craughwell has commented, "If any saint [was] bad to the bone, it was Olga, princess of Kiev<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>... [She] took viciousness to a new level."<sup id="cite_ref-ozy_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ozy-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Russian historian <a href="/wiki/Boris_Akunin" title="Boris Akunin">Boris Akunin</a> argues though she certainly reconquered the Drevlians, only her killing of their first envoy is plausible, since Iskorosten was just two days' ride from Kiev, making it difficult to conceal the first public murder.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Arts_and_literature">Arts and literature</span></h3>

</p>
