					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">King of the English (975-978)</div>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Not to be confused with <a href="/wiki/Edmund_the_Martyr" title="Edmund the Martyr">Edmund the Martyr</a> or <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Confessor" title="Edward the Confessor">Edward the Confessor</a>.</div>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1066479718">.mw-parser-output .infobox-subbox{padding:0;border:none;margin:-3px;width:auto;min-width:100%;font-size:100%;clear:none;float:none;background-color:transparent}.mw-parser-output .infobox-3cols-child{margin:auto}.mw-parser-output .infobox .navbar{font-size:100%}body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-header,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-subheader,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-above,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-title,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-image,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-full-data,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-below{text-align:center}</style><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above fn" style="background-color: #cbe; later <a href="/wiki/Shaftesbury" title="Shaftesbury">Shaftesbury</a>; later <a href="/wiki/Woking" title="Woking">Woking</a><sup id="cite_ref-weir_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-weir-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></div></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Dynasty" title="Dynasty">House</a></th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/House_of_Wessex" title="House of Wessex">Wessex</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Father</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Edgar,_King_of_the_English" class="mw-redirect" title="Edgar, King of the English">Edgar, King of the English</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Mother</th><td class="infobox-data">Æthelflæd or <a href="/wiki/Wulfthryth_of_Wilton" title="Wulfthryth of Wilton">Wulfthryth</a></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p><b>Edward</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a>&#58; <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">Eadweard</i></span>, <small>pronounced&#160;</small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="ang-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Old_English" title="Help:IPA/Old English">[ˈæːɑdwæɑrˠd]</a></span>; <span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;962</span> &#160;&#8211;&#32;18 March 978), often called <b>the Martyr</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> was <a href="/wiki/King_of_the_English" class="mw-redirect" title="King of the English">King of the English</a> from 975 until he was murdered in 978.<sup id="cite_ref-weir_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-weir-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Edward was the eldest son of <a href="/wiki/Edgar,_King_of_the_English" class="mw-redirect" title="Edgar, King of the English">King Edgar</a>, but was not his father's acknowledged <a href="/wiki/Heir_apparent" title="Heir apparent">heir</a>. On Edgar's death, the leadership of England was contested, with some supporting Edward's claim to be king and others supporting his younger half-brother <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_the_Unready" title="Æthelred the Unready">Æthelred the Unready</a>, recognised as a legitimate son of Edgar. Edward was chosen as king and was crowned by his main clerical supporters, the archbishops <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">Dunstan</a> of Canterbury and <a href="/wiki/Oswald_of_Worcester" title="Oswald of Worcester">Oswald of York</a>.
</p><p>The great nobles of the kingdom, <a href="/wiki/Ealdormen" class="mw-redirect" title="Ealdormen">ealdormen</a> <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfhere,_Ealdorman_of_Mercia" title="Ælfhere, Ealdorman of Mercia">Ælfhere</a> and <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwine,_Ealdorman_of_East_Anglia" title="Æthelwine, Ealdorman of East Anglia">Æthelwine</a>, quarrelled, and civil war almost broke out. In the so-called anti-monastic reaction, the nobles took advantage of Edward's weakness to dispossess the <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> reformed monasteries of lands and other properties that King Edgar had granted to them.
</p><p>Edward's short reign was brought to an end by his murder at <a href="/wiki/Corfe_Castle" title="Corfe Castle">Corfe Castle</a> in 978 in circumstances that are not altogether clear.<sup id="cite_ref-weir_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-weir-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He was hurriedly buried at <a href="/wiki/Wareham,_Dorset" title="Wareham, Dorset">Wareham</a>, but was reburied with great ceremony at <a href="/wiki/Shaftesbury_Abbey" title="Shaftesbury Abbey">Shaftesbury Abbey</a> in <a href="/wiki/Dorset" title="Dorset">Dorset</a> early in 979. In 1001 Edward's remains were moved to a more prominent place in the abbey, probably with the blessing of his half-brother King Æthelred. Edward was already reckoned a saint by this time.
</p><p>A number of <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">lives</a> of Edward were written in the centuries following his death in which he was portrayed as a <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a>, generally seen as a victim of the Queen Dowager <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfthryth_(wife_of_Edgar)" title="Ælfthryth (wife of Edgar)">Ælfthryth</a>, mother of Æthelred. He is today recognised as a saint in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, and the <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Family">Family</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edward_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Family">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Edward's date of birth is unknown, but he was the eldest of <a href="/wiki/Edgar,_King_of_England" title="Edgar, King of England">King Edgar</a>'s three children. He was probably in his teens when he succeeded his father, who died at age 32 in 975.<sup id="cite_ref-Higham7_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Higham7-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Edward was known to be King Edgar's son, but he was not the son of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfthryth_(wife_of_Edgar)" title="Ælfthryth (wife of Edgar)">Queen Ælfthryth</a>, the third wife of Edgar. This much and no more is known from contemporary <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_charters" title="Anglo-Saxon charters">charters</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Later sources of questionable reliability address the identity of Edward's mother. The earliest such source is a life of <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">Dunstan</a> by <a href="/wiki/Osbern_of_Canterbury" title="Osbern of Canterbury">Osbern of Canterbury</a>, probably written in the 1080s. Osbern writes that Edward's mother was a nun at <a href="/wiki/Wilton_Abbey" title="Wilton Abbey">Wilton Abbey</a> whom the king seduced.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> When <a href="/wiki/Eadmer" title="Eadmer">Eadmer</a> wrote a life of Dunstan some decades later, he included an account of Edward's parentage obtained from <a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicholas_of_Worcester&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Nicholas of Worcester (page does not exist)">Nicholas of Worcester</a>. This denied that Edward was the son of a liaison between Edgar and a nun, presenting him as the son of Æthelflæd, daughter of Ordmær, "ealdorman of the East Anglians", whom Edgar had married in the years when he ruled <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercia</a> (between 957 and <a href="/wiki/Eadwig" title="Eadwig">Eadwig</a>'s death in 959).<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Additional accounts are offered by <a href="/wiki/Goscelin" title="Goscelin">Goscelin</a> in his life of Edgar's daughter Saint <a href="/wiki/Edith_of_Wilton" title="Edith of Wilton">Edith of Wilton</a> and in the histories of <a href="/wiki/John_of_Worcester" title="John of Worcester">John of Worcester</a> and <a href="/wiki/William_of_Malmesbury" title="William of Malmesbury">William of Malmesbury</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Together these various accounts suggest that Edward's mother was probably a noblewoman named Æthelflæd, surnamed <i>Candida</i> or <i>Eneda</i>—"the White" or "White Duck".<sup id="cite_ref-Higham6_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Higham6-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A charter of 966 describes Ælfthryth, whom Edgar had married in 964, as the king's "lawful wife", and their eldest son Edmund as the legitimate son of the king. Edward is noted as the king's son.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Bishop <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester" title="Æthelwold of Winchester">Æthelwold of Winchester</a> was a supporter of Ælfthryth and Æthelred, but <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">Dunstan</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, appears to have supported Edward, and a genealogy created at his <a href="/wiki/Glastonbury_Abbey" title="Glastonbury Abbey">Glastonbury Abbey</a> circa 969 gives Edward precedence over Edmund and Æthelred.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  Ælfthryth was the widow of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwald,_Ealdorman_of_East_Anglia" title="Æthelwald, Ealdorman of East Anglia">Æthelwald, Ealdorman of East Anglia</a>, and perhaps Edgar's third wife.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>  Cyril Hart argues that the contradictions regarding the identity of Edward's mother, and the fact that Edmund appears to have been regarded as the legitimate heir until his death in 971, suggest that Edward was probably illegitimate.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> However, <a href="/wiki/Barbara_Yorke" title="Barbara Yorke">Barbara Yorke</a> thinks that Æthelflæd was Edgar's wife, but Ælfthryth was a consecrated queen when she gave birth to her sons, who were therefore considered more "legitimate" than Edward.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Æthelwold denied that Edward was legitimate, but Yorke considers this "opportunist special pleading".<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edmund's full brother <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_the_Unready" title="Æthelred the Unready">Æthelred</a> may have inherited his position as <a href="/wiki/Heir_apparent" title="Heir apparent">heir</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> On a charter to the <a href="/wiki/New_Minster,_Winchester" title="New Minster, Winchester">New Minster</a> at <a href="/wiki/Winchester" title="Winchester">Winchester</a>, the names of Ælfthryth and her son Æthelred appear ahead of Edward's name.<sup id="cite_ref-Higham7_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Higham7-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> When Edgar died on 8 July 975, Æthelred was probably nine and Edward only a few years older.<sup id="cite_ref-miller_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-miller-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Disputed_succession">Disputed succession</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edward_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Disputed succession">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Edgar had been a strong ruler who had forced monastic reforms on a probably unwilling church and nobility, aided by the leading clerics of the day, <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">Dunstan</a>, <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>; <a href="/wiki/Oswald_of_Worcester" title="Oswald of Worcester">Oswald of Worcester</a>, <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_York" title="Archbishop of York">Archbishop of York</a>; and Bishop <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester" title="Æthelwold of Winchester">Æthelwold of Winchester</a>. By endowing the reformed <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> monasteries with the lands required for their support, he had dispossessed many lesser nobles, and had rewritten leases and loans of land to the benefit of the monasteries. Secular clergy, many of whom would have been members of the nobility, had been expelled from the new monasteries. While Edgar lived, he strongly supported the reformers, but following his death, the discontents which these changes had provoked came into the open.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The leading figures had all been supporters of the reform, but they were no longer united. Relations between Archbishop Dunstan and Bishop Æthelwold may have been strained.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Archbishop Oswald was at odds with Ealdorman <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfhere,_Ealdorman_of_Mercia" title="Ælfhere, Ealdorman of Mercia">Ælfhere, Ealdorman of Mercia</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> while Ælfhere and his kin were rivals for power with the affinity of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwine,_Ealdorman_of_East_Anglia" title="Æthelwine, Ealdorman of East Anglia">Æthelwine, Ealdorman of East Anglia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Dunstan was said to have questioned Edgar's marriage to Queen Dowager Ælfthryth and the legitimacy of their son Æthelred.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>These leaders were divided as to whether Edward or Æthelred should succeed Edgar. Neither law nor precedent offered much guidance. The choice between the sons of <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Elder" title="Edward the Elder">Edward the Elder</a> had divided his kingdom, and Edgar's elder brother <a href="/wiki/Eadwig" title="Eadwig">Eadwig</a> had been forced to give over a large part of the kingdom to Edgar.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> The Queen Dowager certainly supported the claims of her son Æthelred, aided by Bishop Æthelwold; and Dunstan supported Edward, aided by his fellow archbishop Oswald. It is likely that Ealdorman Ælfhere and his allies supported Æthelred and that Æthelwine and his allies supported Edward, although some historians suggest the opposite.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Later sources suggest that perceptions of legitimacy played a part in the arguments, as did the relative age of the two candidates. In time, Edward was anointed by Archbishops Dunstan and Oswald at <a href="/wiki/Kingston_upon_Thames" title="Kingston upon Thames">Kingston upon Thames</a>, most likely in 975.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> There is evidence that the settlement involved a degree of compromise. Æthelred appears to have been given lands which normally belonged to the king's sons, some of which had been granted by Edgar to <a href="/wiki/Abingdon_Abbey" title="Abingdon Abbey">Abingdon Abbey</a> and which were forcibly repossessed for Æthelred by the leading nobles.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Edward.27s_reign"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Edward's_reign">Edward's reign</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edward_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Edward&#039;s reign">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:EdwardMartyr.gif" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/41/EdwardMartyr.gif" decoding="async" width="200" height="194" class="thumbimage" data-file-width="200" data-file-height="194" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:EdwardMartyr.gif" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>A penny minted during Edward's reign at <a href="/wiki/Stamford,_Lincolnshire" title="Stamford, Lincolnshire">Stamford, Lincolnshire</a>, one of the <a href="/wiki/Five_Burghs" class="mw-redirect" title="Five Burghs">Five Burghs</a></div></div></div>
<p>After recording Edward's succession, the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> reports that a <a href="/wiki/Comet" title="Comet">comet</a> appeared, and that famine and "manifold disturbances" followed.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> The "manifold disturbances", sometimes called the anti-monastic reaction, appear to have started soon after Edgar's death. During this time, the experienced Ealdorman <a href="/wiki/Oslac_of_York" title="Oslac of York">Oslac of Northumbria</a>, effective ruler of much of northern England, was exiled due to unknown circumstances.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> Oslac was followed as ealdorman by Thored, either Oslac's son of that name or Thored Gunnar's son mentioned by the <i>Chronicle</i> in 966.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edward, or rather those who were wielding power on his behalf, also appointed a number of new ealdormen to positions in <a href="/wiki/Wessex" title="Wessex">Wessex</a>. Little is known of two of these men, and it is difficult to determine which faction, if any, they belonged to. Edwin, probably ruling in <a href="/wiki/Sussex" title="Sussex">Sussex</a>, and perhaps also parts of <a href="/wiki/Kent" title="Kent">Kent</a> and <a href="/wiki/Surrey" title="Surrey">Surrey</a>, was buried at Abingdon, an abbey patronised by Ælfhere. Æthelmær, who oversaw <a href="/wiki/Hampshire" title="Hampshire">Hampshire</a>, held lands in <a href="/wiki/Rutland" title="Rutland">Rutland</a>, perhaps suggesting links to Æthelwine.
</p><p>The third ealdorman, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelweard_(historian)" title="Æthelweard (historian)">Æthelweard</a>, today best known for his <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> history, ruled in the west. Æthelweard was a descendant of King <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_of_Wessex" class="mw-redirect" title="Æthelred of Wessex">Æthelred of Wessex</a> and probably the brother of King Eadwig's wife. He appears to have been a supporter of Edward rather than of either faction.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In some places, the <a href="/wiki/Secular_clergy" title="Secular clergy">secular clergy</a> who had been removed from the monasteries returned, driving the <a href="/wiki/Regular_clergy" title="Regular clergy">regular clergy</a> out in their turn. Bishop Æthelwold had been the main supporter of the regulars, and Archbishop Dunstan appears to have done little to aid his fellow reformer at this time.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> More generally, the magnates took the opportunity to undo many of Edgar's grants to monasteries and to force the abbots to rewrite leases and loans to favour the local nobility. Ealdorman Ælfhere was the leader in this regard, attacking Oswald's network of monasteries across Mercia.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> Ælfhere's rival Æthelwine, while a staunch protector of his family monastery of <a href="/wiki/Ramsey_Abbey" title="Ramsey Abbey">Ramsey Abbey</a>, treated <a href="/wiki/Ely_Abbey" class="mw-redirect" title="Ely Abbey">Ely Abbey</a> and other monasteries harshly.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> At some point during these disorders, Ælfhere and Æthelwine appear to have come close to open warfare. This may well have been related to Ælfhere's ambitions in East Anglia and to attacks upon Ramsey Abbey. Æthelwine, supported by his kinsman Ealdorman <a href="/wiki/Byrhtnoth" title="Byrhtnoth">Byrhtnoth</a> of Essex and others unspecified, mustered an army and caused Ælfhere to back down.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:A_Chronicle_of_England_-_Page_072_-_Edward_Murdered_at_Corfe.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/A_Chronicle_of_England_-_Page_072_-_Edward_Murdered_at_Corfe.jpg/220px-A_Chronicle_of_England_-_Page_072_-_Edward_Murdered_at_Corfe.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="236" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/A_Chronicle_of_England_-_Page_072_-_Edward_Murdered_at_Corfe.jpg/330px-A_Chronicle_of_England_-_Page_072_-_Edward_Murdered_at_Corfe.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/A_Chronicle_of_England_-_Page_072_-_Edward_Murdered_at_Corfe.jpg/440px-A_Chronicle_of_England_-_Page_072_-_Edward_Murdered_at_Corfe.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1238" data-file-height="1327" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:A_Chronicle_of_England_-_Page_072_-_Edward_Murdered_at_Corfe.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>In the 19th-century depiction by <a href="/wiki/James_William_Edmund_Doyle" title="James William Edmund Doyle">James William Edmund Doyle</a>, Edward the Martyr is offered a cup of mead by Ælfthryth, widow of the late Edgar, unaware that her attendant is about to murder him.</div></div></div>
<p>Very few <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_charters" title="Anglo-Saxon charters">charters</a> survive from Edward's reign, perhaps as few as three, leaving Edward's brief reign in obscurity.  By contrast, numerous charters survived from the reigns of his father Edgar and half-brother Æthelred.  All of the surviving Edward charters concern the royal heartland of <a href="/wiki/Wessex" title="Wessex">Wessex</a>; two deal with <a href="/wiki/Crediton" title="Crediton">Crediton</a> where Edward's former tutor <a href="/wiki/Sideman_(bishop)" title="Sideman (bishop)">Sideman</a> was <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Crediton" title="Bishop of Crediton">bishop</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> During Edgar's reign, dies for coins were cut only at <a href="/wiki/Winchester" title="Winchester">Winchester</a> and distributed from there to other mints across the kingdom.  Edward's reign permitted dies to be cut locally at <a href="/wiki/York" title="York">York</a> and at <a href="/wiki/Lincoln,_Lincolnshire" class="mw-redirect" title="Lincoln, Lincolnshire">Lincoln</a>. The general impression is of a reduction or breakdown of royal authority in the midlands and north.<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> The machinery of government continued to function, as councils and synods met as customary during Edward's reign, at <a href="/wiki/Kirtlington" title="Kirtlington">Kirtlington</a> in <a href="/wiki/Oxfordshire" title="Oxfordshire">Oxfordshire</a> after Easter 977, and again at <a href="/wiki/Calne" title="Calne">Calne</a> in <a href="/wiki/Wiltshire" title="Wiltshire">Wiltshire</a> the following year. During the meeting at Calne, some councillors were killed and others injured by the collapse of the floor of their room.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edward_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Edward_King_of_Wessex_at_Corfe_Castle.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3e/Edward_King_of_Wessex_at_Corfe_Castle.jpg/220px-Edward_King_of_Wessex_at_Corfe_Castle.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3e/Edward_King_of_Wessex_at_Corfe_Castle.jpg/330px-Edward_King_of_Wessex_at_Corfe_Castle.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3e/Edward_King_of_Wessex_at_Corfe_Castle.jpg/440px-Edward_King_of_Wessex_at_Corfe_Castle.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3024" data-file-height="4032" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Edward_King_of_Wessex_at_Corfe_Castle.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Commemorative sign for Edward the Martyr at Corfe Castle, Dorset, UK. The sign is an artist's impression of Edward, and underneath is written "Edward the Martyr King of Wessex treacherously stabbed at Corves gate in A.D. 978 by his stepmother Elfrida".</div></div></div>
<p>The version of the <i>Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</i> containing the most detailed account records that Edward was murdered in the evening of 18 March 978, while visiting Ælfthryth and Æthelred, probably at or near the mound on which the ruins of <a href="/wiki/Corfe_Castle" title="Corfe Castle">Corfe Castle</a> now stand. It adds that he was buried at Wareham "without any royal honours". The compiler of this version of the <i>Chronicle</i>, manuscript E, called the <a href="/wiki/Peterborough" title="Peterborough">Peterborough</a> Chronicle, says:
</p>
<blockquote><p>"No worse deed for the English race was done than this was, since they first sought out the land of Britain. Men murdered him, but God exalted him. In life he was an earthly king; after death he is now a heavenly saint. His earthly relatives would not avenge him, but his Heavenly Father has much avenged him."<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Other recensions of the <i>Chronicle</i> report less detail, the oldest text stating only that he was killed, while versions from the 1040s say that he was <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyred</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Corfe_Castle2.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Corfe_Castle2.jpg/220px-Corfe_Castle2.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Corfe_Castle2.jpg/330px-Corfe_Castle2.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Corfe_Castle2.jpg/440px-Corfe_Castle2.jpg 2x" data-file-width="960" data-file-height="1280" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Corfe_Castle2.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Corfe_Castle" title="Corfe Castle">Corfe Castle</a> from below</div></div></div>
<p>Of other early sources, the life of Oswald of Worcester, attributed to <a href="/wiki/Byrhtferth" title="Byrhtferth">Byrhtferth</a> of Ramsey, adds that Edward was killed by Æthelred's advisers, who attacked him when he was dismounting. It agrees that he was buried without ceremony at Wareham.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Wulfstan_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Wulfstan II">Wulfstan II</a> alludes to the killing of Edward in his <i><a href="/wiki/Sermo_Lupi_ad_Anglos" title="Sermo Lupi ad Anglos">Sermo Lupi ad Anglos</a>,</i> written not later than 1016. A recent study translates his words as follows:
</p>
<blockquote><p>"And a very great betrayal of a lord it is also in the world, that a man betray his lord to death, or drive him living from the land, and both have come to pass in this land: Edward was betrayed, and then killed, and after that burned ..."<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Death_of_edward_martyr.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f2/Death_of_edward_martyr.jpg/220px-Death_of_edward_martyr.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="267" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f2/Death_of_edward_martyr.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="292" data-file-height="355" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Death_of_edward_martyr.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Ælfthryth looks on as Edward is stabbed to death: from a Victorian edition of <a href="/wiki/John_Foxe" title="John Foxe">Foxe's</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Book_of_Martyrs" class="mw-redirect" title="Book of Martyrs">Book of Martyrs</a></i></div></div></div>
<p>Later sources, further removed from events, such as the late 11th-century <i>Passio S. Eadwardi</i> and <a href="/wiki/John_of_Worcester" title="John of Worcester">John of Worcester</a>, claim that Ælfthryth organised the killing of Edward, while <a href="/wiki/Henry_of_Huntingdon" title="Henry of Huntingdon">Henry of Huntingdon</a> wrote that she killed Edward herself.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Modern historians have offered a variety of interpretations of Edward's killing. Three main theories have been proposed. Firstly, that Edward was killed, as the life of Oswald claims, by nobles in Æthelred's service, either as a result of a personal quarrel, or to place their master on the throne.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> The life of Oswald portrays Edward as an unstable young man who, according to <a href="/wiki/Frank_Stenton" title="Frank Stenton">Frank Stenton</a>: "had offended many important persons by his intolerable violence of speech and behaviour. Long after he had passed into veneration as a saint it was remembered that his outbursts of rage had alarmed all who knew him, and especially the members of his own household."<sup id="cite_ref-Stenton_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Stenton-43">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup>  This may be a <a href="/wiki/Trope_(literature)" title="Trope (literature)">trope</a> of <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the second version, Ælfthryth was implicated, either beforehand by plotting the killing, or afterwards in allowing the killers to go free and unpunished.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A third alternative, noting that Edward in 978 was very close to ruling on his own, proposes that Ealdorman Ælfhere was behind the killing so as to preserve his own influence and to prevent Edward taking revenge for Ælfhere's actions earlier in the reign.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> John notes this and interprets Ælfhere's part in Edward's reburial as being a penance for the assassination.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Reburial_and_early_cult">Reburial and early cult</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edward_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Reburial and early cult">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:151px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Shaftesbury_Abbey.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/cd/Shaftesbury_Abbey.jpg" decoding="async" width="149" height="191" class="thumbimage" data-file-width="149" data-file-height="191" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Shaftesbury_Abbey.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>The Great Seal of <a href="/wiki/Shaftesbury_Abbey" title="Shaftesbury Abbey">Shaftesbury Abbey</a>, where Edward's relics lay until the <a href="/wiki/English_Reformation" title="English Reformation">English Reformation</a></div></div></div>
<p>Edward's body lay at Wareham for a year before being disinterred. Ælfhere initiated the reinterment, perhaps as a gesture of reconciliation. According to the life of Oswald, Edward's body was found to be <a href="/wiki/Incorrupt" class="mw-redirect" title="Incorrupt">incorrupt</a> when it was disinterred (which was taken as a miraculous sign). The body was taken to <a href="/wiki/Shaftesbury_Abbey" title="Shaftesbury Abbey">Shaftesbury Abbey</a>, a Benedictine house of nuns, with royal connections which had been endowed by King <a href="/wiki/Alfred_the_Great" title="Alfred the Great">Alfred the Great</a> and where Edward and Æthelred's grandmother <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfgifu_of_Shaftesbury" title="Ælfgifu of Shaftesbury">Ælfgifu</a> had spent her latter years.
</p><p>Edward's remains were reburied with lavish public ceremony. Later versions, such as the <i>Passio S. Eadwardi,</i> have more complicated accounts.  It said that Edward's body was concealed in a marsh, where it was revealed by miraculous events. The <i>Passio</i> dates the reburial to 18 February.<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1001, Edward's relics (he was considered a saint, although never canonised) were <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relics)" class="mw-redirect" title="Translation (relics)">translated</a> to a more prominent place within the abbey church at Shaftesbury. The ceremonies are said to have been led by the then-<a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Sherborne_(historic)" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Sherborne (historic)">Bishop of Sherborne</a>, <a href="/wiki/Wulfsige_III" title="Wulfsige III">Wulfsige III</a>, accompanied by a senior cleric whom the <i>Passio</i> calls Elsinus, sometimes identified with Ælfsige, the abbot of the <a href="/wiki/New_Minster,_Winchester" title="New Minster, Winchester">New Minster, Winchester</a>. King Æthelred, preoccupied with the threat of a Danish invasion, did not attend in person, but he issued a charter to the Shaftesbury nuns late in 1001 granting them lands at <a href="/wiki/Bradford_on_Avon" class="mw-redirect" title="Bradford on Avon">Bradford on Avon</a>, which is thought to be related. A 13th-century <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">calendar of saints</a> gives the date of this translation as 20 June.<sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The rise of Edward's cult has been interpreted in various ways. It is sometimes portrayed as a popular movement, or as the product of a political attack on King Æthelred by former supporters of Edward. Alternatively, Æthelred has been seen as one of the key forces in the promotion of Edward's cult and that of their sister Eadgifu (<a href="/wiki/Edith_of_Wilton" title="Edith of Wilton">Edith of Wilton</a>). He was thought to make the charter in 1001 granting land to Shaftesbury at the elevation of Edward's relics, and some accounts suggest that Æthelred legislated the observation of Edward's feast days across England in a law code of 1008. It is unclear whether this innovation, seemingly drafted by Archbishop Wulfstan II, dates from Æthelred's reign.  It may instead have been promulgated by King <a href="/wiki/Cnut_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Cnut the Great">Cnut</a>. David Rollason has drawn attention to the increased importance of the cults of other murdered royal saints in this period. Among these are the cults of King <a href="/wiki/Ecgberht_of_Kent" title="Ecgberht of Kent">Ecgberht of Kent</a>'s nephews, whose lives form part of the <a href="/wiki/Mildrith" title="Mildrith">Mildrith</a> Legend, and those of the <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercian</a> Saints <a href="/wiki/Kenelm" class="mw-redirect" title="Kenelm">Kenelm</a> and <a href="/wiki/Wigstan" title="Wigstan">Wigstan</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Later_cult">Later cult</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edward_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Later cult">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:162px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Edward_the_Martyr_Shrine_Brookwood.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/St_Edward_the_Martyr_Shrine_Brookwood.jpg/160px-St_Edward_the_Martyr_Shrine_Brookwood.jpg" decoding="async" width="160" height="181" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/St_Edward_the_Martyr_Shrine_Brookwood.jpg/240px-St_Edward_the_Martyr_Shrine_Brookwood.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/St_Edward_the_Martyr_Shrine_Brookwood.jpg/320px-St_Edward_the_Martyr_Shrine_Brookwood.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2980" data-file-height="3375" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Edward_the_Martyr_Shrine_Brookwood.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>The Shrine of St Edward in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_St._Edward_the_Martyr,_Brookwood" class="mw-redirect" title="Church of St. Edward the Martyr, Brookwood">Church of St. Edward the Martyr, Brookwood</a> (2018)</div></div></div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:162px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Edward_the_Martyr_Plaque_Brookwood.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Edward_the_Martyr_Plaque_Brookwood.jpg/160px-Edward_the_Martyr_Plaque_Brookwood.jpg" decoding="async" width="160" height="109" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Edward_the_Martyr_Plaque_Brookwood.jpg/240px-Edward_the_Martyr_Plaque_Brookwood.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Edward_the_Martyr_Plaque_Brookwood.jpg/320px-Edward_the_Martyr_Plaque_Brookwood.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2018" data-file-height="1370" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Edward_the_Martyr_Plaque_Brookwood.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Plaque to John Edward Wilson-Claridge at the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_St._Edward_the_Martyr,_Brookwood" class="mw-redirect" title="Church of St. Edward the Martyr, Brookwood">Church of St. Edward the Martyr, Brookwood</a>; he recovered and donated the supposed remains of Edward the Martyr</div></div></div>
<p>During the sixteenth century and English Reformation, <a href="/wiki/Henry_VIII_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Henry VIII of England">King Henry VIII</a> led the <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries" title="Dissolution of the monasteries">dissolution of the monasteries</a> and many holy places were demolished.  Edward's remains were hidden so as to avoid <a href="/wiki/Desecration" title="Desecration">desecration</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-life_51-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-life-51">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1931, the relics were recovered by Wilson-Claridge during an <a href="/wiki/Archaeological" class="mw-redirect" title="Archaeological">archaeological</a> excavation at Shaftesbury abbey ruins; their identity was confirmed by Dr. <a href="/wiki/Thomas_E._A._Stowell" title="Thomas E. A. Stowell">T. E. A. Stowell</a>, an osteologist. In 1970, examinations performed on the relics suggested that the young man had died in the same manner as Edward.<sup id="cite_ref-NN_52-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NN-52">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup>  Wilson-Claridge wanted the relics to go to the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church_Outside_Russia" class="mw-redirect" title="Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia">Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia</a>. His brother, however, wanted them to be returned to <a href="/wiki/Shaftesbury_Abbey" title="Shaftesbury Abbey">Shaftesbury Abbey</a>. For decades, the relics were kept in a cutlery box in a bank vault at the <a href="/wiki/Midland_Bank" title="Midland Bank">Midland Bank</a> in <a href="/wiki/Woking" title="Woking">Woking</a>, <a href="/wiki/Surrey" title="Surrey">Surrey</a><sup id="cite_ref-53" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-53">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup> because of the unresolved dispute about which of two churches should have them.<sup id="cite_ref-LL_54-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-LL-54">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In time, the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia was granted the remains, and they were placed in a church in <a href="/wiki/Brookwood_Cemetery" title="Brookwood Cemetery">Brookwood Cemetery</a> in Woking, with the enshrinement ceremony occurring in September 1984.<sup id="cite_ref-NN_52-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NN-52">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> The St Edward Brotherhood of monks was organised there as well.<sup id="cite_ref-NN_52-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NN-52">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> The church is now named <a href="/wiki/St_Edward_the_Martyr_Orthodox_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="St Edward the Martyr Orthodox Church">St Edward the Martyr Orthodox Church</a>, and it is under the jurisdiction of a traditionalist Greek Orthodox community. However, while the bones are of approximately the right date, they are of a man in his late twenties or early thirties rather than a youth in his mid teens.<sup id="cite_ref-55" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-55">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the Orthodox Church, St Edward is ranked as a <a href="/wiki/Passion-bearer" class="mw-redirect" title="Passion-bearer">Passion-bearer</a>, a type of saint who accepts death out of love for Christ.<sup id="cite_ref-NN_52-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NN-52">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> Edward was widely venerated before the canonisation process was formalised,<sup id="cite_ref-56" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-56">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> and he is also regarded as a saint in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-NN_52-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NN-52">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Ste_57-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ste-57">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup> His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is celebrated on 18 March, the day of his murder. The Orthodox Church commemorates him a second time each year on <a href="/wiki/September_3_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics)" title="September 3 (Eastern Orthodox liturgics)">3 September</a> and commemorates the translation of his relics into Orthodox possession on <a href="/wiki/February_13_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics)" title="February 13 (Eastern Orthodox liturgics)">13 February</a>.
</p>
