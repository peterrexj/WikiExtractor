<p><b>Hippolytus of Rome</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="&#39;h&#39; in &#39;hi&#39;">h</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;p&#39; in &#39;pie&#39;">p</span><span title="/ɑː/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;father&#39;">ɑː</span><span title="&#39;l&#39; in &#39;lie&#39;">l</span><span title="/ɪ/: &#39;i&#39; in &#39;kit&#39;">ɪ</span><span title="&#39;t&#39; in &#39;tie&#39;">t</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;s&#39; in &#39;sigh&#39;">s</span></span>/</a></span></span>, <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Ἱππόλυτος</span>; Romanized: <i>Ippólytos</i>, c. 170 – c. 235 AD) was one of the most important second-third century Christian theologians, whose provenance, identity and corpus remain elusive to scholars and historians. Suggested communities include Rome, Palestine, Egypt, Anatolia and other regions of the Middle East. The best historians of literature in the ancient church, including <a href="/wiki/Eusebius" title="Eusebius">Eusebius of Caesarea</a> and <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>, openly confess they cannot name where Hippolytus the biblical commentator and theologian served in leadership. They had read his works but did not possess evidence of his community. <a href="/wiki/Photios_I_of_Constantinople" title="Photios I of Constantinople">Photios I of Constantinople</a> describes him in his <i><a href="/wiki/Bibliotheca_(Photius)" title="Bibliotheca (Photius)">Bibliotheca</a></i> (cod. 121) as a disciple of <a href="/wiki/Irenaeus" title="Irenaeus">Irenaeus</a>, who was said to be a disciple of <a href="/wiki/Polycarp" title="Polycarp">Polycarp</a>, and from the context of this passage it is supposed that he suggested that Hippolytus so styled himself.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> This assertion is doubtful.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> One older theory asserts he came into conflict with the <a href="/wiki/Pope" title="Pope">popes</a> of his time and seems to have headed a schismatic group as a rival to the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Rome">bishop of Rome</a>, thus becoming an <a href="/wiki/Antipope" title="Antipope">antipope</a>. In this view, he opposed the Roman Popes who softened the penitential system to accommodate the large number of new pagan converts. However, he was reconciled to the Church before he died as a <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyr" title="Christian martyr">martyr</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Starting in the fourth century, various legends arose about him, identifying him as a priest of the <a href="/wiki/Novatianism" title="Novatianism">Novatianist schism</a> or as a soldier converted by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Lawrence" title="Saint Lawrence">Saint Lawrence</a>. He has also been confused with another martyr of the same name.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_IV" title="Pope Pius IV">Pope Pius IV</a> identifies him as "Saint Hippolytus, Bishop of <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Pontus" title="Diocese of Pontus">Pontus</a>" who was martyred in the reign of <a href="/wiki/Severus_Alexander" title="Severus Alexander">Severus Alexander</a> through his inscription on a statue found at the Church of Saint Lawrence in Rome and kept at the Vatican as photographed and published in Bunsen.<sup id="cite_ref-Brunsen_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brunsen-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hippolytus_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Little is known for certain about his community of origin. One Victorian theory suggested that as a <a href="/wiki/Presbyter" title="Presbyter">presbyter</a> of the church at Rome under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Zephyrinus" title="Pope Zephyrinus">Pope Zephyrinus</a> (199–217 AD), Hippolytus was distinguished for his learning and eloquence. It was at this time that <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>, then a young man, heard him preach.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In this view, Hippolytus accused Pope Zephyrinus of <a href="/wiki/Modalism" class="mw-redirect" title="Modalism">modalism</a>, the heresy which held that the names Father and Son are simply different names for the same subject. Hippolytus championed the Logos doctrine of the Greek apologists, most notably <a href="/wiki/Justin_Martyr" title="Justin Martyr">Justin Martyr</a>, which distinguished the Father from the Logos ("Word"). An ethical conservative, he was scandalized when <a href="/wiki/Pope_Callixtus_I" title="Pope Callixtus I">Pope Callixtus I</a> (217–222 AD) extended absolution to Christians who had committed grave sins, such as adultery.<sup id="cite_ref-EBO_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EBO-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Some suggest Hippolytus himself advocated a pronounced rigorism.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirsch_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirsch-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> At this time, he seems to have allowed himself to be elected as a rival Bishop of Rome, and continued to attack <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_I" title="Pope Urban I">Pope Urban I</a> (222–230 AD) and <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pontian" title="Pope Pontian">Pope Pontian</a> (230–235 AD).<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> G. Salmon suggests that Hippolytus was the leader of the Greek-speaking Christians of Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-dcb_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dcb-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Allen Brent sees the development of Roman house-churches into something akin to Greek philosophical schools gathered around a compelling teacher.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Also under this view: during the persecution at the time of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Maximinus_Thrax" title="Maximinus Thrax">Maximinus Thrax</a>, Hippolytus and Pontian were exiled together in 235 to <a href="/wiki/Sardinia" title="Sardinia">Sardinia</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> likely dying in the mines.<sup id="cite_ref-dcb_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dcb-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> It is quite probable that, before his death there, he was reconciled to the other party at Rome, for, under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Fabian" title="Pope Fabian">Pope Fabian</a> (236–250 AD), his body and that of Pontian were brought to Rome. The so-called <i><a href="/wiki/Chronography_of_354" class="mw-redirect" title="Chronography of 354">Chronography of 354</a></i> (more precisely, the <i><a href="/wiki/Liberian_Catalogue" title="Liberian Catalogue">Liberian Catalogue</a></i>) reports that on August 13, probably in 236, the two bodies were interred in Rome, that of Hippolytus in a cemetery on the <a href="/wiki/Via_Tiburtina" title="Via Tiburtina">Via Tiburtina</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> his funeral being conducted by <a href="/wiki/Justin_the_Confessor" title="Justin the Confessor">Justin the Confessor</a>. This document indicates that, by about 255, Hippolytus was considered a martyr and gives him the rank of a priest, not of a bishop, an indication that before his death the schismatic was received again into the Church.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legends">Legends</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hippolytus_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legends">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The name Hippolytus appears in various hagiographical and martyrological sources of the early Church. The facts about the life of the writer Hippolytus, as opposed to other celebrated Christians who bore the name Hippolytus, were eventually lost in the West, perhaps partly because he wrote in <a href="/wiki/Hellenic_Greek" class="mw-redirect" title="Hellenic Greek">Hellenic Greek</a>. <a href="/wiki/Pope_Damasus_I" title="Pope Damasus I">Pope Damasus I</a> dedicated to a Hippolytus one of his famous epigrams,<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> referring to a priest of the <a href="/wiki/Novatianism" title="Novatianism">Novatianist</a> schism, a view later forwarded by <a href="/wiki/Prudentius" title="Prudentius">Prudentius</a> in the 5th century in his "Passion of St Hippolytus". In the Passionals of the 7th and 8th centuries he is represented as a soldier converted by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Lawrence" title="Saint Lawrence">Saint Lawrence</a>, a legend that long survived in the Roman <a href="/wiki/Breviary" title="Breviary">Breviary</a>. He was also confused with a martyr of the same name who was buried in <a href="/wiki/Portus" title="Portus">Portus</a>, of which city he was believed to have been a bishop,<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> who was put to death by drowning in a deep well.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Prudentius' account, a martyr Hippolytus was dragged to death by wild horses,<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> a striking parallel to the story of the mythological <a href="/wiki/Hippolytus_(mythology)" class="mw-redirect" title="Hippolytus (mythology)">Hippolytus</a>, who was dragged to death by wild horses at Athens.  He described the subterranean tomb of the saint and states that he saw there a picture representing Hippolytus' execution. He also confirms August 13 as the date on which a Hippolytus was celebrated but this again refers to the convert of Lawrence, as preserved in the <a href="/wiki/Menaion" title="Menaion">Menaion</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>.
</p><p>The latter account led to a Hippolytus being considered the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of horses. During the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>, sick horses were brought to <a href="/wiki/St_Ippolyts" title="St Ippolyts">St Ippolyts</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hertfordshire" title="Hertfordshire">Hertfordshire</a>, England, where a church is dedicated to him.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hippolytus_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:HippolytusStatue.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/HippolytusStatue.JPG/220px-HippolytusStatue.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="361" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/HippolytusStatue.JPG/330px-HippolytusStatue.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9b/HippolytusStatue.JPG 2x" data-file-width="376" data-file-height="617" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Ancient_Roman" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Roman">Roman</a> sculpture, maybe of Hippolytus, found in 1551 and used for the attribution of the Apostolic Tradition</figcaption></figure>
<p>Controversy surrounds the corpus of the writer Hippolytus. In the Victorian Era, scholars claimed his principal work to be the <i><a href="/wiki/Refutation_of_all_Heresies" class="mw-redirect" title="Refutation of all Heresies">Refutation of all Heresies</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Of its ten books, Book I was the most important.<sup id="cite_ref-EBO_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EBO-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> It was long known and was printed (with the title <i><a href="/wiki/Philosophumena" class="mw-redirect" title="Philosophumena">Philosophumena</a></i>) among the works of Origen. Books II and III are lost, and Books IV–X were found, without the name of the author,<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> in a monastery of <a href="/wiki/Mount_Athos" title="Mount Athos">Mount Athos</a> in 1842. E. Miller published them in 1851 under the title <i>Philosophumena</i>, attributing them to Origen of Alexandria. Recent scholarship prefers to treat the text as the work of an unknown author, perhaps of Roman origin. 
</p><p>In 1551 a marble statue of a seated figure (originally female, perhaps personifying one of the sciences) was purportedly found in the cemetery of the Via Tiburtina and was heavily restored. On the sides of the seat was carved a <a href="/wiki/Paschal_cycle" title="Paschal cycle">paschal cycle</a>, and on the back the titles of numerous writings by Hippolytus.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirsch_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirsch-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Many other works are listed by <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea" class="mw-redirect" title="Eusebius of Caesarea">Eusebius of Caesarea</a> and <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>. The research of Guarducci showed the original statue was a representation of a female figure, reopening the question of its original purpose. Allen Brent analyzed the title list of the statue, questioning Hippolytan authorship of some works. 
</p><p>Hippolytus' voluminous writings, which for variety of subject can be compared with those of Origen, embrace the spheres of <a href="/wiki/Exegesis" title="Exegesis">exegesis</a>, <a href="/wiki/Homiletics" title="Homiletics">homiletics</a>, <a href="/wiki/Apologetics" title="Apologetics">apologetics</a> and <a href="/wiki/Polemic" title="Polemic">polemic</a>, <a href="/wiki/Chronography" class="mw-redirect" title="Chronography">chronography</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Canon_law" title="Canon law">ecclesiastical law</a>. The Apostolic Tradition, if it is the work of Hippolytus, recorded the first <a href="/wiki/Liturgy" title="Liturgy">liturgical</a> reference to the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a>, as part of the ordination rite of a bishop.<sup id="cite_ref-McNally68_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-McNally68-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Of exegetical works attributed to Hippolytus, the best preserved are the <i>Commentary on the Prophet <a href="/wiki/Daniel_(biblical_figure)" title="Daniel (biblical figure)">Daniel</a></i> and the <i>Commentary on the <a href="/wiki/Song_of_Songs" title="Song of Songs">Song of Songs</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_2-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> This is the earliest attested Christian interpretation of the Song, covering only the first three chapters to Song 3:7.
</p><p>The commentary on the <a href="/wiki/Song_of_Songs" title="Song of Songs">Song of Songs</a> survives in two Georgian manuscripts, a Greek <a href="/wiki/Epitome" title="Epitome">epitome</a>, a Paleo-Slavonic <a href="/wiki/Florilegium" title="Florilegium">florilegium</a>, and fragments in Armenian and Syriac as well as in many patristic quotations, especially in <a href="/wiki/Ambrose_of_Milan" class="mw-redirect" title="Ambrose of Milan">Ambrose of Milan</a>'s <i>Exposition on Psalm 118 (119)</i>. It is generally regarded as an instruction relating to a post-Baptismal rite of anointing with oil as a symbol of receiving the Holy Spirit. The commentary was originally written as part of a <a href="/wiki/Rite_of_Christian_Initiation_of_Adults" class="mw-redirect" title="Rite of Christian Initiation of Adults">mystagogy</a>, an instruction for new Christians. Scholars have usually assumed the <i>Commentary On the Song of Songs</i> was originally composed for use during <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Easter</a>, a season favored in the West for Baptism.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Hippolytus supplied his commentary with a fully developed introduction known as the <i>schema isagogicum</i>, indicating his knowledge of the rhetorical conventions for teachers discussing classical works.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> He employs a common rhetorical trope, <a href="/wiki/Ekphrasis" title="Ekphrasis">ekphrasis</a>, using images on the walls or floors of Greco-Roman homes, and in the catacombs as paintings or mosaics.<sup id="cite_ref-Smith_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Smith-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Origen felt that the Song should be reserved for the spiritually mature and that studying it might be harmful for the novice.
</p><p>Scholars generally ascribe to Hippolytus<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> a work now entitled the <i><a href="/wiki/Apostolic_Tradition" title="Apostolic Tradition">Apostolic Tradition</a></i>, which contains the earliest known ritual of ordination.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The influence of Hippolytus was felt chiefly through his works on chronography and ecclesiastical law.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> His chronicle of the world, a compilation embracing the whole period from the <a href="/wiki/Genesis_creation_narrative" title="Genesis creation narrative">creation of the world</a> up to the year 234, formed a basis for many chronographical works both in the East and West.<sup id="cite_ref-Schmidt_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Schmidt-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> It is from the <i>Apostolic Tradition</i> that the current words of episcopal ordination in the Catholic Church come from, as updated by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI" title="Pope Paul VI">Pope Paul VI</a>.
</p><p>In the great compilations of ecclesiastical law that arose in the East since the 3rd century, the <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Church_Orders" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Church Orders">Church Orders</a> many canons were attributed to Hippolytus, for example in the <a href="/wiki/Canons_of_Hippolytus" title="Canons of Hippolytus">Canons of Hippolytus</a> or <a href="/wiki/Apostolic_Constitutions#Epitome" title="Apostolic Constitutions">the Constitutions through Hippolytus</a>. How much of this material is genuinely his, how much of it worked over, and how much of it wrongly attributed to him, can no longer be determined beyond dispute,<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> however a great deal was incorporated into the <i><a href="/wiki/Fetha_Negest" title="Fetha Negest">Fetha Negest</a></i>, which once served as the constitutional basis of law in <a href="/wiki/Ethiopia" title="Ethiopia">Ethiopia</a> — where he is still remembered as <b>Abulides</b>. During the early 20th century the work known as <i>The Egyptian Church Order</i> was identified as the <a href="/wiki/Apostolic_Tradition" title="Apostolic Tradition">Apostolic Tradition</a> and attributed to Hippolytus; at present this attribution is hotly contested.
</p><p>Differences in style and theology lead some scholars to conclude that some of the works attributed to Hippolytus actually derive from a second author.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_2-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Two small but potentially important works, <i>On the Twelve Apostles of Christ</i> and <i>On the <a href="/wiki/Seventy_Apostles" class="mw-redirect" title="Seventy Apostles">Seventy Apostles</a> of Christ,</i> are often neglected because the manuscripts were lost during most of the church age and then found in Greece in the 19th century. . The two are included in an appendix to the works of Hippolytus in the voluminous collection of Early Church Fathers.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> The work on the 70 apostles is noteworthy as a (potentially) early source.
</p><p>A consensus of scholarship agrees on a core of authentic texts composed by the second-third century writer Hippolytus, regardless of disputes concerning his community, or the exact dates of his biography: these are the biblical commentaries, including On Daniel, On David and Goliath, On the Song of Songs (partially extant), On the Blessings of Isaac and Jacob, and On the Antichrist. These form a sound basis for exploring and understanding his theology and biblical doctrines.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Eschatology">Eschatology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hippolytus_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Eschatology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Hippolytus is an important figure in the development of <a href="/wiki/Christian_eschatology" title="Christian eschatology">Christian eschatology</a>. In his biblical compendium and topical study <i>On Christ and the Antichrist</i> and in his <i>Commentary on the Prophet Daniel</i> Hippolytus gave his interpretation of the second advent of Christ.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>With the onset of persecutions during the reign of <a href="/wiki/Septimius_Severus" title="Septimius Severus">Septimius Severus</a>, many early Christian writers treated topics of apocalyptic eschatology. <i>On Christ and the Antichrist</i> is one of the earliest works. It is thought Hippolytus was generally influenced by Irenaeus.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> However, unlike Irenaeus, Hippolytus focuses on the meaning of prophecy for the Church in his own time. Of the dogmatic works, <i>On <a href="/wiki/Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Christ">Christ</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Antichrist" title="Antichrist">Antichrist</a></i> survives in a complete state and was probably written about 202.
</p><p>Hippolytus follows the long-established usage in interpreting Daniel's seventy prophetic weeks to be weeks of literal years. Hippolytus gave an explanation of Daniel's paralleling prophecies of chapters 2 and 7, which he, as with the other fathers, specifically relates to the Babylonians, Persians, Greeks, and Romans. His interpretation of events and their significance is Christological.<sup id="cite_ref-Daley_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Daley-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hippolytus did not subscribe to the belief that the Second Coming was imminent.<sup id="cite_ref-Cummings_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cummings-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> In his commentary on Daniel he criticizes those who predict the Second Coming in the near future, and then says that six thousand years must pass from Creation before the Second Coming. He also says that Christ was born 5500 years after Adam, so 500 years have to pass from the birth of Christ "to the consummation of the six thousand years, and in this way the end will be".<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Feast_days">Feast days</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hippolytus_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Feast days">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> of St Hippolytus falls on August 13, which is also the <a href="/wiki/Afterfeast" title="Afterfeast">Apodosis</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Great_Feasts" class="mw-redirect" title="Great Feasts">Feast</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Transfiguration_of_Jesus" title="Transfiguration of Jesus">Transfiguration</a>. Because on the Apodosis the hymns of the Transfiguration are to be repeated, the feast of St. Hippolytus may be transferred to the day before or to some other convenient day. The Eastern Orthodox Church also celebrates the feast of <i>"St Hippolytus Pope of Rome"</i> on January 30, who may or may not be the same individual.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a> celebrates St Hippolytus jointly with <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pontian" title="Pope Pontian">St Pontian</a> on August 13. The feast of Saint Hippolytus formerly celebrated on 22 August as one of the companions of Saint <a href="/wiki/Symphorian_and_Timotheus" title="Symphorian and Timotheus">Timotheus</a> was a duplicate of his 13 August feast and for that reason was deleted when the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> was <a href="/wiki/Mysterii_Paschalis" title="Mysterii Paschalis">revised in 1969</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Earlier editions of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a> referred to the 22 August Hippolytus as Bishop of Porto. The <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia" title="Catholic Encyclopedia">Catholic Encyclopedia</a> sees this as "connected with the confusion regarding the Roman presbyter resulting from the Acts of the Martyrs of Porto. It has not been ascertained whether the memory of the latter was localized at Porto merely in connection with the legend in Prudentius, without further foundation, or whether a person named Hippolytus was really martyred at Porto, and afterwards confounded in legend with Hippolytus of Rome."<sup id="cite_ref-Kirsch_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirsch-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> This opinion is shared by a Benedictine source.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Earlier editions of the Roman Martyrology also mentioned on 30 January a Hippolytus venerated at <a href="/wiki/Antioch" title="Antioch">Antioch</a>, but the details were borrowed from the story of Hippolytus of Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> Modern editions of the Martyrology omit mention of this supposed Saint Hippolytus of Antioch.
</p>
