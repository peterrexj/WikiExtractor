<p><b>Kaleb</b> (<a href="/wiki/Ge%27ez_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ge&#39;ez language">Ge'ez</a>: <span lang="gez">ካሌብ</span>), also known as <b>Saint Elesbaan</b>, was <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum" title="Kingdom of Aksum">King of Aksum</a>, which was situated in modern-day <a href="/wiki/Eritrea" title="Eritrea">Eritrea</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ethiopia" title="Ethiopia">Ethiopia</a>.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Procopius" title="Procopius">Procopius</a> calls him "Hellestheaeus", a variant of <a href="/wiki/Koin%C4%93_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Koinē Greek language">Koinē Greek</a>: <span lang="grc">Ελεσβόάς</span> version of his <a href="/wiki/Regnal_name" title="Regnal name">regnal name</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ge%27ez_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ge&#39;ez language">Ge'ez</a>: <span lang="gez">እለ አጽብሐ</span>, <small>romanized:&#160;</small><span title="Ge&#39;ez-language romanization"><i lang="gez-Latn">ʾƎllä ʾAṣbəḥa</i></span>  (<i>Histories</i>, 1.20). Variants of his name are Hellesthaeus, Ellestheaeus, Eleshaah, Ellesboas, Elesbaan, and Elesboam.
</p><p>At Aksum, in inscription RIE 191, his name is rendered in unvocalized Gə‘əz as KLB ’L ’ṢBḤ WLD TZN (Kaleb ʾElla ʾAṣbeḥa, son of Tazena). In vocalized Gə‘əz, it is <span title="Ge&#39;ez-language text"><span lang="gez">ካሌብ እለ አጽብሐ</span></span> (Kaleb ʾƎllä ʾAṣbəḥa).
</p><p>Kaleb, a name derived from the Biblical character <a href="/wiki/Caleb" title="Caleb">Caleb</a>, was his given name; on both his coins and inscriptions he left at <a href="/wiki/Axum" title="Axum">Axum</a>, as well as Ethiopian hagiographical sources and king lists, he refers to himself as the son of Tazena.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kaleb_of_Axum&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Procopius, <a href="/wiki/John_of_Ephesus" title="John of Ephesus">John of Ephesus</a>, and other contemporary historians recount Kaleb's invasion of <a href="/wiki/Yemen" title="Yemen">Yemen</a> around 520, against the <a href="/wiki/Himyarite_Kingdom" title="Himyarite Kingdom">Himyarite king</a>, Yusuf Asar Yathar, known as <a href="/wiki/Dhu_Nuwas" title="Dhu Nuwas">Dhu Nuwas</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Jews" title="Jews">Jew</a> who was persecuting the <a href="/wiki/Christian_community_of_Najran" title="Christian community of Najran">Christian community of Najran</a>. After much fighting, Kaleb's soldiers eventually routed Yusuf's forces and killed the king, allowing Kaleb to appoint Sumuafa' Ashawa', a native Christian (named <a href="/wiki/Esimiphaios" class="mw-redirect" title="Esimiphaios">Esimiphaios</a> by Procopius), as his <a href="/wiki/Viceroy" title="Viceroy">viceroy</a> of Himyar.
</p><p>As a result of his protection of the Christians, Kaleb is known as Saint Elesbaan after the sixteenth-century <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_(Catholic_Church)" title="Cardinal (Catholic Church)">Cardinal</a> <a href="/wiki/Caesar_Baronius" title="Caesar Baronius">Caesar Baronius</a> added him to his edition of the <i><a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a></i> despite his being a <a href="/wiki/Miaphysitism" title="Miaphysitism">Miaphysite</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:402px;"><a href="/wiki/File:EthioYemenCities.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/EthioYemenCities.jpg/400px-EthioYemenCities.jpg" decoding="async" width="400" height="297" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/EthioYemenCities.jpg/600px-EthioYemenCities.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/03/EthioYemenCities.jpg 2x" data-file-width="771" data-file-height="572" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:EthioYemenCities.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>A reference map of the empire of Kaleb of Axum.</div></div></div>
<p>Axumite control of <a href="/wiki/Arabia_Felix" title="Arabia Felix">Arabia Felix</a> continued until c. 525 when Sumuafa' Ashawa' was deposed by <a href="/wiki/Abraha" title="Abraha">Abraha</a>, who made himself king. Procopius states that Kaleb made several unsuccessful attempts to recover his overseas territory; however, his successor later negotiated a peace with Abraha, where Abraha acknowledged the Axumite king's authority and paid tribute.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> Munro-Hay opines that by this expedition Axum overextended itself, and this final intervention across the <a href="/wiki/Red_Sea" title="Red Sea">Red Sea</a>, "was Aksum's swan-song as a great power in the region."<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A historical record survives of a meeting between the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine</a> ambassador and historian <a href="/wiki/Nonnosus_(historian)" title="Nonnosus (historian)">Nonnosus</a> and Kaleb in the year 530.<sup id="cite_ref-Bowersock,_G.W_2013_p._109_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bowersock,_G.W_2013_p._109-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Ethiopian tradition states that Kaleb eventually <a href="/wiki/Abdication" title="Abdication">abdicated</a> his throne, gave his crown to the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre" title="Church of the Holy Sepulchre">Church of the Holy Sepulchre</a> at <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>, and retired to a <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Tomb_of_St._Elesbaan_of_Axum_(2836160935).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/The_Tomb_of_St._Elesbaan_of_Axum_%282836160935%29.jpg/220px-The_Tomb_of_St._Elesbaan_of_Axum_%282836160935%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="145" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/The_Tomb_of_St._Elesbaan_of_Axum_%282836160935%29.jpg/330px-The_Tomb_of_St._Elesbaan_of_Axum_%282836160935%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/The_Tomb_of_St._Elesbaan_of_Axum_%282836160935%29.jpg/440px-The_Tomb_of_St._Elesbaan_of_Axum_%282836160935%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2826" data-file-height="1866" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Tomb_of_St._Elesbaan_of_Axum_(2836160935).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>The tomb of King Elesbaan</div></div></div>
<p>Later historians who recount the events of King Kaleb's reign include <a href="/wiki/Ibn_Hisham" title="Ibn Hisham">ibn Hisham</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ibn_Ishaq" title="Ibn Ishaq">ibn Ishaq</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Al-Tabari" title="Al-Tabari">al-Tabari</a>. Taddesse Tamrat records a tradition he heard from an aged priest in <a href="/wiki/Lalibela" title="Lalibela">Lalibela</a> that "Kaleb was a man of <a href="/wiki/Lasta" title="Lasta">Lasta</a> and his palace was at <a href="/wiki/Bugna" title="Bugna">Bugna</a> where it is known that <a href="/wiki/Gebre_Mesqel_Lalibela" class="mw-redirect" title="Gebre Mesqel Lalibela">Gebre Mesqel Lalibela</a> had later established his centre. The relevance of this tradition for us is the mere association of the name of Kaleb with the evangelization of this interior province of Aksum."<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Besides several inscriptions bearing his name,<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Axum also contains a pair of ruined structures, one said to be his tomb and its partner said to be the tomb of his son, Gabra Masqal. (Tradition gives him a second son, Israel, who, it has been suggested, is identical with king <a href="/wiki/Israel_of_Axum" title="Israel of Axum">Israel of Axum</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>) This structure was first examined as an archaeological subject by <a href="/wiki/Henry_Salt_(Egyptologist)" title="Henry Salt (Egyptologist)">Henry Salt</a> in the early 19th century; almost a century later, it was partially cleared and mapped out by the Deutsche Aksum-Expedition in 1906. The most recent excavation of this tomb was in 1973 by the <a href="/wiki/British_Institute_in_Eastern_Africa" class="mw-redirect" title="British Institute in Eastern Africa">British Institute in Eastern Africa</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> commemorates Kaleb as <i>"Saint Elesbaan, King of Ethiopia"</i> on <a href="/wiki/October_24_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics)" title="October 24 (Eastern Orthodox liturgics)">24 October</a> (<a href="/wiki/Adoption_of_the_Gregorian_calendar#Adoption_in_Eastern_Europe" title="Adoption of the Gregorian calendar">O.S.</a>) / 6 November (<a href="/wiki/Adoption_of_the_Gregorian_calendar#Adoption_in_Eastern_Europe" title="Adoption of the Gregorian calendar">N.S.</a>).
</p>
