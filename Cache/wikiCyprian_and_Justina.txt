<p><b>Saints Cyprian and Justina</b> are honored in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> and <a href="/wiki/Oriental_Orthodoxy" class="mw-redirect" title="Oriental Orthodoxy">Oriental Orthodoxy</a> as <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christians</a> of <a href="/wiki/Antioch" title="Antioch">Antioch</a>, who in 304, during the <a href="/wiki/Diocletianic_Persecution" title="Diocletianic Persecution">Diocletianic Persecution</a>, suffered <a href="/wiki/Martyrdom" class="mw-redirect" title="Martyrdom">martyrdom</a> at <a href="/wiki/Nicomedia" title="Nicomedia">Nicomedia</a> (modern-day <a href="/wiki/%C4%B0zmit" title="İzmit">İzmit</a>, <a href="/wiki/Turkey" title="Turkey">Turkey</a>) on September 26. According to Roman Catholic sources, no Bishop of Antioch bore the name of Cyprian.<sup id="cite_ref-Meier_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Meier-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Origin">Origin</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyprian_and_Justina&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Origin">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The story must have arisen as early as the 4th century, as it is mentioned by both <a href="/wiki/St._Gregory_Nazianzen" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Gregory Nazianzen">St. Gregory Nazianzen</a> and <a href="/wiki/Prudentius" title="Prudentius">Prudentius</a>; both, nevertheless, have conflated Cyprian with <a href="/wiki/St._Cyprian_of_Carthage" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Cyprian of Carthage">St. Cyprian of Carthage</a>, a mistake often repeated. The attempt has been made to find in Cyprian a mystical prototype of the <a href="/wiki/Faust" title="Faust">Faustian</a> legend. The legend is given in <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Greek language">Greek</a> and <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> in <i>Acta SS.</i> September, VII. Ancient <a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ethiopic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ethiopic">Ethiopic</a> versions of it have been published.<sup id="cite_ref-Meier_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Meier-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Their story is told in the <a href="/wiki/Golden_Legend" title="Golden Legend">Golden Legend</a>. 
</p><p>The outline of the legend or allegory is found with diffuse descriptions and dialogues in the unreliable <a href="/wiki/Symeon_the_Metaphrast" title="Symeon the Metaphrast">Symeon Metaphrastes</a> and was made the subject of a poem by Empress <a href="/wiki/Aelia_Eudocia" title="Aelia Eudocia">Aelia Eudocia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Meier_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Meier-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legend">Legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyprian_and_Justina&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Cyprianandjustinagoldenlegend.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d6/Cyprianandjustinagoldenlegend.jpg/220px-Cyprianandjustinagoldenlegend.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="279" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d6/Cyprianandjustinagoldenlegend.jpg/330px-Cyprianandjustinagoldenlegend.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d6/Cyprianandjustinagoldenlegend.jpg/440px-Cyprianandjustinagoldenlegend.jpg 2x" data-file-width="500" data-file-height="633" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Cyprianandjustinagoldenlegend.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Saint Cyprian and the demon, 14th-century manuscript of the <i><a href="/wiki/Golden_Legend" title="Golden Legend">Golden Legend</a></i>.</div></div></div>
<p>Cyprian, known by the title of "the Magician", to distinguish him from <a href="/wiki/Cyprian" title="Cyprian">Cyprian, Bishop of Carthage</a>, received a liberal education in his youth, and particularly applied himself to astrology; after which he traveled for improvement through Greece, Egypt, India, etc.<sup id="cite_ref-tow_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-tow-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Cyprian was a magician in Antioch and dealt in sorcery. 
</p><p>Justina is known for converting Cyprian,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and is said to have been a young woman who took private <a href="/wiki/Vow_of_chastity" class="mw-redirect" title="Vow of chastity">vows of chastity</a>. She was killed during the persecutions of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_emperor" title="Roman emperor">Roman emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a> in the year 304 AD. 
</p><p>A would-be suitor – a <a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">pagan</a> lawyer – sought the aid of Cyprian's magic to induce Justina into marrying him.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Cyprian sent a demon to torment Justina, hoping to arouse carnal passions within her. The charms and spells had no effect on Justina, who, in chastity and purity, spent her time in prayer and fasting. Cyprian again employed sorcery, sending "the chief of the demons" to Justina, who likewise was conquered by a certain "<a href="/wiki/Sign_of_the_cross" title="Sign of the cross">Sign of Christ</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-:0_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Brought to despair, Cyprian made the Sign himself and in this way was freed from the toils of <a href="/wiki/Satan" title="Satan">Satan</a>. Cyprian summarily burned any books of spells and sorcery in his possession in front of a bishop of the Church, sealing his commitment to a new life as a Christian. He was baptized and received into the Church and was made preeminent by miraculous gifts. Cyprian then became, in succession, a <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a>, <a href="/wiki/Priest" title="Priest">priest</a>, and finally, <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a> after being named as the successor to Anthimus, bishop of Antioch, while Justina became <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> of a <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-oca_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During the <a href="/wiki/Diocletian_Persecution" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocletian Persecution">Diocletian Persecution</a>, both were seized and taken to <a href="/wiki/Damascus" title="Damascus">Damascus</a>, where they were tortured. As their faith never wavered, they were brought before Diocletian at Nicomedia, where at his command they were <a href="/wiki/Decapitation" title="Decapitation">beheaded</a><sup id="cite_ref-oca_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> on the bank of the Gallus River, a <a href="/wiki/Tributary" title="Tributary">tributary</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Sakarya_River" title="Sakarya River">Sakarya River</a>. The same fate befell a man named Theoctistus, who upon observing Cyprian's faith, declared himself a Christian.<sup id="cite_ref-oca_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
After the bodies of the saints had lain unburied for six days, they were taken by Christian sailors to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, where they were interred on the estate of a noble lady named Rufina and later entombed in Constantine's <a href="/wiki/Basilica" title="Basilica">basilica</a>. 
</p><p>Justina is mentioned in <i><a href="/wiki/Foxe%27s_Book_of_Martyrs" title="Foxe&#39;s Book of Martyrs">Foxe's Book of Martyrs</a></i>. It was under the 10th Persecution in 303 AD while Diocletian was Emperor of Rome. It says:
</p>
<blockquote><p>" In the course of time he [Cyprian] became acquainted with Justina, a young lady of Antioch, whose birth, beauty, and accomplishments, rendered her the admiration of all who knew her. A pagan gentleman applied to Cyprian, to promote his suit with the beautiful Justina; this he undertook, but soon himself converted, burnt his books of astrology and magic, received baptism, and felt animated with a powerful spirit of grace. The conversion of Cyprian had a great effect on the pagan gentleman who paid his addresses to Justina, and he in a short time embraced Christianity. During the persecutions of Diocletian, Cyprian and Justina were seized upon as Christians, the former was torn with pincers, and the latter chastised; and, after suffering other torments, both were beheaded."<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2019)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup></p></blockquote>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration_and_liturgical_celebration">Veneration and liturgical celebration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyprian_and_Justina&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration and liturgical celebration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Their feast day appeared in the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> of <a href="/wiki/Roman_Rite" title="Roman Rite">Roman Rite</a> from the thirteenth century until it was deleted in 1969 because of the lack of historical evidence of their existence.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Their names were also removed from the 2001 revision of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a>, the official but professedly incomplete list of saints recognized by the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>. The Roman Martyrology, however, includes five other saints called Cyprian and two named Justina. Some <a href="/wiki/Traditionalist_Catholics" class="mw-redirect" title="Traditionalist Catholics">traditionalist Catholics</a> continue to observe their feast based on pre-1970 versions of the Roman Calendar.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_popular_culture">In popular culture</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyprian_and_Justina&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: In popular culture">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Spanish author, <a href="/wiki/Pedro_Calder%C3%B3n_de_la_Barca" title="Pedro Calderón de la Barca">Pedro Calderón de la Barca</a>, took the story as the basis of a drama: <i>El mágico prodigioso</i>. In 2005, American author Tono Rondone published a novel, <i>The Martyrs</i>, which is a continuation of this tradition.
<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><i><a href="/wiki/The_Great_Book_of_Saint_Cyprian" class="mw-redirect" title="The Great Book of Saint Cyprian">The Great Book of Saint Cyprian</a></i> is full of prayers and spells, and is widely sold in the Portuguese- and Spanish-speaking world.
</p><p><i><a href="/wiki/Cyprianus" title="Cyprianus">Cyprianus</a></i> is a popular name for a <a href="/wiki/Grimoire" title="Grimoire">grimoire</a> in Scandinavian folklore.
</p>
