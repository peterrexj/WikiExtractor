<p><b>Saints Chrysanthus and Daria</b> (3rd century – 283.AD) are <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saints</a> of the Early Christian period. Their names appear in the <i><a href="/wiki/Martyrologium_Hieronymianum" title="Martyrologium Hieronymianum">Martyrologium Hieronymianum</a></i>, an early <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyrs</a> list, and a church in their honour was built over their reputed grave in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legend">Legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chrysanthus_and_Daria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Acts_of_the_Martyrs" title="Acts of the Martyrs">Acts of the Martyrs</a> relating the legend of Chrysanthus and Daria exist in Greek and Latin versions, dating from the fifth century and all "without historical value", according to <a href="/wiki/Johann_Peter_Kirsch" title="Johann Peter Kirsch">Johann Peter Kirsch</a>, writing in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia" title="Catholic Encyclopedia">Catholic Encyclopedia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-newadvent_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-newadvent-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>According to legend, Chrysanthus was the only son of an <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egyptian</a> <a href="/wiki/Patrician_(ancient_Rome)" title="Patrician (ancient Rome)">patrician</a>, named Polemius or Poleon, who lived in the reign of <a href="/wiki/Numerian" title="Numerian">Numerian</a>. His father moved from <a href="/wiki/Alexandria" title="Alexandria">Alexandria</a> to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, and Chrysanthus was educated in the finest manner of the era. Disenchanted with the excess in the Roman world, he began reading the <a href="/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles" title="Acts of the Apostles">Acts of the Apostles</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-oca_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>He was then baptized and educated in the Christian faith by a priest named Carpophorus. His father was unhappy with Chrysanthus's conversion, and attempted to inculcate secular ways into his son by arranging a marriage to Daria, a Roman priestess of Minerva,<sup id="cite_ref-oca_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> or as other accounts state, a <a href="/wiki/Vestal_Virgin" title="Vestal Virgin">Vestal Virgin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Moss_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Moss-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Chrysanthus managed to convert his wife, and the couple agreed to lead <a href="/wiki/Celibacy" title="Celibacy">celibate</a> lives. They went on to convert a number of Romans. 
</p><p>When this was made known to Claudius, the <a href="/wiki/Tribune" title="Tribune">tribune</a>, Chrysanthus was arrested and tortured. Chrysanthus's faith and fortitude under torture were so impressive to Claudius that he and his wife, Hilaria, two sons named Maurus and Jason, and seventy of his soldiers became Christians. For this betrayal, the Emperor had Claudius drowned, his sons beheaded, and his wife hanged at the gallows. The legend states that Daria was forced to live as a prostitute, as virgins could not be executed, but her <a href="/wiki/Chastity" title="Chastity">chastity</a> was defended by a lioness. She was brought before <a href="/wiki/Numerian" title="Numerian">Numerian</a> and he ordered her death. There are many variations to the means, from <a href="/wiki/Lapidation" class="mw-redirect" title="Lapidation">execution by stoning</a>, while others say she was beheaded, and yet others claim she was buried alive in a deep pit beside her husband. It appears the last torment was chosen for Daria, as it was the execution method reserved for unchaste Vestal Virgins.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> They were entombed in a sand pit near the Via Salaria Nova, the <a href="/wiki/Catacombs" title="Catacombs">catacombs</a> in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-newadvent_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-newadvent-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The surviving "Acts" of Chrysanthus and Daria state that on the anniversary of their deaths, a large number of Christians had gathered at their underground crypt to pay their respects when Roman persecutors surprised them, filled the crypt with stones and buried them all alive, including Diodorus, a priest, and Marianus, a deacon.<sup id="cite_ref-newadvent_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-newadvent-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Historicity">Historicity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chrysanthus_and_Daria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Historicity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 2011 a scientific investigation of the bodies, conducted by a group of scholars coordinated by Ezio Fulcheri, paleopathologist at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Genoa" title="University of Genoa">University of Genoa</a>, highlighted the compatibility of the finds with the story handed down by tradition. 
</p><p>Radiocarbon dating reveals that the remains of the two young people, a male between the age of 17 and 18 and a female between 20 and 25, date back to an era of between 80 and 340 AD. A high concentration of lead was found in the bones, a sign of their wealthy background as only the rich had running water in the houses, conducted through lead pipes. None of the bodies showed signs of violence or illness, so the cause of death could be compatible with suffocation.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Relics">Relics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chrysanthus_and_Daria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Relics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>At least three places claim to possess the remains of Chrysanthus and Daria. In the ninth century, their reputed remains  were brought to <a href="/wiki/Pr%C3%BCm" title="Prüm">Prüm</a> in modern-day <a href="/wiki/Rhineland-Palatinate" title="Rhineland-Palatinate">Rhineland-Palatinate</a>, and these relics are presently in the church of Chrysanthus and Daria, <a href="/wiki/Bad_M%C3%BCnstereifel" title="Bad Münstereifel">Bad Münstereifel</a>, <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>  In 1011, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Sergius_IV" title="Pope Sergius IV">Pope Sergius IV</a> gave <a href="/wiki/Fulk_III,_Count_of_Anjou" title="Fulk III, Count of Anjou">Fulk III, Count of Anjou</a>, the reputed bodies of Chrysanthus and Daria upon his return from a <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> to <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>. Fulk gave them to the monastery of Belli Locus (now <a href="/wiki/Beaulieu-l%C3%A8s-Loches" title="Beaulieu-lès-Loches">Beaulieu-lès-Loches</a>), which he had recently established.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The cathedral of <a href="/wiki/Reggio_Emilia" title="Reggio Emilia">Reggio Emilia</a> in northern Italy also contains relics reputed to be those of Daria and Chrysanthus. A scientific study of some of the bones there confirmed that they were those of a young man and a young woman in their late teens, with a <a href="/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating" title="Radiocarbon dating">radiocarbon date</a> between 80 and 340.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
