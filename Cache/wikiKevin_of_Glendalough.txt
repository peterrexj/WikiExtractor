<p><b>Saint Kevin</b> (modern Irish <i><span title="Irish-language text"><i lang="ga">Caoimhín</i></span></i>; <a href="/wiki/Old_Irish" title="Old Irish">Old Irish</a> <i><span title="Old Irish (to 900)-language text"><i lang="sga">Cóemgen</i></span></i>, <i><span title="Old Irish (to 900)-language text"><i lang="sga">Caemgen</i></span></i>; latinized <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Coemgenus</i></span></i>; 498 (reputedly)–3 June 618) is an <a href="/wiki/Irish_saint" class="mw-redirect" title="Irish saint">Irish saint</a>, known as the founder and first abbot of <a href="/wiki/Glendalough" title="Glendalough">Glendalough</a> in County Wicklow, Ireland.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> His <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a> is 3 June.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kevin_of_Glendalough&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Kevin's life is not well-documented because no contemporaneous material survives.
</p><p>There is a late-medieval Latin <i>Vita</i>, preserved among the records of the Franciscan Convent in Dublin, edited by <a href="/wiki/John_Colgan" title="John Colgan">John Colgan</a> as part of the <i><a href="/wiki/Acta_Sanctorum_Hiberniae" title="Acta Sanctorum Hiberniae">Acta Sanctorum Hiberniae</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> According to that account, Kevin (like St. Columba) was of noble birth, the son of Coemlog and Coemell of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Leinster" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of Leinster">Leinster</a>. It says he was born in 498 AD at the Fort of the White Fountain and baptized by Saint <a href="/wiki/Cronan_of_Roscrea" class="mw-redirect" title="Cronan of Roscrea">Cronan of Roscrea</a>.  His given name <i>Coemgen</i> (anglicized <i><a href="/wiki/Kevin" title="Kevin">Kevin</a></i>) means  "fair-begotten", or "of noble birth".<sup id="cite_ref-edmonds_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-edmonds-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  A tradition cited in the 17th century makes Kevin the pupil of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Petroc" title="Saint Petroc">Saint Petroc</a> of <a href="/wiki/Cornwall" title="Cornwall">Cornwall</a>, who had come to Leinster about 492. That  claim is not found in the extant late-medieval and early-modern <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> of the saint, and appears to be based in a  <i>Vita breviora</i> which the <a href="/wiki/Bollandist" title="Bollandist">Bollandist</a> editors obtained from <a href="/wiki/Henry_Fitzsimon" title="Henry Fitzsimon">Henry Fitzsimon</a>, but which is no longer extant.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The <i>Vita</i> also contains a number of legends which, according to Colgan's co-editor Francis Baert, are of "doubtful veracity", but were kept in the 17th-century edition because they were assumed to date to the medieval period.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> For example, the text includes an infancy legend involving a white cow, said to have come to his parents' house every morning and evening, which supplied the milk for the baby.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Glendalough">Glendalough</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kevin_of_Glendalough&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Glendalough">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Glendalough" title="Glendalough">Glendalough</a></div>
<p>Glendalough, or the Glen of two Lakes, is one of the most important sites of monastic ruins in Ireland. Before the arrival of St. Kevin this glen would have been desolate and remote and would have been ideal for a secluded retreat.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Hermitage">Hermitage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kevin_of_Glendalough&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Hermitage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:S.kevin%27s_bed.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/S.kevin%27s_bed.jpg/220px-S.kevin%27s_bed.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/S.kevin%27s_bed.jpg/330px-S.kevin%27s_bed.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/S.kevin%27s_bed.jpg/440px-S.kevin%27s_bed.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3888" data-file-height="2592" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:S.kevin%27s_bed.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>St. Kevin's bed</div></div></div>
<p>Kevin was ordained by Bishop Lugidus and, following his ordination, he moved on to Glendalough in order to avoid the company of his followers. He lived as a hermit in a partially man made cave (sometimes incorrectly described as a Bronze age tomb) now known as St. Kevin's Bed, to which he was led, in the account of the <i>Vita</i>, by an angel. 
</p><p>St. Kevin's Bed can best be described as a man-made cave cut in the rock face very close to the edge of the mountain. It overlooks the <a href="/wiki/Glendalough_Upper_Lake" title="Glendalough Upper Lake">upper lake</a> from a height of about 10 metres (30 feet). The approach to the cave is very difficult, with access to it through a rectangular space and a short passageway 1 metre (3 feet) high and <style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1020198016">.mw-parser-output .frac{white-space:nowrap}.mw-parser-output .frac .num,.mw-parser-output .frac .den{font-size:80%;line-height:0;vertical-align:super}.mw-parser-output .frac .den{vertical-align:sub}.mw-parser-output .sr-only{border:0;clip:rect(0,0,0,0);height:1px;margin:-1px;overflow:hidden;padding:0;position:absolute;width:1px}</style>80 centimetres (<span class="frac" role="math">2<span class="sr-only">+</span><span class="num">1</span>&#8260;<span class="den">2</span></span> feet) wide. The inner or main part of the cave is just 1.2 metres (4 feet) wide and less than 1 metre (3 feet) high. It is reasonable to assume that the cave could only have been used as a sleeping place, and would have been impossible for an adult to stand upright in, so it is quite likely that St Kevin only used it as his bed, or a place for pious prayer or <a href="/wiki/Meditation" title="Meditation">meditation</a>. 
</p><p>There is a legend which claims that <a href="/wiki/Lorc%C3%A1n_Ua_Tuathail" title="Lorcán Ua Tuathail">St Laurence O'Toole</a> used the "bed" as he frequently made penitential visits to Glendalough, especially during the season of <a href="/wiki/Lent" title="Lent">Lent</a>. <a href="/wiki/Michael_Dwyer" title="Michael Dwyer">Michael Dwyer</a>, the famous Wicklow rebel, is reputed to have taken shelter in the "bed" while he was on the run from British soldiers. The story goes that he escaped capture one morning by diving into the lake and swimming to the opposite side. Today, it is highly dangerous to try to approach the "bed" from the side of Lugduff mountain. Visitors, in the interests of their own safety, should be content with a distant view of it.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastery">Monastery</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kevin_of_Glendalough&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Monastery">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Kevin_-_Glendalough.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9c/St_Kevin_-_Glendalough.jpg/220px-St_Kevin_-_Glendalough.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9c/St_Kevin_-_Glendalough.jpg/330px-St_Kevin_-_Glendalough.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9c/St_Kevin_-_Glendalough.jpg/440px-St_Kevin_-_Glendalough.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1728" data-file-height="1152" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Kevin_-_Glendalough.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>The chapel of St Kevin at <a href="/wiki/Glendalough" title="Glendalough">Glendalough</a></div></div></div>
<p>Kevin lived the life of a hermit there with an extraordinary closeness to nature. His companions were the animals and birds all around him. He lived as a hermit for seven years wearing only animal skins, sleeping on stones and eating very sparingly.
</p><p>He went barefoot and spent his time in prayer. Disciples were soon attracted to Kevin and a further settlement enclosed by a wall, called Kevin's Cell, was established nearer the lakeshore. By 540 Saint Kevin's fame as a teacher and holy man had spread far and wide. Many people came to seek his help and guidance. In time Glendalough grew into a renowned seminary of saints and scholars and the parent of several other monasteries.
</p><p>In 544, Kevin went to the Hill of Uisneach in <a href="/wiki/County_Westmeath" title="County Westmeath">County Westmeath</a> to visit the holy abbots, Sts. <a href="/wiki/Columba" title="Columba">Columba</a>, <a href="/wiki/Comgall" title="Comgall">Comgall</a> and Cannich. He then proceeded to <a href="/wiki/Clonmacnoise" title="Clonmacnoise">Clonmacnoise</a>, where <a href="/wiki/Cieran" class="mw-redirect" title="Cieran">St. Cieran</a> had died three days before. Having firmly established his community, he retired into solitude for four years, and only returned to Glendalough at the earnest entreaty of his monks.<sup id="cite_ref-edmonds_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-edmonds-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Until his death around 618, Kevin presided over his monastery in Glendalough, living his life by fasting, praying and teaching.
St Kevin is one of the patron saints of the diocese of Dublin.
</p><p>He belonged to the second order of Irish saints.<sup id="cite_ref-edmonds_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-edmonds-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Eventually, Glendalough, with its seven churches, became one of the chief <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> destinations in Ireland.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy_and_veneration">Legacy and veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kevin_of_Glendalough&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Legacy and veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Kevin of Glendalough was canonized by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_X" title="Pope Pius X">Pope Pius X</a> on 9 December 1903 (<a href="/wiki/Cultus_confirmation" class="mw-redirect" title="Cultus confirmation">cultus confirmation</a>). 
</p><p>One of the most widely known poems of the Nobel prizewinner <a href="/wiki/Seamus_Heaney" title="Seamus Heaney">Seamus Heaney</a>, 'St Kevin and the Blackbird', relates the story of Kevin holding out his hand with trance-like stillness while a blackbird builds a nest in it, lays eggs, the eggs hatch and the chicks fledge.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
A  series of paintings by the Welsh artist <a href="/wiki/Clive_Hicks-Jenkins" title="Clive Hicks-Jenkins">Clive Hicks-Jenkins</a> around 2009 depicted the story of Kevin and the blackbird, by way of Heaney's poem.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Kevin is remembered in popular culture as an <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a>. This is commemorated in a <a href="/wiki/Folk_song" class="mw-redirect" title="Folk song">folk song</a> about him which describes a legend claiming that he drowned a woman who attempted to seduce him. This was recorded and made popular by <a href="/wiki/The_Dubliners" title="The Dubliners">The Dubliners</a>. The opening verse is as follows: "In Glendalough, there lived an auld saint, renowned for his learning and piety, his manners were curious and quaint, and he looked upon girls with disparity."
</p><p>The independent film-maker <a href="/wiki/Kevin_Smith" title="Kevin Smith">Kevin Smith</a> refers irreverently to his namesake "Saint Kevin" and the key events of his life in the introduction to <i><a href="/wiki/Sold_Out:_A_Threevening_with_Kevin_Smith" title="Sold Out: A Threevening with Kevin Smith">Sold Out: A Threevening with Kevin Smith</a></i>, his 2008 live Q &amp; A show.
</p><p>Kevin is referenced several times in <i><a href="/wiki/Finnegans_Wake" title="Finnegans Wake">Finnegans Wake</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/James_Joyce" title="James Joyce">James Joyce</a>. The longest episode is found in Pt IV, pgs 604–607 (Faber &amp; Faber, Viking editions). It was one of the earliest-drafted "vignettes" and much revised before final publication.
</p>
