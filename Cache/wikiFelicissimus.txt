<p><b>Felicissimus</b> (died 271?) was a public officer in <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Rome" title="Ancient Rome">Ancient Rome</a>, during the reign of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Aurelian" title="Aurelian">Aurelian</a>. He is famous because he led an uprising of mint workers against the Emperor, but was defeated and killed, possibly in 274, but more probably in 271.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mint_workers_revolt">Mint workers revolt</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Felicissimus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Mint workers revolt">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Felicissimus was a <i><a href="/wiki/Rationalis" title="Rationalis">rationalis</a></i>, the chief of the state treasury. One of the responsibilities of the rationalis was to administer the imperial mints. The workers of the mint in Rome had been engaged in adulterating the coinage for their own profit.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> This involved the Divus Claudius coin, which was issued during Aurelian's early reign to establish his right to be the successor of <a href="/wiki/Claudius_Gothicus" title="Claudius Gothicus">Claudius</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Felicissimus was held responsible and when he was challenged by Aurelian, he incited the mint workers to revolt.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> In the uprising that followed, it is reported that the workers were massacred while 7,000 soldiers were killed during this revolt (Aurelius Victor xxxv 6; <i>Historia Augusta</i>, <i>Aurelianus</i>, xxxviii 2–4). The battle took place on the <a href="/wiki/Caelian_Hill" title="Caelian Hill">Caelian Hill</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> It is possible that this uprising was somehow connected with the senatorial and equestrian classes, as Aurelian executed several senators.
</p><p>The fact that the mint of Rome was inactive for a short time before the monetary reform of 274 could be a consequence of this revolt, and the poor quality of the coinage at the beginning of Aurelian's reign supports the suggestion that the workers at the mint were adulterating the coinage. Aurelian chose <a href="/wiki/Gaius_Valarius_Sabinus" title="Gaius Valarius Sabinus">Gaius Valerius Sabinus</a> as Felicissimus' successor and the mint at Rome was transferred to <a href="/wiki/Mediolanum" title="Mediolanum">Mediolanum</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
