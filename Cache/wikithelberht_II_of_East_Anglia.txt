<p><b>Æthelberht</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_English" title="Old English">Old English</a>: <i>Æðelbrihte</i>, <i>ÆÞelberhte</i>), also called <b>Saint Ethelbert the King</b> (died 20 May 794 at <a href="/wiki/Sutton_Walls_Hill_Fort" title="Sutton Walls Hill Fort">Sutton Walls</a>, <a href="/wiki/Herefordshire" title="Herefordshire">Herefordshire</a>), was an eighth-century <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and a <a href="/wiki/King" title="King">king</a> of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">East Anglia</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxon</a> kingdom which today includes the English counties of <a href="/wiki/Norfolk" title="Norfolk">Norfolk</a> and <a href="/wiki/Suffolk" title="Suffolk">Suffolk</a>. Little is known of his reign, which may have begun in 779, according to later sources, and very few of the coins he issued have been discovered. It is known from the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> that he was killed on the orders of <a href="/wiki/Offa_of_Mercia" title="Offa of Mercia">Offa of Mercia</a> in 794.
</p><p>Æthelberht was locally <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonised</a> and became the focus of <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cults</a> in East Anglia and at <a href="/wiki/Hereford" title="Hereford">Hereford</a>, where the shrine of the saintly king once existed. In the absence of known historical facts, mediaeval <a href="/wiki/Chroniclers" class="mw-redirect" title="Chroniclers">chroniclers</a> provided their own details for his ancestry, life as king, and death at the hands of Offa. His feast day is 20 May. There are churches in Norfolk, Suffolk and the west of England dedicated to him, and he is a joint <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron</a> of <a href="/wiki/Hereford_Cathedral" title="Hereford Cathedral">Hereford Cathedral</a>.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life_and_reign">Life and reign</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelberht_II_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life and reign">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Williamson_p16_3.svg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Williamson_p16_3.svg/220px-Williamson_p16_3.svg.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="193" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Williamson_p16_3.svg/330px-Williamson_p16_3.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Williamson_p16_3.svg/440px-Williamson_p16_3.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="691" data-file-height="607" /></a><figcaption>The <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">kingdom of East Anglia</a></figcaption></figure>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle_-_Aethelberhte.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d7/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle_-_Aethelberhte.jpg/220px-Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle_-_Aethelberhte.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="62" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d7/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle_-_Aethelberhte.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="247" data-file-height="70" /></a><figcaption>Æthelberht's  name in the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i></figcaption></figure>
<p>Little is known of Æthelberht's life or reign, as few <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">East Anglian</a> records have survived from this period.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200258_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200258-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Æthelberht's reign may have begun in 779, the date provided on the uncertain authority of a much later <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">saint's life</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200264_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200264-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Mediaeval <a href="/wiki/Chroniclers" class="mw-redirect" title="Chroniclers">chroniclers</a> have provided dubious accounts of his life, in the absence of any real details. According to <a href="/wiki/Richard_of_Cirencester" title="Richard of Cirencester">Richard of Cirencester</a>, writing in the 15th century, Æthelberht's parents were <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_I_of_East_Anglia" title="Æthelred I of East Anglia">Æthelred I of East Anglia</a> and Leofrana of <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercia</a>. Richard narrates in detail a story of Æthelberht's piety, election as king and wise rule. Urged to marry against his will, he apparently agreed to wed <a href="/wiki/Eadburh" title="Eadburh">Eadburh</a>, the daughter of <a href="/wiki/Offa_of_Mercia" title="Offa of Mercia">Offa of Mercia</a>, and set out to visit her, despite his mother's forebodings and his experiences of terrifying events—an earthquake, a <a href="/wiki/Solar_eclipse" title="Solar eclipse">solar eclipse</a> and a vision.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopedia_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopedia-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Four pennies minted by Æthelberht are known (as of 2014<sup class="plainlinks noexcerpt noprint asof-tag update" style="display:none;"><a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelberht_II_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit">&#91;update&#93;</a></sup>)—two of which have been known since the 18th century, and one since the beginning of the 20th century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENaismith2014230_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENaismith2014230-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> One of these, a 'light' penny, said to have been discovered in 1908 at <a href="/wiki/Tivoli,_Lazio" title="Tivoli, Lazio">Tivoli</a>, near Rome, is similar in type to the coinage of Offa. On one side is the word <i>REX</i>, with an image of <a href="/wiki/Romulus" title="Romulus">Romulus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Remus" class="mw-redirect" title="Remus">Remus</a> suckling a wolf: the obverse names the king and his moneyer, Lul, who also struck coins for Offa and <a href="/wiki/Coenwulf_of_Mercia" title="Coenwulf of Mercia">Coenwulf of Mercia</a>. The author Andy Hutcheson has suggested that the use of runes on the coin may signify "continuing strong control by local leaders".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHutcheson2009203_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHutcheson2009203-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Numismatist" title="Numismatist">numismatist</a> <a href="/wiki/Marion_Archibald" title="Marion Archibald">Marion Archibald</a> notes that the issuing of "flattering" coins of this type, with the intention to win friends in Rome, probably indicated that as a sub-king, Æthelberht was assuming "a greater degree of independence than [Offa] was prepared to tolerate".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArchibald198534_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArchibald198534-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The coins provide one of the few contemporary sources for Æthelberht. In March 2014 the fourth coin was discovered in <a href="/wiki/Sussex" title="Sussex">Sussex</a> by a <a href="/wiki/Metal_detector" title="Metal detector">metal detectorist</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENaismith2014230_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENaismith2014230-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> According to the British numismatist Rory Naismith, "Æthelberht’s coins could have been issued over a number of years, either during a spell when some or all of East Anglia asserted independence from Offa, or by some sort of arrangement to share <a href="/wiki/Minting_rights" title="Minting rights">minting rights</a> with the Mercian ruler."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENaismith2014231_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENaismith2014231-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Offa stopped Æthelberht from minting his own coins.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKirby2000147_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKirby2000147-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 793 the vulnerability of the English east coast was exposed when the monastery at <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a> was looted by <a href="/wiki/Vikings" title="Vikings">Vikings</a>, and a year later <a href="/wiki/Monkwearmouth%E2%80%93Jarrow_Abbey" title="Monkwearmouth–Jarrow Abbey">Jarrow</a> was also attacked, which the historian Steven Plunkett reasons would ensure that the East Anglians were "forced to seek firm leadership" in order to strengthen the region's defences.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005171–172_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005171–172-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Æthelberht's claim to belong to the ruling <a href="/wiki/Wuffingas" title="Wuffingas">Wuffingas</a> dynasty, suggested by the use of a <a href="/wiki/Romulus_and_Remus" title="Romulus and Remus">Roman she-wolf</a> and the title <i>REX</i> on his coins, arose from the need for strong kingship in response to the Viking attacks.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005171–172_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005171–172-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_canonisation">Death and canonisation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelberht_II_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Death and canonisation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1066479718"><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div style="display:inline;" class="fn">Æthelberht II of East Anglia</div></th></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Venerated&#160;in</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a>,
 <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><span class="nowrap">Major <a href="/wiki/Shrine" title="Shrine">shrine</a></span></th><td class="infobox-data">previously at <a href="/wiki/Hereford_Cathedral" title="Hereford Cathedral">Hereford Cathedral</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Feast</a></th><td class="infobox-data">20 May, 
29 May in Eastern Orthodox Church</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Æthelberht was put to death by Offa under unclear circumstances, apparently at the <a href="/wiki/Royal_vill" title="Royal vill">royal vill</a> at <a href="/wiki/Sutton_Walls_Hill_Fort" title="Sutton Walls Hill Fort">Sutton Walls</a> in <a href="/wiki/Herefordshire" title="Herefordshire">Herefordshire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke20029_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke20029-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> According to the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i>, he was <a href="/wiki/Decapitation" title="Decapitation">beheaded</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESwanton199755_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESwanton199755-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Mediaeval sources tell how he was captured while visiting his future bride <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfthryth_of_Crowland" title="Ælfthryth of Crowland">Ælfthryth</a> and was then murdered and buried. In Richard of Cirencester's account, which cannot be substantiated, Offa's evil queen <a href="/wiki/Cynethryth" title="Cynethryth">Cynethryth</a> persuaded her husband to kill his guest, who was then bound and beheaded by a certain Grimbert, and his body disposed of. The mediaeval historian <a href="/wiki/John_Brompton" title="John Brompton">John Brompton</a>'s <i>Chronicon</i> describes how the king's detached head fell off a cart into a ditch where it was found, before it restored a blind man's sight. According to the <i>Chronicon</i>, Ælfthyth became a recluse at <a href="/wiki/Crowland_Abbey" title="Crowland Abbey">Crowland</a> and her remorseful father founded <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monasteries</a>, gave land to the <a href="/wiki/Christian_Church" title="Christian Church">Church</a> and travelled on a <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> to Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopedia_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopedia-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The execution of an Anglo-Saxon king on the orders of another ruler was very rare, although criminals were hanged and beheaded, as has been discovered at <a href="/wiki/Sutton_Hoo" title="Sutton Hoo">Sutton Hoo</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005173_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005173-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Æthelberht's death made the possibility of any peaceful union between the Anglian peoples, including Mercia, less likely than before.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKirby2000148_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKirby2000148-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>In 2014, metal-detectorist Darrin Simpson found a coin minted by Æthelbert in a <a href="/wiki/Sussex" title="Sussex">Sussex</a> field. Such coins, struck as a sign of independence, may have led to Æthelbert's death.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> It sold at auction on 11 June 2014 for £78,000.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelberht_II_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelberht_II_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Ethelbert_the_King,_with_Christ.png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/St._Ethelbert_the_King%2C_with_Christ.png/220px-St._Ethelbert_the_King%2C_with_Christ.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="253" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/St._Ethelbert_the_King%2C_with_Christ.png/330px-St._Ethelbert_the_King%2C_with_Christ.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/St._Ethelbert_the_King%2C_with_Christ.png/440px-St._Ethelbert_the_King%2C_with_Christ.png 2x" data-file-width="473" data-file-height="544" /></a><figcaption>St. Ethelbert (<i>left</i>) with Christ, from St. Ethelbert's Church, <a href="/wiki/Alby_with_Thwaite" title="Alby with Thwaite">Alby, Norfolk</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>After his death, Æthelberht was <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonised</a> by the English Church. A number of <i><a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">Lives</a></i> of St Æthelberht were written during the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>, including those by <a href="/wiki/Gerald_of_Wales" title="Gerald of Wales">Gerald of Wales</a> and <a href="/wiki/Osbert_of_Clare" title="Osbert of Clare">Osbert of Clare</a> in the 12th century, and hagiographies by <a href="/wiki/Roger_of_Wendover" title="Roger of Wendover">Roger of Wendover</a> and <a href="/wiki/Matthew_Paris" title="Matthew Paris">Matthew Paris</a> the following century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETodd2004_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETodd2004-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He was venerated in religious cults in both East Anglia and at Hereford. The Anglo-Saxon church of the <a href="/wiki/Diocese" title="Diocese">episcopal</a> estate at <a href="/wiki/Hoxne" title="Hoxne">Hoxne</a> was one of several dedicated to him in Suffolk. The church is mentioned in the  will of Theodreusus, Bishop of London and Hoxne (c. 938 – c. 951), which is a possible indication of the existence of a <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">religious cult</a> devoted to the saintly king.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWarner1996123_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWarner1996123-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Only three dedications for Æthelberht are near where he died – <a href="/wiki/Marden,_Herefordshire" title="Marden, Herefordshire">Marden</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hereford_Cathedral" title="Hereford Cathedral">Hereford Cathedral</a> and <a href="/wiki/Littledean" title="Littledean">Littledean</a> – the other eleven being in Norfolk or Suffolk. The historian Lawrence Butler has argued that this unusual pattern may be explained by the existence of a royal cult in East Anglia, which represented a "revival of Christianity after the Danish settlement by commemorating a politically 'safe' and corporeally distant local ruler".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButler198644–50_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButler198644–50-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Christian_buildings_dedicated_to_.C3.86thelberht"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Christian_buildings_dedicated_to_Æthelberht">Christian buildings dedicated to Æthelberht</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelberht_II_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Christian buildings dedicated to Æthelberht">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Portion_of_Saint_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Portion_of_Saint_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg/220px-Portion_of_Saint_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="220" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Portion_of_Saint_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg/330px-Portion_of_Saint_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Portion_of_Saint_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg/440px-Portion_of_Saint_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3024" data-file-height="3024" /></a><figcaption>Portion of the Saint Ethelbert Gate at <a href="/wiki/Norwich_Cathedral" title="Norwich Cathedral">Norwich Cathedral</a></figcaption></figure>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Upper_part_of_St_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Upper_part_of_St_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg/220px-Upper_part_of_St_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="220" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Upper_part_of_St_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg/330px-Upper_part_of_St_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Upper_part_of_St_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg/440px-Upper_part_of_St_Ethelbert_Gate_at_Norwich_Cathedral.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3024" data-file-height="3024" /></a><figcaption>Upper part of the St Ethelbert Gate at Norwich Cathedral</figcaption></figure>
<p>The Blessed Virgin Mary and St. Ethelbert are joint patrons of Hereford Cathedral, where the music for the <i>Office of St Ethelbert</i> survives in the 13th century <i>Hereford Noted <a href="/wiki/Breviary" title="Breviary">Breviary</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECaldwell200139_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECaldwell200139-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>St. Ethelbert's Gate is one of the two main entrances to the precinct of <a href="/wiki/Norwich_Cathedral" title="Norwich Cathedral">Norwich Cathedral</a>. The chapel at <i>Albrightestone</i>, at a location near an important excavated Anglo-Saxon cemetery at Boss Hall in <a href="/wiki/Ipswich" title="Ipswich">Ipswich</a>, was dedicated to Æthelberht. In Wiltshire, the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England_parish_church" title="Church of England parish church">Church of England parish church</a> at <a href="/wiki/Luckington" title="Luckington">Luckington</a> is dedicated to St Mary and St Ethelbert. In Norfolk, the Church of England parish churches at <a href="/wiki/Alby_with_Thwaite" title="Alby with Thwaite">Alby</a>, <a href="/wiki/Wretham" title="Wretham">East Wretham</a>, <a href="/wiki/Larling" title="Larling">Larling</a>, <a href="/wiki/Thurton" title="Thurton">Thurton</a>, <a href="/wiki/Mundham,_South_Norfolk" class="mw-redirect" title="Mundham, South Norfolk">Mundham</a> and <a href="/wiki/Burnham_Market" title="Burnham Market">Burnham Sutton</a> (where there are remains of the ruined church) and the Suffolk churches at <a href="/wiki/Falkenham" title="Falkenham">Falkenham</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hessett" title="Hessett">Hessett</a>, <a href="/wiki/Herringswell" title="Herringswell">Herringswell</a> and <a href="/wiki/Tannington" title="Tannington">Tannington</a> are all dedicated to the saint. In neighbouring <a href="/wiki/Essex" title="Essex">Essex</a>, the parish church at <a href="/wiki/Belchamp_Otten" title="Belchamp Otten">Belchamp Otten</a> is dedicated to St Ethelbert and All Saints, and the church at <a href="/wiki/Stanway,_Essex" title="Stanway, Essex">Stanway</a>, originally an Anglo-Saxon chapel, is dedicated to St Albright, which is believed to be the same saint.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBuckler1856242_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuckler1856242-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> In 1937, St Ethelbert's name was added to the <a href="/wiki/St_George_and_St_Ethelbert%27s_Church,_East_Ham" title="St George and St Ethelbert&#39;s Church, East Ham">parish church of St George</a> in <a href="/wiki/East_Ham" title="East Ham">East Ham</a>, Essex (now London), at the behest of Hereford Cathedral which had funded the rebuilding of the church, previously a temporary wooden structure.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
