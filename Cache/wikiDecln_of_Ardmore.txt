<p><b>Declán of Ardmore</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_Irish_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old Irish language">Old Irish</a>: <i lang="sga">Declán mac Eircc</i>; <a href="/wiki/Irish_language" title="Irish language">Irish</a>: <i lang="ga">Deaglán, Deuglán</i>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Declanus</i>; died 5th century AD), also called <b>Déclán</b>, was an early <a href="/wiki/Irish_people" title="Irish people">Irish</a> saint of the <a href="/wiki/D%C3%A9isi_Muman" class="mw-redirect" title="Déisi Muman">Déisi Muman</a>, who was remembered for having converted the Déisi in the late 5th century and for having founded the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Ardmore,_County_Waterford" title="Ardmore, County Waterford">Ardmore</a> (<i>Ard Mór</i>) in what is now County Waterford.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNBMunster_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNBMunster-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The principal source for his life and cult is a Latin <i>Life</i> of the 12th century. Like <a href="/wiki/Ailbe_of_Emly" title="Ailbe of Emly">Ailbe of Emly</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ciar%C3%A1n_of_Saighir" class="mw-redirect" title="Ciarán of Saighir">Ciarán of Saigir</a> and <a href="/wiki/Abban_of_Magheranoidhe" class="mw-redirect" title="Abban of Magheranoidhe">Abbán of Moyarney</a>, Declán is presented as a <a href="/wiki/Munster" title="Munster">Munster</a> saint who preceded <a href="/wiki/Saint_Patrick" title="Saint Patrick">Saint Patrick</a> in bringing Christianity to Ireland. He was regarded as a patron saint of the <a href="/wiki/D%C3%A9isi" title="Déisi">Déisi</a> of East Munster.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNBMunster_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNBMunster-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> 
</p>

<p>The main source for Declán's life and cult is a Latin <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">Life</a> or <i>vita</i>, which, however, survives only in a redaction of the late 12th century.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNBMunster_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNBMunster-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> It is witnessed by two manuscript texts which Charles Plummer has shown to derive from the same original: (1) Dublin, TCD, MS E.3.11 (dubbed T by Plummer), f. 66b-71d; and (2) a somewhat more damaged version in Dublin, Primate <a href="/wiki/Marsh%27s_Library" title="Marsh&#39;s Library">Marsh's Library</a>, MS V.3.4 (Plummer's M), f. 101 ff. These two manuscripts are also collectively known as the Dublin Collection (or the <i>Codex Kilkenniensis</i>, though the name is also used to refer to the Primate Marsh's Library manuscript only).<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> In its received form, the <i>Life</i> leans heavily on the <i>Life</i> of St <a href="/wiki/Ailbe_of_Emly" title="Ailbe of Emly">Ailbe of Emly</a> in the <i><a href="/wiki/Codex_Salmanticensis" title="Codex Salmanticensis">Codex Salmanticensis</a></i>, but earlier materials may have been incorporated.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNBMunster_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNBMunster-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The introductory chapters draw on early Irish sagas, notably the origin legend of Déisi related in the <i><a href="/wiki/Expulsion_of_the_D%C3%A9isi" class="mw-redirect" title="Expulsion of the Déisi">Expulsion of the Déisi</a></i> and the story of <a href="/wiki/Lugaid_Riab_nDerg" title="Lugaid Riab nDerg">Lugaid Riab nDerg</a> in <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Aided_Meidbe&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Aided Meidbe (page does not exist)">Aided Meidbe</a></i> and <i><a href="/wiki/Medb" title="Medb">Medb</a>'s Three Husbands</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Declán is emphatically designated as a bishop of the Déisi, which appears to echo the monastery's political ambitions in the 12th century, when the Irish Church was reformed into a <a href="/wiki/Diocese" title="Diocese">diocesan</a> system following the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Rathbreasail" class="mw-redirect" title="Synod of Rathbreasail">Synod of Rathbreasail</a> and <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Kells" title="Synod of Kells">Synod of Kells</a>. Ardmore aspired to the status of <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_see" title="Episcopal see">episcopal see</a> in the new diocese, but the privilege went instead to <a href="/wiki/Lismore,_County_Waterford" title="Lismore, County Waterford">Lismore</a>, founded by <a href="/wiki/St_Mochuda" class="mw-redirect" title="St Mochuda">St Mochuda</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNBMunster_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNBMunster-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Declán's Latin <i>Life</i> was later translated into Irish. This vernacular version, sometimes referred to as <i>Betha Decclain</i>, is preserved in two classes of copies. The earliest of these is a copy made, with some revision, by <a href="/wiki/M%C3%ADche%C3%A1l_%C3%93_Cl%C3%A9irigh" title="Mícheál Ó Cléirigh">Mícheál Ó Cléirigh</a> in 1629 and catalogued as Brussels, Royal Library, MS 4190–4200. Ó Cléirigh reports that his ultimate source was an "old book" (<i>seinleabhar</i>), but his direct exemplar was a manuscript dated 1582, in the possession of Eochaidh Ui Ifernain (Eochy O'Heffernan).<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Power_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Power-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The two remaining copies are Dublin, Royal Irish Academy MS 23 M 50, pp. 109–120, in the hand of one John Murphy "na Raheenach" and dated 1740, and a further manuscript once in private possession. The exemplar which underlies either of these is itself an imperfectly transmitted text.<sup id="cite_ref-Power_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Power-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Genealogies relevant to the saint are included in the <i><a href="/wiki/Book_of_Leinster" title="Book of Leinster">Book of Leinster</a></i>, <i><a href="/wiki/An_Leabhar_Breac" title="An Leabhar Breac">An Leabhar Breac</a></i>, <i><a href="/wiki/Book_of_Ballymote" title="Book of Ballymote">Book of Ballymote</a></i>, and a gloss to <i><a href="/wiki/F%C3%A9lire_%C3%93engusso" class="mw-redirect" title="Félire Óengusso">Félire Óengusso</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and <a href="/wiki/Rawlinson_B_502" class="mw-redirect" title="Rawlinson B 502">Rawlinson B 502</a>.
</p>

<p>It was through his father that Declán belonged to the royal dynasty of the <a href="/wiki/D%C3%A9isi_Muman" class="mw-redirect" title="Déisi Muman">Déisi Muman</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNBMunster_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNBMunster-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The Latin <i>Life</i> names his father Erc(c), as do the <i>Félire Óengusso</i> and the genealogy in the Book of Ballymote, f. 231b. Variant traditions are recorded in the Book of Leinster (f. 348c) and the Book of Ballymote, f. 218b, which call his father Ernbrand, and in the Leabhar Breac (f. 15d), which calls him Ross (or Russ). The conflated version Ercbrand is found in Rawlinson B 502.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Declán's mother Dethiden or Dethidin, as she is called in the Latin <i>Life</i> (§ 3), is not given any pedigree in the sources.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNBMunster_1-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNBMunster-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Declán's birthplace is said to be Drumroe, near <a href="/wiki/Cappoquin" title="Cappoquin">Cappoquin</a> (western County Waterford).<sup id="cite_ref-DIB_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DIB-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the Latin <i>Life</i>, Declán first embarks on a journey to Rome, where he studied and was ordained bishop by the Pope.<sup id="cite_ref-DIB_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DIB-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> At Rome, he meets his fellow countryman St Ailbe of Emly, and on returning to Ireland, he meets <a href="/wiki/St_Patrick" class="mw-redirect" title="St Patrick">St Patrick</a>. Throughout the text, Declán recognises the supreme authority of both saints and with Patrick he comes to an arrangement about the sphere of their mission in Ireland.<sup id="cite_ref-DIB_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DIB-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> On St Patrick's instructions, Declán founds the monastery of Ardmore (Irish <i>Ard Mór</i>), which lies near the Irish coast, in the southeast of the kingdom of the Déisi Muman, and having obtained Patrick's blessing, goes on to convert the Déisi to Christianity.
</p><p>The span of Declán's lifetime and career is extended in another chapter (§ 15), which makes him a contemporary of <a href="/wiki/Saint_David" title="Saint David">Saint David</a> of Wales in the 6th century. Likewise, the even later saint <a href="/wiki/Ultan_of_Ardbraccan" title="Ultan of Ardbraccan">Ultan of Ardbraccan</a> (d. 655 x 657) is presented as Declán's pupil.<sup id="cite_ref-DIB_10-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DIB-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Lives</i> also relate that the saint later paid a visit to the Déisi of Mide/Meath, where the King of Tara welcomed him and granted him land for the purpose of founding a "monastery of canons". The monastery founded there became known as Cill Décláin (Killegland, <a href="/wiki/Ashbourne,_County_Meath" title="Ashbourne, County Meath">Ashbourne, County Meath</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-DIB_10-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DIB-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="The_pre-Patrician_saints_of_Munster">The pre-Patrician saints of Munster</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Decl%C3%A1n_of_Ardmore&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: The pre-Patrician saints of Munster">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Frameless"><a href="/wiki/File:Britain.Deisi.Laigin.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Britain.Deisi.Laigin.jpg/180px-Britain.Deisi.Laigin.jpg" decoding="async" width="180" height="228" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Britain.Deisi.Laigin.jpg/270px-Britain.Deisi.Laigin.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Britain.Deisi.Laigin.jpg/360px-Britain.Deisi.Laigin.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1880" data-file-height="2386" /></a><figcaption></figcaption></figure>
<p>Declan is one of four Munster saints who had <i>Lives</i> written for them claiming that they founded monasteries and preached the Gospel in Munster before their younger contemporary Saint Patrick ever set foot in Ireland. These bishop saints, known since the 17th century as <i>quattuor sanctissimi episcopi</i>, also included <a href="/wiki/Ailbe_of_Emly" title="Ailbe of Emly">Ailbe of Emly</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ciar%C3%A1n_of_Saighir" class="mw-redirect" title="Ciarán of Saighir">Ciarán of Saigir</a> and <a href="/wiki/Abban_of_Magheranoidhe" class="mw-redirect" title="Abban of Magheranoidhe">Abbán of Moyarney</a>. The same claim was apparently made for <a href="/wiki/Saint_Ibar" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Ibar">Íbar</a> of Beggery Island, according to the <i>Life</i> of St Abbán, which identifies him as Abbán's uncle and teacher, but no separate <i>Life</i> survives which offers any information to this effect.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-RR19_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RR19-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> The relevant <i>Lives</i> are all found in the so-called Dublin Collection (see above), which bears a stamp of editorial intervention.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Their testimony, late though it seems, has often been treated in relation to the historical question of pre-Patrician Christianity in the south of Ireland. It has been argued that before the coming of Patrick, the south coast of Munster would have provided the most likely point of entry for the introduction of Christianity via Britain or via Gaul. The settlements of the Déisi and the <a href="/wiki/U%C3%AD_Liath%C3%A1in" title="Uí Liatháin">Uí Liatháin</a> in southwest Wales, as evidenced by the distribution of ogam-stones, provided an important connection between Britain and Ireland.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> A key aspect of this overseas link, the import of slaves, usually British Christians, by Irish raiders would have directly exposed Munster to the influence of Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-RR18_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RR18-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Further, Munster, lying opposite to Gaul, would have represented a first destination for Irish trading connections with the Continent. In the context of wine-trade, this is in some way corroborated by the archaeological record for pottery in Munster settlements.<sup id="cite_ref-RR18_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RR18-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The credit traditionally given to Saint Patrick for bringing Christianity to the island appears to owe much to the propaganda of one particular foundation. As early as the 7th century, <a href="/wiki/Armagh" title="Armagh">Armagh</a> was busy bolstering its claim to the status of the principal house founded by St Patrick. By promoting the cult of the saint, which entailed that Patrick was propagated as the apostle and first bishop of the Irish, it sought to establish and control a network of religious houses throughout the country.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> The fact that a missionary sent by Rome, <a href="/wiki/Palladius_(bishop_of_Ireland)" title="Palladius (bishop of Ireland)">Palladius</a>, had been active before St Patrick, in 431, possibly in Leinster, did not sit well with its agenda. In the writings of Armagh scholars, notably <a href="/wiki/T%C3%ADrech%C3%A1n" title="Tírechán">Tírechán</a> and <a href="/wiki/Muirch%C3%BA_moccu_Machtheni" title="Muirchú moccu Machtheni">Muirchú</a>, Palladius' activities were therefore belittled as a failure, ignored or, as T.F. O'Rahilly famously argues in his hypothesis of the 'Two Patricks', silently conflated with those of Patrick. In Armagh historiography, the conversion of Munster became embodied in the story of the conversion of <a href="/wiki/%C3%93engus_mac_Nad_Fro%C3%ADch" title="Óengus mac Nad Froích">Óengus mac Nad Froích</a> by St Patrick at <a href="/wiki/Cashel,_County_Tipperary" title="Cashel, County Tipperary">Cashel</a>, first told by <a href="/wiki/T%C3%ADrech%C3%A1n" title="Tírechán">Tírechán</a> and subsequently elaborated many times over.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Lives</i> of Ailbe, Declán, Ciarán and Abbán in the Dublin Collection appear to reflect the need of the Munster houses to offer some counterweight against the Patrician dossier promoted by Armagh, even though they do not deny the national importance of Saint Patrick. Historian <a href="/wiki/Richard_Sharpe_(historian)" title="Richard Sharpe (historian)">Richard Sharpe</a> proposed the earlier <i>Life</i> of Ailbe in the <i>Codex Salmanticensis</i> was originally composed in the eighth century to further the cause of the <a href="/wiki/%C3%89oganachta" class="mw-redirect" title="Éoganachta">Éoganacht</a> church of Emly. In the same century, the <i>Law of Ailbe</i> (784) was issued, possibly in response to the <i>Law of Patrick</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-RR19_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RR19-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The Dublin Collection, however, goes further when it attributes to the saints an important pre-Patrician career. Pre-eminence is given to Ailbe, whose Dublin <i>Life</i> asserts that Munster was entrusted to him by St Patrick, while to similar effect, Ailbe is called a "second Patrick and patron of Munster" (<i>secundus Patricius et patronus Mumenie</i>) in Declán's <i>Life</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-RR19_12-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RR19-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Dagmar Ó Riain-Raedel has argued that this way of promoting Munster saints was anticipated in texts emanating from the <i><a href="/wiki/Schottenkl%C3%B6ster" class="mw-redirect" title="Schottenklöster">Schottenklöster</a></i> or Irish Benedictine monasteries of southern Germany, whose principal house was at <a href="/wiki/Regensburg" title="Regensburg">Regensburg</a>. Not only was there a strong Munster presence, but many such texts were written down in recognition of the generous donations received from the kings of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Desmond" title="Kingdom of Desmond">Desmond</a> and <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Thomond" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of Thomond">Thomond</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-RR20-1_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RR20-1-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The most substantial achievement is the hagiographic compilation known as <i>Magnum Legendarium Austriacum</i> (<i>The Great Austrian Legendary</i>), begun sometime in the 1160s or 1170s. The prologue to a recension of St Patrick's <i>Life</i> preserved incomplete at <a href="/wiki/G%C3%B6ttweig" class="mw-redirect" title="Göttweig">Göttweig</a> (Austria) asserts that disciples of one Mansuetus, an Irish <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Toul" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Toul">bishop of Toul</a>, had set themselves up as bishops in Ireland to prepare the way for St Patrick.<sup id="cite_ref-RR20-1_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RR20-1-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> In the mid-12th century, a <i>Life</i> was composed at Regensburg relating the life and miracles of Ailbe, under his German name St Albert. Ó Riain-Raedel connects this to the establishment of <a href="/wiki/Cashel,_County_Tipperary" title="Cashel, County Tipperary">Cashel</a> as an archiepiscopal seat in 1111, because it was Ailbe, being the patron saint of the nearby foundation of Emly, who played a key role in advertising its new status.<sup id="cite_ref-RR20-1_18-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RR20-1-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Commemoration">Commemoration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Decl%C3%A1n_of_Ardmore&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Commemoration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Declan_statue,_Ardmore.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/St_Declan_statue%2C_Ardmore.jpg/220px-St_Declan_statue%2C_Ardmore.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/St_Declan_statue%2C_Ardmore.jpg/330px-St_Declan_statue%2C_Ardmore.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/St_Declan_statue%2C_Ardmore.jpg/440px-St_Declan_statue%2C_Ardmore.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4896" data-file-height="3672" /></a><figcaption>Memorial stone in Ardmore</figcaption></figure>
<p>According to his <i>Life</i>, Declán is reposed in the Lord at his monastery in Ardmore and was subsequently buried there. His <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a> in the martyrologies is 24 July.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNBMunster_1-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNBMunster-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-DIB_10-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DIB-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> A Middle Irish note added to the <i>Félire Óengusso</i>, which is of no historical value, tells that Declán was responsible for introducing <a href="/wiki/Rye" title="Rye">rye</a> (Irish <i>secal</i>, from Latin <i>secale</i>) into Ireland.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Declan%27s_Stone,_Ardmore.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/St_Declan%27s_Stone%2C_Ardmore.jpg/220px-St_Declan%27s_Stone%2C_Ardmore.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/St_Declan%27s_Stone%2C_Ardmore.jpg/330px-St_Declan%27s_Stone%2C_Ardmore.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/St_Declan%27s_Stone%2C_Ardmore.jpg/440px-St_Declan%27s_Stone%2C_Ardmore.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4896" data-file-height="3672" /></a><figcaption>St Declan's Stone on Ardmore beach; supposedly, it carried his bell to Ireland.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup></figcaption></figure>
<p>Declán has enjoyed a steady cult in Waterford, where many church dedications still name him.<sup id="cite_ref-DIB_10-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DIB-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Every year on his feast-day, locals and people from the region celebrate his <i><a href="/wiki/Pattern_(devotional)" title="Pattern (devotional)">pattern</a></i>. The pattern includes various devotional acts at sites associated with his life.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> The path walked by Declan from Ardmore to <a href="/wiki/Cashel,_County_Tipperary" title="Cashel, County Tipperary">Cashel, County Tipperary</a> has been restored as St Declan's Pilgrim Path, and an annual walk of the path, nicknamed the "Irish <a href="/wiki/Way_of_St._James" class="mw-redirect" title="Way of St. James">Camino</a>", is organised from 24 to 28 July, beginning in 2013.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A round tower still stands at the site of the saint's monastery at Ardmore<sup id="cite_ref-ODNBMunster_1-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNBMunster-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> as well as earlier ecclesiastical ruins, such as a stone oratory and a small stone church.<sup id="cite_ref-DIB_10-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DIB-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> The diocese of Ardmore and its episcopal church lasted until the 13th century.<sup id="cite_ref-DIB_10-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DIB-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
