<p><b>Didymus the Blind</b> (alternatively spelled <b>Dedimus</b> or <b>Didymous</b>)<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> (c. 313&#160;&#8211;&#32;398) was a <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Theologian" class="mw-redirect" title="Theologian">theologian</a> in the Church of <a href="/wiki/Alexandria" title="Alexandria">Alexandria</a>, where he taught for about half a century. He was a student of <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>, and, after the <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Constantinople" title="Second Council of Constantinople">Second Council of Constantinople</a> condemned Origen, Didymus's works were not copied. Many of his writings are lost, but some of his commentaries and essays survive. He was seen as intelligent and a good teacher.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 101">&#58;&#8202;101&#8202;</span></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_education">Early life and education</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Didymus_the_Blind&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life and education">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Didymus became blind at the age of four, before he had learned to read.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was a loyal follower of <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Oxford_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Oxford-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and opposed <a href="/wiki/Arian" class="mw-redirect" title="Arian">Arian</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pneumatomachi" title="Pneumatomachi">Macedonian</a> teachings.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Despite his blindness, Didymus excelled in scholarship because of his incredible memory. He found ways to help blind people to read, experimenting with carved wooden letters similar to <a href="/wiki/Braille" title="Braille">Braille</a> systems used by the blind today.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He recalled and contemplated information while others slept.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Teacher_in_Alexandria">Teacher in Alexandria</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Didymus_the_Blind&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Teacher in Alexandria">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to Rufinus, Didymus was "a teacher in the Church school", who was "approved by Bishop Athanasius" and other learned churchmen. Later scholars believed he was the head of the <a href="/wiki/Catechetical_School_of_Alexandria" title="Catechetical School of Alexandria">Catechetical School of Alexandria</a>. However, the Catechetical School of Alexandria may not have existed in Didymus' time, and Rufinus may have been referring to a different school.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Didymus remained a layman all his life and became one of the most learned ascetics of his time.<sup id="cite_ref-Brit_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brit-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Palladius, <a href="/wiki/Tyrannius_Rufinus" title="Tyrannius Rufinus">Rufinus</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> were among his pupils.<sup id="cite_ref-Layton_2004_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Layton_2004-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 1">&#58;&#8202;1&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>Rufinus was Didymus's pupil for eight years. When he translated Origen's <i>De principiis</i> into Latin, he referenced Didymus's commentary on it. Jerome mentions Didymus's contributions to his ideas in the prefaces of many of his books, and called Didymus "Didymus the Seer."<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Rufinus remained loyal to Didymus after Jerome condemned Didymus and Origen.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Didymus was viewed as an orthodox Christian teacher and was greatly respected and admired up until at least 553.<sup id="cite_ref-Layton_2004_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Layton_2004-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Socrates_of_Constantinople" title="Socrates of Constantinople">Socrates of Constantinople</a> compared Didymus's faithfulness to the <a href="/wiki/Nicene_Creed" title="Nicene Creed">Nicene Creed</a> to <a href="/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea" title="Basil of Caesarea">Basil of Caesarea</a> and <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Nazianzus" title="Gregory of Nazianzus">Gregory of Nazianzus</a>. In his position as a teacher, he held discussions and learned from Jews, pagans, Manichees, and other Christian teachers. Records of Didymus's lectures and the questions students asked show that he taught the same educated pupils multiple times.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Several Oriental Orthodox Churches refer to him as Saint Didymus the Blind.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Second_Council_of_Constantinople">Second Council of Constantinople</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Didymus_the_Blind&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Second Council of Constantinople">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 553 the <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Constantinople" title="Second Council of Constantinople">Second Council of Constantinople</a> condemned his works, along with those of Origen and Evagrius, but not his person. In the <a href="/wiki/Third_Council_of_Constantinople" title="Third Council of Constantinople">Third Council of Constantinople</a> in 680, and in the 787 <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Nicaea" title="Second Council of Nicaea">Second Council of Nicaea</a>, Didymus was again linked with and condemned with Origen.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Many unconventional views became associated with Origen, and the 15 anathemas attributed to the council condemn a form of apocatastasis along with the <a href="/wiki/Pre-existence" title="Pre-existence">pre-existence</a> of the soul, animism (in this context, a heterodox Christology), and a denial of real and lasting resurrection of the body.<sup id="cite_ref-The_Anathemas_Against_Origen_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-The_Anathemas_Against_Origen-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In spite of the condemnation of his works, he is still listed as "St. Didymus the Blind" in the <a href="/wiki/Serbian_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Serbian Orthodox">Serbian Orthodox</a> Prologue of Ochrid which gives his feast date as October 18.<sup id="cite_ref-Archived_copy_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Archived_copy-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Didymus_the_Blind&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>As a result of his condemnation, many of his works were not copied during the Middle Ages and were subsequently lost. Of his lost compositions we can gather a partial list from the citations of ancient authors which includes <i>On Dogmas, On The Death of Young Children, Against the Arians, First Word,</i> and others.<sup id="cite_ref-eastern-fathers_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-eastern-fathers-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 225–227">&#58;&#8202;225–227&#8202;</span></sup> One of Didymus's lost works is a commentary on Origen's <i>First Principles</i> which, according to Jerome, tried to interpret an orthodox understanding of the <a href="/wiki/Trinity" title="Trinity">Trinity</a> from Origen's theology. In it, he assumed the <a href="/wiki/Pre-existence" title="Pre-existence">pre-existence</a> of souls and <a href="/wiki/Apocatastasis" title="Apocatastasis">Apocatastasis</a>. He staunchly defended the doctrine of the Trinity. He argued that Christ's body and soul were human, but that Christ was sinless.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 98–100">&#58;&#8202;98–100&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>Excerpts from Didymus's Biblical commentary have been found in the <a href="/wiki/Catena_(biblical_commentary)" title="Catena (biblical commentary)">Catena</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 96–98">&#58;&#8202;96–98&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>Modern knowledge of Didymus has been greatly increased by a group of 6th or 7th century papyrus <a href="/wiki/Codices" class="mw-redirect" title="Codices">codices</a> discovered in 1941 at a munitions dump near Toura, Egypt (south of <a href="/wiki/Cairo" title="Cairo">Cairo</a>). These include his commentaries on Zechariah, Genesis 1–17, part of Job and parts (of uncertain authenticity) on Ecclesiastes and Psalms 20–46.<sup id="cite_ref-Oxford_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Oxford-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In these commentaries, Didymus discusses long quotations from the Bible, and refrains from speculation, which he considered sophistry. However, he interprets scriptures allegorically, seeing symbols everywhere. For example, he wrote that the mountains in Zachariah represented the two Testaments of the Bible. Didymus saw an individual's movement towards virtue as emerging from their interaction with scripture.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 96–98">&#58;&#8202;96–98&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>Didymus probably wrote the treatise <i>On The Holy Spirit</i> (written sometime before 381 in Greek), which was preserved in a Latin translation by Jerome.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 92">&#58;&#8202;92&#8202;</span></sup> <i>Commentary on the Catholic Epistles</i> also is dubiously attributed to Didymus.<sup id="cite_ref-Brit_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brit-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The treatise <i>Against the Manichees</i> was also probably written by Didymus. There has been greater doubt over two further works traditionally attributed to Didymus. <i>On The Trinity</i>, identified in the eighteenth century as being Didymus' work, saw twentieth-century doubts, largely on grounds of lack of 'provenance' and alleged inconsistencies with the commentaries discovered at Tura in 1941, but many would still see this as Didymus' work.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 92">&#58;&#8202;92&#8202;</span></sup> Additionally, scholars do not believe that Didymus authored the work preserved as books 4 and 5 of Basil's <i>Against Eunomius</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-Young_3-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Young-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 95">&#58;&#8202;95&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>Within the Commentary on Zechariah, Didymus shows himself to be a thoroughly intertextual reader of scripture. He moves from the text he is commenting on to a wide variety of other passages, quoting less frequently from the historical books which do not suit his allegorical method. Besides the gift of having a mind like a concordance, he also shows familiarity with philosophical terms and categories of the <a href="/wiki/Stoics" class="mw-redirect" title="Stoics">Stoics</a>, <a href="/wiki/Epicureans" class="mw-redirect" title="Epicureans">Epicureans</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Pythagoreans" class="mw-redirect" title="Pythagoreans">Pythagoreans</a> (from whom, with <a href="/wiki/Philo" title="Philo">Philo</a>, he derives his occasional number symbolism hermeneutic). His works also seem to cite passages from the <a href="/wiki/Deuterocanonical_books" title="Deuterocanonical books">deuterocanonical books</a> of the Old Testament as well as <a href="/wiki/Epistle_of_Barnabas" title="Epistle of Barnabas">Barnabas</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Shepherd_of_Hermas" class="mw-redirect" title="Shepherd of Hermas">Shepherd of Hermas</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Acts_of_john" class="mw-redirect" title="Acts of john">Acts of John</a>. According to <a href="/wiki/Bart_D._Ehrman" title="Bart D. Ehrman">Bart Ehrman</a>, his canon extended to at least include Barnabas and the Shepherd. It has been suggested by R.M. Grant regarding Origen's similarly expanded canon that while he lived in Alexandria he accepted the broader tradition of the church in Alexandria, but upon moving to Caesarea and finding the books were not accepted there henceforth manifested greater reserve towards them. Why Didymus would not have inherited his teachers later hesitation is unclear. Among his peers his hermeneutical method seems to have been met with mixed reactions. Jerome, who requested his commentary and considered him a mentor, is still baffled by Didymus's use of what he considered apocryphal works. Readers such as <a href="/wiki/Diodore_of_Tarsus" class="mw-redirect" title="Diodore of Tarsus">Diodore</a> in Antioch found his hermeneutical approach somewhat gratuitous and arbitrary. What none seem to deny, however, is that Didymus was unhindered by blindness in his remarkable ability to recall the sacred text.<sup id="cite_ref-Hill_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hill-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A commentary attributed to Didymus that survives only in Latin suggests the epistle <a href="/wiki/2_Peter" class="mw-redirect" title="2 Peter">2 Peter</a> was forged, anticipating the beliefs of later scholars who would agree with the position that Peter was not the author of the letter.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>  That said, it is doubtful that Didymus was the true author, as his other writings treat 2 Peter as canonical.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The no longer extant treatise <i>On The Death of Young Children</i> was addressed to <a href="/wiki/Tyrannius_Rufinus" title="Tyrannius Rufinus">Tyrannius Rufinus</a> to answer his question "Why do infants die?". According to <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>, Didymus's answer was that these infants "had not sinned much [in the pre-existence], and therefore it was enough punishment for them just to have touched their bodily prisons".<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Thought">Thought</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Didymus_the_Blind&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Thought">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Thoroughly Trinitarian, Didymus' makes God completely transcendent and only capable of being spoken of by images and <a href="/wiki/Apophatic_Theology" class="mw-redirect" title="Apophatic Theology">apophatic</a> means. He repeatedly emphasizes that God's essence is beyond essence, and uses a term only seen otherwise in <a href="/wiki/Cyril_of_Alexandria" title="Cyril of Alexandria">Cyril of Alexandria</a>, "without quantity."<sup id="cite_ref-eastern-fathers_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-eastern-fathers-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 228">&#58;&#8202;228&#8202;</span></sup> There can be seen in his works influence from the <a href="/wiki/Cappadocian_Fathers" title="Cappadocian Fathers">Cappadocian Fathers</a>, focusing the concept of <a href="/wiki/Hypostasis_(philosophy_and_religion)" title="Hypostasis (philosophy and religion)">Hypostasis (philosophy)</a> to express the independent reality of the three persons of the Trinity rather than beginning with the one divine substance (ουσια) as his starting point.<sup id="cite_ref-Hill_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hill-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 21">&#58;&#8202;21&#8202;</span></sup> Within these three persons, the Father is the root of the Divinity, the Spirit proceeds from the Father, and the Son is generated. Didymus seemed very concerned with stressing the equality of the persons of the Trinity. In Georges Florovsky's opinion, "Didymus does not strive for precision in his formulations. This is a general feature of the school of Alexandria."<sup id="cite_ref-eastern-fathers_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-eastern-fathers-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 228">&#58;&#8202;228&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>In combating the heresies of the <a href="/wiki/Manichaeism" title="Manichaeism">Manichaean</a> Docetists and <a href="/wiki/Apollinarism" title="Apollinarism">Apollinarians</a>, we should not be surprised to find Didymus insisting on the fullness of the human nature of Christ. He concludes there must be two natures united in Christ, not speculating on precisely how these work together but restricting himself to the expression "a single Christ." In his atonement theory, Didymus does not mention <a href="/wiki/Apotheosis" title="Apotheosis">deification</a>, but rather focuses on the ransom and the restoration of the image and the likeness. The fragmentary nature of his writing at this point does not allow us to draw definite conclusions, but he does speak of "universal salvation." Jerome, probably correctly, accused Didymus of confessing the ultimate restoration of the devil.<sup id="cite_ref-eastern-fathers_13-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-eastern-fathers-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 227–232">&#58;&#8202;227–232&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>Didymus seems to have also accepted the pre-existence of souls, and considers the afterlife as a process of purification, though, according to Florovsky, he rejects <a href="/wiki/Transmigration_of_the_soul" class="mw-redirect" title="Transmigration of the soul">metempsychosis</a>. He describes the <a href="/wiki/Day_of_the_Lord" class="mw-redirect" title="Day of the Lord">Day of the Lord</a> as an internal illumination of the soul, and in the future world he believes that evil "as a quality" will no longer exist. For him, as in <a href="/wiki/Clement_of_Alexandria" title="Clement of Alexandria">Clement</a> and Origen, the <i>true</i> gnostics possess a divine philosophy, one which allows them to defend themselves against heretics by giving a clear confession of the faith. Throughout his theology the influence of Origen is revealed, various aspects of which, particularly his eschatology, must have led to the condemnation of his works.<sup id="cite_ref-eastern-fathers_13-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-eastern-fathers-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 227–232">&#58;&#8202;227–232&#8202;</span></sup>
</p>

</p>
