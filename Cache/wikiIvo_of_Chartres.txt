<p><b>Ivo of Chartres</b> (also <b>Ives</b>, <b>Yves</b>, or <b>Yvo</b>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Ivo Carnutensis</i>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 1040 – 23 December 1115), also known as <b>Saint Ivo</b> in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>, was the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Chartres" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Chartres">Bishop of Chartres</a>, <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a> from 1090 until his death, and an important <a href="/wiki/Canon_lawyer" class="mw-redirect" title="Canon lawyer">canonist</a> during the <a href="/wiki/Investiture_Crisis" class="mw-redirect" title="Investiture Crisis">Investiture Crisis</a>.
</p><p>Three extensive canonical works, namely <i>Tripartita</i>, <i>Decretum</i>, and <i>Panormia</i>, are attributed to him. He corresponded extensively. His liturgical feast is observed on 23 May.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ivo_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Ivo of Chartres was born in or near <a href="/wiki/Chartres" title="Chartres">Chartres</a> circa 1040 to a family of relatively low social status.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERolker20107_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERolker20107-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He is claimed to have studied first in <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a>, then in <a href="/wiki/Bec_Abbey" title="Bec Abbey">Abbey of Bec</a> in <a href="/wiki/Normandy" title="Normandy">Normandy</a> where, according to <a href="/wiki/Robert_of_Torigni" title="Robert of Torigni">Robert of Torigni</a>, he studied under <a href="/wiki/Lanfranc" title="Lanfranc">Lanfranc</a> along with <a href="/wiki/Anselm_of_Canterbury" title="Anselm of Canterbury">Anselm of Canterbury</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERolker20107_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERolker20107-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Ghellinck_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ghellinck-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Not much is known of him until some time after he was admitted to the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> clergy. His first benefice was at Nesle in Picardy.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERolker20107_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERolker20107-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> In 1067 Bishop Gui asked him to become the abbot of the new Augustinian house of St. Quentin at Beauvais. Ivo was skeptical of religious excess and always stressed moderation in practice. He remained at St. Quentin for twenty years and established himself as one of the best teachers in France. St. Quentin came to be known as a great school of theology.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His knowledge of <a href="/wiki/Canon_law" title="Canon law">canon law</a>, both as a lawyer and cleric, most probably earned him in 1090 the office of <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Chartres" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of Chartres">Bishop of Chartres</a>. His predecessor, Geoffrey, had been removed from office by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_II" title="Pope Urban II">Pope Urban II</a>. Geoffrey's relatives and supporters initially opposed Ivo's appointment, but with the backing of Pope Urban II, <a href="/wiki/Philip_I_of_France" title="Philip I of France">King Philip</a>, and the influential <a href="/wiki/Adela_of_Normandy" title="Adela of Normandy">Countess Adela of Blois</a>, Ivo was eventually grudgingly accepted.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERolker201015_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERolker201015-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In light of the events preceding his appointment to the office, his strong opposition to the practice of simony may have been the impetus to his episcopal elevation.
</p><p>During his twenty-five year episcopacy at Chartres, Ivo was involved in conflicts with many magnates including <a href="/wiki/Philip_I_of_France" title="Philip I of France">King Philip I of France</a>, Archbishop Richer of Sens, the papal legate <a href="/wiki/Hugh_of_Die" title="Hugh of Die">Hugh of Die</a>, and several local nobles. The most famous case concerned the marriage of King Philip, who in the early 1090s tried to repudiate his wife <a href="/wiki/Bertha_of_Holland" title="Bertha of Holland">Bertha of Holland</a> in order to marry <a href="/wiki/Bertrade_of_Anjou" class="mw-redirect" title="Bertrade of Anjou">Bertrade of Anjou</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERolker201015_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERolker201015-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Local baron Hugh Le Puiset took advantage of the situation to seize episcopal lands and imprison the bishop for a short time.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERolker201016_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERolker201016-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Ivo was an acquaintance of Countess <a href="/wiki/Adele_of_Blois" class="mw-redirect" title="Adele of Blois">Adele of Blois</a>, who helped him reform the Abbey of <a href="/w/index.php?title=St._Jean-en-Vall%C3%A9e&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="St. Jean-en-Vallée (page does not exist)">St. Jean-en-Vallée</a>. In addition, on several occasions he defended her decisions, most notably during the events regarding <a href="/wiki/Rotrou_III_of_Perche" class="mw-redirect" title="Rotrou III of Perche">Rotrou III of Perche</a>, when he refused to assert ecclesiastical sanctions against him.
</p><p>Around 1114, Ivo granted to <a href="/wiki/Bernard_of_Thiron" title="Bernard of Thiron">Bernard of Abbeville</a> land in Thiron-Gardais, where Bernard established the monastery that would become the <a href="/wiki/Tiron_Abbey" class="mw-redirect" title="Tiron Abbey">Abbey of the Holy Trinity of Tiron</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Caldey_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Caldey-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>During his episcopacy he wrote the majority of his extant works, for which he later became famous and considered among the greatest scholars of the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">mediaeval era</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ivo_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Ivo_Carnotensis_%E2%80%93_Canones,_12th-century_%E2%80%93_BEIC_10016771.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Ivo_Carnotensis_%E2%80%93_Canones%2C_12th-century_%E2%80%93_BEIC_10016771.jpg/220px-Ivo_Carnotensis_%E2%80%93_Canones%2C_12th-century_%E2%80%93_BEIC_10016771.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="324" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Ivo_Carnotensis_%E2%80%93_Canones%2C_12th-century_%E2%80%93_BEIC_10016771.jpg/330px-Ivo_Carnotensis_%E2%80%93_Canones%2C_12th-century_%E2%80%93_BEIC_10016771.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Ivo_Carnotensis_%E2%80%93_Canones%2C_12th-century_%E2%80%93_BEIC_10016771.jpg/440px-Ivo_Carnotensis_%E2%80%93_Canones%2C_12th-century_%E2%80%93_BEIC_10016771.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1175" data-file-height="1729" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Ivo_Carnotensis_%E2%80%93_Canones,_12th-century_%E2%80%93_BEIC_10016771.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Decretum</i>, 12th-century manuscript. Paris, <a href="/wiki/Biblioth%C3%A8que_Nationale_de_France" class="mw-redirect" title="Bibliothèque Nationale de France">Bibliothèque Nationale de France</a>, lat. 14315.</div></div></div>
<p>Ivo was a prolific writer but is most known for his canonical works: the <i>Decretum</i> of seventeen books; the <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Tripartita&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Tripartita (page does not exist)">Tripartita</a></i>, of very substantial material, divided in three parts, and attributed to him; and the <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Panormia&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Panormia (page does not exist)">Panormia</a></i> of eight books attributed to him. All three are primarily works of <a href="/wiki/Canon_law" title="Canon law">canon law</a>. The Prologue to the <i>Decretum</i> deals with the interpretation of canon law, and specifically argues that <i>caritas</i> was the solution for <a href="/wiki/Sin" title="Sin">sin</a>, and not harsh punishment without <a href="/wiki/Contrition" title="Contrition">contrition</a>. "He was called to teach. His lesson was love. It was all that mattered.".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrasington2006147_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrasington2006147-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Ivo is also famous for his 288 letters of correspondence. These letters often dealt with liturgical, canonical, and dogmatic questions and, much like his major works, are from the perspective of <i>caritas</i>. Several of his extant sermons, totaling 25, treat of the same topics as his other writings and letters.
</p><p>It has also been suggested that his doctrines influenced the final agreement of the <a href="/wiki/Concordat_of_Worms" title="Concordat of Worms">Concordat of Worms</a> in 1122.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Subsequent_influence_and_veneration">Subsequent influence and veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ivo_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Subsequent influence and veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Ivo's writings had considerable influence in the twelfth century and beyond. Many of his letters and sermons circulated already in his lifetime, and were copied widely especially in the mid-twelfth century. The same is true for the canonical collections attributed to him; they were copied frequently and used in the making of other collections.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERolker2010278-280_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERolker2010278-280-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> For example, <a href="/wiki/Gratian" title="Gratian">Gratian’s</a> <i>Concordia Discordantium Canonum</i> (commonly denominated <i><a href="/wiki/Decretum_Gratiani" title="Decretum Gratiani">Decretum Gratiani</a></i>) draws on both the Tripartita and the Panormia. <a href="/wiki/Alger_of_Li%C3%A8ge" title="Alger of Liège">Alger of Liège</a> was strongly influenced by Ivo's <a href="/w/index.php?title=Prologus&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Prologus (page does not exist)">Prologus</a> and quoted from his Decretum. <a href="/wiki/Peter_Abelard" title="Peter Abelard">Peter Abelard</a> in his <i>Sic et Non</i> used the Prologue, too, and apparently quoted both from Ivo's Decretum and from the Panormia.
</p><p>Although it is not known when he was canonized, 23 May is his present liturgical memorial.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Before 1570 it was observed on 20 May.<sup id="cite_ref-Ghellinck_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ghellinck-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
