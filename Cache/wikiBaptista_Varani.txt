<p><b>Camilla Battista da Varano <a href="/wiki/Poor_Clares" title="Poor Clares">OSCl</a></b>, (9 April 1458 – 31 May 1524),<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> from <a href="/wiki/Camerino" title="Camerino">Camerino</a>, <a href="/wiki/Italy" title="Italy">Italy</a>, was an <a href="/wiki/Italians" title="Italians">Italian</a> <a href="/wiki/Princess" title="Princess">princess</a> and a <a href="/wiki/Poor_Clares" title="Poor Clares">Poor Clare</a> <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nun</a> and <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a>. She is <a href="/wiki/Veneration" title="Veneration">venerated</a> as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in the Catholic Church.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Camilla_Battista_da_Varano&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Varano was born out of wedlock in <a href="/wiki/Camerino" title="Camerino">Camerino</a>, <a href="/wiki/Macerata" title="Macerata">Macerata</a>, in the <a href="/wiki/Marche" title="Marche">Marche</a> region of Italy, the daughter of <a href="/wiki/Nobility" title="Nobility">noblewoman</a> Cecchina di Maestro Giacomo and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Giulio_Cesare_da_Varano&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Giulio Cesare da Varano (page does not exist)">Giulio Cesare da Varano</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Duke" title="Duke">Duke</a> of Camerino.<sup id="cite_ref-zenit_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-zenit-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> She was raised by Giulio Cesare's wife, Giovanna Malatesta. Both her father and stepmother were very fond of her, and she grew up in the splendour of the court, receiving an education which included grammar and <a href="/wiki/Rhetoric" title="Rhetoric">rhetoric</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  
</p><p>When she was 10 years old Varano was so impressed with the preaching of Friars Domenico of Leonessa and Peter of Mogliano that every Friday she would who meditate on the passion of Christ.<sup id="cite_ref-Pigozzi_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Pigozzi-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> One day Verano came across a booklet that contained a meditation on the Passion of Christ divided into fifteen parts (to be recited like a <a href="/wiki/Rosary" title="Rosary">Rosary</a>) and she began to read it every Friday, while on her knees before a crucifix. She also then began other practices such as fasting on bread and water, keeping night vigils. At this time she continued to spend time playing music, singing, dancing, promenading, and other youthful pursuits abundant in court life.<sup id="cite_ref-langhorne_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-langhorne-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>From the ages of 18–21, she went through three years of deep spiritual struggles against the allurements of the world. Her father did all in his power to force his daughter into marriage, even to the extent of imprisoning her. During these two and half years, she reported having very deep conversions with Christ, and she received many divine visitations. She claimed that Jesus had given her 'three fragrant spring lilies': an intense hatred of the world, a heart-felt humility and a burning desire to endure evil. She composed her first written work in this time, a <i>Lauda</i> ("Praise"), which was about the joy she felt in knowing that Christ loved her. She claimed that she once saw Christ (in answer to her desire to see Him), but she saw only His back as He was walking away. She also experienced seven months of severe physical illness and depression.
</p><p>Camilla resisted her father's plans so firmly that after two and a half years he restored her to liberty, for fear, as he said, of drawing upon himself divine vengeance, and gave his consent to her becoming a nun.<sup id="cite_ref-woywood_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-woywood-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> During the <a href="/wiki/Lent" title="Lent">Lent</a> of 1479 Varano listened to a sermon of Observant Franciscan friar Francesco of Urbino, whom she described as "the trumpet of the Holy Spirit". This sermon struck her deeply. After another sermon by the same friar (with whom she secretly corresponded) on the feast of the Annunciation, 24 March 1479, she then took a vow of chastity; she was 21 at the time. At this same time, she also began to increasingly hear voices inside her telling her that her only hope of salvation was to become a nun.
</p><p>Varano then had a bitter internal struggle, while dealing with sneers and gossip behind her back by members of the court, and her father initially opposed her wish to enter into <a href="/wiki/Consecrated_life" title="Consecrated life">consecrated life</a>, wishing her to marry. After a confession of her sins to a certain Friar Oliviero on Saturday within the <a href="/wiki/Octave_(liturgy)" title="Octave (liturgy)">Octave</a> of <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Easter</a>, 17 April 1479, she decided that she would enter the Poor Clare monastery at <a href="/wiki/Urbino" title="Urbino">Urbino</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-langhorne_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-langhorne-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> which was under the reform of the Strict Observance of the Order.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastic_life">Monastic life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Camilla_Battista_da_Varano&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Monastic life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On 14 November 1481, Camilla entered the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> of the Poor Clares at <a href="/wiki/Urbino" title="Urbino">Urbino</a> and took the name Baptista.<sup id="cite_ref-franciscan_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-franciscan-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> She described the step in Biblical terms, as having been freed from the 'slavery of Egypt' (referring to the world), and from 'the hands of powerful Pharaoh' (referring to her father), that she had 'crossed the Red Sea' (left the court life), and was 'placed in the desert of holy religion' (entered a monastery).<sup id="cite_ref-langhorne_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-langhorne-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Varano made her profession in 1483 and claimed it was a bittersweet moment for her, as there was much political and religious controversy about her decision to become a nun. It was during her stay at Urbino that she wrote <i>Ricordi di Gesu</i>, a meditation in the form of a letter from Jesus to her. She intensified her meditations on the Passion and claimed to enter more deeply into the mental pains of the heart of Jesus.<sup id="cite_ref-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 4 January 1484 Varano and eight other of her companions transferred to the new <a href="/wiki/Santa_Chiara,_Camerino" title="Santa Chiara, Camerino">Monastery of Santa Maria Nuova at Camerino</a> (located near her father's castle),<sup id="cite_ref-zenit_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-zenit-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-TRP_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TRP-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> in a monastery which her father had purchased from the Olivetan monks and restored in order to have his daughter closer to him. Her father had made arrangements with the <a href="/wiki/Vicar_General" class="mw-redirect" title="Vicar General">Vicar General</a> of the Observant Franciscans, under whose authority the Poor Clares operated, and the pope, in order to have her re-located there. Battista, however, was reluctant to do so and moved only under obedience.<sup id="cite_ref-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>One of the most significant points in Varano's spiritual life occurred then, when she had a vision lasting fifteen days of St. <a href="/wiki/Clare_of_Assisi" title="Clare of Assisi">Clare of Assisi</a>. She wrote that she did not recognize the nun at first, but afterwards she knew that it was Clare, the foundress of her Order, and the experience caused her love and devotion to St. Clare to intensify. It was a few days after this that she had a vision of two <a href="/wiki/Cherub" title="Cherub">cherubim</a> holding her at the bleeding feet of Christ (described below), which lasted two months. She had another vision following this of God's love, which afterwards convinced her of her unworthiness, and she asked God to always remain prostrate at Christ's feet. The next five years she recorded as being filled with inner suffering that gave her a desire to leave the body and be with Christ.<sup id="cite_ref-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1488, Camilla wrote <i>I dolori mentali di Gesu nella sua Passione</i> (<i>The mental sorrows of Christ during his Passion</i>), which followed from her long meditations on this topic. It was written as a meditation by an anonymous nun to her abbess, and it consists in Christ's presenting eight of His sorrows: the damned, the elect, his mother, Mary Magdalen, the apostles, Judas, the Jewish people, and the ingratitude of all creation.<sup id="cite_ref-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The following five years were ones wherein she experienced a spiritual crisis. She wrote that she was battling with the devil, as she felt abandonment and desolation while being haunted with temptations to rebel against God and to disbelieve the scriptures. During this period, between 27 February and 13 March 1491 she composed 'Vita Spirituale', (Spiritual Life, or her Autobiography) which was a long letter to Domenico of Leonessa (the preacher who had inspired her tears as a child). In the letter she told him how he had inspired her spiritual life and expressed his gratitude to him. She thought that this would be her last testament before dying, but she was to live on for another 30 years.<sup id="cite_ref-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1492 she met <a href="/wiki/Don_(honorific)" title="Don (honorific)">Don</a> Antonio, a <a href="/wiki/Spanish_people" class="mw-redirect" title="Spanish people">Spanish</a> <a href="/wiki/Olivetans" title="Olivetans">Olivetan</a> <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a>, who became her <a href="/wiki/Spiritual_director" class="mw-redirect" title="Spiritual director">spiritual director</a> for four years.
</p><p>Her father founded a new monastery of that Order at Camerino, and presented it to his daughter. By the time she turned 35 years of age, she was elected Abbess for the first time, a position she continued to hold for several terms.<sup id="cite_ref-woywood_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-woywood-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> She was elected abbess of her monastery in 1500 and she was re-elected in 1507, 1513 and 1515.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Flight_from_Camerino">Flight from Camerino</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Camilla_Battista_da_Varano&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Flight from Camerino">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1501 Duke Giulio Cesare was <a href="/wiki/Excommunication" title="Excommunication">excommunicated</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Alexander_VI" title="Pope Alexander VI">Pope Alexander VI</a> for hosting enemies of the pope and for allegedly assassinating a cousin of the pope. The papal forces, led by <a href="/wiki/Cesare_Borgia" title="Cesare Borgia">Cesare Borgia</a>, captured Camerino in 1502 and the Duke and three of his sons were imprisoned and then strangled, though her mother and youngest brother escaped. <a href="/wiki/Mother_(religious_title)" class="mw-redirect" title="Mother (religious title)">Mother</a> Battista fled the city and sought refuge in Fermo, but the local population, terrified of facing the wrath of Cesare Borgia, rejected her. She found refuge in the village of Atri, in the <a href="/wiki/Abruzzo" title="Abruzzo">Abruzzo</a> region of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Naples" title="Kingdom of Naples">Kingdom of Naples</a>, with the <a href="/wiki/Duchy_of_Amalfi" title="Duchy of Amalfi">Duchess of Amalfi</a>, Isabella <a href="/wiki/Piccolomini" title="Piccolomini">Piccolomini</a> Todeschini, staying there until 1503 when with <a href="/wiki/Pope_Julius_II" title="Pope Julius II">Julius II</a> the new pope she felt safe to return to Camerino.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Later_life">Later life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Camilla_Battista_da_Varano&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Later life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1505, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Julius_II" title="Pope Julius II">Pope Julius II</a> sent Varano to found a monastery in <a href="/wiki/Fermo" title="Fermo">Fermo</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-carballo_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-carballo-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> She went and stayed for two years.
</p><p>In 1512 through her intervention in <a href="/wiki/San_Severino_Marche" title="San Severino Marche">San Severino Marche</a> (also in the Italian <a href="/wiki/Marches" class="mw-redirect" title="Marches">Marches</a>) she successfully stopped the execution of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Napoleone_of_Camerino&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Napoleone of Camerino (page does not exist)">Napoleone of Camerino</a> for murder. Varano wrote to her brother-in-law, Muzio Colonna, to ask that he spare the inhabitants of <a href="/wiki/Montecchio" title="Montecchio">Montecchio</a> during his military expedition against Fermo in 1515.
</p><p>In 1521 Varano herself traveled to San Severino Marche to train a monastery of nuns who had just adopted the <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_St._Clare" class="mw-redirect" title="Rule of St. Clare">Rule of St. Clare</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-carballo_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-carballo-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> She wrote a letter to the Vicar General of the Observant Franciscans, Giovanni of Fano, to whom her last written work 'Trattato della Purita di cuore' was dedicated in the same year. She died in her monastery in Camerino during a plague on 31 March 1524, on the <a href="/wiki/Feast_of_Corpus_Christi" title="Feast of Corpus Christi">feast of Corpus Christi</a>, at the age of 66.<sup id="cite_ref-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Varano's remains were placed to rest in the crypt of the  Monastery of the Poor Clares of Camerino.<sup id="cite_ref-OFM_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-OFM-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Visions">Visions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Camilla_Battista_da_Varano&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Visions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>She is said to have experienced visions. According to St. <a href="/wiki/Alphonsus_Liguori" title="Alphonsus Liguori">Alphonsus Liguori</a>, in his "Discourse IX of the Dolors of Mary", Passino writes that Jesus Christ Himself one day, speaking to blessed Baptista Varani of Camerino, assured her that when on the cross, so great was His affliction at seeing His Mother at His feet in such bitter anguish, that compassion for her caused Him to die without consolation; so much so, that the blessed Baptista, being supernaturally enlightened as to the greatness of this suffering of Jesus, exclaimed, "O Lord, tell me no more of this Thy sorrow, for I can no longer bear it."<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Camilla_Battista_da_Varano&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>She wrote with equal facility in Latin and Italian, and who was accounted one of the most accomplished scholars of her day. Camilla wrote extensively.  Her work includes Pregheria a Dio (1488–1490), <i>Remembrances of Jesus</i> (<i>Ricordi di Gesu</i>) (1483–1491), <i>Praise of the Vision of Christ</i> (1479–1481), and <i>The Spiritual Life</i> (<i>Vita Spirituale</i>) (1491), an <a href="/wiki/Autobiography" title="Autobiography">autobiography</a> from 1466 to 1491 which is considered a "jewel of art" and of the <a href="/w/index.php?title=Spiritual_life&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Spiritual life (page does not exist)">spiritual life</a>. In this work, she describes how two <a href="/wiki/Seraphim" class="mw-redirect" title="Seraphim">seraphim</a> with wings of gold, appeared to her because they were assigned to help her understand the mysterious working of unitive love.<sup id="cite_ref-TRP_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TRP-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Two angels came to me, dressed in resplendent white garments which I have seen only worn by Jesus. They had wings of gold. One of them took my soul from the right side, the other from the left side, and they elevated it in the air, laying it down near the crucified feet of the Son of God made Man. This state lasted about two months almost continually; I seem to walk, to speak, and do what I wished, deprived however of my soul. It remained there where the two Angels had placed it but they never abandoned it. </p>
<p>...They <i>(the celestial spirits)</i> declare to me that they were so intimate with God that God is not ever separated from them. They also explained to me that the <a href="/wiki/Seraph" title="Seraph">seraphim</a> were likewise united to the cherubim in that none of them could ever go without the other to a soul.

</p><p>— Camilla Varano, <i>The Spiritual Life</i></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Completed in 1488, <i>Treatise on the Mental Sufferings of Jesus Christ our Lord</i> (I dolori mentale di Gesu nella sua passione), is considered a masterpiece and is her best-known work.<sup id="cite_ref-franciscan_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-franciscan-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> It is largely a series of translations of revelations which she received.<sup id="cite_ref-TRP_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TRP-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Also attributed to Varano are three brief hand-written compositions, a short letter to her brother-in-law Muzio Colonna (1515), a Memoria recording her first encounter with the Benedictine-Olivetan monk Antonio di Segovia (1492), a eulogy in honour of the death of the Observant Franciscan Pietro da Mogliano (1491), as well brief prayers, letters, poems, tracts and revelations.<sup id="cite_ref-Pigozzi_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Pigozzi-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Her works also include the: "Recordationes et instructiones spirituales novem", which she wrote about 1491; "Opus de doloribus mentalibus D.N.J.C.", written during 1488-91 and first published at Camerino in 1630; "Liber suae conversionis", a story of her life, written in 1491, and first published at Macerata in 1624. These works have been edited by the Bollandists in connection with some of Baptista's letters. But most of her "Epistolae spirituales ad devotas personas" as well as her "Carmina pleraque latina et vulgaria" are still unpublished.<sup id="cite_ref-woywood_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-woywood-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Varano wrote in the dialect of the Marches region, while quoting scripture in Latin, which preserved the usage of the language at that period.<sup id="cite_ref-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25_3-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Her writings represented a high point in the Poor Clare tradition through its emphasis on the following of the "poor and crucified Christ", articulated by St. <a href="/wiki/Bonaventure" title="Bonaventure">Bonaventure</a> as a mark of Franciscan life, as well as mystical espousal with Christ. An element of her writing not found in St Clare's was her stress on Christ's inner sufferings and the need to suffer the evil that had befallen Him.<sup id="cite_ref-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25_3-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Paul_Lachance_1524_pp._19-25-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As a whole the writings of Baptista are remarkable for originality of thought, spirituality, and vivid language. Br. José Rodríguez Carballo, ofm, Minister General of the Friars Minor quotes Camilla Baptista:
</p>
<blockquote><p>O Most Clement God, if you were to reveal to me all the secrets of your Sacred Heart and everyday were to show me the Angelic Hierarchies; if everyday I could raise the dead, it would not be because of these things that you love me with an infinite love. Rather, it would be because I have done good to the one who has wronged me, said good things of and praise the one who has spoken ill of me and wronged me unjustly.<sup id="cite_ref-carballo_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-carballo-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Camilla_Battista_da_Varano&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Both <a href="/wiki/Philip_Neri" title="Philip Neri">Philip Neri</a> and Alphonsus Liguori recorded their admiration for her.
</p><p>On 8 April 1821 Pope Leo XII approved the acts of the process for her canonization. Varano was <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_XVI" title="Pope Gregory XVI">Pope Gregory XVI</a> in 1843, following recognition of her long-standing public cult. On 4 February 1893 her writings were also approved. On 17 October 2010 <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a> <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> her, along with five others.<sup id="cite_ref-OFM_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-OFM-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Her feast was originally kept in the Franciscan Order on 2 June but is more recently commemorated on 30 May.
</p>
