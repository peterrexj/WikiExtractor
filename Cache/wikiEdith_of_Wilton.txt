<p class="mw-empty-elt">

</p>

<p><b>Edith of Wilton</b> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;961</span><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2008145_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2008145-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> – 16 September 984<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>) was an English <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nun</a>, <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>, and the only daughter of <a href="/wiki/Edgar,_King_of_England" title="Edgar, King of England">Edgar, King of England</a> (r. 959–975), and <a href="/wiki/Wulfthryth_of_Wilton" title="Wulfthryth of Wilton">Saint Wulfthryth</a>, who later became abbess of <a href="/wiki/Wilton_Abbey" title="Wilton Abbey">Wilton Abbey</a>. Edgar most likely abducted Wulfthryth from Wilton; when Edith was an infant, Wulfthryth brought her back to the convent, where they both spent the rest of their lives.
</p><p>Like her mother, Edith was educated at Wilton; she chose to enter the religious life from a very early age, although there is some controversy regarding if she was a nun or a secular member of the Wilton community. <a href="/wiki/Goscelin" title="Goscelin">Goscelin</a> completed her <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> in <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1080</span>; he reports that Edith "always dressed magnificantly"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901253_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901253-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> because her status as a member of the royal family obligated her to fulfill certain roles to ensure the continued royal patronage of the Wilton community. Goscelin's main sources for his <i>Vita</i> were the oral testimony of the Wilton nuns and their abbess, as well as "from existing books",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198840_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198840-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> and it was dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Lanfranc" title="Lanfranc">Lanfranc</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>.
</p><p>When Edith was 15, her father offered her the position of <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> of three convents, which she refused. In 978, after the murder of her half-brother <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Martyr" title="Edward the Martyr">Edward the Martyr</a>, she might have been offered the English throne, which she also refused.
</p><p>In 984, Edith built a chapel at Wilton Abbey dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Saint_Denis_of_Paris" title="Saint Denis of Paris">St Denys</a>. The chapel was dedicated by the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">St Dunstan</a>, who foretold her immanent death and that the thumb on her right hand would remain uncorrupted. She died three weeks later, at the age of 23, on September 16, 984 and as she instructed, was buried at the chapel she built. Dunstan presided at her <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">translation</a>, which occurred on November 3, 987; her thumb, as Dunstan had foretold, had not decomposed.
</p><p>Few miracles were performed to support Edith's sainthood and her cult did not become popular and widespread for 13 years after her death. Many of the miracles that occurred later focused on the protection of Edith's <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> and the property owned by the Wilton community, often to retaliate, at times violently, against those who wanted to take or steal them. The support of Edith's elevation to sainthood by both secular and religious authorities was most likely political, in order to establish the legitimacy of their power and to connect themselves to King Edgar's children.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Birth_and_childhood">Birth and childhood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Birth and childhood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Edith, the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Wilton_Abbey" title="Wilton Abbey">Wilton Abbey</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a> in <a href="/wiki/Wiltshire" title="Wiltshire">Wiltshire, England</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Salisbury" title="Salisbury">Salisbury</a>, was born <i>c.</i> 961 in <a href="/wiki/Kemsing" title="Kemsing">Kemsing</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Kent" title="Kent">Kent, England.</a><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> She was the only daughter of <a href="/wiki/Edgar,_King_of_England" title="Edgar, King of England">Edgar</a> and <a href="/wiki/Wulfthryth_of_Wilton" title="Wulfthryth of Wilton">Wulfthryth</a>, a member of the royal family, who was educated at Wilton and later became its <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2008145_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2008145-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> There is some dispute that Edith's parents were married; historian Susan J. Ridyard calls Wulfthryth Edgar's "wife or <a href="/wiki/Concubinage" title="Concubinage">concubine</a>"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198837_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198837-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> and the <i><a href="/wiki/Oxford_Dictionary_of_Saints" title="Oxford Dictionary of Saints">Oxford Dictionary of Saints</a></i> calls Wulfthryth his concubine,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> but most historians, including Edith's <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographer</a> <a href="/wiki/Goscelin" title="Goscelin">Goscelin</a>, state that she was Edgar's wife.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231,_245_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231,_245-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2008144_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2008144-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> When Edith was an infant, her parents' marriage was dissolved, which allowed Edgar to remarry;<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2008144_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2008144-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> scholar <a href="/wiki/Sabine_Baring-Gould" title="Sabine Baring-Gould">Sabine Baring-Gould</a>, writing in 1875, states that Edgar "carried off the young [Wulfthryth] from Wilton Abbey, where she was being educated...[and] flying from her ravisher, [she] took refuge in Wilton Abbey, where she assumed the veil, and there brought up her daughter Edith, who was trained from her mother's breast in a convent".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875269_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875269-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Hagiographer <a href="/w/index.php?title=Agnes_B.C._Dunbar&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Agnes B.C. Dunbar (page does not exist)">Agnes Dunbar</a> reported that Edgar, under the direction of his <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a>, <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">Dunstan</a>, did <a href="/wiki/Penance" title="Penance">penance</a> for kidnapping Wulfthryth and marrying her against her will, by not wearing his crown for seven years.
</p><p>Edgar arranged that Edith be educated at Wilton by "two foreign chaplains",<sup id="cite_ref-x11_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-x11-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Radbod of Rheims and Benno of Trier, in order to provide her with the best possible education.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988144_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988144-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Dunbar states that Edith became a nun at Wilton "very early with her father's consent", but scholar Stephanie Hollis believes that Edith remained a <a href="/wiki/Laity" title="Laity">lay member of the community</a>, choosing at the age of two to stay there with her mother instead of living at the royal court until her death in <i>c.</i> 984.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDiener20144_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDiener20144-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis200437_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis200437-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> For example, Hollis calls Edith's status as a member of the Wilton community "ambiguous",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004249_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004249-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> stating that she was a secular member rather than a professed nun.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004249–250_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004249–250-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin, however, relates a story about Edith that Ridyard considers similar to many stories told by hagiographers to emphasize both the royal status of many saints and their choice to renounce it for the religious life. When Edith was two years old, her father visited her at Wilton; he placed before her the finest of clothes and jewellery worn by royalty, while her mother placed before her religious objects. Goscelin claims that Edith chose what her mother offered her.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198884_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198884-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard states that Goscelin emphasizes this incident, along with her concern and service to criminals and for the sick and the destitute, to demonstrate Edith's humility.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198887_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198887-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard also states that Goscelin describes a ceremony, attended by her father and other royal officials, on the occasion of her entry into and commitment to religious life.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988143_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988143-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Career">Career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Goscelin reports that Edith "always dressed magnificantly",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901253_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901253-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> wearing what Baring-Gould calls "gay clothing".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875269_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875269-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> When admonished for it by <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester" title="Æthelwold of Winchester">Æthelwold</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Winchester" title="Bishop of Winchester">Bishop of Winchester</a>, Edith responded, "My father, the mind may be as modest and God-fearing under fine clothes as under a serge habit. The God I love looks to the heart and not to the dress".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875269_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875269-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Hollis reports that Edith's wardrobe choices were vindicated when a candle was accidentally dropped into a chest that stored her clothes; the chest was burnt but her clothes remained untouched and the chest remained in the convent as a reminder of the miracle.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004262_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004262-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> When researching <i>Vita Edithe</i>, his hagiography about Edith, Goscelin viewed her possessions, including "a sumptuous linen <a href="/wiki/Alb" title="Alb">alb</a>" she had designed and embroidered, a skill aristocratic women educated in convents like Wilton learned along with literacy, languages, and the arts.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDiener20144_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDiener20144-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:172px;"><a href="/wiki/File:New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg/170px-New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="237" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg/255px-New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg/340px-New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg 2x" data-file-width="776" data-file-height="1080" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Edgar,_King_of_England" title="Edgar, King of England">King Edgar</a>, Edith's father</div></div></div>
<p>According to Hollis, Goscelin resolves the apparent contradiction between Edith's humility and her rejection of her royal status with her practice of wearing fine clothing by reporting that her status obliged her to do so in order to maintain the court's patronage of Wilton, which Hollis states "might have rivalled a royal court in its display of affluence",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> but that she wore them reluctantly and with a <a href="/wiki/Cilice" title="Cilice">cilice</a> underneath. It is also how Goscelin explains other behaviours such as her design and creation of ecclesiastical <a href="/wiki/Vestment" title="Vestment">vestments</a>, her possession of a menagerie of exotic native and imported animals and "a metal casket for heating her bath water", and her and her mother's rebuilding of the convent, which included a chapel built and designed by Edith. Goscelin implies that she held positions of power and influence at her father's court and probably at the courts of her half-brothers <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Martyr" title="Edward the Martyr">Edward the Martyr</a> and <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_the_Unready" title="Æthelred the Unready">Æthelred II</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251_23-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Hollis calls Edith's manner of dress "evidently habitual",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263-24">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> relating Goscelin's story about Edith assisting the workmen who built her chapel by carrying stones in her sleeves and his insistence that her fine clothing, her involvement in royal politics, and her periodic visits to her father's court did not make her less approachable to "the common people".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263_24-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263-24">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin also relates that she "was highly regarded by the nobility of England and abroad; foreign kings and their ambassadors sought her favour by letters and gifts, and high-ranking ecclesiastics sought her intercession".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004264_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004264-25">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/w/index.php?title=Katie_Ann-Marie_Bugyis&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Katie Ann-Marie Bugyis (page does not exist)">Katie Ann-Marie Bugyis</a> reports that according to Goscelin, Edith copied a manual of prayers used at Wilton Abbey, which the nuns there preserved as her <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a>. Goscelin praised Edith for her many talents, which included singing, <a href="/wiki/Embroidery" title="Embroidery">embroidery</a>, painting, writing, composing, praying, and "a blazing intellect in reading".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201934_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201934-26">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Edith was fifteen, her father appointed her abbess of three other convents in <a href="/wiki/Hyde_Abbey" title="Hyde Abbey">Winchester</a>, <a href="/wiki/Barking_Abbey" title="Barking Abbey">Barking</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Amesbury_Abbey" title="Amesbury Abbey">Amesbury</a>, which she refused, preferring instead "the obscurity of the cloister"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011136_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011136-27">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> and choosing to remain at Wilton with her mother.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875270_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875270-28">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin relates, in an anecdote Hollis calls a possible "hagiographic invention",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> that in 978, after the murder of her half-brother Edward the Martyr, Edith was offered the throne by opponents of Æthelred II, which she also refused. Dunbar relates an anecdote regarding Edith's dream about losing her right eye, which Edith believed foretold her brother's imminent death.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875270_28-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875270-28">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901252–253_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901252–253-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard calls the anecdote about the offer "highly improbable"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198841_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198841-31">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> and "the creation of an eleventh-century hagiographic imagination which found in the story of Edith's rejection of the earthly a poignant illustration of her devotion to the heavenly".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198841_31-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198841-31">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Hollis that it likely occurred because it was an attempt to legitimize the succession of Æthelred and because it was consistent with Edith's manner of dress and her close involvement in royal politics.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263–264_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263–264-32">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard states that Goscelin relates both stories, Edith's refusal to accept a promotion to abbess and her rejection of the throne, in order to emphasize the saint's extreme reluctance to accept a position of authority and influence".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198841–42_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198841–42-33">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A <a href="/wiki/Seal_(emblem)" title="Seal (emblem)">seal</a>, created during Edith's lifetime was adopted and used as its official emblem until Wilton Abbey the abbey was <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries" title="Dissolution of the monasteries">dissolved</a> in 1539. Bugyis states that it "figures her in half-length, frontal view, raising her right hand in a sign of benediction and holding up a book, perhaps the manual of prayers she copied, with her left hand".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201935_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201935-34">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/w/index.php?title=Alison_Hudson&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Alison Hudson (page does not exist)">Alison Hudson</a>, an archivist at the <a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>, Edith's seal gives "a rare contemporary insight into the priorities, identities and possibly even the jewellery of a young princess in late tenth century England".<sup id="cite_ref-x11_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-x11-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 984, Edith built a chapel at Wilton Abbey dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Saint_Denis_of_Paris" title="Saint Denis of Paris">St Denys</a>. According to Ridyard, Goscelin's account about the chapel's dedication "is skillfully juxaposed with an account of Edith's rejection of an earthly kingdom and is intended to be a final and dramatic illustration of her commitment to the heavenly"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-35">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> and supports his themes in her <i>Vita</i> about how King Edgar's patronage of the abbey served "the purposes of the heavenly kingdom".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_35-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-35">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard called the construction of the chapel "Edith's last and most spectacular act of patronage".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_35-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-35">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin reports that the chapel was dedicated by St Dunstan; towards the end of the ceremony, he foretold that she would die in three weeks. Dustan was impressed by the way she made the <a href="/wiki/Sign_of_the_cross" title="Sign of the cross">sign of the cross</a>, described by Hollis as executed "idiosyncratically and repeatedly".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Dunstan clasped her right hand, and weeping, said, "Never shall this thumb which makes the sign of our salvation see corruption".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_35-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-35">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> She died as he foretold, at the age of 23, on September 16, 984.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Hollis, Goscelin reports that at the moment of Edith's death, a nun went to the door of the abbey's church, and looking in, she saw "angels standing in ranks and singing sweetly";<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> then one angel with a shining face came up to the nun and told her, "Go back; the angels await the good maiden".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Edith was buried at St Denys' chapel as she directed. Ridyard states that the nuns at Wilton, including her own mother, began to experience strange events at her tomb. Goscelin reports that Dunstan, who might have been her spiritual mentor, was present at Edith's deathbed and presided at her burial.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004259_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004259-36">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> Thirty days after Edith's death, she appeared to Wulfthryth in a vision, assuring her that "she had been well received by her king in eternal grace"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_35-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-35">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> and that "the devil had tried to accuse her, but she had broken his head".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901253_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901253-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Dustan presided at her <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">translation</a>, which occurred on November 3, 987.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004260_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004260-37">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004269_38-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004269-38">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Her thumb, as Dunstan had foretold, had not decomposed; it was enshrined separately and as Baring-Gould says, was "long exposed to the veneration of pilgrims."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875271_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1875271-39">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Ridyard, Edith's feast, 16 September, is recorded in five 10th- and 11th-century <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">calendars</a>. The feast of her translation, 3 November, is recorded in two calendars of the period.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175-40">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg/220px-Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="274" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg/330px-Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg/440px-Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2249" data-file-height="2800" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>St Edith (image created in 1605)</div></div></div>
<p>Ridyard calls Goscelin's <i>Vita Edithe</i> "a vocation narrative"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198829_41-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198829-41">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> and one of the "defensive hagiographies"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175_40-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175-40">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> written during the period "to vindicate not only the status of a saint but also the history, the traditions and the political status of a religious community with which that saint was associated" and "an act of monastic propaganda on a grand scale".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175_40-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175-40">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> Hollis, however, considers it one of the "only surviving near-contemporary accounts of female communities in the late Anglo-Saxon period”,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246_29-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> an important historical document, and the earliest and most important source about Edith's legend and cult.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246_29-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Vita</i> was completed in  <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1080</span>, several generations after the events it describes and as Ridyard states, was "firmly grounded in the traditions current at Wilton in the final quarter of the eleventh century"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198838_42-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198838-42">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> and demonstrates his familiarity with and affection for the abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004218_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004218-43">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin claims that he wrote the <i>Vita</i> at the request of both the Wilton community and his patron, <a href="/wiki/Herman_(bishop_of_Salisbury)" title="Herman (bishop of Salisbury)">Bishop Herman of Salisbury</a>. He claims that his main sources were the oral testimony of the Wilton nuns and their abbess,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198838–39_44-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198838–39-44">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> as well as "from existing books".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198840_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198840-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Vita</i> was dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Lanfranc" title="Lanfranc">Lanfranc</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004218_43-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004218-43">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Eve of Wilton (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1058</span>&#160;&#8211;&#32;<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1125</span>), the Benedictine nun, anchoress, and later abbess of Wilton, about whom Goscelin also wrote a hagiography, had a devotion to Edith. According to Hollis, the community began to increase their devotion to Edith and experience more visions of her during Eve's tenure.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004232_45-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004232-45">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> A later Wilton abbess, Godiva (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1067</span>&#160;&#8211;&#32;1090), commissioned Goscelin to write <i>Vita Edithe</i> in 1080 because Wilton "needed the assistance of a powerful supporter".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004234_46-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004234-46">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> Hollis states that because of Edith's lack of popularity at Wilton and promotion of her cult in the decades following her death, the memories of the nuns who  lived there were not subject to what Hollis calls "hagiolatrous transformations and inventions".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004247_47-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004247-47">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> Much of the information Goscelin gathered about Edith came from Brihtgifu (d. 1065), who came to the abbey as a child during Wulfthryth's lifetime and later became abbess in the years immediately before the <a href="/wiki/Norman_Conquest" title="Norman Conquest">Norman Conquest</a> (c. 1040–1065).<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> According to Ridyard, Edith "lived on in the memory of the Wilton nuns",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988148_49-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988148-49">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup> although Hollis reports that there were few miracles supporting Edith's sainthood in the years following her death. Edith's mother Wulfthryth expressed her grief with commemorative masses and by building an <a href="/wiki/Almshouse" title="Almshouse">almshouse</a> in Edith's memory, but she tended to resist the making public of the only miracle reported immediately after Edith's death, Edith's retaliation against a young woman, not identified as a nun, who attempted to steal one of Edith's relics.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin writes that after Edith's death, a group of foreign clerks were unable to remove Edith's relics from Wilton because the relics became too heavy to move, which according to Ridyard, is a common theme in hagiographic literature, in which the saint expresses their preferences about their tomb and relics.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988146_50-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988146-50">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edith was elevated to sainthood by her half-brother King Æthelred II, with the support of Dunstan and other ecclesiastical support, 13 years after her death.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004269_38-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004269-38">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-51" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-51">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard explains the reason for the interval between her death and the development of her cult, even though "the scene seems set for the smooth and rapid development of Edith's cult"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152_52-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152-52">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> by stating that her promotion to sainthood was removed from the control of the Wilton nuns and taken over by "a number of prominent individuals",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152_52-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152-52">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> all of whom received visions of Edith urging them to elevate her remains. She appeared to Æthelred, to an official with a rank second to the king, to an "unnamed secular magnate",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152_52-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152-52">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> and to Dunstan, who was reminded of his prophecy about her incorruption and was ordered to travel to Wilton, "where he would find the body not only incorrupt but also raised up, as though already prepared to leave the tomb".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152_52-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152-52">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> Dunstan received another vision by St Denys, who directed him to perform Edith's translation, which occurred <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;997</span> and solidified the development of her cult.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152_52-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152-52">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271_53-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271-53">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard states that both Æthelred and <a href="/wiki/Cnut" title="Cnut">Cnut</a> supported the promotion of her cult to establish the legitimacy of their reigns and to connect themselves to Edgar's children.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988168_54-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988168-54">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> As Ridyard put it, "The Wilton nuns must have been aware from the outset of at least some of those political implications and of their potential usefulness".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988169_55-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988169-55">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Shortly after her translation, there were two attempted relic thefts, one by a Wilton nun who tried to steal Edith's headband but was stopped when Edith's own head was "menacingly raised against her".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_56-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-56">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> and the other by a monk from <a href="/wiki/Glastonbury" title="Glastonbury">Glastonbury</a>, who was horrified when he tried to remove a fragment of Edith's clothing from her grave and his knife slipped and touched her body; "a wave of blood gushed forth, as if drawn from a living vein".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_56-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-56">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> King Cnut, who reigned England beginning in 1016, had a devotion to Edith after she interceded on his behalf and saved him from a seastorm, the reason, along with wanting to associate himself with Æthelred and his family, he built Edith a golden shrine storing her relics.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150,_152_57-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150,_152-57">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup> Cnut, "through divine intervention on Edith's behalf",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_3-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> was able to punish some craftsmen who attempted to steal gold from it and as Ridyard puts it, "Divine vengeance followed quickly"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_58-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-58">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> when the workmen were struck blind. Edith performed few miracles between her translation and Cnut's death in 1035, including two healings of Wilton nuns. In the late 1030s, a nobleman named Agamund who had stolen Wilton properties had a vision of Edith on his deathbed, in which she tormented him by preventing him from dying in peace and entering heaven until he returned them;<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271_53-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271-53">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988149_59-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988149-59">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup> Hollis states this story "testifies to fear of Edith among the laity".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271_53-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271-53">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Ridyard, Edith "terrorised those who were foolish enough to invade the lands of her church and instilled fear in the hearts of those who might be tempted to emulate those invaders".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_56-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-56">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> Another man named Brexius seized land owned by the abbey and refused to make amends on his deathbed. His relative who was also a nun at Wilton reported having a vision in which she witnessed "the rough treatment which he subsequently received at the hands of the saint".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_56-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-56">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard also reports that not only did Edith protect the convent's properties, she protected, at times violently, "one further possession which was essential to its prestige, its prosperity and even its identity—the body of St Edith herself",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_56-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-56">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> as well as the right of the Wilton community to control her remains and the relics associated with her life and burial. For example, a woman trying to steal the linen frontal from Edith's tomb was miraculously unable to move.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_56-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-56">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin relates another miracle, of uncertain date, in which a nun ordered a dying child's mother out of the room where Edith's mother Wulfthryth died because the nun thought it was disrespectful; the mother brought the baby to Edith's shrine by mistake, but the child was miraculously healed.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004272_60-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004272-60">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edith's cult did not seem to have been established in the Wilton community until <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1040</span>, when she appeared in almost identical dreams to <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfgifu_of_York" title="Ælfgifu of York">Ælfgifu</a>, the wife of Æthelred II (who promoted Edith's cult) and <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lffl%C3%A6d_(wife_of_Edward_the_Elder)" title="Ælfflæd (wife of Edward the Elder)">Ælfflæd</a>, wife of <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Elder" title="Edward the Elder">King Edward the Elder</a>, in which Edith prophesied that Ælfgifu, whose devotion of Edith began after Edith healed Ælfgifu of an eye disease during a dream vision, would become abbess of Wilton; Edith's prophesy came true, but Ælfgifu's tenure was short (c. 1065–1067) also as Edith had predicted.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004272_60-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004272-60">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231–232_61-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231–232-61">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup> Ælfgifu made a deathbed request for Edith's relics and intercession; as she lay dying, Edith told an unnamed nun through a verbal communication in the nun's sleep that the Virgin Mary, through Edith's intercession, "was waiting with a choir of virgin saints to receive her soul".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004273_62-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004273-62">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup>  After Ælfgifu's burial, however, Edith told another unnamed nun during a dream vision that the community should pray for Ælfgifu because Edith "had obtained forgiveness from the Lord for all but one of Ælfgifu's offenses, and that she would not cease to intercede for Ælfgifu until she obtained pardon for this offense".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004273_62-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004273-62">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup> As Ridyard put it, however, "The purposes of the monastic patron, however, were not served exclusively by dire threats and dreadful punishments".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_58-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-58">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> For example, after King Cnut was saved from a seastorm through Edith's intercession, he expressed his gratitude by endowing gifts to the Wilton convent. Ridyard considers this miracle an example of the connection between Edith's "performance of conventional curative miracles"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_58-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-58">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> and the popular and widespread recognition of her cult.
</p><p>
Hollis reports that Wilton's faith in Edith had declined in the years following the Norman Conquest because despite being their patron saint, she had failed to help them recover some of the land and fortune they had lost and to protect them from a plague.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004232_45-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004232-45">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> Up to that point, Edith was remembered more for her wardrobe, her private zoo, her political influence, her artistic and literary talents, and the church she built and decorated, than for her holy life and miraculous powers.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246_29-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> As both Hollis and Ridyard points out, the Wilton nuns saw Edith as indifferent to her own convent's interests, even when the nuns there were stricken by illness;<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_58-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-58">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988267_63-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988267-63">&#91;60&#93;</a></sup> as Hollis put it, "It wasn't that she couldn't help them, apparently, but that she wouldn't; either that or she was the laziest saint in England".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267_64-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267-64">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup> Edith countered their expectations of her by communicating in "an unattributed dream vision"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267_64-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267-64">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup> describing her obligations as a universal saint and that it was her duty to assist everyone who asked for it because she saw no one as an outsider. Edith also affirmed her special relationship with Wilton, but that they had to "accept their temporal sufferings as a means of becoming spotless brides of the Lord".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267–268_65-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267–268-65">&#91;62&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard reports, however, that Goscelin attributes to both Edith and Wulfthryth "a number of domestic cures".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_58-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-58">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup><style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1011085734">.mw-parser-output .reflist{font-size:90%;margin-bottom:0.5em;list-style-type:decimal}.mw-parser-output .reflist .references{font-size:100%;margin-bottom:0;list-style-type:inherit}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns-2{column-width:30em}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns-3{column-width:25em}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns{margin-top:0.3em}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns ol{margin-top:0}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns li{page-break-inside:avoid;break-inside:avoid-column}.mw-parser-output .reflist-upper-alpha{list-style-type:upper-alpha}.mw-parser-output .reflist-upper-roman{list-style-type:upper-roman}.mw-parser-output .reflist-lower-alpha{list-style-type:lower-alpha}.mw-parser-output .reflist-lower-greek{list-style-type:lower-greek}.mw-parser-output .reflist-lower-roman{list-style-type:lower-roman}</style></p><div class="reflist">
