<p><b>Edith of Wilton</b> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;961</span><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2008145_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2008145-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> – <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;984</span><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003103_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003103-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>) was an English <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988148_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988148-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nun</a> and member of the community at <a href="/wiki/Wilton_Abbey" title="Wilton Abbey">Wilton Abbey</a>, and the daughter of <a href="/wiki/Edgar,_King_of_England" title="Edgar, King of England">Edgar, King of England</a> (r. 959–975) and <a href="/wiki/Wulfthryth_of_Wilton" title="Wulfthryth of Wilton">Saint Wulfthryth</a>. Edith's parents might have been married and Edgar might have abducted Wulfthryth from Wilton Abbey, but when Edith was an infant, Wulfthryth returned with Edith and their marriage was dissolved. Edith and her mother remained at Wilton for the rest of their lives.
</p><p>Like her mother, Edith was educated at Wilton. From a young age, Edith chose to enter the religious life, although it is uncertain whether she became a nun or a secular member of the Wilton community. <a href="/wiki/Goscelin" title="Goscelin">Goscelin</a>, who completed her <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> around 1080, reports that Edith "always dressed magnificently"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901253_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901253-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> because it reflected her status as a member of the royal family and because she was obliged to fulfil certain roles to ensure the continued royal patronage of the Wilton community. Goscelin based his <i>Vita</i> on the oral testimony of the Wilton nuns and their abbess, as well as on existing written sources. The work was dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Lanfranc" title="Lanfranc">Lanfranc</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>.
</p><p>When she was 15 years old, Edith's father offered her the position of <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> of three convents, but she declined. In 978, after the murder of her half-brother, <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Martyr" title="Edward the Martyr">Edward the Martyr</a>, she may have been offered the English throne, which she also refused.
</p><p>In 984, Edith constructed a chapel at <a href="/wiki/Wilton_Abbey" title="Wilton Abbey">Wilton Abbey</a> dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Saint_Denis_of_Paris" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Denis of Paris">St Denys</a>. The chapel was consecrated by <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">St Dunstan</a>, the Archbishop of Canterbury, who reportedly foretold her imminent death and that the thumb on her right hand would remain uncorrupted. She died three weeks later, at the age of 23, on 16 September 984 and was buried at the chapel she built as she had instructed. Dunstan presided at her <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">translation</a>, which occurred on 3 November 987; her thumb, as Dunstan had foretold, had not decomposed.
</p><p>Few miracles were attributed to her that supported Edith's canonisation and her cult did not become popular and widespread for 13 years after her death. Many of the miracles that were reported later focused on the protection of Edith's <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> and the property owned by the Wilton community, often violently retaliating against those who sought to take or steal them. The support of Edith's elevation to sainthood by both secular and religious authorities was probably politically motivated, in order to establish their power and to connect themselves to King Edgar's descendants. Her feast day is 16 September.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Birth_and_childhood">Birth and childhood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Birth and childhood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Edith_of_Wilton_-_MS_Royal_14_B_V.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="Circular-spaced miniature of St. Edith, pictured in the centre with her hands raised and flanked by plant-like objects" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Edith_of_Wilton_-_MS_Royal_14_B_V.jpg/220px-Edith_of_Wilton_-_MS_Royal_14_B_V.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="221" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Edith_of_Wilton_-_MS_Royal_14_B_V.jpg/330px-Edith_of_Wilton_-_MS_Royal_14_B_V.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/62/Edith_of_Wilton_-_MS_Royal_14_B_V.jpg 2x" data-file-width="388" data-file-height="389" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Miniature_(illuminated_manuscript)" title="Miniature (illuminated manuscript)">Miniature</a> of Edith of Wilton in a 13th-century royal <a href="/wiki/Genealogy" title="Genealogy">genealogy</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>Edith was born around 961 at the royal village in <a href="/wiki/Kemsing" title="Kemsing">Kemsing</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Kent" title="Kent">Kent, England.</a><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> She was the daughter of <a href="/wiki/Edgar,_King_of_England" title="Edgar, King of England">King Edgar</a> and <a href="/wiki/Wulfthryth_of_Wilton" title="Wulfthryth of Wilton">Wulfthryth</a>, who was of noble birth, received her education at <a href="/wiki/Wilton_Abbey" title="Wilton Abbey">Wilton Abbey</a>, and later became its abbess. Her brothers, <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Martyr" title="Edward the Martyr">Edward the Martyr</a> and <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_the_Unready" title="Æthelred the Unready">Æthelred II</a>, both succeeded their father as king.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200397_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200397-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Barbara Yorke reports that <a href="/wiki/Goscelin" title="Goscelin">Goscelin</a>, Edith's <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographer</a>, is unclear regarding the exact date of her birth.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003103_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003103-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Yorke also reports that modern scholars are uncertain regarding Wulfthryth's status.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200397_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200397-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin states that Wulfthryth was Edgar's wife and Yorke calls Wulfthryth his second wife, but Susan J. Ridyard calls Wulfthryth Edgar's "wife or <a href="/wiki/Concubinage" title="Concubinage">concubine</a>"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198837_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198837-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> and the <i><a href="/wiki/Oxford_Dictionary_of_Saints" title="Oxford Dictionary of Saints">Oxford Dictionary of Saints</a></i> calls Wulfthryth his concubine.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011135-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231,_245_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231,_245-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2008144_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2008144-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> When Edith was an infant, her parents' marriage was dissolved, in either 963 or 964, which allowed Edgar to remarry and allowed Wulfthryth to become abbess of Wilton shortly after Edith's birth.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2008144_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2008144-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200398_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200398-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin calls Wulfthryth "a sister who had once been [Edgar's] wife".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200398_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200398-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Edith was dedicated to entering the religious life at Wilton at the age of two. Goscelin describes a ceremony, which Yorke calls "a major state occasion",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> attended by her father and other royal officials.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201999_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201999-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988143_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988143-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/w/index.php?title=Stephanie_Hollis&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Stephanie Hollis (page does not exist)">Stephanie Hollis</a> believes that Edith remained a <a href="/wiki/Laity" title="Laity">lay member of the community</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDiener20144_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDiener20144-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis200437_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis200437-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> although Hollis suggests that Edith's status as a member of the Wilton community was "ambiguous".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004249_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004249-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> According to Goscelin, when Edith was two years old, her father visited her at Wilton and presented her with the finest of clothes and jewellery worn by royalty, while her mother placed before her religious objects. Goscelin relates that Edith chose what her mother offered her. Ridyard considers Goscelin's story about Edith similar to many stories told by hagiographers, told to emphasise both the royal status of many saints and their choice to renounce it for the religious life. Ridyard states that Goscelin emphasises this incident, along later stories with her concern and service to criminals, the sick, and the destitute, to demonstrate Edith's humility.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198884_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198884-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198887_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198887-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Edgar arranged for Edith the best possible education at Wilton by employing "two foreign chaplains",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHudson2017n.d._21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHudson2017n.d.-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Radbod of Rheims and Benno of Trier.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988144_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988144-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Career">Career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Dress_and_response_to_criticism">Dress and response to criticism</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Dress and response to criticism">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>According to Goscelin, Edith's rich and elaborate wardrobe was vindicated when a candle was accidentally dropped into a chest that stored her clothes; the chest was burnt but her clothes remained untouched and the chest remained in the convent as a reminder of the miracle.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004262_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004262-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> When researching <i>Vita Edithe</i>, his hagiography about Edith, Goscelin viewed her possessions, including "a sumptuous linen <a href="/wiki/Alb" title="Alb">alb</a>" she had designed and embroidered, a skill aristocratic women educated in convents like Wilton learned along with literacy, languages, and the arts. Edith wore the garment on special occasions like the reception of royalty or bishops and on high feast days.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDiener20144_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDiener20144-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201994_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201994-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="King Edgar, looking up with arms outstretched, dressed in red and blue" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg/170px-New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="237" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg/255px-New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg/340px-New_Minster_Charter_966_detail_Edgar.jpg 2x" data-file-width="776" data-file-height="1080" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Edgar,_King_of_England" title="Edgar, King of England">King Edgar</a>, Edith's father</figcaption></figure>
<p>According to Hollis, Goscelin reports that Edith was obliged to wear fine clothing due to her high status and in order to maintain the court's patronage of Wilton, which Hollis states "might have rivalled a royal court in its display of affluence".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251-28">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> Other displays of Edith's status and affluence were her design and creation of ecclesiastical <a href="/wiki/Vestment" title="Vestment">vestments</a>, "a metal casket for heating her bath water",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251_28-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251-28">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> her possession of a menagerie of exotic native and imported animals placed at the north wall of the convent, and her mother's rebuilding of the convent, which included a chapel built and designed by Edith.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251_28-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004251-28">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003106_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003106-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Yorke insists, however, that it appeared that "to all outward appearances she dressed as befitted a princess rather than a specialist in humility".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105_8-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Yorke states that despite Goscelin's claims that Edith and Wulfthryth renounced their wealth and status, they gave up neither. They instead retained their private wealth, a common practice of royals who resided in monasteries and convents, with which they could purchase relics and fund expensive building programmes.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105_8-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003105-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>   
</p><p>Katie Ann-Marie Bugyis reports that according to Goscelin, Edith copied a manual of prayers used at Wilton Abbey, which the nuns there, along with her <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a>, preserved. Goscelin praised Edith for her many talents, which included music, <a href="/wiki/Embroidery" title="Embroidery">embroidery</a>, <a href="/wiki/Calligraphy" title="Calligraphy">calligraphy</a>, painting, writing, composing, praying,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2004_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2004-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201934_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201934-31">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> as well as "a blazing intellect in reading".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201934_31-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201934-31">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Hollis calls Edith's manner of dress "evidently habitual",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263-32">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> relating Goscelin's story about Edith assisting the workmen who built her chapel by carrying stones in her sleeves. Goscelin insists that her fine clothing, her involvement in royal politics, and her periodic visits to her father's court did not make her less approachable to "the common people".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263_32-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263-32">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> 
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Political_and_religious_influence">Political and religious influence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Political and religious influence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Histories of the politics of the period only mention Edith in passing, but Yorke argues that claims made by Goscelin in his <i>Vita</i> of Edith that she and her mother played an important role in the intrigues concerning the succession to Edgar should be taken seriously.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200397_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200397-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003106_29-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003106-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> According to Goscelin, the English nobility and kings from other countries and their ambassadors held her in high regard and "sought her favour by letters and gifts, and high-ranking ecclesiastics sought her intercession".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004264_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004264-33">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin states that Edith was able to convince Edgar to overturn court judgements or procure gifts for churches, "with the result that she was besieged by petitioners from all strata of society who hoped she could intervene with the king on their behalf", and that officials from Rome, France, and Germany would visit Edith if they wanted favours from Edgar, often with an addition to her zoo.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003106_29-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003106-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Goscelin, when Edith was fifteen years old, her father appointed her abbess of three convents,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201990_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201990-34">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> but <a href="/wiki/Sarah_Foot" title="Sarah Foot">Sarah Foot</a> describes the appointments as "somewhat implausible"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFoot2019231_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFoot2019231-35">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> and suggests that Goscelin confused Edith of Wilton with other women named Edith.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFoot2019231_35-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFoot2019231-35">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard also doubts the veracity of Goscelin and others' claim that Edith was made abbess of any convent.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198841_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198841-36">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin claimed that Edith initially refused the positions, preferring instead, as David Hugh Farmer states in the <i><a href="/wiki/Oxford_Dictionary_of_Saints" title="Oxford Dictionary of Saints">Oxford Dictionary of Saints</a></i>, "the obscurity of the cloister".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011136_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011136-37">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin also stated that Edith insisted on staying under the authority of her mother not because she felt incompetent, but "by an overabundance of humility".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201991_38-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201991-38">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> According to Goscelin, Edith was an effective abbess of the communities despite her distance and that she visited them often. Very little is known about Edith's governance of the three convents outside of Goscelin's works about her.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201991_38-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201991-38">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Both Edith and <a href="/wiki/Eadburh_of_Winchester" title="Eadburh of Winchester">St Eadburgh of Winchester</a> were praised by <a href="/wiki/William_of_Malmesbury" title="William of Malmesbury">William of Malmesbury</a> for their prayers and intercessions on behalf of their respective communities, as well as their members' "unfailing obedience"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019226_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019226-39">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> and devotion to their leaders and teachers.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019226_39-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019226-39">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Goscelin relates that in 978, after the murder of  Edward the Martyr, Edith's half-brother, she was offered the throne by opponents of Æthelred II, which she also refused. Edith also had a dream about losing her right eye, which Edith believed foretold her brother's imminent death,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901252–253_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar1901252–253-40">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> which Ridyard calls "highly improbable"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198841_36-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198841-36">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> and "the creation of an eleventh-century hagiographic imagination which found in the story of Edith's rejection of the earthly a poignant illustration of her devotion to the heavenly".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198841_36-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198841-36">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> Hollis that it probably occurred because it was an attempt to legitimise the succession of Æthelred and because it was consistent with Edith's manner of dress and her close involvement in royal politics.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263–264_41-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004263–264-41">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard argues that Goscelin relates both stories in order to emphasise "the saint's extreme reluctance to accept a position of authority and influence".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198841–42_42-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198841–42-42">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> The claim that Edith was offered the throne is generally dismissed by historians as a "hagiographic invention", but Yorke argues that it may not have been as far-fetched as it seems, stating that it was "a possibility that Edith would leave Wilton to be married if circumstances dictated" and that her upbringing and education at Wilton, as it was for other well-born Anglo-Saxon women educated there, was to prepare her for a future public role.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003109_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003109-43">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A <a href="/wiki/Seal_(emblem)" title="Seal (emblem)">seal</a> was created during Edith's lifetime; later members of Wilton Abbey adopted and used it as its official emblem until the abbey was <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries" title="Dissolution of the monasteries">dissolved</a> in 1539.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019236_44-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019236-44">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> The seal demonstrates the Wilton community's "confidence in its ability to represent their patron saint as the guarantor of their documents' authenticity and in her guarantee's enduring significance to those in and out of the community".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019236_44-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019236-44">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> Bugyis states that it "figures her in half-length, frontal view, raising her right hand in a sign of benediction and holding up a book, perhaps the manual of prayers she copied, with her left hand".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201935_45-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201935-45">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> She is described as a "royal sister" on the seal.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2004_30-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2004-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> According to Alison Hudson, an archivist at the <a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>, Edith's seal gives "a rare contemporary insight into the priorities, identities and possibly even the jewellery of a young princess in late tenth century England".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHudson2017n.d._21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHudson2017n.d.-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Yorke states that Edith's seal was another indication of her status and independent wealth, as well as her affirmation of her status as Edgar's daughter.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2004_30-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2004-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003108_46-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003108-46">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Bugyis, Edith was not recorded as a former abbess in Nunnaminster sources, but claims that Goscelin described what he regarded as two relics of Edith's rule over Nunnaminster, both of which he considered full of mystical significance and were still used by the community when he wrote about them. One relic was an alb (mentioned above) and the other was a flowering rod, which also according to Bugyis, Goscelin stated had continued to grow even after Edith's death and was reminiscent of <a href="/wiki/Aaron%27s_rod" title="Aaron&#39;s rod">Aaron's rod</a>, symbolising her innocence.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201992_26-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201992-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> According to Bugyis, Edith's alb reflected her identification with <a href="/wiki/Mary_Magdalene" title="Mary Magdalene">Mary Magdalene</a>'s need to be forgiven of her sins and be remembered for her faith, devotion, and piety, as well as Edith's view of her office as abbess. As Bugyis claims, the alb "reflected the virtue she prized most in a monastic leader—humility",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201993_47-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201993-47">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> a duty the <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict" title="Rule of Saint Benedict">Benedictine Rule</a> required from its monastic leaders, washing the feet in humble service for members of their communities, as Christ washed his disciples' feet and the woman (often conflated with Mary Magdalene) washed Christ's feet in the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_John" title="Gospel of John">Gospel of John</a>. Also according to Bugyis, Edith might have placed herself as Magdalene on her alb in order to represent her commitment to serve the poor and the outcast.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201993_47-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201993-47">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> Bugyis speculates that Edith's choice of the costly gems, golden thread, and pearls might have also demonstrated the conflict between her roles as abbess and as the king's daughter, as well as a way to inspire her sisters and to provide them with a model of her kind of service and leadership to the community.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201994_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201994-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> The alb also demonstrated, as Bugyis claims, that "the liturgical and pastoral ministries of medieval monastic leaders were often inextricably entwined".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201994_25-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201994-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;984</span>, shortly before her death, Edith used her own funds to build a chapel at Wilton Abbey dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Saint_Denis_of_Paris" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Denis of Paris">St Denys</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2004_30-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2004-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_48-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-48">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> Yorke states that its construction was an indication of her status and wealth; it was made from wood, but had "lavish fittings of gold and semiprecious stones and decorated with a cycle of wall-paintings".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2004_30-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2004-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> According to Ridyard, Goscelin's account of the chapel's dedication "is skilfully juxtaposed with an account of Edith's rejection of an earthly kingdom and is intended to be a final and dramatic illustration of her commitment to the heavenly".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_48-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-48">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> It also supports his themes in her <i>Vita</i> about how King Edgar's patronage of the abbey served "the purposes of the heavenly kingdom".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_48-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-48">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard called the construction of the chapel "Edith's last and most spectacular act of patronage".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_48-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-48">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin states that the chapel was dedicated by St Dunstan, who towards the end of the ceremony, foretold that she would die in three weeks. According to Goscelin, Dustan, who was impressed by the way she made the <a href="/wiki/Sign_of_the_cross" title="Sign of the cross">sign of the cross</a>, clasped her right hand, and weeping, said, "Never shall this thumb which makes the sign of our salvation see corruption".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_48-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-48">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>According to Hollis, Goscelin reports that at the moment of Edith's death, a nun went to the door of the abbey's church, and looking in, she saw "angels standing in ranks and singing sweetly",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> One angel with a shining face then approached the nun and told her, "Go back; the angels await the good maiden".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Edith was buried at St Denys' chapel according to her instructions. Ridyard relates Goscelin's account that after Edith's death, the nuns at Wilton, including her mother, experienced strange events at her tomb. Goscelin also reports that Dunstan, who might have been her spiritual mentor, was present at Edith's deathbed and presided at her burial.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004259_49-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004259-49">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup> Her funeral and burial was "a state occasion attended by the great and good of the kingdom".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003106_29-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003106-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Thirty days after her death, Ridyard reports that Edith appeared to Wulfthryth in a vision, assuring her that "she had been well received by her king in eternal grace".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147_48-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988147-48">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> Dustan presided at her <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">translation</a>, which occurred on 3 November 987. Hollis reports that Edith's thumb, as Dunstan had foretold, had not decomposed.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004260_50-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004260-50">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004269_51-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004269-51">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Edith's feast, 16 September, is recorded in five 10th- and 11th-century <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">calendars</a>. The feast of her translation, 3 November, is recorded in two calendars of the period.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2004_30-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2004-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175_52-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175-52">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERollason1989140_53-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERollason1989140-53">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="St, Edith as a young woman, dressed in white and black religious garb, holding a royal crown and pink rose while being invested with a crown of flowers" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg/220px-Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="274" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg/330px-Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg/440px-Santa_Editha_-_Juan_de_Roelas_20140426.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2249" data-file-height="2800" /></a><figcaption>St Edith (image created in 1605)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Yorke argues that Edith had a more interesting career after her death, both for the royal house and for her convent.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200397_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200397-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard characterises Goscelin's <i>Vita Edithe</i>, which was written between 1078 and 1087 and is considered the main source for Edith's life,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2004_30-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2004-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERollason1989140_53-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERollason1989140-53">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> as "a vocation narrative"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198829_54-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198829-54">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup> and as one of the "defensive hagiographies"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175_52-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175-52">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup> written during the period "to vindicate not only the status of a saint but also the history, the traditions and the political status of a religious community with which that saint was associated" and "an act of monastic propaganda on a grand scale".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175_52-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988175-52">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin, who had a long-standing association with Wilton, may have been for some time chaplain to the nuns, and he seems to have had a personal devotion to Edith. He wrote poems dedicated to her which were published in a later revision of her <i>Vita</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200398_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200398-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Hollis, however, considers the <i>Vita</i> one of the "only surviving near-contemporary accounts of female communities in the late Anglo-Saxon period",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246_55-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246-55">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> an important historical document, and the earliest and most important source about Edith's legend and cult.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246_55-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246-55">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The <i>Vita</i> was completed several generations after the events it describes and was "firmly grounded in the traditions current at Wilton in the final quarter of the eleventh century";<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198838_56-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198838-56">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup> it demonstrates Goscelin's familiarity with and affection for the abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004218_57-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004218-57">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> He states that he wrote the <i>Vita</i> at the request of both the Wilton community and his patron, <a href="/wiki/Herman_(bishop_of_Salisbury)" title="Herman (bishop of Salisbury)">Bishop Herman of Salisbury</a>. He draws on the oral testimony of the Wilton nuns and their abbess,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198838–39_58-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198838–39-58">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup> as well as "from existing books",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard198840_59-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard198840-59">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup> as his sources. The <i>Vita</i> was dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Lanfranc" title="Lanfranc">Lanfranc</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004218_57-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004218-57">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin may have written another volume about the Wilton community, the <i>Wilton Chronicle</i>, which also included stories about Edith.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201930_60-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201930-60">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Eve of Wilton, the Benedictine nun, anchoress, and later abbess of Wilton, about whom Goscelin also wrote a hagiography, had a devotion to Edith. According to Hollis, the community began to increase their devotion to Edith and experience more visions of her during Eve's tenure.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004232_61-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004232-61">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup> A later Wilton abbess, Godiva, commissioned Goscelin to write <i>Vita Edithe</i> in 1080 because Wilton "needed the assistance of a powerful supporter".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004234_62-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004234-62">&#91;60&#93;</a></sup> Hollis states that because of Edith's lack of popularity at Wilton and promotion of her cult in the decades following her death, the memories of the nuns who lived there were not subject to what Hollis calls "hagiolatrous transformations and inventions".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004247_63-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004247-63">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup> Much of the information Goscelin gathered about Edith came from Brihtgifu (d. 1065), the daughter of noble parents, who came to the abbey as a child during Wulfthryth's lifetime and later became abbess in the years immediately before the <a href="/wiki/Norman_Conquest" title="Norman Conquest">Norman Conquest</a> (c. 1040–1065). Goscelin states that Brihtgifu received her call to religious life from Edith, who was to be Brihtgifu's <a href="/wiki/Godparent" title="Godparent">godmother</a>, but died 30 days before her birth, and during Brihtgifu's baptism at <a href="/wiki/Winchester_Cathedral" title="Winchester Cathedral">Winchester Cathedral</a> by Æfheah, who became bishop in 984, Edith appeared and helped the infant grasp her baptismal candle; the bishop instructed Brihtgifu's parents to send her to Wilton to be raised and educated.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWatt2019103_64-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatt2019103-64">&#91;62&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201997_65-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201997-65">&#91;63&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2003103_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2003103-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-66" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-66">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup> Bugyis considered Edith's choice to serve as a godparent an important part of Edith's role as a spiritual mother and as a part of her duties as abbess, even though monks and nuns were forbidden to serve as godparents beginning in the sixth century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201997_65-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201997-65">&#91;63&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Edith%27s_Church,_Bishop_Wilton.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c6/St_Edith%27s_Church%2C_Bishop_Wilton.jpg/220px-St_Edith%27s_Church%2C_Bishop_Wilton.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c6/St_Edith%27s_Church%2C_Bishop_Wilton.jpg/330px-St_Edith%27s_Church%2C_Bishop_Wilton.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c6/St_Edith%27s_Church%2C_Bishop_Wilton.jpg/440px-St_Edith%27s_Church%2C_Bishop_Wilton.jpg 2x" data-file-width="640" data-file-height="480" /></a><figcaption>St Edith's Church in <a href="/wiki/Bishop_Wilton" title="Bishop Wilton">Bishop Wilton</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>According to Ridyard, Edith "lived on in the memory of the Wilton nuns",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988148_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988148-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> although Hollis reports that there were few miracles supporting Edith's sainthood in the years following her death. Edith's mother, Wulfthryth, expressed her grief through commemorative masses and by building an <a href="/wiki/Almshouse" title="Almshouse">almshouse</a> in Edith's memory, but she resisted making public the only miracle reported immediately after Edith's death, Edith's retaliation against a young woman, not identified as a nun, who attempted to steal one of Edith's relics.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin writes that after Edith's death, a group of foreign clerks were unable to remove Edith's relics from Wilton because the relics became too heavy to move, which according to Ridyard, is a common theme in hagiographic literature, in which the saint expresses their preferences about their tomb and relics.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988146_67-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988146-67">&#91;64&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There are two churches dedicated to Edith of Wilton. St Edith's Church in <a href="/wiki/Bishop_Wilton" title="Bishop Wilton">Bishop Wilton</a>, a small village in <a href="/wiki/Yorkshire" title="Yorkshire">Yorkshire</a>, continues the tradition of celebrating its village feast on the same day as the saint's feast day, which for Edith, is 16 September. St Editha's Church in <a href="/wiki/Baverstock" title="Baverstock">Baverstock</a>, a small village four miles from Wilton, is also dedicated to Edith. A third church in <a href="/wiki/Limpley_Stoke" title="Limpley Stoke">Limpley Stoke</a> near Bath was dedicated to Edith for 500 years, but its dedication was changed to St Mary in the 16th century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPratt20061_68-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPratt20061-68">&#91;65&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Sainthood">Sainthood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Sainthood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Canonisation_and_miracles">Canonisation and miracles</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Canonisation and miracles">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Edith was elevated to sainthood by her brother King Æthelred II, with the support of Dunstan and other ecclesiastical support, thirteen years after her death.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004269_51-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004269-51">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-69" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-69">&#91;d&#93;</a></sup> Her status as a saint is supported by the listing of her tomb at Wilton in <i><a href="/wiki/On_the_Resting-Places_of_the_Saints" title="On the Resting-Places of the Saints">On the Resting-Places of the Saints</a></i> (the <i>Secgan</i>).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERollason1989140_53-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERollason1989140-53">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard explains the interval between her death and the development of her cult, despite the favourable circumstances, by stating that her promotion to sainthood was removed from the control of the Wilton nuns and taken over by "a number of prominent individuals",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152_70-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152-70">&#91;66&#93;</a></sup> all of whom received visions of Edith urging them to elevate her remains. She appeared to Æthelred, to an official with a rank second to the king, to an "unnamed secular magnate",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152_70-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152-70">&#91;66&#93;</a></sup> and to Dunstan. According to Goscelin, Dustan was reminded of his prophecy about her incorruption and was ordered to travel to Wilton, "where he would find the body not only incorrupt but also raised up, as though already prepared to leave the tomb".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152_70-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152-70">&#91;66&#93;</a></sup> Dunstan received another vision of St Denys, who directed him to perform Edith's translation, which occurred <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;997</span> and solidified the development of her cult.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152_70-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988152-70">&#91;66&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271_71-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271-71">&#91;67&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard states that both Æthelred and <a href="/wiki/Cnut" title="Cnut">Cnut</a> supported the promotion of her cult to establish their legitimacy as rulers and to connect themselves to the lineage of Edgar's children.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988168_72-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988168-72">&#91;68&#93;</a></sup> Cnut, for example, may have viewed his support of Edith's elevation to sainthood as a way to solidify his connection to West Saxon royalty, which began with his marriage to Æthelred's widow. Goscelin compares Cnut's connection to Edith, especially her piety and miracles, to her familial affections.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERollason1989140_53-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERollason1989140-53">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> The Wilton nuns were probably aware of Edith's familial awareness, as well as their "political implications and of their potential usefulness".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988169_73-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988169-73">&#91;69&#93;</a></sup> David Rollason states that the promotion of Edith's sainthood, along with that of Edward, was "a contribution to the prestige and status of the English kings" and that their cults, along with cults of Eadburg of Winchester and <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfgifu_of_York" title="Ælfgifu of York">Ælfgifu</a>, "were bolstering the ambition and pretension of the English kings".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERollason1989144_74-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERollason1989144-74">&#91;70&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Shortly after her translation, there were reports of two attempts to steal Edith's relics. According to Goscelin, one involved a Wilton nun who tried to steal Edith's headband but was stopped when Edith's own head was "menacingly raised against her".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_75-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-75">&#91;71&#93;</a></sup> The other attempt involved a monk from <a href="/wiki/Glastonbury" title="Glastonbury">Glastonbury</a>, horrified when he tried to remove a fragment of Edith's clothing from her grave and his knife slipped and touched her body; "a wave of blood gushed forth, as if drawn from a living vein".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_75-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-75">&#91;71&#93;</a></sup> Cnut, who became king of England in 1016, had a devotion to Edith after she interceded on his behalf and saved him from a sea storm. His experience, along with wanting to associate himself with Æthelred and his family, inspired him to build a golden shrine to store Edith's relics.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150,_152_76-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150,_152-76">&#91;72&#93;</a></sup> Cnut, "through divine intervention on Edith's behalf",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> was able to punish some craftsmen who attempted to steal gold from the shrine and as Ridyard puts it, "Divine vengeance followed quickly"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_77-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-77">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup> when the workmen were struck blind. Edith performed few miracles between her translation and Cnut's death in 1035, including two healings of Wilton nuns. Both Hollis and Ridyard relate the popular story about a nobleman named Agamund who in the late 1030s, had stolen Wilton properties and had a vision of Edith on his deathbed. She tormented him by preventing him from dying in peace and entering heaven until he returned what he had stolen;<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271_71-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271-71">&#91;67&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988149_78-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988149-78">&#91;74&#93;</a></sup> Hollis states this story "testifies to fear of Edith among the laity".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271_71-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004271-71">&#91;67&#93;</a></sup> As Bugyis put it, Edith's had dominion over both the condemnation and salvation of individual souls and demonstrated both her power of judgment and forgiveness, especially as it related to the violation of her community's spiritual and temporal possessions.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019214,_215_79-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019214,_215-79">&#91;75&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Ridyard and as reported by Goscelin, Edith "terrorized those who were foolish enough to invade the lands of her church and instilled fear in the hearts of those who might be tempted to emulate those invaders".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_75-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-75">&#91;71&#93;</a></sup> For example, when a man named Brexius seized land owned by the abbey and refused to make amends on his deathbed, one of his relatives who was also a nun at Wilton, reported having a vision in which she witnessed "the rough treatment"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_75-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-75">&#91;71&#93;</a></sup> at the hands of Edith. Also according to Ridyard and reported by Goscelin,  not only did Edith protect the convent's properties, but she also protected, at times violently, "one further possession which was essential to its prestige, its prosperity and even its identity—the body of St Edith herself".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150_75-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988150-75">&#91;71&#93;</a></sup> According to William of Malmesbury, on a visit to Wilton King Cnut started jeering at Edith, declaring that he did not believe in the sanctity of a daughter of King Edgar, "an especial slave to lust, and more tyrant than king". When <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelnoth_(archbishop_of_Canterbury)" title="Æthelnoth (archbishop of Canterbury)">Æthelnoth, Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, defended her, Cnut ordered her tomb to be broken open so that she could prove her sanctity. William claims that Edith emerged from her tomb and launched herself at Cnut, upon which he fainted with fright. On coming to he repented his scepticism and as a result Edith's feast day was widely kept.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELawson2011144–145_80-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELawson2011144–145-80">&#91;76&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWinterbottom2007298–201_81-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWinterbottom2007298–201-81">&#91;77&#93;</a></sup> In Yorke's view, the story was intended to highlight Edgar's reputation for womanising.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2008143,_146_82-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2008143,_146-82">&#91;78&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Prophecies_and_dreams">Prophecies and dreams</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edith_of_Wilton&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Prophecies and dreams">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Edith's cult did not seem to have been established in the Wilton community until <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1040</span>, when she appeared in almost identical dreams to Ælfgifu, the wife of Æthelred II (who promoted Edith's cult) and <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lffl%C3%A6d_(wife_of_Edward_the_Elder)" title="Ælfflæd (wife of Edward the Elder)">Ælfflæd</a>, wife of <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Elder" title="Edward the Elder">King Edward the Elder</a>. Ælfgifu's devotion to Edith began after claiming that Edith had healed Ælfgifu of an eye disease and prophesised her promotion to abbess at Wilton during a dream vision. According to the claim, Edith's prophesy came true, but Ælfgifu's tenure was short (c. 1065–1067) also as Edith had predicted.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004272_83-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004272-83">&#91;79&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231–232_84-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004231–232-84">&#91;80&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin writes that Edith chose Ælfgifu to be her successor through a vision received by Ælfgifu and her mentor, Ælfhild, during the tenure of Edith's godchild Brihtgifu as abbess. Goscelin reports that Edith emerged from her tomb and appeared to Ælfgifu while she was praying before another abbess' tomb. Edith bestowed symbols of convent authority upon Ælfgifu, which Bugyis argues justified and endorsed the community's choice of their abbesses during a time when such decisions were made by kings and nobles. Edith's bestowal of the objects to Ælfgifu was also viewed as a sacramental act usually performed by a bishop.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019116–117_85-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019116–117-85">&#91;81&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In another vision, Ælfgifu made a deathbed request for Edith's relics and intercession; as she lay dying, Edith told an unnamed nun through a verbal communication in the nun's sleep that the Virgin Mary, through Edith's intercession, "was waiting with a choir of virgin saints to receive her soul".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004273_86-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004273-86">&#91;82&#93;</a></sup> After Ælfgifu's burial, Edith another unnamed nun during a dream vision that the community should pray for Ælfgifu because Edith "had obtained forgiveness from the Lord for all but one of Ælfgifu's offenses, and that she would not cease to intercede for Ælfgifu until she obtained pardon for this offense".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004273_86-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004273-86">&#91;82&#93;</a></sup> As Ridyard put it, "The purposes of the monastic patron, however, were not served exclusively by dire threats and dreadful punishments".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_77-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-77">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup> For instance, after King Cnut was seemingly saved from a sea storm through Edith's intercession, he expressed his gratitude by endowing gifts to the Wilton convent, including a golden shrine for Edith's body, which was decorated with scenes from the <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke2004_30-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke2004-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_77-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-77">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard considers this miracle an example of the connection between Edith's "performance of conventional curative miracles"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_77-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-77">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup> and the popular and widespread recognition of her cult.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_77-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-77">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hollis states that the Wilton community's faith in Edith had diminished after the Norman Conquest because she had failed to help them recover some lost land and fortune and to protect them from a plague.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004232_61-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004232-61">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup> Up to that point, Edith was remembered more for her wardrobe, her private zoo, her political influence, her artistic and literary talents, and the church she built and decorated, than for her holy life and miraculous powers.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246_55-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004246-55">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> As both Hollis and Ridyard point out, the Wilton nuns saw Edith as indifferent to the interests of her own convent, even when the nuns there were stricken by illness;<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_77-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-77">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988267_87-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988267-87">&#91;83&#93;</a></sup> as Hollis put it, "It wasn't that she couldn't help them, apparently, but that she wouldn't; either that or she was the laziest saint in England".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267_88-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267-88">&#91;84&#93;</a></sup> Edith countered their expectations of her by communicating in "an unattributed dream vision"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267_88-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267-88">&#91;84&#93;</a></sup> describing her duties as a universal saint and that it was her duty to assist everyone who asked for her help because she saw no one as an outsider. Edith also affirmed her special relationship with Wilton, but reminded them that they had to "accept their temporal sufferings as a means of becoming spotless brides of the Lord".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267–268_89-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004267–268-89">&#91;85&#93;</a></sup> Ridyard reports that Goscelin attributes to both Edith and Wulfthryth "a number of domestic cures".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151_77-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERidyard1988151-77">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Goscelin reports that Edith performed miracles that demonstrated her concern for prisoners and sinners. For example, after a pilgrim from Saxony, along with his twelve companions, were sentenced to being shackled together for a year, which resulted in perpetual movement that did not stop when they were released. The pilgrims travelled to Wilton, slept before Edith's tomb for three days, and woke up to find himself healed, thus releasing him from judgment of his crimes.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019213_90-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019213-90">&#91;86&#93;</a></sup> Goscelin compared this miracle and others to the binding and loosing as described in the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_Matthew" title="Gospel of Matthew">Gospel of Matthew</a>, stating that "it superseded the power that was given to the apostles' priestly successors—whatever they bound as punishment for sin, she could ultimately loose".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019214_91-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis2019214-91">&#91;87&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

Her virginal hands worked this valuable piece with such mystical faith that it should give pleasure for its holiness as much as for its rich embellishment".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollis2004262_23-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollis2004262-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Bugyis states that Edith's alb is important to scholars of medieval religious women's arts and crafts because "it offers suggestive evidence of their textile work and the interpretative freedom they exercised in recreating biblical scenes in this medium".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBugyis201992_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBugyis201992-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> </span>
