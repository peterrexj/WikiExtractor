<p><b>Giles of Assisi</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Aegidius</i>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 1190 – 1262), was one of the original companions of <a href="/wiki/Francis_of_Assisi" title="Francis of Assisi">Francis of Assisi</a> and holds a leading place among them. St. Francis called him "The Knight of our Round Table".
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Giles_of_Assisi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Of Giles' origins and early life nothing certain is known, other than that he was a simple farmer. In April, 1209, moved by the example of two leading fellow-Assisians, who had already become the first followers of St. Francis, he begged permission to join the little band, and on the feast of St. George (23 April) was invested in a poor <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">religious habit</a> which St. Francis had begged for him. Almost immediately afterwards he set out with St. Francis to preach in the <a href="/wiki/Marches_of_Ancona" class="mw-redirect" title="Marches of Ancona">Marches of Ancona</a>. He accompanied Francis of Assisi to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> when the first Rule was approved orally by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Innocent_III" title="Pope Innocent III">Pope Innocent III</a>, and appears to have then received the monastic <a href="/wiki/Tonsure" title="Tonsure">tonsure</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Robinson_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Robinson-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> 
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Bartolome_murillo-venerable_gil.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/Bartolome_murillo-venerable_gil.JPG/220px-Bartolome_murillo-venerable_gil.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="196" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/Bartolome_murillo-venerable_gil.JPG/330px-Bartolome_murillo-venerable_gil.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/Bartolome_murillo-venerable_gil.JPG/440px-Bartolome_murillo-venerable_gil.JPG 2x" data-file-width="640" data-file-height="570" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Bartolome_murillo-venerable_gil.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Giles before Pope Gregory, Murillo</div></div></div>
<p>About 1212 Giles made a <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> to the tomb of <a href="/wiki/James,_son_of_Zebedee" class="mw-redirect" title="James, son of Zebedee">St. James</a> at Compostella, in <a href="/wiki/Spain" title="Spain">Spain</a>. Shortly after his return to Assisi he started for <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>, to venerate the Holy Places, visiting on his way home the Italian shrines of <a href="/wiki/St._Michael" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Michael">St. Michael</a>, at Monte Gargano, and <a href="/wiki/St._Nicholas" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Nicholas">St. Nicholas</a>, at <a href="/wiki/Bari" title="Bari">Bari</a>. He is next found in Rome and still later at <a href="/wiki/Tunis" title="Tunis">Tunis</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Robinson_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Robinson-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In these journeys Giles was always at pains to procure by manual labor what food and shelter he needed. At <a href="/wiki/Ancona" title="Ancona">Ancona</a> he made reed baskets; at <a href="/wiki/Brindisi" title="Brindisi">Brindisi</a> he carried water and helped to bury the dead; at Rome he cut wood, trod the wine-press, and gathered nuts; while the guest of a cardinal at Rieti he insisted on sweeping the house and cleaning the knives.  He said: "Happiness is to do bodily labor for the love of the Most High and not to take any lesser wages than Paradise for the good work one does."<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A keen observer of people and events, Giles acquired in the course of these travels much valuable knowledge and experience, which he turned to good account. He took every opportunity to preach to the people. His sermons were brief and heartfelt talks, replete with homely wisdom; he never minced his words, but spoke to all with apostolic freedom. After some years of activity Giles was assigned by St. Francis to the hermitage of Monteripido, outside Perugia, where he began a life of contemplation and ecstasy which continued until his death.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Giles was a stranger to theological and classical learning, but engaged in constant contemplation of heavenly things. Men of all social classes were drawn to Perugia to hear Giles teach. The answers and advice these visitors received were remembered, talked over, and committed to writing, and thus was formed a collection of the familiar "Dicta" or "Sayings" of Brother Giles.<sup id="cite_ref-ncd_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ncd-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> St. Bonaventure held these "Sayings" in high esteem, and they are cited in the works of many subsequent ascetical writers. They are short, pithy, popular counsels on Christian perfection, applicable to all classes. Known for their <a href="/wiki/Mysticism" title="Mysticism">mysticism</a>, humanity, and originality, they faithfully reflect the early Franciscan spirit and teaching.<sup id="cite_ref-Robinson_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Robinson-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It was in 1262, on the fifty-second anniversary of his reception into the Order of Friars Minor, that Giles died, already revered as a saint. His immemorial cultus was confirmed by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_VI" title="Pope Pius VI">Pope Pius VI</a> in 1777, and his <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is celebrated on the 23 April.<sup id="cite_ref-ncd_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ncd-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
