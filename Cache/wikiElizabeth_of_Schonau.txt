class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">Canonized</a></th><td class="infobox-data">pre-congregation</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Feast</a></th><td class="infobox-data">18 <p><b>Elisabeth of Schönau</b> (c. 1129 – 18 June 1164) was a German <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> visionary.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> She was an abbess at the <a href="/wiki/Sch%C3%B6nau_Abbey_(Nassau)" title="Schönau Abbey (Nassau)">Schönau Abbey</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Duchy_of_Nassau" title="Duchy of Nassau">Duchy of Nassau</a>, and reportedly experienced numerous religious <a href="/wiki/Vision_(spirituality)" title="Vision (spirituality)">visions</a>, for which she became widely sought after by many powerful men as far away as France and England.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Schoenau_kirche_004.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Schoenau_kirche_004.jpg/200px-Schoenau_kirche_004.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="267" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Schoenau_kirche_004.jpg/300px-Schoenau_kirche_004.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Schoenau_kirche_004.jpg/400px-Schoenau_kirche_004.jpg 2x" data-file-width="487" data-file-height="650" /></a><figcaption>Altar of St. Elisabeth of Schönau (with the reliquary in which Elisabeth's skull is kept) in the monastery church of St. Florin, Kloster Schönau im Taunus</figcaption></figure>
<p>Elisabeth was born about 1129, of family named Hartwig  of the Middle Rhine. She was educated at the double monastery of <a href="/wiki/Sch%C3%B6nau_Abbey_(Nassau)" title="Schönau Abbey (Nassau)">Schönau</a> in <a href="/wiki/Nassau_(state)" class="mw-redirect" title="Nassau (state)">Nassau</a> and made her profession as a Benedictine in 1147. In 1157 she became <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nuns</a> under the supervision of <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">Abbot</a> Hildelin.<sup id="cite_ref-mershman_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mershman-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> F.W.E. Roth points out that in the 12th century only women of noble birth were promoted to spiritual offices in the Benedictine order; it seems probable that Elisabeth was of noble birth.<sup id="cite_ref-steele_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-steele-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Her <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> describes her as given to works of piety from her youth, much afflicted with bodily and mental suffering, a zealous observer of the <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict" title="Rule of Saint Benedict">Rule of Saint Benedict</a> and of the regulation of her convent, and devoted to practices of <a href="/wiki/Mortification_of_the_flesh" title="Mortification of the flesh">mortification</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-mershman_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mershman-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> In the years 1147 to 1152 Elisabeth suffered recurrent disease, anxiety and depression as a result of her strict asceticism. <a href="/wiki/Hildegard_of_Bingen" title="Hildegard of Bingen">Hildegard of Bingen</a> admonished Elisabeth in letters to be prudent in the ascetic life.
</p><p>In 1152, Elisabeth began to experience ecstatic visions of various kinds. This was "a year after Hildegard of Bingen published her first book of visions, the <i>Scivias</i>, a work which seems to have influenced Elisabeth."<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> These visions generally occurred on Sundays and Holy Days at Mass or Divine Office or after hearing or reading the lives of saints. Christ, the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a>, an <a href="/wiki/Angel" title="Angel">angel</a>, or the special saint of the day would appear to her and instruct her; or she would see quite realistic representations of the Passion, Resurrection, and Ascension, or other scenes of the Old and New Testaments.<sup id="cite_ref-mershman_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mershman-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>She died on 18 June 1164<sup id="cite_ref-steele_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-steele-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and was buried in the abbey church of <a href="/wiki/Florinus_of_Rem%C3%BCs" title="Florinus of Remüs">St. Florin</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau,_German,_16th_century_(Morgan_Library_and_Museum,_New_York_City).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7c/Saint_Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau%2C_German%2C_16th_century_%28Morgan_Library_and_Museum%2C_New_York_City%29.jpg/220px-Saint_Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau%2C_German%2C_16th_century_%28Morgan_Library_and_Museum%2C_New_York_City%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="670" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7c/Saint_Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau%2C_German%2C_16th_century_%28Morgan_Library_and_Museum%2C_New_York_City%29.jpg/330px-Saint_Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau%2C_German%2C_16th_century_%28Morgan_Library_and_Museum%2C_New_York_City%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7c/Saint_Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau%2C_German%2C_16th_century_%28Morgan_Library_and_Museum%2C_New_York_City%29.jpg/440px-Saint_Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau%2C_German%2C_16th_century_%28Morgan_Library_and_Museum%2C_New_York_City%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1271" data-file-height="3870" /></a><figcaption>Statue likely representing Saint Elisabeth of Schönau, German, 16th century (<a href="/wiki/Morgan_Library_and_Museum" class="mw-redirect" title="Morgan Library and Museum">Morgan Library and Museum</a>, New York City)</figcaption></figure>
<p>What Elisabeth saw and heard she put down on wax tablets. Her abbot, Hildelin, told her to relate these things to her brother <a href="/wiki/Eckebert" class="mw-redirect" title="Eckebert">Eckebert</a>, then a cleric at Saint Cassius in <a href="/wiki/Bonn" title="Bonn">Bonn</a>, who acted as an editor.<sup id="cite_ref-mooney_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mooney-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 36">&#58;&#8202;36&#8202;</span></sup> At first she hesitated fearing lest she be deceived or be looked upon as a deceiver; but she obeyed. Eckebert (who became a monk of Schönau in 1155 and eventually succeeded Hildelin as second abbot) put everything in writing, later arranged the material at leisure, and then published all under his sister's name. While this relationship between brother and sister allowed for Elisabeth's wide broadcasting of her visionary experiences, it is evident that Eckebert attempted to have a degree of authority over Elisabeth.<sup id="cite_ref-mooney_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mooney-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 38">&#58;&#8202;38&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>The events in the first book probably took place before Hildelin intervened and told her to write these things down, while the things in the later books may have been after this point in time and occurred when Elisabeth had already begun writing.<sup id="cite_ref-clark_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Thus came into existence three books of "Visions". Of these, the first is written in very simple language and an unaffected style. The other two are more elaborate and replete with theological terminology, so that they show more of the work of Eckebert than of Elisabeth.<sup id="cite_ref-mershman_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mershman-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<ul><li><i>Liber viarum Dei</i>. This seems to be an imitation of the <i><a href="/wiki/Scivias" title="Scivias">Scivias</a></i> (scire vias Domini) of <a href="/wiki/Hildegarde_of_Bingen" class="mw-redirect" title="Hildegarde of Bingen">Hildegarde of Bingen</a>, her friend and correspondent. It contains admonitions to all classes of society, to the clergy and laity, to the married and unmarried. Here the influence of Eckebert is plain. She utters prophetic threats of judgment against priests who are unfaithful shepherds of the flock of Christ, against the avarice and worldliness of the monks who only wear the garb of poverty and self-denial, against the vices of the laity, and against bishops and superiors delinquent in their duty; she urges all to combat earnestly the heresy of the <a href="/wiki/Cathari" class="mw-redirect" title="Cathari">Cathari</a>; she declares <a href="/wiki/Antipope_Victor_IV_(1159%E2%80%931164)" title="Antipope Victor IV (1159–1164)">Victor IV</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Antipope" title="Antipope">antipope</a> supported by <a href="/wiki/Frederick_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Frederick I, Holy Roman Emperor">Holy Roman Emperor Frederick I</a> against <a href="/wiki/Pope_Alexander_III" title="Pope Alexander III">Pope Alexander III</a>, as the one chosen of God. All of this appears in Eckebert's own writings.<sup id="cite_ref-mershman_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mershman-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>The revelation on the martyrdom of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Ursula" title="Saint Ursula">Ursula</a> and her companions. This is full of fantastic exaggerations and anachronisms, but has become the foundation of the subsequent Ursula legends.<sup id="cite_ref-mershman_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mershman-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<p>On one occasion of religious frustration and fear, she wrote down an experience she supposedly had at a mass on a Saturday when the Blessed Virgin was being celebrated, when she saw in the heavens "an image of a regal woman, standing on high, clothed in white vestments and wrapped with a purple mantle".<sup id="cite_ref-clark_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The lady then eventually came closer to Elisabeth and blessed her with the sign of the cross, and reassured her that she would not be harmed by the things she had been frightened of. After receiving communion at the mass, she then went into an ecstatic trance and had another vision, declaring "I saw my Lady standing beside the altar, in a garment like a priestly chasuble and she had on her head a glorious crown".<sup id="cite_ref-clark_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> In her third text, she has Mary acting as an intercessor to hold back the anger of her Son from punishing the world in His anger for sin.<sup id="cite_ref-clark_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Her works are found in the 195th volume of the <i><a href="/wiki/Patrologia_Latina" title="Patrologia Latina">Patrologia Latina</a></i>.
</p>
<h2><span id="Eckebert.27s_role_in_managing_Elisabeth.27s_visions"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Eckebert's_role_in_managing_Elisabeth's_visions">Eckebert's role in managing Elisabeth's visions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Eckebert&#039;s role in managing Elisabeth&#039;s visions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Oftentimes Eckebert felt that it was necessary to further investigate the meaning of Elisabeth's visions, rather than to simply record her words.<sup id="cite_ref-mooney_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mooney-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 38">&#58;&#8202;38&#8202;</span></sup>  Elisabeth had a vision in which she saw the virgin emerging with the sun surrounding her on all sides, and the virgin's great brilliance seemed to illuminate the entire earth.<sup id="cite_ref-thiebaux_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-thiebaux-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 362">&#58;&#8202;362&#8202;</span></sup>  Along with the great brilliance which emanated from the virgin, a dark cloud appeared, which Elisabeth described as "extremely dark and horrible to see".<sup id="cite_ref-thiebaux_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-thiebaux-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 362">&#58;&#8202;362&#8202;</span></sup>  Elisabeth asked the holy Angel of God, who often appeared to her during visions, what the latter vision meant. The Angel said that the virgin in the vision she saw is the humanity of the Lord Jesus. The Angel went on to explain that the darkness represents God's anger with the world, but that the brightness signifies that he has not altogether stopped watching over the earth.<sup id="cite_ref-thiebaux_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-thiebaux-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 363">&#58;&#8202;363&#8202;</span></sup>  As ordered  by Abbot Hildelin, Elisabeth revealed her vision to Eckebert.
</p><p>Eckebert was struck with confusion after hearing this vision; he could not understand the purpose of the Lord's humanity being represented by a virgin instead of a man. Elisabeth relayed the answer provided to her by the Angel, explaining that "the humanity of Christ was symbolized by a female figure so that the symbol could also refer to the Virgin Mary".<sup id="cite_ref-mooney_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mooney-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 38">&#58;&#8202;38&#8202;</span></sup> However, Elisabeth did not waiver from her initial explanation of her vision. In the end, Eckebert does choose to publish this vision, and includes that the Lord the Savior's humanity is represented by a virgin. This incident represents Eckebert's strong belief in the reality of his sister's visionary experiences.<sup id="cite_ref-mooney_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mooney-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 38">&#58;&#8202;38&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>There is a great diversity of opinion in regard to her revelations. The church has never passed any ruling on them nor even examined them. Elizabeth herself was convinced of their supernatural character, as she states in a letter to Hildegarde; her brother held the same opinion. <a href="/wiki/Johannes_Trithemius" title="Johannes Trithemius">Johannes Trithemius</a> considers them genuine; <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_Amort" title="Eusebius Amort">Eusebius Amort</a> (De revelationibus visionibus et apparitionibus privatis regulae tutae, etc., Augsburg, 1744) holds them to be nothing more than Elizabeth's own imagination, or illusions of the devil, since in some things they disagree with history and with other revelations (Acta SS., Oct, IX, 81).<sup id="cite_ref-mershman_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mershman-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> In 1884, F.W.E. Roth published a treatise on Elisabeth of Schönau, <i>Die Visionen der Heilage Elisabeth von Schönau</i>. Although a great admirer of Elizabeth, Roth does not believe in her visions as divine revelation. He considers her a susceptible individual, living in a cloistered environment under a strict rule, and focus on prayer and contemplation developed into fanatical devotion.<sup id="cite_ref-steele_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-steele-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> 
</p>

<p>Because the population soon venerated Elisabeth as a saint, her bones were reburied between 1420 and 1430 in a special chapel. This chapel was destroyed in the great fire of the Schönau Abbey in 1723 and not rebuilt.
</p><p>During the <a href="/wiki/Thirty_Years_War" class="mw-redirect" title="Thirty Years War">Thirty Years War</a> <a href="/wiki/Sweden" title="Sweden">Swedish</a> and Hessian soldiers attacked Schönau Monastery. The Swedes expelled the monks, plundered the monastery, broke into the grave of Elisabeth, and scattered her bones. Only the skull was saved. It is now preserved in a <a href="/wiki/Reliquary" title="Reliquary">reliquary</a> on the right side of the altar of the church.
</p><p>The parish of St. Florin Schönau Monastery annually celebrates the traditional <i>Elisabethen-Fest</i> on the Sunday after 18 June.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Reputation">Reputation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elisabeth_of_Sch%C3%B6nau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Reputation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Elisabeth's popularity is evident considering those who called upon her for advice. The number of men who were very learned and religious who asked for letters from Elisabeth is astonishing. One monk, from an abbey in Busendorf, came to meet with Elisabeth in order to be provided with a deeper understanding of Elisabeth's visions, and in an effort to comprehend what God was doing with this woman. Upon leaving, he asked Elisabeth if he might be worthy enough to receive a letter from her. Elisabeth fulfilled his request and wrote the abbot of Busendorf a letter that provided him and his monks with spiritual advice through the grace of God. The letter emphasized the importance of the abbot's responsibility over his monks and the guidance the abbot must extend to his monks. Elisabeth states the importance of living one's life for God and of not getting sidetracked by worldly affairs.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Elisabeth wrote to powerful men when they did not ask for it, as well. Elisabeth scolded the archbishop, Hillin, of the city of Trier for not telling her divine message of condemnation to the people of Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-epistolae.ccnmtl.columbia.edu_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-epistolae.ccnmtl.columbia.edu-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>  She commanded that he, "Rise up in the spirit of humility and fear of the Lord your God."<sup id="cite_ref-epistolae.ccnmtl.columbia.edu_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-epistolae.ccnmtl.columbia.edu-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Throughout the letter it is evident that Elisabeth's Angel has spoken to her, and she is relaying this message to the archbishop. The letter is filled with anger and shows great disappointment in the archbishop of Trier, for God has taken notice of his failure to carry out his episcopal duties. Elisabeth informs him that if he does not tell the people of Rome the divine message in which Elisabeth has revealed to him, he will suffer on the final judgment day.<sup id="cite_ref-epistolae.ccnmtl.columbia.edu_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-epistolae.ccnmtl.columbia.edu-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>  Again, as in her letter to the abbot of Busendorf, her authority and belief in herself of a vessel of God is evident, and she has clearly gone above and beyond the limits of the traditional female gender role.
</p>
