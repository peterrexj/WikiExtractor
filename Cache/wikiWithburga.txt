<p><b>Wihtburh</b> (also <b>Withburga</b> or <b>Withburge</b>; died 743) was an <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_the_East_Angles" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of the East Angles">East Anglian</a> saint, princess and <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a>. According to tradition, she was the youngest daughter of <a href="/wiki/Anna_of_East_Anglia" title="Anna of East Anglia">Anna</a>, <a href="/wiki/King_of_the_East_Angles" class="mw-redirect" title="King of the East Angles">king of the East Angles</a>, but <a href="/wiki/Virginia_Blanton" title="Virginia Blanton">Virginia Blanton</a>&#160;has suggested that the royal connection was probably a fabrication. One story says that the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a> sent a pair of female <a href="/wiki/Deer" title="Deer">deer</a> to provide milk for Wihtburh's workers during the construction of her <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a> at <a href="/wiki/Dereham" title="Dereham">Dereham</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Norfolk" title="Norfolk">Norfolk</a>. When a local official attempted to hunt down the does, he was thrown from his horse and killed.
</p><p>Withburh died in 743 and was buried at Dereham. Her body was said to be <a href="/wiki/Incorruptibility" title="Incorruptibility">uncorrupted</a> by age or decay when her tomb was opened half a century after her death, and the church and the tomb subsequently became a place of&#160;<a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a>. When <a href="/wiki/Furta_sacra" title="Furta sacra">her relics were stolen</a> on the orders of the abbot of <a href="/wiki/Ely_Cathedral" title="Ely Cathedral">Ely Abbey</a>, the remains were re-interred at Ely next to her sisters <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelthryth" title="Æthelthryth">Æthelthryth</a> and <a href="/wiki/Seaxburh_of_Ely" title="Seaxburh of Ely">Seaxburh</a>. In 1106, Withburh's body was again examined and found to be intact. 
</p><p>Wihtburh’s cult in Eastern England, which was never large, was closely linked with that of her sister Æthelthryth. It was suppressed during the <a href="/wiki/English_Reformation" title="English Reformation">Reformation</a> in the 1540s, and her relics were all destroyed.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Family">Family</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Wihtburh&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Family">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Wihtburh was supposedly one of the daughters of <a href="/wiki/Anna_of_East_Anglia" title="Anna of East Anglia">Anna of East Anglia</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> a son of Eni, a member of the <a href="/wiki/Wuffingas" title="Wuffingas">Wuffingas</a> dynasty, and a nephew of <a href="/wiki/R%C3%A6dwald_of_East_Anglia" title="Rædwald of East Anglia">Rædwald</a>, king of the East Angles from 600 to 625.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKelly2013_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKelly2013-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">East Anglia</a> was an early and long-lived <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon England">Anglo-Saxon</a> kingdom that corresponds with the modern English counties of Norfolk and Suffolk.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELapidge2001154_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELapidge2001154-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Due to their rivalry for control over the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Angles" title="Middle Angles">Middle Anglian</a> people, East Anglia and its neighbour <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercia</a> probably became hereditary enemies, and Mercia's king <a href="/wiki/Penda" class="mw-redirect" title="Penda">Penda</a> repeatedly attacked the East Angles from the mid-630s to 654.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDumville1989132_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDumville1989132-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The sources for information about Wihtburh's family and the life and reign of Anna include the <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_ecclesiastica_gentis_Anglorum" class="mw-redirect" title="Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum">Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum</a></i> (<i>Ecclesiastical History of the English People</i>), completed in <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbria</a> by the English monk <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> in 731, and the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i>, which dates the from 9th century. The <i><a href="/wiki/Liber_Eliensis" title="Liber Eliensis">Liber Eliensis</a></i>, written at <a href="/wiki/Ely,_Cambridgeshire" title="Ely, Cambridgeshire">Ely</a> in the 12th century, also provides information about Anna and his daughters.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFairweather20058–10_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFairweather20058–10-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Wihtburh is not mentioned by Bede, whose writings about her elder sisters <a href="/wiki/Seaxburh_of_Ely" title="Seaxburh of Ely">Seaxburh of Ely</a>, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelthryth" title="Æthelthryth">Æthelthryth</a>, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelburh_of_Faremoutiers" title="Æthelburh of Faremoutiers">Æthelburh of Faremoutiers</a> and <a href="/wiki/S%C3%A6thryth" title="Sæthryth">Sæthryth</a>, her older half-sister, indicate that he was well-informed about the family.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200270_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200270-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004132_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004132-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> References to Wihtburh first appear in 10th and 11th century records, and the <a href="/wiki/Medieval_studies" title="Medieval studies">medievalist</a> <a href="/wiki/Virginia_Blanton" title="Virginia Blanton">Virginia Blanton</a> has suggested that the connection between Wihtburh and the family of Anna is likely to be a fabrication, invented to enhance the status of Ely Abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legend_of_the_does">Legend of the does</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Wihtburh&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legend of the does">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:East_Dereham_town_sign_(geograph_6796427_by_Adrian_S_Pye).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/East_Dereham_town_sign_%28geograph_6796427_by_Adrian_S_Pye%29.jpg/220px-East_Dereham_town_sign_%28geograph_6796427_by_Adrian_S_Pye%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="220" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/East_Dereham_town_sign_%28geograph_6796427_by_Adrian_S_Pye%29.jpg/330px-East_Dereham_town_sign_%28geograph_6796427_by_Adrian_S_Pye%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/East_Dereham_town_sign_%28geograph_6796427_by_Adrian_S_Pye%29.jpg/440px-East_Dereham_town_sign_%28geograph_6796427_by_Adrian_S_Pye%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2540" data-file-height="2540" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/East_Dereham" class="mw-redirect" title="East Dereham">Dereham</a>'s town sign, showing the legend of Wihtburh</figcaption></figure>
<p>After the death of her father in <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;653</span>, Wihtburh built a <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a> in <a href="/wiki/East_Dereham" class="mw-redirect" title="East Dereham">Dereham</a>, <a href="/wiki/Norfolk" title="Norfolk">Norfolk</a>. In the <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographical</a> account of Æthelthryth's life in the <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Liber Eliensis</i></span>, Wihtburh is said to have "voluntarily elected to live in solitude near Dereham".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004135_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004135-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A traditional story relates that while she was building the convent, she had nothing but dry bread to give to the workmen.  She prayed to the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a> and was told to send her maids to a local well each morning. There they found two wild <a href="/wiki/Deer" title="Deer">does</a> that were gentle enough to be milked, and so provided a nutritious drink for the workers. According to the story, a local official did not approve of the miracle, and decided to hunt down the does with his dogs and prevent them from coming to be milked.  He was punished for this cruelty when he was thrown from his horse and broke his neck.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAnon.185221_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnon.185221-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1897310_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaring-Gould1897310-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrowningTink2020189_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrowningTink2020189-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The legend is commemorated in the <a href="/wiki/Village_sign" title="Village sign">town sign</a> in the centre of Dereham.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrowningTink2020192_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrowningTink2020192-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The original sign, which was made in 1954 by Harry Carter and boys from <a href="/wiki/The_Nicholas_Hamond_Academy" title="The Nicholas Hamond Academy">Hamond's Grammar School</a>, was replaced in 2004 by a <a href="/wiki/Fibreglass" class="mw-redirect" title="Fibreglass">fibreglass</a> replica.<sup id="cite_ref-Vic_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vic-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_veneration_at_Dereham">Death and veneration at Dereham</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Wihtburh&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Death and veneration at Dereham">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Wihtburh died at a great age in 743, and was buried at Dereham.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArnold-Forster1899370_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold-Forster1899370-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The historian <a href="/wiki/Barbara_Yorke" title="Barbara Yorke">Barbara Yorke</a> has commented on this date for Wihtburh's death, stating that it is "rather late for a daughter of Anna".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200270_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200270-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Wihtburh is mentioned in the <i>Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</i> <a href="/wiki/Annal" class="mw-redirect" title="Annal">annal</a> for 799, in an addition to the original text that was written after the <a href="/wiki/Norman_Conquest" title="Norman Conquest">Norman Conquest</a> of 1066:<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004132_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004132-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPestell200490_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPestell200490-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>A.D. 799. This year Archbishop Ethelbert, and Cynbert, Bishop of Wessex, went to Rome. In the meantime Bishop Alfun died at Sudbury, and was buried at Dunwich. After him Tidfrith was elected to the see; and Siric, king of the East Saxons, went to Rome. In this year the body of Witburga was found entire, and free from decay, at Dercham, after a lapse of five and fifty years from the period of her decease.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite><i>Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</i><sup id="cite_ref-Yal_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yal-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup></cite></div></blockquote>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Incorruptibility" title="Incorruptibility">incorruptibility</a> of Wihtburh's body was considered a <a href="/wiki/Miracle" title="Miracle">miracle</a> and her remains were re-interred in the church which she had built in Dereham. The church and the tomb became a place of <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a>. The large church at Dereham has a <a href="/wiki/Chapel" title="Chapel">chapel</a> dedicated to Wihtburh, and a plan that, according to the historian Tim Pestell, "is possibly indicative of its former status".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPestell200489_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPestell200489-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECruz197761_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECruz197761-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Removal_to_Ely">Removal to Ely</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Wihtburh&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Removal to Ely">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_Wihtbuhr_(MS_London,_British_Library,_Stowe_944).png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Saint_Wihtbuhr_%28MS_London%2C_British_Library%2C_Stowe_944%29.png/280px-Saint_Wihtbuhr_%28MS_London%2C_British_Library%2C_Stowe_944%29.png" decoding="async" width="280" height="170" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Saint_Wihtbuhr_%28MS_London%2C_British_Library%2C_Stowe_944%29.png/420px-Saint_Wihtbuhr_%28MS_London%2C_British_Library%2C_Stowe_944%29.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Saint_Wihtbuhr_%28MS_London%2C_British_Library%2C_Stowe_944%29.png/560px-Saint_Wihtbuhr_%28MS_London%2C_British_Library%2C_Stowe_944%29.png 2x" data-file-width="781" data-file-height="473" /></a><figcaption>A detail from <a href="/wiki/Folio" title="Folio">folio</a> 36.r of <a href="/wiki/Stowe_manuscripts" title="Stowe manuscripts">MS Stowe</a> 944 (the Hyde Register), which names the saint as being interred at <a href="/wiki/Ely_Cathedral" title="Ely Cathedral">Ely Cathedral</a> near to her sisters: <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">And Sancta Wihtburh hyre sweostor mid hyre nu þǽr resteð.</i></span> (<a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>)</figcaption></figure>
<p>In 974, Brithnoth, the abbot of <a href="/wiki/Ely_Abbey" class="mw-redirect" title="Ely Abbey">Ely</a>, accompanied by <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monks</a> and armed men, travelled to Dereham with the intention of taking Wihtburh's body by force. They organised a feast for the townspeople as a diversion tactic. After waiting until the Dereham men were properly drunk, Brithnoth stole Wihtburh's body and set off during the night for the <a href="/wiki/Isle_of_Ely" title="Isle of Ely">Isle of Ely</a>. After discovering the theft, the people of Dereham set off after the tomb-robbers, who were attacked at <a href="/wiki/Brandon,_Suffolk" title="Brandon, Suffolk">Brandon</a> by the townspeople.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPestell200490_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPestell200490-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Using their knowledge of <a href="/wiki/The_Fens" title="The Fens">the Fens</a> to evade their pursuers, Brithnoth and his men successfully reached Ely, and Wihtburh's remains were re-interred there.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004136_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004136-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;note 1&#93;</a></sup> When the Dereham men returned home, they discovered that a spring had arisen in Wihtburh's tomb.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPestell200489_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPestell200489-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>  
</p><p>Saints' <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cults</a> were a feature of <a href="/wiki/Christianity_in_Anglo-Saxon_England" title="Christianity in Anglo-Saxon England">Christianity in Anglo-Saxon England</a>, and their remains attracted benefactions. Smaller monasteries may not have been able to resist the requests of places like Ely to acquire their relics, which accounts for the theft of the remains of Wihtburh in 974.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPestell200489_18-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPestell200489-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> The story of the theft, which appeared in the <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Liber Eliensis</i></span>, was taken from an earlier <i>Life</i> of the saint. The abbey attempted to relate the story as an "appropriate holy sacrilege", which gave her honour, as she was being laid to rest close to the remains of her older sister, Æthelthryth.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPestell200490_16-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPestell200490-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Site_of_Withburga%27s_Tomb,_East_Dereham_-_geograph.org.uk_-_85479.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Site_of_Withburga%27s_Tomb%2C_East_Dereham_-_geograph.org.uk_-_85479.jpg/220px-Site_of_Withburga%27s_Tomb%2C_East_Dereham_-_geograph.org.uk_-_85479.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Site_of_Withburga%27s_Tomb%2C_East_Dereham_-_geograph.org.uk_-_85479.jpg/330px-Site_of_Withburga%27s_Tomb%2C_East_Dereham_-_geograph.org.uk_-_85479.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Site_of_Withburga%27s_Tomb%2C_East_Dereham_-_geograph.org.uk_-_85479.jpg/440px-Site_of_Withburga%27s_Tomb%2C_East_Dereham_-_geograph.org.uk_-_85479.jpg 2x" data-file-width="480" data-file-height="640" /></a><figcaption>The site of Withburga's tomb in <a href="/wiki/East_Dereham" class="mw-redirect" title="East Dereham">East Dereham</a>, <a href="/wiki/Norfolk" title="Norfolk">Norfolk</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>At the site of Wihtburh's tomb outside the west end of the town's <a href="/wiki/Parish_church" title="Parish church">parish church</a>, there is the remains of a <a href="/wiki/Holy_well" title="Holy well">holy well</a> associated with the saint,<sup id="cite_ref-Nor_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Nor-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> which according to her legend rose on the site of her grave after her body had been stolen. Of a second spring, located further west and called Wihtburh's Well, which was recorded in the 18th century, no remains exist.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPestell200489_18-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPestell200489-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Wihtburh's interment at Ely is first recorded in the Hyde Register, an 11th century list of the burial places of English saints.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004132_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004132-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStenton1970195_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStenton1970195-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> A sentence of the text of the register, now kept in the <a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a> as <a href="/wiki/Stowe_manuscripts" title="Stowe manuscripts">MS Stowe</a> 944, reads:
<span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">And Sancta Wihtburh hyre sweostor mid hyre nu þǽr resteð.</i></span><sup id="cite_ref-Hal_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hal-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> According to the register, she was placed near Æthelthryth, Sexburga, and <a href="/wiki/Ermenilda_of_Ely" title="Ermenilda of Ely">Ermenilda</a>, Sexburga's daughter.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECruz197761_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECruz197761-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>In 1106, when their remains were moved closer to the main <a href="/wiki/Altar" title="Altar">altar</a>, the bodies of Wihtburh and her sisters were publicly displayed before a group of <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishops</a>, <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbots</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Clergy" title="Clergy">clergymen</a>, including <a href="/wiki/Anselm_of_Canterbury" title="Anselm of Canterbury">Anselm, Archbishop of Canterbury</a>. It was found that the body of Æthelthryth had been preserved and that Wihtburh was so conserved but that her limbs were flexible, her cheeks were rosy, and her breasts were firm, a sign of her body's vitality, youthfulness and "burgeoning productivity".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004138–139_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004138–139-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The description of her life and miraculous incorruptibility closely follows that of Æthelthryth, who was also a virgin who founded an East Anglian monastery. According to Blanton, "the prominence of the tombs [of Wihtburh and other members of her family at Ely] demonstrated that kinship was an important ideological construct that needed to be presented visually".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> This, and the family's inclusion in texts such the <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Liber Eliensis</i></span>, "indicated a complementary focus on the strength and cohesion of this holy family as the cornerstone of the monastery's history".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Other documents originating from the abbey show that the cults of Wihtburh and her sisters Æthelthryth and Seaxburh formed part of what the historian Virginia Blanton describes as Ely's "ideology of kinship".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004141_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004141-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> Ely strove to promote and enhance itself as a place renowned for its holiness and connections with the East Anglian royal family, something that was achieved by placing the royal tombs of Wihtburh and her sisters in close proximity, and documenting that the daughters of Anna were abbesses at Ely.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129,_134_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004129,_134-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The relics of the sisters were all destroyed during the <a href="/wiki/Reformation" title="Reformation">Reformation</a>; no trace of the royal tombs, including that of Wihtburh, now exists.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECruz197761_19-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECruz197761-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Dissemination_of_the_legend">Dissemination of the legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Wihtburh&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Dissemination of the legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Extract_from_the_legend_of_Saint_Wihtburh_by_John_of_Tynemouth.png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Extract_from_the_legend_of_Saint_Wihtburh_by_John_of_Tynemouth.png/220px-Extract_from_the_legend_of_Saint_Wihtburh_by_John_of_Tynemouth.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="199" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Extract_from_the_legend_of_Saint_Wihtburh_by_John_of_Tynemouth.png/330px-Extract_from_the_legend_of_Saint_Wihtburh_by_John_of_Tynemouth.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/73/Extract_from_the_legend_of_Saint_Wihtburh_by_John_of_Tynemouth.png 2x" data-file-width="413" data-file-height="374" /></a><figcaption>Extract from the legend of Wihtburh by <a href="/wiki/John_of_Tynemouth_(chronicler)" title="John of Tynemouth (chronicler)">John of Tynemouth</a> from his  <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Sanctilogium Angliae</i></span> (1516)</figcaption></figure>
<p>The process by which the story of Wihtburh was disseminated is not known for certain. Between 1325 and 1340, the English <a href="/wiki/Chronicler" class="mw-redirect" title="Chronicler">chronicler</a> <a href="/wiki/John_of_Tynemouth_(chronicler)" title="John of Tynemouth (chronicler)">John of Tynemouth</a> included the <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">vitae</i></span> of the Ely saints, including Wihtburh, in his <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Sanctilogium Angliae</i></span>.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;note 2&#93;</a></sup> The work was enlarged during the 15th century and a revised edition printed in English by <a href="/wiki/Wynkyn_de_Worde" title="Wynkyn de Worde">Wynkyn de Worde</a> in 1516 and translated into English by <a href="/wiki/Richard_Pynson" title="Richard Pynson">Richard Pynson</a> the same year.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004142_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004142-32">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Wihtburh is included as "St Withburge" in <i>The Lives of Women Saints of our Contrie of England, also Some Other Liues of Holie Women Written by Some of the Auncient Fathers</i>, written during the first half of the 1610s.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004142_32-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004142-32">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The story of Wihtburh appears only to have been influential at a local level; four images depict Wihtburh (as well as Æthelthryth) on Norfolk church <a href="/wiki/Rood_screen" title="Rood screen">rood screens</a>. According to Blanton, the depiction of the sisters together in these churches "was a result of, if not a directive of, Ely's narrative history".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004143–144_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004143–144-33">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;note 3&#93;</a></sup> The localized concentration of images suggests that Wihtburh's cult was never large, and was closely linked with the cult associated with her more illustrious sister, Æthelthryth.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004148_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlanton2004148-39">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
