<p><b>Ninian</b> is a <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> saint, first mentioned in the 8th century as being an early <a href="/wiki/Missionary" title="Missionary">missionary</a> among the <a href="/wiki/Picts" title="Picts">Pictish</a> peoples of what is now <a href="/wiki/Scotland" title="Scotland">Scotland</a>. For this reason he is known as the <b>Apostle to the Southern Picts</b>, and there are numerous dedications to him in those parts of Scotland with a Pictish heritage, throughout the <a href="/wiki/Scottish_Lowlands" title="Scottish Lowlands">Scottish Lowlands</a>, and in parts of Northern <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbrian</a> heritage. He is also known as <b>Ringan</b> in Scotland, and as <b>Trynnian</b> in Northern England.
</p><p>Ninian's major shrine was at <a href="/wiki/Whithorn" title="Whithorn">Whithorn</a> in <a href="/wiki/Galloway" title="Galloway">Galloway</a>, where he is associated with the <a href="/wiki/Candida_Casa" title="Candida Casa">Candida Casa</a> (<a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> for 'White House'). Nothing is known about his teachings, and there is no unchallenged authority for information about his life.
</p><p>The nature of Ninian's identity is uncertain, and historians have identified the name "Ninian" with other historical figures. A popular hypothesis proposed by <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Owen_Clancy" title="Thomas Owen Clancy">Thomas Owen Clancy</a>, a researcher and professor of Celtic studies, posits that Ninian can be identified with three other historical figures: Saint Finnian of Moville, Saint Finnian of Clonard, and Saint Finbarr of Cork. Linguistic variations across the territories associated with each saint have provided evidence that the Ninian preserved in literary tradition originated from this individual.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> This article discusses the particulars and origins of what has come to be known as the "traditional" stories of Saint Ninian.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background">Background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ninian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Southern Picts, for whom Ninian is held to be the apostle, were the Picts south of the mountains known as the <a href="/wiki/Mounth" title="Mounth">Mounth</a>, which cross Scotland north of the Firths of <a href="/wiki/Firth_of_Clyde" title="Firth of Clyde">Clyde</a> and <a href="/wiki/Firth_of_Forth" title="Firth of Forth">Forth</a>. That they had once been Christian is known from a 5th-century mention of them by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Patrick" title="Saint Patrick">Saint Patrick</a> in his <i>Letter to Coroticus</i>, where he refers to them as '<a href="/wiki/Apostasy" title="Apostasy">apostate</a> Picts'.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Patrick could not have been referring to the Northern Picts who were converted by <a href="/wiki/Columba" title="Columba">Saint Columba</a> in the 6th century because they were not yet Christian, and thus could not be called 'apostate'. Northumbria had established a bishopric among the Southern Picts at <a href="/wiki/Abercorn" title="Abercorn">Abercorn</a> in 681, under <a href="/wiki/Trumwine_of_Abercorn" title="Trumwine of Abercorn">Bishop Trumwine</a>. This effort was abandoned shortly after the Picts defeated the Northumbrians at the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Dun_Nechtain" title="Battle of Dun Nechtain">Battle of Dun Nechtain</a> in 685.
</p><p>Christianity had flourished in Galloway in the 6th century.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> By the time of <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>'s account in 731, the Northumbrians had enjoyed an unbroken relationship with Galloway for a century or longer, beginning with the Northumbrian predecessor state of <a href="/wiki/Bernicia" title="Bernicia">Bernicia</a>. The full nature of the relationship is uncertain. Also at this time, Northumbria was establishing bishoprics in its sphere of influence, to be subordinate to the Northumbrian <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_York" title="Archbishop of York">Archbishop of York</a>. One such bishopric was established at <a href="/wiki/Whithorn" title="Whithorn">Whithorn</a> in 731, and Bede's account serves to support the legitimacy of the new Northumbrian bishopric. The Bernician name <i>hwit ærn</i> is <a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a> for the <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> <i>candida casa</i>, or 'white house' in modern English, and it has survived as the modern name of Whithorn.
</p><p>There is as yet no unchallenged connection of the historical record to the person who was Bede's Ninian. However, the unlikelihood that the reputable historian Bede invented Ninian without some basis in the historical record, combined with an increased knowledge of Ireland's early saints and Whithorn's early Christian connections, has led to serious scholarly efforts to find Bede's basis. <a href="/wiki/James_Henthorn_Todd" title="James Henthorn Todd">James Henthorn Todd</a>, in his 1855 publication of the <i>Leabhar Imuinn</i> (The Book of Hymns of the Ancient Church of Ireland), suggested that it was <a href="/wiki/Finnian_of_Moville" class="mw-redirect" title="Finnian of Moville">Finnian of Moville</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> and that view has gained traction among modern scholars.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Traditional_story">Traditional story</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ninian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Traditional story">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The earliest mention of Ninian of Whithorn is in a short passage of <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_ecclesiastica_gentis_Anglorum" class="mw-redirect" title="Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum">The Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i> by the <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbrian</a> monk <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> in <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;731</span>. The 8th-century poem <i><a href="/wiki/Miracula_Nyniae_Episcopi" title="Miracula Nyniae Episcopi">Miracula Nyniae Episcopi</a></i> records some of the miracles attributed to him. A <i><a href="/wiki/Vita_Sancti_Niniani" title="Vita Sancti Niniani">Life of Saint Ninian</a></i> (<i>Vita Sancti Niniani</i>) was written around 1160 by <a href="/wiki/Ailred_of_Rievaulx" class="mw-redirect" title="Ailred of Rievaulx">Ailred of Rievaulx</a>, and in 1639 <a href="/wiki/James_Ussher" title="James Ussher">James Ussher</a> discusses Ninian in his <i>Brittanicarum Ecclesiarum Antiquitates</i>. These are the sources of information about Ninian of Whithorn, and all provide seemingly innocuous personal details about his life. However, there is no unchallenged historical evidence to support any of their stories, and all sources had political and religious agendas that were served by their accounts of Saint Ninian (discussed below).
</p><p>Tradition holds that Ninian was a <a href="/wiki/Britons_(historical)" class="mw-redirect" title="Britons (historical)">Briton</a> who had studied in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, that he established an episcopal see at the <i><a href="/wiki/Candida_Casa" title="Candida Casa">Candida Casa</a></i> in Whithorn, that he named the see for <a href="/wiki/Martin_of_Tours" title="Martin of Tours">Saint Martin of Tours</a>, that he converted the southern <a href="/wiki/Picts" title="Picts">Picts</a> to <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a>, and that he is buried at Whithorn. Variations of the story add that he had actually met St Martin, that his father was a Christian king, and that he was buried in a stone sarcophagus near the altar of his church. Further variations assert that he left for <a href="/wiki/Ireland" title="Ireland">Ireland</a>, and died there in 432. Dates for his birth are derived from the traditional mention of St Martin, who died in 397.
</p>
<h3><span id="Bede_.28c._731.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Bede_(c._731)">Bede (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;731</span>)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ninian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Bede (c. 731)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Venerable_Bede_translates_John_1902.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/The_Venerable_Bede_translates_John_1902.jpg/220px-The_Venerable_Bede_translates_John_1902.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="206" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/The_Venerable_Bede_translates_John_1902.jpg/330px-The_Venerable_Bede_translates_John_1902.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/78/The_Venerable_Bede_translates_John_1902.jpg 2x" data-file-width="336" data-file-height="314" /></a><figcaption><i><a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">The Venerable Bede</a> translates John</i>, by <a href="/wiki/James_Doyle_Penrose" class="mw-redirect" title="James Doyle Penrose">J. D. Penrose</a>, c. 1902.</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> says that Ninian (whose name he only renders in the ablative case <i>Nynia</i>) was a <a href="/wiki/Britons_(historical)" class="mw-redirect" title="Britons (historical)">Briton</a> who had been instructed in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>; that he made his church of stone, which was unusual among the Britons; that his <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_see" title="Episcopal see">episcopal see</a> was named after <a href="/wiki/Martin_of_Tours" title="Martin of Tours">Saint Martin of Tours</a>; that he preached to and converted the southern <a href="/wiki/Picts" title="Picts">Picts</a>; that his base was called <i>Ad Candidam Casam</i>, which was in the province of the <a href="/wiki/Bernicia" title="Bernicia">Bernicians</a>; and that he was buried there, along with many other saints.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint.Aelred.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Saint.Aelred.jpg/100px-Saint.Aelred.jpg" decoding="async" width="100" height="203" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Saint.Aelred.jpg/150px-Saint.Aelred.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Saint.Aelred.jpg/200px-Saint.Aelred.jpg 2x" data-file-width="888" data-file-height="1800" /></a><figcaption>Saint Ailred (or Aelred), from an 1845 book.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></figcaption></figure>
<h3><span id="Aelred_.28c._1160.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Aelred_(c._1160)">Aelred (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1160</span>)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ninian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Aelred (c. 1160)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Leaving aside the stories regarding miracles, in the <i><a href="/wiki/Vita_Sancti_Niniani" title="Vita Sancti Niniani">Vita Sancti Niniani</a></i> <a href="/wiki/Aelred_of_Rievaulx" title="Aelred of Rievaulx">Aelred</a> includes the following incidental information regarding Saint Ninian: that his father was a Christian king; that he was consecrated a bishop in Rome and that he met Saint Martin in Tours; that Saint Martin sent masons with him on his homeward journey, at his request; that these masons built a church of stone, situated on the shore, and that on learning of Saint Martin's death, Ninian dedicated the church to him; that a certain rich and powerful "King Tuduvallus" was converted by him; that he died after having converted the Picts and returned home, being buried in a stone sarcophagus near the altar of his church; and that he had once travelled with his brother, named "Plebia".<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Aelred said that in addition to finding information about Ninian in Bede, he took much additional information for his <i>Life of S. Ninian</i> from a source written in a "barbarous language"; there is no further information about this text. Aelred wrote his <i>Life of S. Ninian</i> sometime after spending ten years at the Scottish court and thus had close connections both to the Scottish royal family and to <a href="/wiki/Fergus_of_Galloway" title="Fergus of Galloway">Fergus of Galloway</a> (who would resurrect the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Galloway" title="Bishop of Galloway">Bishopric of Galloway</a>), all of whom would have been pleased to have a manuscript with such a glowing description of a Galwegian and Scottish saint. His work is what Thomas Heffernan refers to as a "sacred biography", probably intended for a politically ambitious audience.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Ussher_.281639.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Ussher_(1639)">Ussher (1639)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ninian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Ussher (1639)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:James_Ussher_by_Sir_Peter_Lely.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/James_Ussher_by_Sir_Peter_Lely.jpg/220px-James_Ussher_by_Sir_Peter_Lely.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="263" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/James_Ussher_by_Sir_Peter_Lely.jpg/330px-James_Ussher_by_Sir_Peter_Lely.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/James_Ussher_by_Sir_Peter_Lely.jpg/440px-James_Ussher_by_Sir_Peter_Lely.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2400" data-file-height="2871" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/James_Ussher" title="James Ussher">James Ussher</a>, Archbishop of Armagh, Primate of All Ireland.</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/wiki/James_Ussher" title="James Ussher">James Ussher</a> wrote that Ninian left Candida Casa for <i>Cluayn-coner</i> in Ireland, and eventually died in Ireland; that his mother was a Spanish princess; that his father wished to regain him after having assented to his training for an ecclesiastical state; that a bell comes from heaven to call together his disciples; that a wooden church was raised by him, with beams delivered by stags; and that a <a href="/wiki/Harp" title="Harp">harper</a> with no experience at architecture was the builder of the church. He adds that a smith and his son, named respectively "Terna" and "Wyn", witnessed a miracle by Ninian and that the saint was granted lands to be called "Wytterna".<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In addition, <a href="/wiki/William_Forbes_Skene" title="William Forbes Skene">Skene</a> attributes the "traditional" date of Ninian's death (16 September 432) ultimately to Ussher's <i>Life of Ninian</i>, noting that the date is "without authority".<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Ussher's contribution is often disparaged,<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> as he both invented fictitious histories and misquoted legitimate manuscripts to suit his own purposes.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Still, he had access to legitimate manuscripts, and he has contributed to some versions of the traditional stories.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_sources">Other sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ninian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Other sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Others who wrote of Saint Ninian used the accounts of Bede, Aelred, or Ussher, or used derivatives of them in combination with information from various manuscripts. This includes <a href="/wiki/John_Capgrave" title="John Capgrave">John Capgrave</a> (1393–1464), John of Tinmouth (<abbr title="floruit (&#39;flourished&#39;&#160;– known to have been active at a particular time or during a particular period)">fl.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1366</span></span>), <a href="/wiki/John_Colgan" title="John Colgan">John Colgan</a> (died <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1657</span>), and many others,<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> up to the present day.
</p><p>The anonymously written 8th-century hagiographic <i><a href="/wiki/Miracula_Nyniae_Episcopi" title="Miracula Nyniae Episcopi">Miracula Nynie Episcopi</a></i> (<i>Miracles of Bishop Ninian</i>) is discounted as a non-historical account, and copies are not widely extant.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div style="clear:both;"></div>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Dedications_to_St_Ninian">Dedications to St Ninian</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ninian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Dedications to St Ninian">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St.Ninian.dedications.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/St.Ninian.dedications.jpg/200px-St.Ninian.dedications.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="309" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/St.Ninian.dedications.jpg/300px-St.Ninian.dedications.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/St.Ninian.dedications.jpg/400px-St.Ninian.dedications.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1550" data-file-height="2392" /></a><figcaption>Dedications to St Ninian (England, Scotland, Isle of Man).<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup></figcaption></figure>
<p>Dedications to Saint Ninian are expressions of respect for the good works that are attributed to him, and the authenticity of the stories about him are not relevant to that point. Almost all of the dedications have their origins in the medieval era, after Aelred wrote his account.
</p><p>The dedications are found throughout the lands of the ancient <a href="/wiki/Picts" title="Picts">Picts</a> of Scotland, throughout Scotland south of the Firths of <a href="/wiki/Firth_of_Clyde" title="Firth of Clyde">Clyde</a> and <a href="/wiki/Firth_of_Forth" title="Firth of Forth">Forth</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Orkney" title="Orkney">Orkney</a> and <a href="/wiki/Shetland" title="Shetland">Shetland</a>, and in parts of northern England. <a href="/wiki/St_Ninian%27s_Cathedral" title="St Ninian&#39;s Cathedral">St Ninian's Cathedral</a> is the Episcopal cathedral in <a href="/wiki/Perth,_Scotland" title="Perth, Scotland">Perth</a>. <a href="/wiki/St_Ninian_and_Triduana%27s_Church,_Edinburgh" title="St Ninian and Triduana&#39;s Church, Edinburgh">St Ninian and Triduana's Church, Edinburgh</a> is a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> church dedicated to Ninian.
</p><p>Dedications on the <a href="/wiki/Isle_of_Man" title="Isle of Man">Isle of Man</a> date from the time of medieval Scottish dominance, and are not natively inspired.
</p><p>There are dedications to Ninian in <a href="/wiki/County_Donegal" title="County Donegal">East Donegal</a> and <a href="/wiki/Belfast" title="Belfast">Belfast</a>; and a spot formerly on the shore of <a href="/wiki/Belfast_Lough" title="Belfast Lough">Belfast Lough</a> was traditionally known as St Ninian's Point, where the missionary reputedly landed after a voyage from Scotland. These connections reflect a strong Ulster-Scots heritage in both areas of Ulster.
</p><p>There are also dedications elsewhere in the world where there is a Scottish heritage, such as <a href="/wiki/Nova_Scotia" title="Nova Scotia">Nova Scotia</a>. St Ninian's Cathedral is located in <a href="/wiki/Antigonish" class="mw-redirect" title="Antigonish">Antigonish</a>, Nova Scotia.
</p><p>There is a noticeable lack of dedications in the <a href="/wiki/Scottish_Highlands" title="Scottish Highlands">Scottish Highlands</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hebrides" title="Hebrides">Isles</a>, save for Kilninian and the Holy Spring of St Ninian of the Orthodox Mull Monastery on the Isle of Mull.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the modern era, <a href="/wiki/St_Ninian_and_Triduana%27s_Church,_Edinburgh" title="St Ninian and Triduana&#39;s Church, Edinburgh">St Ninian and Triduana's Church, Edinburgh</a> is a Roman Catholic church constructed in 1932 that is dedicated to Ninian. St Martin and St Ninian is a Catholic church in <a href="/wiki/Whithorn" title="Whithorn">Whithorn</a>, constructed in 1959–60 in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Galloway" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of Galloway">Roman Catholic Diocese of Galloway</a>. The architect was <a href="/wiki/Harry_Stuart_Goodhart-Rendel" title="Harry Stuart Goodhart-Rendel">Harry Stuart Goodhart-Rendel</a> (1887–1959).
</p><p>In Scotland the date 16 September is celebrated as St Ninian's <a href="/wiki/Feast_Day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast Day">Feast Day</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Who_was_Saint_Ninian?_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Who_was_Saint_Ninian?-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Ninian is honoured in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">Church of England</a> and in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church</a> on <a href="/wiki/September_16" title="September 16">16 September</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
