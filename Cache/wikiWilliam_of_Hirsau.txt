</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Wilhelm_Hirsau.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/45/Wilhelm_Hirsau.jpg/220px-Wilhelm_Hirsau.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="292" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/45/Wilhelm_Hirsau.jpg/330px-Wilhelm_Hirsau.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/45/Wilhelm_Hirsau.jpg/440px-Wilhelm_Hirsau.jpg 2x" data-file-width="450" data-file-height="598" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Wilhelm_Hirsau.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>William of Hirsau, from the <a href="/wiki/Cartulary" title="Cartulary">cartulary</a> of <a href="/wiki/Reichenbach_Priory_(Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg)" title="Reichenbach Priory (Baden-Württemberg)">Reichenbach Priory</a></div></div></div>
<p><b>William of Hirsau</b> (or <b>Wilhelm von Hirschau</b>) (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 1030 – 5 July 1091) was a <a href="/wiki/Order_of_St._Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of St. Benedict">Benedictine</a> abbot and monastic reformer. He was abbot of <a href="/wiki/Hirsau_Abbey" title="Hirsau Abbey">Hirsau Abbey</a>, for whom he created the <i>Constitutiones Hirsaugienses</i>, based on the uses of <a href="/wiki/Cluny_Abbey" title="Cluny Abbey">Cluny</a>, and was the father of the <b>Hirsau Reforms</b>, which influenced many Benedictine monasteries in <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a>.  He supported the <a href="/wiki/Papacy" class="mw-redirect" title="Papacy">papacy</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Investiture_controversy" class="mw-redirect" title="Investiture controversy">Investiture Controversy</a>. In the Roman Catholic Church, he is a Blessed, the second of three steps toward recognition as a saint.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Hirsau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>William was born in <a href="/wiki/Bavaria" title="Bavaria">Bavaria</a>, possibly in about 1030; nothing more is known of his origins. As a <i><a href="/wiki/Puer_oblatus" class="mw-redirect" title="Puer oblatus">puer oblatus</a></i> entrusted to the Benedictines he received his education as a monk in <a href="/wiki/St._Emmeram%27s_Abbey" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Emmeram&#39;s Abbey">St. Emmeram's Abbey</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-ott_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ott-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> a private church of the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Regensburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Regensburg">Bishop of Regensburg</a>, where the famous <a href="/wiki/Otloh_of_St._Emmeram" class="mw-redirect" title="Otloh of St. Emmeram">Otloh of St. Emmeram</a> was William's teacher. It is generally believed that it was here that William first became friends with <a href="/wiki/Ulrich_of_Zell" title="Ulrich of Zell">Ulrich of Zell</a> (later distinguished as a <a href="/wiki/Cluniac" class="mw-redirect" title="Cluniac">Cluniac</a> reformer and a saint), a friendship which lasted to the end of his life. William combined personal asceticism with an amiability toward others.<sup id="cite_ref-walz_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-walz-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Activities">Activities</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Hirsau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Activities">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>William became a priest and taught mathematics and astronomy.<sup id="cite_ref-kloster_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kloster-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was a skilled musician and made various improvements on the <a href="/wiki/Flute" title="Flute">flute</a>. About the middle of the 11th century, William composed learned treatises on astronomy and music, disciplines that formed part of the <a href="/wiki/Quadrivium" title="Quadrivium">quadrivium</a>, in the knowledge of which William was considered unsurpassed in his day.
</p><p>He constructed various astronomical instruments, made a <a href="/wiki/Sun-dial" class="mw-redirect" title="Sun-dial">sun-dial</a> which showed the variations of the heavenly bodies, the <a href="/wiki/Solstice" title="Solstice">solstices</a>, <a href="/wiki/Equinox" title="Equinox">equinoxes</a> and other phenomena. His famous stone <a href="/wiki/Astrolabe" title="Astrolabe">astrolabe</a> can still be seen today in <a href="/wiki/Regensburg" title="Regensburg">Regensburg</a>: more than 2.5 metres high, it is engraved on the front with an astrolabe sphere, while on the reverse side is the figure of a man gazing into the heavens, presumed to be the <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greece" title="Ancient Greece">Greek</a> astronomer and poet <a href="/wiki/Aratos_of_Soloi" class="mw-redirect" title="Aratos of Soloi">Aratos of Soloi</a> (of the 3rd century B.C.).
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Abbacy">Abbacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Hirsau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Abbacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Counts of <a href="/wiki/Calw" title="Calw">Calw</a> had dismissed Abbot Friedrich of <a href="/wiki/Hirsau_Abbey" title="Hirsau Abbey">Hirsau Abbey</a>.  as elected successor to the deposed Abbot Frederick. Their distant connection to the <a href="/wiki/List_of_the_prince-bishops_and_bishops_of_Regensburg" class="mw-redirect" title="List of the prince-bishops and bishops of Regensburg">Bishop of Regensburg</a> probably led to William's being sent as successor in May 1069. He immediately took over the management of the monastery, but refused to accept the abbatial benediction till after the death of his unjustly deposed predecessor in 1071.<sup id="cite_ref-ott_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ott-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He was solemnly inaugurated by the Bishop of Speyer on Ascension Day in 1071.
</p><p>In his first years of office he pursued the goal of making the abbey independent of secular powers,<sup id="cite_ref-kloster_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kloster-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> on the basis of the reforms of <a href="/wiki/Gorze_Abbey" title="Gorze Abbey">Gorze Abbey</a> in <a href="/wiki/Duchy_of_Lorraine" title="Duchy of Lorraine">Lorraine</a> and of Cluny, which had begun to take effect some time previously.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> This policy put him in direct opposition to Hirsau's powerful <a href="/wiki/Lay_abbot" title="Lay abbot">lay abbots</a>, the Counts of Calw. A writ of <a href="/wiki/Henry_IV,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Henry IV, Holy Roman Emperor">Emperor Henry IV</a>, probably drafted shortly after 1070, although it created the important link between the abbey and the monarchy, nevertheless largely confirmed the status of Hirsau as a private monastery of the counts.
</p><p>However, a <a href="/wiki/Papal_privilege" class="mw-redirect" title="Papal privilege">privilege</a> of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_VII" title="Pope Gregory VII">Pope Gregory VII</a>, drawn up between 1073 and 1075, put Hirsau under papal protection.
</p><p>William eventually prevailed against Count Adalbert II of Calw, who renounced his lay lordship over the abbey. Henry IV immediately put the monastic community under his own protection, although Hirsau was not made an <a href="/wiki/Imperial_abbey" class="mw-redirect" title="Imperial abbey">imperial abbey</a> directly answerable to the monarch (<i><a href="/wiki/Reichsunmittelbar" class="mw-redirect" title="Reichsunmittelbar">reichsunmittelbar</a></i>). The count received by royal grant the <i><a href="/wiki/Vogt" class="mw-redirect" title="Vogt">Vogtei</a></i> of the abbey. The abbey, by deed of 9 October 1075, received the "complete freedom of the monastery", which included the freedom to elect and invest the abbot, and to elect or dismiss the <i><a href="/wiki/Vogt" class="mw-redirect" title="Vogt">Vogt</a></i>, although it is true that the choice of candidates for the latter position was restricted to the kin of the founder.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:302px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Hirsau_kloster_5.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/Hirsau_kloster_5.jpg/300px-Hirsau_kloster_5.jpg" decoding="async" width="300" height="225" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/Hirsau_kloster_5.jpg/450px-Hirsau_kloster_5.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/Hirsau_kloster_5.jpg/600px-Hirsau_kloster_5.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2048" data-file-height="1536" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Hirsau_kloster_5.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Ruins of Hirsau Abbey</div></div></div>
<p>Under William's abbacy, Hirschau reached the zenith of its glory and, despite the unusually strict monastic discipline which he introduced from Cluny, the number of priest-monks increased from 15 to 150. As the monastery, dedicated to Saint Aurelius, was cramped, over-crowded and subject to flooding, He built a new monastic complex on the opposite side of the <a href="/wiki/Nagold_(river)" title="Nagold (river)">Nagold</a>. There, sometime after 1083, was built the largest monastery complex in Germany of the time, with its great <a href="/wiki/Romanesque_architecture" title="Romanesque architecture">Romanesque</a> church dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Simon_Peter" class="mw-redirect" title="Simon Peter">Saint Peter</a>.
The former site, he converted into a priory. In 1075 William went to Rome to obtain the papal confirmation for the exemption of Hirschau. On this occasion he became acquainted with <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_VII" title="Pope Gregory VII">Pope Gregory VII</a>, with whose efforts towards reforms he was in deep sympathy and whom he afterwards strongly supported in the <a href="/wiki/Investiture_Controversy" title="Investiture Controversy">Investiture Controversy</a> against Henry IV.<sup id="cite_ref-ott_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ott-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Hirsau_Reforms">Hirsau Reforms</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Hirsau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Hirsau Reforms">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>William became the leading agent of the Gregorian reform in Germany.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He introduced to Hirsau, from no later than 1079, a number of <a href="/wiki/Cluniac_Reforms" title="Cluniac Reforms">reforms originating in Cluny</a>, on which he based the "Constitutiones Hirsaugienses" ("Customs of Hirsau"), which later became very widespread as a result of the "Hirsau Reforms".<sup id="cite_ref-walz_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-walz-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> These reforms particularly focused on discipline and obedience, tough punishments for infringements of the rules and continuous supervision of the monks.
</p><p>Parallel with these developments he found it necessary, in order to bring under some sort of control the great numbers of laymen flocking to Hirsau, to create the institution of the <i><a href="/wiki/Lay_brother" title="Lay brother">conversi</a></i> in the German Benedictine monasteries. Before this there were certainly men-servants in the monasteries, but they lived outside the monastery, wore no specifically religious clothing and took no vows.
</p><p>"The monastic reforms he called for in his "Hirsau Reform" became the most influential reforms of the 11th and 12th centuries east of the Rhine river, and served as an example to other monasteries."<sup id="cite_ref-kloster_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kloster-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Many monasteries, perhaps as many as 200, both newly founded and long established, embraced the Hirsau Reforms. New abbeys, settled by monks from Hirsau, included <a href="/wiki/Zwiefalten_Abbey" title="Zwiefalten Abbey">Zwiefalten</a>, <a href="/wiki/Blaubeuren_Abbey" title="Blaubeuren Abbey">Blaubeuren</a>, <a href="/wiki/St._Peter%27s_Abbey_in_the_Black_Forest" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Peter&#39;s Abbey in the Black Forest">St. Peter im Schwarzwald</a> and <a href="/wiki/St._George%27s_Abbey_in_the_Black_Forest" title="St. George&#39;s Abbey in the Black Forest">St. Georgen im Schwarzwald</a> in <a href="/wiki/Swabia" title="Swabia">Swabia</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Reinhardsbrunn" title="Reinhardsbrunn">Reinhardsbrunn</a> in <a href="/wiki/Thuringia" title="Thuringia">Thuringia</a>. Already existing monasteries which accepted the reforms included <a href="/wiki/Petershausen_Abbey" title="Petershausen Abbey">Petershausen</a> near <a href="/wiki/Konstanz" title="Konstanz">Konstanz</a>, <a href="/wiki/Schaffhausen" title="Schaffhausen">Schaffhausen</a>, <a href="/wiki/Comburg" title="Comburg">Comburg</a>, and St. Peter's in <a href="/wiki/Erfurt" title="Erfurt">Erfurt</a>. Finally, there were the priories such as <a href="/wiki/Reichenbach_Priory_(Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg)" title="Reichenbach Priory (Baden-Württemberg)">Reichenbach</a> in <a href="/wiki/Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg" title="Baden-Württemberg">Baden-Württemberg</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sch%C3%B6nrain_Priory" title="Schönrain Priory">Schönrain</a> in <a href="/wiki/Franconia" title="Franconia">Franconia</a> and <a href="/wiki/Fischbachau_Priory" title="Fischbachau Priory">Fischbachau</a> in Bavaria.
</p><p>He also had a standard edition of the <a href="/wiki/Vulgate" title="Vulgate">Vulgate</a> made for all the monasteries of the reform.<sup id="cite_ref-walz_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-walz-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Political_implications">Political implications</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Hirsau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Political implications">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Support for the reforms came primarily from <a href="/wiki/Swabia" title="Swabia">Swabia</a> and <a href="/wiki/Franconia" title="Franconia">Franconia</a>, with a smaller following in Central and East Germany. The spread of the Hirsau Reforms was directly related to the reputation William had acquired through the ecclesio-political propaganda of the Investiture Controversy, as the main support of Pope Gregory's faction in Germany and in Swabia. He was on the side of the <a href="/wiki/Anti-king" title="Anti-king">counter-kings</a> <a href="/wiki/Rudolf_of_Swabia" class="mw-redirect" title="Rudolf of Swabia">Rudolf of Swabia</a> (1077–1080) and <a href="/wiki/Herman_of_Luxemburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Herman of Luxemburg">Herman of Luxemburg</a>, Count of Salm (1081–1088). Among other things, the tenacity of the Gregorian party in south-west Germany was due to him, quite apart from the reputation of Hirsau Abbey among ecclesiastical reformers.
</p><p>William died on 5 July 1091 and was buried in the abbey church.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Hirsau&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Besides composing the <i>Constitutiones Hirsaugienses</i><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> William of Hirsau was the author of the treatises "De astronomia", of which only the prologue is printed<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and "De musica".<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His life is recorded in the <i>Vita Willihelmi abbatis Hirsaugiensis</i>.
</p><p>William of Hirsau is commemorated by the Benedictines on 5 July.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
