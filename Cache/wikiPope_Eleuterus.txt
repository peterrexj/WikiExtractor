<p><b>Pope Eleutherius</b> (died 24 May 189), also known as <b>Eleutherus</b>, was the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Rome">bishop of Rome</a> from c. 174 to his death.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKirsch1909_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKirsch1909-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> His pontificate is alternatively dated to 171-185 or 177-193.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> He is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>.
</p><p>He is linked to a number of legends, one of them credited him with receiving a letter from "<a href="/wiki/Lucius_of_Britain" title="Lucius of Britain">Lucius</a>, <a href="/wiki/List_of_legendary_kings_of_Britain" title="List of legendary kings of Britain">King of Britain</a>", but which is now generally considered to be a <a href="/wiki/Forgery" title="Forgery">forgery</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Eleutherius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to the <i><a href="/wiki/Liber_Pontificalis" title="Liber Pontificalis">Liber Pontificalis</a></i>, he was an <a href="/wiki/Albanians" title="Albanians">Albanian</a> born in <a href="/wiki/Nicopolis" title="Nicopolis">Nicopolis</a> in <a href="/wiki/Epirus" title="Epirus">Epirus</a>, <a href="/wiki/Greece" title="Greece">Greece</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrusher1980_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrusher1980-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButlerAttwaterThurston1956_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButlerAttwaterThurston1956-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His contemporary <a href="/wiki/Hegesippus_(chronicler)" title="Hegesippus (chronicler)">Hegesippus</a> wrote that he was a <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a> of the Roman Church under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Anicetus" title="Pope Anicetus">Pope Anicetus</a> (c. 154–164), and remained so under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Soter" title="Pope Soter">Pope Soter</a>, whom he succeeded around 174.<sup id="cite_ref-Hegesippus_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hegesippus-4">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Dietary_law">Dietary law</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Eleutherius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Dietary law">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The 6th-century <a href="/wiki/Recension" title="Recension">recension</a> of <i>Liber Pontificalis</i> ('Book of the Popes') known as the "Felician Catalog"<sup id="cite_ref-Felician_Catalog_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Felician_Catalog-5">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> includes additional commentary to the work's earlier entry on Eleutherius. One addition ascribes to Eleutherius the reissuance of a decree:<sup id="cite_ref-Et_hoc_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Et_hoc-6">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDavis19896_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavis19896-7">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> "And he again affirmed that no food should be repudiated by Christians strong in their faith, as God created it, [provided] however that it is sensible and edible." Such a decree might have been issued against early continuations of <a href="/wiki/Jewish_dietary_law" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish dietary law">Jewish dietary law</a> and against similar laws practiced by the <a href="/wiki/Gnosticism" title="Gnosticism">Gnostics</a> and <a href="/wiki/Montanism" title="Montanism">Montanists</a>. It is also possible, however, that the editor of the passage attributed to Eleutherius a decree similar to another issued around the year 500 in order to give it greater authority.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="British_mission">British mission</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Eleutherius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: British mission">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Lucius_of_Britain" title="Lucius of Britain">Lucius of Britain</a></div>
<p>Another addition credited Eleutherius with receiving a letter from "<a href="/wiki/Lucius_of_Britain" title="Lucius of Britain">Lucius</a>, <a href="/wiki/List_of_legendary_kings_of_Britain" title="List of legendary kings of Britain">King of Britain</a>" or "<a href="/wiki/King_of_the_Britons" title="King of the Britons">King of the Britons</a>", declaring an intention to convert to Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-Lucius_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lucius-8">&#91;d&#93;</a></sup> No earlier accounts of this mission have been found. It is now generally considered to be a <a href="/wiki/Pious_forgery" class="mw-redirect" title="Pious forgery">pious forgery</a>, although there remains disagreement over its original purpose. <a href="/wiki/Arthur_West_Haddan" title="Arthur West Haddan">Haddan</a>, <a href="/wiki/William_Stubbs" title="William Stubbs">Stubbs</a>, and Wilkins<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHaddanStubbsWilkins186925_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaddanStubbsWilkins186925-9">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> considered the passage "manifestly written in the time and tone" of <a href="/wiki/Prosper_of_Aquitaine" title="Prosper of Aquitaine">Prosper of Aquitaine</a>, secretary to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Pope Leo the Great">Pope Leo the Great</a> in the mid-5th century, and supportive of the <a href="/wiki/Mission_(Christianity)" class="mw-redirect" title="Mission (Christianity)">missions</a> of <a href="/wiki/Germanus_of_Auxerre" title="Germanus of Auxerre">Germanus of Auxerre</a> and <a href="/wiki/Palladius_(bishop_of_Ireland)" title="Palladius (bishop of Ireland)">Palladius</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHaddanStubbsWilkins186925_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaddanStubbsWilkins186925-9">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Louis-Marie-Olivier_Duchesne" class="mw-redirect" title="Louis-Marie-Olivier Duchesne">Duchesne</a> dated the entry a little later to the pontificate of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_II" title="Pope Boniface II">Boniface&#160;II</a> around 530,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKirsch1909_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKirsch1909-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> and <a href="/wiki/Christian_Matthias_Theodor_Mommsen" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian Matthias Theodor Mommsen">Mommsen</a> to the early 7th century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKirsch1909_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKirsch1909-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Only the last would support the conjecture that it aimed to support the <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_mission" title="Gregorian mission">Gregorian mission</a> to the <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxons</a> led by <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury" title="Augustine of Canterbury">Augustine of Canterbury</a>, who <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_mission#Relations_with_the_British_Christians" title="Gregorian mission">encountered great difficulty</a> with the <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">native British Christians</a>, as at the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Chester" title="Synod of Chester">Synod of Chester</a>. Indeed, the Celtic Christians invoked the antiquity of their church to generally <em>avoid</em> submission to <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Canterbury</a> until the <a href="/wiki/Norman_Conquest" title="Norman Conquest">Norman conquest</a>, but no arguments invoking the mission to Lucius appear to have been made by either side during the <a href="/wiki/Synod" title="Synod">synods</a> among the Welsh and Saxon bishops.
</p><p>The first Englishman to mention the story was <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBede1903Bk_I,_Ch_4_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBede1903Bk_I,_Ch_4-10">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBede1903Bk_V,_Ch_24_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBede1903Bk_V,_Ch_24-11">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and he seems to have taken it, not from native texts or traditions, but from <i><a href="/wiki/Liber_Pontificalis" title="Liber Pontificalis">The Book of the Popes</a></i>. Subsequently, it appeared in the 9th-century <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_Brittonum" title="Historia Brittonum">History of the Britons</a></i> traditionally credited to <a href="/wiki/Nennius" title="Nennius">Nennius</a>: The account relates that a mission from the pope baptised "Lucius, the Britannic king, with all the petty kings of the whole Britannic people".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENennius1848§22_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENennius1848§22-12">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The account, however, dates this baptism to AD&#160;167 (a little before Eleutherius's pontificate) and credits it to <a href="/wiki/Evaristus" class="mw-redirect" title="Evaristus">Evaristus</a> (reigned <span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;99</span>&#160;– c.<span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;107</span>).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENennius1848§22_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENennius1848§22-12">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In the 12th century, more details began to be added to the story. <a href="/wiki/Geoffrey_of_Monmouth" title="Geoffrey of Monmouth">Geoffrey of Monmouth</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Pseudo-history" class="mw-redirect" title="Pseudo-history">pseudohistorical</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_Regnum_Britanniae" class="mw-redirect" title="Historia Regnum Britanniae">History of the Kings of Britain</a></i> goes into great detail concerning Lucius and names the pope's envoys to him as <a href="/wiki/Saint_Fagan" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Fagan">Fagan</a> and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Duvian" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Duvian">Duvian</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGeoffrey_of_Monmouth1848Vol.&amp;nbsp;IV,_Ch.&amp;nbsp;XIX_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGeoffrey_of_Monmouth1848Vol.&amp;nbsp;IV,_Ch.&amp;nbsp;XIX-13">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The 12th-century <i><a href="/wiki/Liber_Landavensis" class="mw-redirect" title="Liber Landavensis">Book of Llandaf</a></i> placed the court of Lucius in southern Wales and names his emissaries to the pope as <a href="/w/index.php?title=Elfan&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Elfan (page does not exist)">Elfan</a> and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Medwy&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Medwy (page does not exist)">Medwy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERees184026,_65_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERees184026,_65-14">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>An echo of this legend penetrated even to <a href="/wiki/Switzerland" title="Switzerland">Switzerland</a>. In a homily preached at <a href="/wiki/Chur" title="Chur">Chur</a> and preserved in an 8th- or 9th-century manuscript, <a href="/wiki/Saint_Timothy" title="Saint Timothy">Timothy</a> is represented as an <a href="/wiki/Apostle" title="Apostle">apostle</a> to <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a>, whence he went into <a href="/wiki/Roman_Britain" title="Roman Britain">Roman Britain</a> and baptised a king named Lucius, who himself became a missionary to Gaul and finally settled at Chur, where he preached the gospel with great success. In this way Lucius, the early missionary of the Swiss district of Chur, became identified with the alleged British king of the <i>Liber Pontificalis</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-Elsensohn_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Elsensohn-15">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Adolf_von_Harnack" title="Adolf von Harnack">Harnack</a> suggests that in the document which the compiler of the <i>Liber Pontificalis</i> drew his information, the name found was not <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Britanio</i></span></i>, but <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Britio</i></span></i>. Now this is the name (<i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la"><a href="/wiki/Birecik" title="Birecik">Birtha</a>-</i></span></i>, <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Britium</i></span></i>) of the fortress of <a href="/wiki/Osroene" title="Osroene">Edessa</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEvon_Harnack1904906–916_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEvon_Harnack1904906–916-16">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> The king in question is, therefore, Lucius Ælius Septimus Megas <a href="/wiki/Abgar_VIII" title="Abgar VIII">Abgar VIII</a>, of Edessa, a Christian king as is well known. The original statement of the <i>Liber Pontificalis</i>, in this hypothesis, had nothing to do with Britain; the compiler of the <i>Liber Pontificalis</i> changed <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Britio</i></span></i> to <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Brittanio</i></span></i>, and in this way made a British king of the <a href="/wiki/History_of_Syria#Medieval_era" title="History of Syria">Syrian</a> Lucius.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Eleutherius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to the <i>Liber Pontificalis</i>, Pope Eleutherius died on 24 May and was buried on the <a href="/wiki/Vatican_Hill" title="Vatican Hill">Vatican Hill</a> (<i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">in Vaticano</i></span></i>) near the body of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Peter" title="Saint Peter">Peter the Apostle</a>. Later tradition has his body moved to the church of <a href="/wiki/San_Giovanni_della_Pigna" class="mw-redirect" title="San Giovanni della Pigna">San Giovanni della Pigna</a>, near the <a href="/wiki/Pantheon,_Rome" title="Pantheon, Rome">pantheon</a>. In 1591, his remains were again moved to the church of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Susanna" class="mw-redirect" title="Santa Susanna">Santa Susanna</a> at the request of Camilla Peretti, the sister of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Sixtus_V" title="Pope Sixtus V">Pope Sixtus V</a>. His feast is celebrated on 26 May.
</p>
