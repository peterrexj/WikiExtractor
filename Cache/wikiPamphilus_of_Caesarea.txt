<p><b>Saint Pamphilus</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Πάμφιλος</span>; latter half of the 3rd century &#8211; February 16, 309 AD), was a <a href="/wiki/Presbyter" title="Presbyter">presbyter</a> of <a href="/wiki/Caesarea_Maritima#Christian_hub" title="Caesarea Maritima">Caesarea</a> and chief among the biblical scholars of his generation. He was the friend and teacher of <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea" class="mw-redirect" title="Eusebius of Caesarea">Eusebius of Caesarea</a>, who recorded details of his career in a three-book <i>Vita</i> that has been lost.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pamphilus_of_Caesarea&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Eusebius' <i>Martyrs of Palestine</i> attests that Pamphilus was of a rich and honorable family of <a href="/wiki/Beirut" title="Beirut">Beirut</a>. This work also asserts that he gave all his property to the poor and attached himself to the "perfect men". <a href="/wiki/Photios_I_of_Constantinople" title="Photios I of Constantinople">Photius</a><sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> quotes Pamphilus's <i>Apology for Origen</i> to the effect that Pamphilus went to <a href="/wiki/Alexandria" title="Alexandria">Alexandria</a>, where his teacher was <a href="/wiki/Pierius" title="Pierius">Pierius</a>, the head of the famous <a href="/wiki/Catechetical_school" class="mw-redirect" title="Catechetical school">catechetical school</a> there, before settling in <a href="/wiki/Caesarea_Maritima" title="Caesarea Maritima">Caesarea Maritima</a>, where he was ordained a <a href="/wiki/Priest" title="Priest">priest</a>. In <a href="/wiki/Alexandria" title="Alexandria">Alexandria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a>, Pamphilus became devoted to the works of <a href="/wiki/Origen_of_Alexandria" class="mw-redirect" title="Origen of Alexandria">Origen of Alexandria</a>. Photius says that Pamphilus was a Phoenician born at Berytus, and a scholar of Pierius, who collected sacred literature. According to Eusebius, he suffered martyrdom in the third year of the <a href="/wiki/Diocletianic_persecution" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocletianic persecution">Diocletianic persecution</a>, after spending two years in prison. While he was in prison, Pamphilus and Eusebius worked together on five books in defense of Origen.<sup id="cite_ref-Translated1955_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Translated1955-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Diocletianic persecution began in earnest in the year 303. In 306 a young man named Apphianus—a disciple of Pamphilus "while no one was aware; he even concealed it from us who were even in the same house"<sup id="cite_ref-Martyrs_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Martyrs-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>—interrupted the governor in the act of offering sacrifice, and paid for his boldness with <a href="/wiki/Martyrdom" class="mw-redirect" title="Martyrdom">martyrdom</a>. His brother Aedesius, also a disciple of Pamphilus, suffered martyrdom about the same time at Alexandria under similar circumstances.<sup id="cite_ref-Martyrs_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Martyrs-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Saint Pamphilus's turn came in November, 307. He was brought before Urbanus, the governor of Palestine,<sup id="cite_ref-Saints_p._212_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Saints_p._212-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and upon refusing to offer sacrifice, was cruelly tortured, and then relegated to prison. In prison he continued copying and correcting manuscripts. He also composed, in collaboration with Eusebius, also imprisoned,<sup id="cite_ref-Saints_p._212_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Saints_p._212-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> an <i>Apology for Origen</i> in five books, which Eusebius edited and to which he added a sixth book. Saint Pamphilus and other members of his household, along with Valens, deacon of the Church of Jerusalem and Paul of Jamnia,<sup id="cite_ref-Saints_p._212_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Saints_p._212-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> men "in the full vigour of mind and body", were without further torture sentenced to be beheaded in February, 309. While sentence was being given a youth named Porphyrius - "the slave of Pamphilus", "the beloved disciple of Pamphilus", who "had been instructed in literature and writing" – demanded the bodies of the confessors for burial. He was cruelly tortured and put to death, the news of his martyrdom being brought to Pamphilus before his own execution. Nearly at the same time another of his companions, Patriklos, suffered a martyr death in Caesarea and was later interred after the payment of a ransom to Diocletian in Cappadocia.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pamphilus_of_Caesarea&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>St Pamphilus is regarded as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Churches</a>. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is celebrated on 16 February (the RC Church lists an additional commemoration on June 1).
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works_and_influence">Works and influence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pamphilus_of_Caesarea&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Works and influence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<ul><li>Of the <i>Apology for Origen</i> only the first book is extant, in a Latin version made by <a href="/wiki/Tyrannius_Rufinus" title="Tyrannius Rufinus">Rufinus</a>. It begins with describing the extravagant bitterness of the feeling against Origen. He was a man of deep humility, of great authority in the Church of his day, and honoured with the priesthood. He was above all things anxious to keep to the rule of faith that had come down from the <a href="/wiki/Apostles_in_the_New_Testament" title="Apostles in the New Testament">Apostles</a>. The soundness of his doctrine concerning the <a href="/wiki/Trinity" title="Trinity">Trinity</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Incarnation" title="Incarnation">Incarnation</a> is then vindicated by copious extracts from his writings. Then nine charges against his teaching are confronted with passages from his works. <a href="/wiki/Saint_Jerome" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Jerome">Saint Jerome</a> stated in his <i>De Viris illustribus</i> that there were two apologies&#8212;one by Pamphilus and another by Eusebius. He discovered his mistake when Rufinus's translation appeared in the height of the controversy over Origen, and rushed to the conclusion that Eusebius was the sole author. He charged Rufinus, among other things, with palming off under the name of the martyr what was really the work of the heterodox Eusebius, and with suppressing unorthodox passages. As to the first accusation there is abundant evidence that the <i>Apology</i> was the joint work of Pamphilus and Eusebius. Against the second may be set the negative testimony of Photius who had read the original; "Photius, who was severe to excess towards the slightest semblance of <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a>, remarked no such taint in the Apology of Origen which he had read in Greek."<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Canon_law" title="Canon law">canons</a> of the alleged Council of the Apostles at Antioch were ascribed by their compiler (late fourth century) to Pamphilus.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>The ascription to Pamphilus, by <a href="/w/index.php?title=Gemmadius&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Gemmadius (page does not exist)">Gemmadius</a>, of a treatise <i>Contra mathematicos</i> was a blunder due to a misunderstanding of Rufinus's preface to the "Apology".<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>A <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Summary_of_the_Acts_of_the_Apostles&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Summary of the Acts of the Apostles (page does not exist)">Summary of the Acts of the Apostles</a></i> among the writings associated with <a href="/wiki/Euthalius" title="Euthalius">Euthalius</a> bears in its inscription the name of Pamphilus.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>While defending Origen from accusations that he held and taught certain <a href="/wiki/Heterodoxy" title="Heterodoxy">heterodox</a> doctrines, Pamphilus endorses <a href="/wiki/Universal_reconciliation" class="mw-redirect" title="Universal reconciliation">universal reconciliation</a> as the orthodox understanding of <i><a href="/wiki/Apokatastasis" class="mw-redirect" title="Apokatastasis">apokatastasis</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Late 19th-century universalist historians <a href="/wiki/Hosea_Ballou_II" title="Hosea Ballou II">Hosea Ballou II</a> and <a href="/wiki/J._W._Hanson" class="mw-redirect" title="J. W. Hanson">J. W. Hanson</a> have cited Pamphilius, among other Fathers of the Church, as an example of early <a href="/wiki/Christian_universalism" title="Christian universalism">Christian universalism</a>, although the exact conception of <i>apokatastasis</i> in patristic writings generally, and Origenian thought in particular, is disputed among historical theologians.</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/David_Hume" title="David Hume">David Hume</a> adopted the evocative pseudonym Pamphilus for his <i><a href="/wiki/Dialogues_Concerning_Natural_Religion" title="Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion">Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion</a></i>.</li></ul>
