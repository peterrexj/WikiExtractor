<p>The <b>Archdiocese of Lyon</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Archidiœcesis Lugdunensis</i>; <a href="/wiki/French_language" title="French language">French</a>: <i lang="fr">Archidiocèse de Lyon</i>), formerly the <b>Archdiocese of Lyon–Vienne–Embrun</b>, is a <a href="/wiki/Metropolis_(religious_jurisdiction)" title="Metropolis (religious jurisdiction)">metropolitan</a> <a href="/wiki/Archdiocese" class="mw-redirect" title="Archdiocese">archdiocese</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Church" title="Latin Church">Latin Church</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_France" title="Catholic Church in France">Catholic Church in France</a>. The archbishops of Lyon serve as successors to Saints <a href="/wiki/Saint_Pothinus" title="Saint Pothinus">Pothinus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Irenaeus" title="Irenaeus">Irenaeus</a>, the first and second <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishops</a> of <a href="/wiki/Lyon" title="Lyon">Lyon</a>, respectively,<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and are also called <a href="/wiki/Primate_of_Gaul" class="mw-redirect" title="Primate of Gaul">primates of Gaul</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-primate_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-primate-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He is usually elevated to the rank of <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_(Catholic_Church)" title="Cardinal (Catholic Church)">cardinal</a>. Bishop <a href="/wiki/Olivier_de_Germay" title="Olivier de Germay">Olivier de Germay</a> was appointed archbishop on 22 October 2020.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Persecution">Persecution</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Persecution">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The "Deacon of Vienne", who was martyred at <a href="/wiki/Lyon" title="Lyon">Lyon</a> during the persecution of 177, was probably a deacon installed at <a href="/wiki/Vienne" title="Vienne">Vienne</a> by the ecclesiastical authority of Lyon. The confluence of the Rhône and the Saône, where sixty Gallic tribes had erected the famous altar to Rome and <a href="/wiki/Augustus" title="Augustus">Augustus</a>, was also the centre from which Christianity was gradually propagated throughout Gaul. The presence at Lyon of numerous Asiatic Christians and their almost daily communications with the <a href="/wiki/Orient" title="Orient">Orient</a> were likely to arouse the susceptibilities of the Gallo-Romans. A persecution arose under <a href="/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius" title="Marcus Aurelius">Marcus Aurelius</a>. Its victims at Lyon numbered forty-eight, half of them of <a href="/wiki/Greeks" title="Greeks">Greek</a> origin, half Gallo-Roman, among others <a href="/wiki/Saint_Blandina" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Blandina">Saint Blandina</a>, and Saint Pothinus, first Bishop of Lyon, sent to Gaul by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Polycarp" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Polycarp">Saint Polycarp</a> about the middle of the 2nd century. The legend according to which he was sent by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Pope Clement I">Saint Clement</a> dates from the 12th century and is without foundation. The letter addressed to the Christians of Asia and Phrygia in the name of the faithful of Vienne and Lyon, and relating the persecution of 177, is considered by <a href="/wiki/Ernest_Renan" title="Ernest Renan">Ernest Renan</a> as one of the most extraordinary documents possessed by any literature; it is the baptismal certificate of Christianity in France. The successor of Saint Pothinus was the illustrious Saint Irenaeus (177-202).<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The discovery on the Hill of Saint Sebastian of ruins of a <a href="/wiki/Naumachia" title="Naumachia">naumachia</a> capable of being transformed into an amphitheatre, and of some fragments of inscriptions apparently belonging to an altar of Augustus, has led several archæologists to believe that the martyrs of Lyon suffered death on this hill. Very ancient tradition, however, represents the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint-Martin_d%27Ainay" title="Basilica of Saint-Martin d&#39;Ainay">church of Ainay</a> as erected at the place of their martyrdom. The crypt of Saint Pothinus, under the choir of the church of St. Nizier, was destroyed in 1884. But there are still revered at Lyon the prison cell of Saint Pothinus, where <a href="/wiki/Anne_of_Austria" title="Anne of Austria">Anne of Austria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Louis_XIV" title="Louis XIV">Louis XIV</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Pius_VII" class="mw-redirect" title="Pius VII">Pius VII</a> came to pray, and the crypt of Saint Irenaeus built at the end of the 5th century by Saint Patiens, which contains the body of Saint Irenaeus. There are numerous funerary inscriptions of primitive Christianity in Lyon; the earliest dates from the year 334. In the 2nd and 3rd centuries, the See of Lyon enjoyed great renown throughout Gaul: witness the local legends of Besançon and of several other cities relative to the missionaries sent out by Saint Irenaeus. Faustinus, bishop in the second half of the 3rd century, wrote to <a href="/wiki/Cyprian" title="Cyprian">Saint Cyprian</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pope_Stephen_I" title="Pope Stephen I">Pope Stephen I</a>, in 254, regarding the <a href="/wiki/Novatian" title="Novatian">Novatian</a> tendencies of Marcian, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Arles" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Arles">Bishop of Arles</a>. But when <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a>'s new provincial organization (<a href="/wiki/Tetrarchy" title="Tetrarchy">tetrarchy</a>) had taken away from Lyon its position as metropolis of the three Gauls, the prestige of Lyon diminished for a time.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Merovingian_period">Merovingian period</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Merovingian period">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>At the end of the empire and during the <a href="/wiki/Merovingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingian">Merovingian</a> period several saints, as follows, are counted among the Bishops of Lyon. <a href="/wiki/Justus_of_Lyon" title="Justus of Lyon">Saint Justus</a> (374-381) who died in a monastery in the <a href="/wiki/Thebaid" title="Thebaid">Thebaid</a> (<a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a>) and was renowned for the orthodoxy of his doctrine in the struggle against <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a> (the church of the Maccabees, whither his body was brought, was as early as the 5th century a place of pilgrimage under the name of the collegiate church of Saint Justus). Saint Alpinus and Saint Martin (disciple of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Martin_of_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Martin of Tours">Saint Martin of Tours</a>; end of 4th century); Saint Antiochus (400-410); Saint Elpidius (410-422); Saint Sicarius (422-33); <a href="/wiki/Eucherius_of_Lyon" title="Eucherius of Lyon">Saint Eucherius</a> (c. 433-50), a monk of <a href="/wiki/L%C3%A9rins_Abbey" title="Lérins Abbey">Lérins</a> and the author of homilies, from whom doubtless dates the foundation at Lyon of the "hermitages" of which more will be said below; Saint Patiens (456-98) who successfully combated the famine and Arianism, and whom <a href="/wiki/Sidonius_Apollinaris" title="Sidonius Apollinaris">Sidonius Apollinaris</a> praised in a poem; <a href="/wiki/Lupicinus_of_Lyon" title="Lupicinus of Lyon">Saint Lupicinus</a> (491-94); <a href="/wiki/Saint_Rusticus_(Archbishop_of_Lyon)" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Rusticus (Archbishop of Lyon)">Saint Rusticus</a> (494-501); Saint Stephanus (d. before 515), who with Saint <a href="/wiki/Avitus_of_Vienne" title="Avitus of Vienne">Avitus of Vienne</a> convoked a council at Lyon for the conversion of the Arians; <a href="/wiki/Saint_Viventiolus" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Viventiolus">Saint Viventiolus</a> (515-523), who in 517 presided with Saint Avitus at the Council of Epaone; Saint Lupus, a monk, afterwards bishop (535-42), probably the first archbishop, who when signing in 438 the Council of Orléans added the title of "metropolitanus"; Saint Sardot or <a href="/wiki/Sacerdos_of_Lyon" title="Sacerdos of Lyon">Sacerdos</a> (549-542), who presided in 549 at the Council of Orléans, and who obtained from King Childebert the foundation of the general hospital; <a href="/wiki/Nicetius_of_Lyon" title="Nicetius of Lyon">Saint Nicetius</a> or Nizier (552-73), who received from the pope the title of patriarch, and whose tomb was honoured by miracles. The prestige of Saint Nicetius was lasting; his successor Saint Priseus (573-588) bore the title of <a href="/wiki/Patriarch" title="Patriarch">patriarch</a>, and brought the council of 585 to decide that national synods should be convened every three years at the instance of the patriarch and of the king; Saint Ætherius (588-603), who was a correspondent of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Gregory_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Gregory the Great">Saint Gregory the Great</a> and who perhaps consecrated <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury" title="Augustine of Canterbury">Saint Augustine</a>, the Apostle of England; <a href="/wiki/Saint_Aredius" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Aredius">Saint Aredius</a> (603-615); <a href="/wiki/Annemund" title="Annemund">Saint Annemundus</a> or Chamond (c. 650), friend of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Wilfrid" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Wilfrid">Saint Wilfrid</a>, godfather of <a href="/wiki/Clotaire_III" class="mw-redirect" title="Clotaire III">Clotaire III</a>, put to death by <a href="/wiki/Ebroin" title="Ebroin">Ebroin</a> together with his brother, and patron of the town of <a href="/wiki/Saint-Chamond,_Loire" title="Saint-Chamond, Loire">Saint-Chamond, Loire</a>; <a href="/wiki/Genesius_of_Lyon" title="Genesius of Lyon">Saint Genesius</a> or Genes (660-679 or 680), Benedictine abbot of <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Wandrille" title="Abbey of Saint Wandrille">Fontenelle</a>, <a href="/wiki/Grand_almoner" class="mw-redirect" title="Grand almoner">grand almoner</a> and minister of Queen <a href="/wiki/Balthild_of_Chelles" title="Balthild of Chelles">Bathilde</a>; Saint Lambertus (c. 680-690), also abbot of Fontenelle.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At the end of the 5th century Lyon was the capital of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Burgundy" title="Kingdom of Burgundy">Kingdom of Burgundy</a>, but after 534 it passed under the domination of the kings of France. Ravaged by the Saracens in 725, the city was restored through the liberality of <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a> who established a rich library in the monastery of Ile Barbe. In the time of Saint Patiens and the priest Constans (d. 488) the school of Lyon was famous; Sidonius Apollinaris was educated there. The letter of Leidrade to Charlemagne (807) shows the care taken by the emperor for the restoration of learning in Lyon. With the aid of the deacon <a href="/wiki/Florus_of_Lyon" title="Florus of Lyon">Florus</a> he made the school so prosperous that in the 10th century Englishmen went there to study.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Carolingian_period">Carolingian period</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Carolingian period">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Under Charlemagne and his immediate successors, the Bishops of Lyon, whose ascendancy was attested by the number of councils over which they were called to preside, played an important theological part. <a href="/wiki/Adoptionism" title="Adoptionism">Adoptionism</a> had no more active enemies than <a href="/wiki/Leidrade" class="mw-redirect" title="Leidrade">Leidrade</a> (798-814) and <a href="/wiki/Agobard" title="Agobard">Agobard</a> (814-840). When <a href="/wiki/Felix_of_Urgel" class="mw-redirect" title="Felix of Urgel">Felix of Urgel</a> continued rebellious to the condemnations pronounced against <a href="/wiki/Adoptionism" title="Adoptionism">adoptionism</a> from 791-799 by the Councils of Ciutad, Friuli, Ratisbon, Frankfort, and Rome, Charlemagne conceived the idea of sending to Urgel with <a href="/wiki/Nebridius" title="Nebridius">Nebridius</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Narbonne" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Narbonne">Bishop of Narbonne</a>, <a href="/wiki/Benedict_of_Aniane" title="Benedict of Aniane">Benedict of Aniane</a>, and Archbishop Leidrade, a native of Nuremberg and Charlemagne's librarian. They preached against Adoptionism in Spain, conducted Felix in 799 to the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Aachen_(799)" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Aachen (799)">Council of Aachen</a> where he seemed to submit to the arguments of Alcuin, and then brought him back to his diocese. But the submission of Felix was not complete; Agobard, "Chorepiscopus" of Lyon, convicted him anew of adoptionism in a secret conference, and when Felix died in 815 there was found among his papers a treatise in which he professed adoptionism. Then Agobard, who had become Archbishop of Lyon in 814 after Leidrade's retirement to the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_St._Medard,_Soissons" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of St. Medard, Soissons">Abbey of St. Medard, Soissons</a>, composed a long treatise against that heresy.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Agobard">Agobard</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Agobard">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><a href="/wiki/Agobard" title="Agobard">Agobard</a> displayed great activity as a pastor and a publicist in his opposition to the <a href="/wiki/Jews" title="Jews">Jews</a> and to various superstitions. His rooted hatred for all <a href="/wiki/Superstition" title="Superstition">superstition</a> led him in his treatise on images into certain expressions which savoured of Iconoclasm. The five historical treatises which he wrote in 833 to justify the deposition of <a href="/wiki/Louis_the_Pious" title="Louis the Pious">Louis the Pious</a>, who had been his benefactor, are a stain on his life. Louis the Pious, having been restored to power, caused Agobard to be deposed in 835 by the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Thionville" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Thionville">Council of Thionville</a>, but three years later gave him back his see, in which he died in 840. During the exile of Agobard the See of Lyon had been for a short time administered by <a href="/wiki/Amalarius_of_Metz" class="mw-redirect" title="Amalarius of Metz">Amalarius of Metz</a>, whom the deacon Florus charged with heretical opinions regarding the "triforme corpus Christi", and who took part in the controversies with <a href="/wiki/Gottschalk_of_Orbais" title="Gottschalk of Orbais">Gottschalk</a> on the subject of predestination.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Amolon (841-852) and Saint Remy (852-75) continued the struggle against the heresy of Valence, which condemned this heresy, and also was engaged in strife with <a href="/wiki/Hincmar" title="Hincmar">Hincmar</a>. From 879-1032 Lyon formed part of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Provence" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of Provence">Kingdom of Provence</a> and afterwards of the <a href="/wiki/Second_Kingdom_of_Burgundy" class="mw-redirect" title="Second Kingdom of Burgundy">second Kingdom of Burgundy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> In 1032 <a href="/wiki/Rudolph_III_of_Burgundy" title="Rudolph III of Burgundy">Rudolph III of Burgundy</a> died and his kingdom eventually went to <a href="/wiki/Conrad_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Conrad II, Holy Roman Emperor">Conrad II</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPrevité-Orton191233–36_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrevité-Orton191233–36-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The portion of Lyon situated on the left bank of the Saône became, at least nominally, an imperial city. Finally Archbishop Burchard II, brother of Rudolph,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPrevité-Orton191210_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrevité-Orton191210-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> claimed rights of sovereignty over Lyon as inherited from his mother, Matilda, daughter of <a href="/wiki/Louis_IV_of_France" title="Louis IV of France">Louis IV of France</a>; in this way the government of Lyon, instead of being exercised by the distant emperor, became a matter of dispute between the counts who claimed the inheritance and the successive archbishops.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Lyon attracted the attention of <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_Hildebrand" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinal Hildebrand">Cardinal Hildebrand</a>, who held a council there in 1055 against the simoniacal bishops. In 1076, as Gregory VII, he deposed Archbishop Humbert (1063–76) for <a href="/wiki/Simony" title="Simony">simony</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Saint_Gebuin" title="Saint Gebuin">Saint Gebuin</a> (Jubinus), who succeeded Humbert, was the confidant of Gregory VII and contributed to the reform of the Church by the two councils of 1080 and 1082, at which were excommunicated <a href="/wiki/Manasses_I,_Archbishop_of_Reims" class="mw-redirect" title="Manasses I, Archbishop of Reims">Manasses of Reims</a>, Fulk of Anjou, and the monks of Marmoutiers.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It was under the episcopate of Saint Gebuin that Gregory VII (20 April 1079) established the primacy of the Church of Lyon over the Provinces of Rouen, Tours, and Sens, which primacy was specially confirmed by <a href="/wiki/Callistus_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Callistus II">Callistus II</a>, despite the letter written to him in 1126 by <a href="/wiki/Louis_VI_of_France" title="Louis VI of France">Louis VI</a> in favour of the church of <a href="/wiki/Sens" title="Sens">Sens</a>. As far as it regarded the Province of <a href="/wiki/Rouen" title="Rouen">Rouen</a> this letter was later suppressed by a decree of the king's council in 1702, at the request of <a href="/wiki/Jacques-Nicolas_Colbert" class="mw-redirect" title="Jacques-Nicolas Colbert">Jacques-Nicolas Colbert</a>, <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Rouen" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Rouen">Archbishop of Rouen</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Hugh_of_Die" title="Hugh of Die">Hugh of Die</a> (1081–1106), the successor of Saint Gebuin, friend of <a href="/wiki/Anselm_of_Canterbury" title="Anselm of Canterbury">Saint Anselm</a>, and for a while legate of Gregory VII in France and Burgundy, had differences later on with <a href="/wiki/Victor_III" class="mw-redirect" title="Victor III">Victor III</a>, who excommunicated him for a time. The latter pope came to Lyon in 1106, consecrated the church of <a href="/wiki/Ainay_Abbey" class="mw-redirect" title="Ainay Abbey">Ainay Abbey</a>, and dedicated one of its altars in honour of the Immaculate Conception. The Feast of the <a href="/wiki/Immaculate_Conception" title="Immaculate Conception">Immaculate Conception</a> was solemnized at Lyon about 1128, perhaps at the instance of Saint <a href="/wiki/Anselm_of_Canterbury" title="Anselm of Canterbury">Anselm of Canterbury</a>, and Saint Bernard wrote to the canons of Lyon to complain that they should not have instituted a feast without consulting the pope.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Sovereignty">Sovereignty</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Sovereignty">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>As soon as <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Becket" title="Thomas Becket">Thomas Becket</a>, <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, had been proclaimed Blessed (1173), his cult was instituted at Lyon. Lyon of the 12th century thus has a glorious place in the history of <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Liturgy" title="Liturgy">liturgy</a> and even of <a href="/wiki/Dogma" title="Dogma">dogma</a>, but the 12th century was also marked by the heresy of <a href="/wiki/Peter_Waldo" title="Peter Waldo">Peter Waldo</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Waldenses" class="mw-redirect" title="Waldenses">Waldenses</a>, the Poor Men of Lyon, who were opposed by <a href="/wiki/John_of_Canterbury" title="John of Canterbury">John of Canterbury</a> (1181–1193), and by an important change in the political situation of the archbishops.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1157 Emperor <a href="/wiki/Frederick_Barbarossa" title="Frederick Barbarossa">Frederick Barbarossa</a> confirmed the sovereignty of the Archbishops of Lyon; thenceforth there was a lively contest between them and the counts. An arbitration effected by the pope in 1167 had no result, but by the treaty of 1173, Guy, <a href="/wiki/Count_of_Forez" class="mw-redirect" title="Count of Forez">Count of Forez</a>, ceded to the canons of the primatial church of St. John his title of count of Lyon and his temporal authority.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Then came the growth of the Commune, more belated in Lyon than in many other cities, but in 1193 the archbishop had to make some concession to the citizens. The 13th century was a period of conflict. Three times, in 1207, 1269, and 1290, grave troubles broke out between the partisans of the archbishop who dwelt in the château of Pierre Seize, those of the count-canons who lived in a separate quarter near the cathedral, and partisans of the townsfolk. <a href="/wiki/Gregory_X" class="mw-redirect" title="Gregory X">Gregory X</a> attempted without success to restore peace by two Acts, 2 April 1273 and 11 November 1274. The kings of France were always inclined to side with the commune; after the siege of Lyon by <a href="/wiki/Louis_X_of_France" title="Louis X of France">Louis X</a> (1310), the treaty of 10 April 1312 definitively attached Lyon to the Kingdom of France, but until the beginning of the 15th century the Church of Lyon was allowed to coin its own money.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>If the 13th century had imperiled the political sovereignty of the archbishops, it had on the other hand made Lyon a kind of second Rome. Gregory X was a former canon of Lyon, while the future <a href="/wiki/Innocent_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Innocent V">Innocent V</a> was Archbishop of Lyon from 1272 to 1273. <a href="/wiki/Innocent_IV" class="mw-redirect" title="Innocent IV">Innocent IV</a> and Gregory X sought refuge at Lyon from the <a href="/wiki/Hohenstaufen" title="Hohenstaufen">Hohenstaufen</a>, and held there two general <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Lyon_(disambiguation)" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Lyon (disambiguation)">councils of Lyon</a>. Local tradition relates that it was on seeing the red hat of the canons of Lyon that the courtiers of Innocent IV conceived the idea of obtaining from the Council of Lyon its decree that the cardinals should henceforth wear red hats. The sojourn of Innocent IV at Lyon was marked by numerous works of public utility, to which the pope gave vigorous encouragement. He granted <a href="/wiki/Indulgence" title="Indulgence">indulgences</a> to the faithful who should assist in the construction of the bridge over the Rhône, replacing that destroyed about 1190 by the passage of the troops of <a href="/wiki/Richard_I_of_England" title="Richard I of England">Richard Cœur de Lion</a> on their way to the Crusade. The building of the churches of St. John and St. Justus was pushed forward with activity; he sent delegates even to England to solicit alms for this purpose and he consecrated the high altar in both churches.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At Lyon were crowned <a href="/wiki/Clement_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Clement V">Clement V</a> (1305) and <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_XXII" title="Pope John XXII">Pope John XXII</a> (1310); at Lyon in 1449 the <a href="/wiki/Antipope_Felix_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Antipope Felix V">antipope Felix V</a> renounced the tiara; there, too, was held in 1512, without any definite conclusion, the last session of the schismatical <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Pisa" title="Council of Pisa">Council of Pisa</a> against <a href="/wiki/Julius_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Julius II">Julius II</a>. In 1560 the Calvinists took Lyon by surprise, but they were driven out by <a href="/w/index.php?title=Antoine_d%27Albon&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Antoine d&#39;Albon (page does not exist)">Antoine d'Albon</a>, <a href="/wiki/Savigny_Abbey,_Rh%C3%B4ne" title="Savigny Abbey, Rhône">Abbot of Savigny</a> and later <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Lyon" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Lyon">Archbishop of Lyon</a>. Again masters of Lyon in 1562, they were driven thence by the <a href="/wiki/Mar%C3%A9chal_de_Vieuville" class="mw-redirect" title="Maréchal de Vieuville">Maréchal de Vieuville</a>. At the command of the famous <a href="/wiki/Baron_des_Adrets" class="mw-redirect" title="Baron des Adrets">Baron des Adrets</a> they committed numerous acts of violence in the region of Montbrison. It was at Lyon that <a href="/wiki/Henry_IV_of_France" title="Henry IV of France">Henry IV of France</a>, the converted Calvinist king, married <a href="/wiki/Marie_de%27_Medici" title="Marie de&#39; Medici">Marie de' Medici</a> (9 December 1600).<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Later_Middle_Ages">Later Middle Ages</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Later Middle Ages">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Gerson, whose old age was spent at Lyon in the abbey of St. Paul, where he instructed poor children, died there in 1429. <a href="/wiki/Saint_Francis_de_Sales" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Francis de Sales">Saint Francis de Sales</a> died at Lyon on 28 December 1622. The Curé Colombet de St. Amour was celebrated at St. Etienne in the 17th century for the generosity with which he founded the Hôtel-Dieu (the charity hospital) and  free schools, and also fed the workmen during the famine of 1693.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>M. Guigue has catalogued the eleven "<a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermitages</a>" (eight of them for men and three for women) which were distinctive of the ascetical life of Christian Lyon in the Middle Ages; these were cells in which persons shut themselves up for life after four years of trial. The system of hermitages along the lines described by <a href="/w/index.php?title=Grimalaius&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Grimalaius (page does not exist)">Grimalaius</a> and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Olbredus&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Olbredus (page does not exist)">Olbredus</a> in the 9th century flourished especially from the 11th to the 13th century, and disappeared completely in the 16th. These hermitages were the private property of a neighbouring church or monastery, which installed therein for life a male or female recluse. The general almshouse of Lyon, or charity hospital, was founded in 1532 after the great famine of 1531, under the supervision of eight administrators chosen from among the more important citizens.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The institution of the jubilee of Saint Nizier dates beyond a doubt to the stay of <a href="/wiki/Innocent_IV" class="mw-redirect" title="Innocent IV">Innocent IV</a> at Lyon. This jubilee, which had all the privileges of the secular jubilees of Rome, was celebrated each time that Low Thursday, the feast of Saint Nizier, coincided with 2 April, i.e. whenever the feast of Easter itself was on the earliest day allowed by the <a href="/wiki/Paschal_cycle" title="Paschal cycle">paschal cycle</a>, namely 22 March. In 1818, when this coincidence occurred, the feast of Saint Nizier was not celebrated. But the cathedral of St. John also enjoys a great jubilee each time that the feast of <a href="/wiki/Saint_John_the_Baptist" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint John the Baptist">Saint John the Baptist</a> coincides with <a href="/wiki/Corpus_Christi_(feast)" class="mw-redirect" title="Corpus Christi (feast)">Corpus Christi</a>, that is, whenever the feast of Corpus Christi falls on 24 June. It is certain that in 1451 the coincidence of these two feasts was celebrated with special splendour by the population of Lyon, then emerging from the troubles of the <a href="/wiki/Hundred_Years%27_War" title="Hundred Years&#39; War">Hundred Years' War</a>, but there is no document to prove that the jubilee indulgence existed at that date. However, Lyonnese tradition places the first great jubilee in 1451; subsequent jubilees took place in 1546, 1666, 1734 and 1886.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>"Among the Churches of France", wrote Saint Bernard to the canons of Lyon, "that of Lyon has hitherto had ascendancy over all the others, as much for the dignity of its see as for its praiseworthy institutions. It is especially in the <a href="/wiki/Liturgy_of_the_Hours" title="Liturgy of the Hours">Divine Office</a> that this judicious Church has never readily acquiesced in unexpected and sudden novelties, and has never submitted to be tarnished by innovations which are becoming only to youth."<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Montazet_controversy">Montazet controversy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Montazet controversy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In the 18th century Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Antoine_de_Montazet" title="Antoine de Montazet">Antoine de Montazet</a>, contrary to the Bull of <a href="/wiki/Pius_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Pius V">Pius V</a> on the <a href="/wiki/Breviary" title="Breviary">breviary</a>, changed the text of the breviary and the <a href="/wiki/Missal" title="Missal">missal</a>, from which there resulted a century of conflict for the Church of Lyon. The efforts of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_IX" title="Pope Pius IX">Pope Pius IX</a> and <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_Bonald" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinal Bonald">Cardinal Bonald</a> to suppress the innovations of Montazet provoked resistance on the part of the canons, who feared an attempt against the traditional Lyonnese ceremonies. This culminated in 1861 in a protest on the part of the clergy and the laity, as much with regard to the civil power as to the Vatican. Finally, on 4 February 1864, at a reception of the parish priests of Lyon, Pius IX declared his displeasure at this agitation and assured them that nothing should be changed in the ancient Lyonnese ceremonies; by a Brief of 17 March 1864, he ordered the progressive introduction of the Roman breviary and missal in the diocese. The primatial church of Lyon adopted them for public services on 8 December 1869. One of the rites of the ancient Gallican liturgy, retained by the Church of Lyon, is the blessing of the people by the bishop at the moment of Communion.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="1800s">1800s</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: 1800s">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Concordat_of_1801" title="Concordat of 1801">Concordat of 1801</a> assigned as the boundaries of the Archdiocese of Lyon the Departments of the <a href="/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne_(department)" title="Rhône (department)">Rhône</a> and <a href="/wiki/Loire" title="Loire">Loire</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Ain" title="Ain">Ain</a> and as <a href="/wiki/Suffragan_bishop" title="Suffragan bishop">suffragans</a> the Dioceses of Mende, Grenoble, and Chambéry. The Archdiocese of Lyon was authorized by Letters Apostolic of 29 November 1801, to unite with his title the titles of the suppressed metropolitan <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Diocese_of_Vienne" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Diocese of Vienne">Sees of Vienne</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Diocese_of_Embrun" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Diocese of Embrun">Embrun</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Thus the dioceses of <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Belley-Ars" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of Belley-Ars">Belley</a> and Mâcon, were suppressed on November 29, 1801 with all of Belley's and some of Mâcon's territory added to the Archdiocese.  The Diocese of Belley was restored on October 6, 1822, while the Archdiocese's name changed to Lyon-Vienne,<sup id="cite_ref-ch_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ch-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> with the title of Embrun passing to the Archbishop of Aix (from whence, 2008, to the Bishop of Gap).
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="1900s">1900s</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: 1900s">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>A new diocese of Saint-Étienne was erected on December 26, 1970, from the Archdiocese's territory.  The Archdiocese's name returned to Lyon on December 15, 2006<sup id="cite_ref-ch_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ch-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> (with the title of Vienne passing to its suffragan Grenoble).
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Saints">Saints</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Saints">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Diocese of Lyon honours as saints: <a href="/wiki/Saint_Epipodius" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Epipodius">Saint Epipodius</a> and his companion Saint Alexander, probably martyrs under <a href="/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius" title="Marcus Aurelius">Marcus Aurelius</a>; the priest <a href="/wiki/Peregrine_of_Auxerre" title="Peregrine of Auxerre">Saint Peregrinus</a> (3rd century); <a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Baldonor&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Saint Baldonor (page does not exist)">Saint Baldonor</a> (Galmier), a native of <a href="/wiki/Aveizieux" title="Aveizieux">Aveizieux</a>, at first a locksmith, whose piety was remarked by the bishop, <a href="/wiki/Saint_Viventiolus" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Viventiolus">Saint Viventiolus</a>: he became a cleric at the Abbey of St. Justus, then subdeacon, and died about 760; the thermal resort of "Aquæ Segestæ", in whose church Viventiolus met him, has taken the name of Saint Galmier; <a href="/w/index.php?title=Viator_of_Lyon&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Viator of Lyon (page does not exist)">Saint Viator</a> (d. about 390), who followed the Bishop Saint Justus to the <a href="/wiki/Thebaid" title="Thebaid">Thebaid</a>; Saints <a href="/wiki/Romanus_of_Condat" title="Romanus of Condat">Romanus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Lupicinus_of_Lyon" title="Lupicinus of Lyon">Lupicinus</a> (5th century), natives of the Diocese of Lyon, who lived as solitaries within the present territory of the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Saint-Claude" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Saint-Claude">Diocese of Saint-Claude</a>; <a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Consortia&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Saint Consortia (page does not exist)">Saint Consortia</a>, d. about 578, who, according to a legend criticized by <a href="/wiki/Tillemont" class="mw-redirect" title="Tillemont">Tillemont</a>, was a daughter of Saint Eucherius; <a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Rambert&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Saint Rambert (page does not exist)">Saint Rambert</a>, soldier and martyr in the 7th century, patron of the town of the same name; <a href="/w/index.php?title=Blessed_Jean_Pierre_N%C3%A9el&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Blessed Jean Pierre Néel (page does not exist)">Blessed Jean Pierre Néel</a>, b. in 1832 at Ste. Catherine sur Riviere, martyred at Kay-Tcheou in 1862.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_2-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
