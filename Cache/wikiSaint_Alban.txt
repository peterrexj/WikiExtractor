<p class="mw-empty-elt">
</p>

<p><b>Saint Alban</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="/ɔː/: &#39;au&#39; in &#39;fraud&#39;">ɔː</span><span title="&#39;l&#39; in &#39;lie&#39;">l</span><span title="&#39;b&#39; in &#39;buy&#39;">b</span><span title="/ən/: &#39;on&#39; in &#39;button&#39;">ən</span></span>,<span class="wrap"> </span><span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="/æ/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;bad&#39;">æ</span><span title="&#39;l&#39; in &#39;lie&#39;">l</span></span>-/</a></span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Albanus</i>) is venerated as the first-recorded <a href="/wiki/Britons_(Celtic_people)" class="mw-redirect" title="Britons (Celtic people)">British</a> <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyrs" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian martyrs">martyr</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Thurston_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Thurston-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> for which reason he is considered to be the British <a href="/wiki/Protomartyr" class="mw-redirect" title="Protomartyr">protomartyr</a>. Along with fellow Saints <a href="/wiki/Julius_and_Aaron" title="Julius and Aaron">Julius and Aaron</a>, Alban is one of three named martyrs recorded at an early date from <a href="/wiki/Roman_Britain" title="Roman Britain">Roman Britain</a> ("<a href="/wiki/Amphibalus" title="Amphibalus">Amphibalus</a>" was the name given much later to the priest he was said to have been protecting). He is traditionally believed to have been beheaded in <a href="/wiki/Verulamium" title="Verulamium">Verulamium</a> (modern <a href="/wiki/St_Albans" title="St Albans">St Albans</a>) sometime during the 3rd or 4th century, and his cult has been celebrated there since ancient times.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Hagiography">Hagiography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Hagiography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Alban lived in <a href="/wiki/Roman_Britain" title="Roman Britain">Roman Britain</a>, but little is known about his religious affiliations, socioeconomic status, or citizenship. According to the most elaborate version of the tale found in <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People" title="Ecclesiastical History of the English People">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i>, in the 3rd or 4th century (see dating controversy below), Christians began to suffer "cruel persecution", and Alban was living in <a href="/wiki/Verulamium" title="Verulamium">Verulamium</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> However, <a href="/wiki/Gildas" title="Gildas">Gildas</a> says he crossed the Thames before his martyrdom, so some authors place his residence and martyrdom in or near London.<sup id="cite_ref-The_Ruin_of_Britain_-_Gildas_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-The_Ruin_of_Britain_-_Gildas-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Both agree that Alban met a Roman Catholic priest fleeing from persecutors and sheltered him in his house for a number of days. The priest, who later came to be called <i><a href="/wiki/Amphibalus" title="Amphibalus">Amphibalus</a></i>, meaning "cloak" in Latin, prayed and "kept watch" day and night, and Alban was so impressed with the priest's faith and piety that he found himself emulating him and soon converted to Christianity. Eventually, it came to the ears of an unnamed "impious prince" that Alban was sheltering the priest. The prince gave orders for Roman soldiers to make a strict search of Alban's house. As they came to seize the priest, Alban put on the priest's cloak and clothing and presented himself to the soldiers in place of his guest.<sup id="cite_ref-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Alban was brought before a judge, who just then happened to be standing at the altar, offering sacrifices to "devils" (Bede's reference to pagan gods). When the judge heard that Alban had offered himself up in place of the priest, he became enraged that Alban would shelter a person who "despised and blasphemed the gods,"<sup id="cite_ref-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and, as Alban had given himself up in the Christian's place, Alban was sentenced to endure all the punishments that were to be inflicted upon the priest, unless he would comply with the pagan rites of their religion. Alban refused, and declared, "I worship and adore the true and living God who created all things." (The words are still used in prayer at St Alban's Abbey). The enraged judge ordered Alban to be <a href="/wiki/Scourged" class="mw-redirect" title="Scourged">scourged</a>, thinking that a whipping would shake the constancy of his heart, but Alban bore these torments patiently and joyfully. When the judge realized that the tortures would not shake his faith, he gave orders for Alban to be beheaded.<sup id="cite_ref-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Alban_-_stained_glass_at_St_Albans%27_Cathedral.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/St_Alban_-_stained_glass_at_St_Albans%27_Cathedral.jpg/230px-St_Alban_-_stained_glass_at_St_Albans%27_Cathedral.jpg" decoding="async" width="230" height="247" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/St_Alban_-_stained_glass_at_St_Albans%27_Cathedral.jpg/345px-St_Alban_-_stained_glass_at_St_Albans%27_Cathedral.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/St_Alban_-_stained_glass_at_St_Albans%27_Cathedral.jpg/460px-St_Alban_-_stained_glass_at_St_Albans%27_Cathedral.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1540" data-file-height="1651" /></a><figcaption>Stained glass in <a href="/wiki/St_Albans_Cathedral" title="St Albans Cathedral">St Albans Cathedral</a> in England, showing the death of Saint Alban</figcaption></figure>
<p>Alban was led to execution, and he presently came to a fast-flowing river that could not be crossed (believed to be the <a href="/wiki/River_Ver" title="River Ver">River Ver</a>). There was a bridge, but a mob of curious townspeople who wished to watch the execution had so clogged the bridge that the execution party could not cross. Filled with an ardent desire to arrive quickly at martyrdom, Alban raised his eyes to heaven, and the river dried up, allowing Alban and his captors to cross over on dry land. The astonished executioner cast down his sword and fell at Alban's feet, moved by divine inspiration and praying that he might either suffer with Alban or be executed for him.<sup id="cite_ref-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-saint_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-saint-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The other executioners hesitated to pick up his sword, and meanwhile, Alban and they went about 500 paces to a gently sloping hill, completely covered with all kinds of wild flowers, and overlooking a beautiful plain. (Bede observes that it was a fittingly beautiful place to be enriched and sanctified by a martyr's blood.)<sup id="cite_ref-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38rMartyrdomAlban.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38rMartyrdomAlban.jpg/200px-DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38rMartyrdomAlban.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="131" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38rMartyrdomAlban.jpg/300px-DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38rMartyrdomAlban.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38rMartyrdomAlban.jpg/400px-DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38rMartyrdomAlban.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1897" data-file-height="1245" /></a><figcaption>The martyrdom of St Alban, from a 13th-century manuscript written and illustrated by <a href="/wiki/Matthew_Paris" title="Matthew Paris">Matthew Paris</a>, now in <a href="/wiki/Trinity_College_Library" class="mw-redirect" title="Trinity College Library">Trinity College Library</a>, Dublin; note the executioner's eyes falling out of his head</figcaption></figure>
<p>When Alban reached the summit of the hill, he began to thirst and prayed God would give him water. A spring immediately sprang up at his feet. It was there that his head was struck off, as well as the head of the first Roman soldier who was miraculously converted and refused to execute him. However, immediately after delivering the fatal stroke, the eyes of the second executioner popped out of his head and dropped to the ground, along with Alban's head, so that this second executioner could not rejoice over Alban's death.<sup id="cite_ref-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In later legends, Alban's head rolled downhill after his execution, and a well sprang up where it stopped.<sup id="cite_ref-Medieval_St_Albans_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Medieval_St_Albans-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
Upon hearing of the miracles, the astonished judge ordered further persecutions to cease, and he began to honour the saint's death.<sup id="cite_ref-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People_2-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St_Albans_Cathedral" title="St Albans Cathedral">St Albans Cathedral</a> now stands near the believed site of his execution, and a well is at the bottom of the hill, Holywell Hill.<sup id="cite_ref-Medieval_St_Albans_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Medieval_St_Albans-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Sources">Sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The earliest mention of Alban's martyrdom is believed to be in <a href="/wiki/Victricius" title="Victricius">Victricius</a>'s <i>De Laude Sanctorum</i> (The Praise of Saints), c. 396. Victricius had just returned from settling an unnamed dispute among the bishops of Britain.<sup id="cite_ref-Victricius_of_Rouen:_Praising_the_Saints_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Victricius_of_Rouen:_Praising_the_Saints-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He does not mention Alban by name, but includes an unnamed martyr, who, "in the hands of the executioners told rivers to draw back, lest he should be delayed in his haste."<sup id="cite_ref-Victricius_of_Rouen:_Praising_the_Saints_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Victricius_of_Rouen:_Praising_the_Saints-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The account closely resembles Alban's martyrdom, and many historians have concluded that this may be a reference to Alban, making it the earliest surviving reference to a British saint. There can be no certainty, however, that the martyr referred to is actually Saint Alban.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The foundational text concerning Alban is the <i><a href="/wiki/Passio_Albani" title="Passio Albani">Passio Albani</a></i>, or the Passion of Alban, which relates the tale of Alban's martyrdom, and <a href="/wiki/Germanus_of_Auxerre" title="Germanus of Auxerre">Germanus of Auxerre</a>'s subsequent visit to the site of Alban's execution. This <i>Passio</i> survives in six <a href="/wiki/Manuscripts" class="mw-redirect" title="Manuscripts">manuscripts</a>, with three different <a href="/wiki/Recension" title="Recension">recensions</a>, referred to as T, P, and E,<sup id="cite_ref-Die_Legende_des_h._Albanus_-_Meyer_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Die_Legende_des_h._Albanus_-_Meyer-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> the oldest of which dates to the eighth century.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The T manuscript is located in <a href="/wiki/Turin" title="Turin">Turin</a>, the P manuscript is found in Paris and the E manuscripts (of which there are 4) are at <a href="/wiki/The_British_Library" class="mw-redirect" title="The British Library">The British Library</a> and <a href="/wiki/Gray%27s_Inn" title="Gray&#39;s Inn">Gray's Inn</a>, both in London, and <a href="/wiki/Autun_Cathedral" title="Autun Cathedral">Autun</a> (France) and <a href="/wiki/Einsiedeln_Abbey" title="Einsiedeln Abbey">Einsiedeln</a> (Switzerland). The <i>Passio</i> is very likely the source text of the more well-known accounts found in Gildas and Bede.
</p><p>Another early text to mention Alban is the <i><a href="/wiki/Vita_Germani" title="Vita Germani">Vita Germani</a></i>, or <i>Life of St Germanus of Auxerre</i>, written about 480 by <a href="/wiki/Constantius_of_Lyon" title="Constantius of Lyon">Constantius of Lyon</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Germanus,_Bishop_of_Auxerre_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Germanus,_Bishop_of_Auxerre-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> The text only very briefly mentions Alban but is an important text concerning his nascent <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cult</a>. According to the <i>Vita</i>, Germanus visited Alban's grave shortly after defeating the <a href="/wiki/Pelagianism" title="Pelagianism">Pelagian heresy</a> in Britain and asked Alban to give thanks to God on his behalf. They once again called on him during their voyage home, and Alban is credited with providing smooth sailing for the voyage back to the continent.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol45rMartyrdomAmphibalus.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol45rMartyrdomAmphibalus.jpg/220px-DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol45rMartyrdomAmphibalus.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="157" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol45rMartyrdomAmphibalus.jpg/330px-DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol45rMartyrdomAmphibalus.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol45rMartyrdomAmphibalus.jpg/440px-DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol45rMartyrdomAmphibalus.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1899" data-file-height="1358" /></a><figcaption>Martyrdom of Amphibalus from the Trinity College <i>Life of St Alban</i></figcaption></figure>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38vHeracliusTakesDownAlbansHead.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38vHeracliusTakesDownAlbansHead.jpg/220px-DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38vHeracliusTakesDownAlbansHead.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="158" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38vHeracliusTakesDownAlbansHead.jpg/330px-DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38vHeracliusTakesDownAlbansHead.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38vHeracliusTakesDownAlbansHead.jpg/440px-DublinTrinityCollegeMSEi40LifeAlbanFol38vHeracliusTakesDownAlbansHead.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1986" data-file-height="1429" /></a><figcaption>Framed tinted drawing of Heraclius taking down the head of Saint Alban, from the Trinity College <i>Life</i></figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/wiki/Gildas" title="Gildas">Gildas</a> gives a short account of Alban's martyrdom in his <i><a href="/wiki/De_Excidio_et_Conquestu_Britanniae" title="De Excidio et Conquestu Britanniae">De Excidio et Conquestu Britanniae</a></i> (c. 570),<sup id="cite_ref-The_Ruin_of_Britain_-_Gildas_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-The_Ruin_of_Britain_-_Gildas-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> gives a much fuller account in his <i><a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People" title="Ecclesiastical History of the English People">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i> (c. 730).<sup id="cite_ref-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People_2-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Gildas calls Alban a martyr of <i>Verulamium</i> but says he crossed the river Thames prior to his execution, during the persecution of <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a>. Bede's account is much more detailed but sets the events during the reign of <a href="/wiki/Septimius_Severus" title="Septimius Severus">Septimius Severus</a> and in the town of <a href="/wiki/Verulamium" title="Verulamium">Verulamium</a>, where a shrine devoted to Alban had been established by at least 429 AD, when <a href="/wiki/Germanus_of_Auxerre" title="Germanus of Auxerre">Germanus of Auxerre</a> is said to have visited the cult centre during his tour of Britain. Alban is also briefly mentioned in the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> (c. 900),<sup id="cite_ref-The_Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-The_Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> and by <a href="/wiki/Geoffrey_of_Monmouth" title="Geoffrey of Monmouth">Geoffrey of Monmouth</a> in <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_Regum_Britanniae" title="Historia Regum Britanniae">Historia Regum Britanniae</a></i> (c. 1136).<sup id="cite_ref-Geoffrey_of_Monmouth_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Geoffrey_of_Monmouth-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> It is also possible that his martyrdom is referenced in the <i><a href="/wiki/Acts_of_the_Martyrs" title="Acts of the Martyrs">Acta Martyrum</a></i>.
</p><p>Another early source for Saint Alban is the <i><a href="/wiki/Martyrologium_Hieronymianum" title="Martyrologium Hieronymianum">Martyrologium Hieronymianum</a></i>, or the so-called 'Martyrology of Saint Jerome' in which the entry <i>In Britannia Albani martyris</i> probably occurred originally under 22 June. In fact, in the extant versions, Alban has acquired numerous companions because of confusion/conflation with other entries. The martyrology is preserved in a 9th-century copy but was probably composed in something close to its present form around 600, with the surviving recension showing some signs of being based on a recension compiled at Auxerre (significantly, the hometown of Saint Germanus)<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> For Thornhill (see above), the date given for Alban's martyrdom is striking for its closeness to the summer solstice (on which some variants of the <i>Hieronymianum</i> actually place the saint's day). Being the day when the sun is at its brightest in midsummer, that might suggest that there is indeed some significance in the literal meaning of the name <i>Albanus</i> (or at least the root <i>albho-</i> on which it is based) as 'white' or 'bright'.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Matthew_Paris" title="Matthew Paris">Matthew Paris</a>, the celebrated <a href="/wiki/English_historians_in_the_Middle_Ages" class="mw-redirect" title="English historians in the Middle Ages">medieval English chronicler</a> and most famous of St Alban's Abbey's monks, produced a beautifully-illustrated <i>Life of St Alban</i> in the 13th century, which is in French verse adapted from a Latin <i>Life of St Alban</i> by <a href="/wiki/William_of_St_Albans" title="William of St Albans">William of St Albans</a>, c. 1178.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> It is now at the <a href="/wiki/Trinity_College,_Dublin#The_Library" class="mw-redirect" title="Trinity College, Dublin">Trinity College Library in Dublin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Disputed_topics">Disputed topics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Disputed topics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Dating">Dating</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Dating">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The date of Alban's execution has never been firmly established. Original sources and modern historians indicate a range of dates between 209 and 313.
</p><p>The <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> lists the year 283,<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> but <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> places it in 305, "when the cruel Emperors first published their edicts against the Christians." In other words, it was sometime after the publication of the edicts by <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Roman_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Roman Emperor">Eastern Roman Emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a> in 303 and before the proclamation of toleration in the <a href="/wiki/Edict_of_Milan" title="Edict of Milan">Edict of Milan</a> by co-ruling <a href="/wiki/Roman_Emperors" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Emperors">Roman Emperors</a> <a href="/wiki/Constantine_I_(emperor)" class="mw-redirect" title="Constantine I (emperor)">Constantine I</a> and <a href="/wiki/Licinius" title="Licinius">Licinius</a>, in 313. Bede was probably following Gildas.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:StAlban.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/StAlban.jpg/220px-StAlban.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="279" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/StAlban.jpg/330px-StAlban.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4d/StAlban.jpg 2x" data-file-width="340" data-file-height="431" /></a><figcaption>Eastern Orthdodox icon of Saint Alban</figcaption></figure>
<p>English historian <a href="/wiki/John_Morris_(historian)" title="John Morris (historian)">John Morris</a> suggests that Alban's martyrdom took place during the persecutions of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Septimius_Severus" title="Septimius Severus">Septimius Severus</a> in 209.<sup id="cite_ref-Morris:_The_Date_of_Saint_Alban_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Morris:_The_Date_of_Saint_Alban-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Morris bases his claims on the Turin version of the <i>Passio Albani</i>, unknown to Bede, which states, "Alban received a fugitive <a href="/wiki/Cleric" class="mw-redirect" title="Cleric">cleric</a> and put on his garment and his cloak (<i>habitu et caracalla</i>) that he was wearing and delivered himself up to be killed instead of the priest… and was delivered immediately to the evil Caesar Severus." According to Morris St <a href="/wiki/Gildas" title="Gildas">Gildas</a> knew the source but mistranslated the name "Severus" as an adjective, wrongly identifying the emperor as Diocletian. Bede accepted the identification as fact and dates St Alban's martyrdom to this later period.  As Morris points out, Diocletian reigned only in the East and would not have been involved in British affairs in 304; Emperor Severus, however, was in Britain from 208 to 211. Morris thus dates Alban's death to 209.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> However, the mention of Severus in the Turin version has been shown to be an interpolation into an original text, which mentioned only a <i>iudex</i>  or 'judge'.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Subsequent scholars (<a href="/wiki/William_Hugh_Clifford_Frend" class="mw-redirect" title="William Hugh Clifford Frend">William Hugh Clifford Frend</a> and <a href="/wiki/Charles_Thomas_(historian)" title="Charles Thomas (historian)">Charles Thomas</a> for example) have argued that such a single, localised British martyrdom in 209 would have been unusual, and they have suggested the period of 251–259 (under the <a href="/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians#Persecution_from_the_2nd_century_to_Constantine" title="Persecution of Christians">persecutors</a> <a href="/wiki/Decius" title="Decius">Decius</a> or <a href="/wiki/Valerian_(emperor)" title="Valerian (emperor)">Valerian</a>) are more likely.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Location">Location</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Location">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>While it is certain that the cult devoted to Saint Alban was established in Verulamium, and his martyrdom was also alleged to have taken place there, the sources are unclear about where he was actually executed. Neither Victricius's <i>De Laude Sanctorum</i> nor the <i>Passio Albani</i> mentions where he was martyred other than that it was in Britain. In the <i>Vita Germani</i>, Germanus visits Alban's tomb and touches droplets of his blood still on the ground, but the text does not name the location of the tomb. It is not until <a href="/wiki/Gildas" title="Gildas">Gildas</a> that Alban was connected with Verulamium.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Historicity">Historicity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Historicity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Little is known about the real Alban (estimated to have died c. 209 – 305 AD, depending on interpretations), as there are no contemporaneous accounts of his martyrdom and the major sources on his life were written hundreds of years after his death, containing wondrous embellishments, which may or may not refer to real events.
</p><p>Saint Alban was long regarded as a genuine martyr saint, the protomartyr of Britain, and for much of the 20th century controversy centred on the date of his martyrdom (see further <a href="#Dating">'Dating controversy</a>', above). More recently, however, some researchers have taken a more sceptical view about his historicity. In the view of <a href="/wiki/Robin_Lane_Fox" title="Robin Lane Fox">Robin Lane Fox</a>, not only is St Alban's date disputable but so is his very existence.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 2008 the historian Ian Wood proposed that Alban was an 'invention' of Germanus of Auxerre.<sup id="cite_ref-Wood:_Germanus,_Alban_and_Auxerre_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Wood:_Germanus,_Alban_and_Auxerre-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Germanus visited Britain in 429, as is known from the nearly-contemporary mention by <a href="/wiki/Prosper_of_Aquitaine" title="Prosper of Aquitaine">Prosper of Aquitaine</a>. His chronicle, in the entry for the year 429 (published in 433), states:
</p>
<dl><dd>Agricola, a Pelagian, the son of the Pelagian bishop Severianus, corrupted the British churches by the insinuation of his doctrine. But at the persuasion of the deacon Palladius, Pope Celestine sent Germanus, bishop of Auxerre, as his representative, and having rejected the heretics, directed the British to the catholic faith.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup></dd></dl>
<p>Meanwhile, it was recorded in the <i><a href="/wiki/Vita_Germani" title="Vita Germani">Vita Germani</a></i> ('Life of St Germanus of Auxerre'), written probably sometime between 450 and 485<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> by Constantius of Lyons, that he, together with his fellow bishop Lupus, having stamped out the heresy of <a href="/wiki/Pelagianism" title="Pelagianism">Pelagianism</a> in Britain, visited the tomb of Saint Alban: 
</p>
<dl><dd>When this damnable heresy had thus been stamped out, its authors refuted, and the minds of all re-established in the true faith, the bishops visited the shrine of the blessed martyr Alban, to give thanks to God through him. (<i>Vita Germani</i> 12)<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup></dd></dl>
<p>The martyr Alban is also mentioned, one more time, in the context of Germanus's return journey, by sea:
</p>
<dl><dd>Their own merits and the intercession of Alban the Martyr secured for them a calm voyage; and a good ship brought them back in peace to their expectant people.  (<i>Vita Germani</i> 13)</dd></dl>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Alban_window_at_Church_of_the_Good_Shepherd_(Rosemont,_Pennsylvania).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/77/St._Alban_window_at_Church_of_the_Good_Shepherd_%28Rosemont%2C_Pennsylvania%29.jpg/220px-St._Alban_window_at_Church_of_the_Good_Shepherd_%28Rosemont%2C_Pennsylvania%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/77/St._Alban_window_at_Church_of_the_Good_Shepherd_%28Rosemont%2C_Pennsylvania%29.jpg/330px-St._Alban_window_at_Church_of_the_Good_Shepherd_%28Rosemont%2C_Pennsylvania%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/77/St._Alban_window_at_Church_of_the_Good_Shepherd_%28Rosemont%2C_Pennsylvania%29.jpg/440px-St._Alban_window_at_Church_of_the_Good_Shepherd_%28Rosemont%2C_Pennsylvania%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1536" data-file-height="2048" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/St._Alban" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Alban">St. Alban</a> window at <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Good_Shepherd_(Rosemont,_Pennsylvania)" title="Church of the Good Shepherd (Rosemont, Pennsylvania)">Church of the Good Shepherd (Rosemont, Pennsylvania)</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>The <i>Vita Germani</i> was long regarded as the earliest source for the martyr Alban, but recent research by Richard Sharpe<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> has suggested the earliest version of the <i><a href="/wiki/Passio_Albani" title="Passio Albani">Passio Albani</a></i><sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> (the official story of the saint's martyrdom) may be even earlier (see below and Sources). Wood's argument was based partly on the idea that the name <i>Albanus</i> is suggestive of Albion as the oldest name for Britain, but for him, the name Alban suggested simply 'the man from Albion' rather than an actual 'personification' of the island and its people. It is, in any case, a part of what suggested to Wood that "it is Germanus who gives Alban a name". That, in turn, encouraged him in his conclusion: 
"The story of the saint's martyrdom seems to have been revealed to, or invented by, Germanus in the context of his anti-Pelagian mission" and in a later article<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> "Alban may, therefore, have been 'discovered' by the bishop of Auxerre".
</p><p>The argument has been accepted by, for instance, Michael Garcia<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> but disputed by, for instance, Professor <a href="/wiki/Nick_Higham_(historian)" class="mw-redirect" title="Nick Higham (historian)">Nick Higham</a>, who, in an article written in 2014,<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> noted that since Germanus brought relics of continental saints with him, which, so the <i>Passio</i> relates, he deposits in the tomb of Saint Alban while removing some bloodstained earth to take back to Gaul, he must have known from the start that he would make a visit to the cult-centre of Saint Alban, as part of his campaign against the Pelagian heresy. On this basis he states: "This would make good sense in terms of his mission, claiming Britain's most famous cult for Catholicism". He therefore argues against the conclusion of Woods and Garcia that the martyr Alban was unknown before being invented by Germanus.
</p><p>Key to the argument is a passage in the T version of the <i>Passio</i> that Sharpe has convincingly argued represents an 'interpolation' to the more original E text. All extant versions of the <i>Passio</i> mention (after describing the story of the saint's martyrdom) Germanus's visit to the tomb of Saint Alban. The E version, followed essentially by the T version, states (in the translation of Sharpe):
</p>
<dl><dd>When Germanus came to Alban's basilica, carrying with him relics of all the apostles and of several martyrs...</dd></dl>
<p>but interpolated at this point in only the T version is 
</p>
<dl><dd>...Alban had revealed himself to Germanus on his journey, and now, so Germanus himself relates, St Alban met him on the stormy seas. But while he had been keeping vigil at night in his basilica, in the dawn when he had given in to sleep St. Alban appeared to him and communicated to him by revelation to him what had happened at the time of his martyrdom and he made this public in order that the events should be preserved in writing on placards....</dd></dl>
<p>after which the T version essentially follows the E version again: 
</p>
<dl><dd>...he ordered the graves to be opened for him to place precious gifts in the same place, in order that the lodging of a single grave might hold membra of saints brought together from various regions whom heaven had received as equal in merit. Once these were honorably disposed and united, with violent devotion and a pious boldness of faith he took from the place where the blood of the martyr had flowed a lump of earth in which it was visible that the ground was red with blood preserved from the martyr's death while the persecutor was pale. When all these things were revealed and made known a huge crowd of people was brought to god with the help of our Lord Jesus Christ to whom is honour and glory for ever and ever. Amen.</dd></dl>
<p>It is possible to deduce from the interpolated passage that the name of the martyr was unknown before being revealed to Germanus, either in a vision he had of the martyr during his sea journey or in the dream he had in the basilica. It is also possible to deduce that it was simply the <i>acta</i>, or 'story of the martyrdom', of an already well known figure that was revealed to Germanus. The <i>acta</i> were then written down in <i>tituli</i> (translated above as 'placards'): that is possibly engraved in the walls of a church with illustrations. This might have been either in a church in Auxerre (Germanus's home town in Gaul) as argued by Sharpe and Wood, or in Britain. If the latter is the case, by being on public display, they might have served to give a definitive version of the saint's martyrdom, which could not be contradicted or reinterpreted (for instance by the addition of 'Pelagian' themes)<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> In any case, it has been argued by Sharpe and Wood that these <i>acta</i> written down in <i>tituli</i> were actually the original, very simple and short, first version of the <i><a href="/wiki/Passio_Albani" title="Passio Albani">Passio Albani</a></i> that has come down in the 'E' and later versions<sup id="cite_ref-Sharpe:_The_Late_Antique_Passion_of_St_Alban_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Sharpe:_The_Late_Antique_Passion_of_St_Alban-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>  That is very possible but, of course, quite unprovable, but it seems clear that the <i>Passio</i> originates with the circle of Germanus at Auxerre. As time went on, more and more details and wondrous events were added to the account its most detailed version in the 8th century, in Bede's <i><a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People" title="Ecclesiastical History of the English People">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i>.
</p><p>The location of the tomb of Saint Alban that Germanus visited is most often thought to have been <i><a href="/wiki/Verulamium" title="Verulamium">Verulamium</a></i>, now St Albans. That is on the basis of what is in fact the earliest mention of the martyr Alban in an indigenous British source, in the <i><a href="/wiki/De_Excidio_et_Conquestu_Britanniae" title="De Excidio et Conquestu Britanniae">De Excidio et Conquestu Britanniae</a></i> probably written in the second quarter of the fifth century,<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> by the British author <a href="/wiki/Gildas" title="Gildas">Gildas</a>. As part of his brief historical account he describes the persecution of Christians in Britain,which he identifies as part of the persecution of Diocletian, adding at the end of a passage about "their graves and the places where they suffered":
"I refer to Saint Alban of Verulam (<i>Verolamiensem</i>), Aaron and Iulius, citizens of Caerleon (<i>Legionum Urbis</i>) and others of both sexes, who in different places, displayed the highest spirit in the battle-line of Christ".  (<i>De Excidio</i> 10)<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Verulamium</i> location is supported by the fact that the topography of the <i>Passio</i> can be broadly, if not quite exactly,<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> matched to that of <i>Verulamium</i>, and Bede describes an important cult of Saint Alban there, by the early eighth century at least.<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> Some doubt, however, is encouraged by the fact that in his account of Albans's martyrdom Gildas (<i>De Excidio</i> 11) describes the martyr as crossing the Thames to his place of execution (at <i>Verulamium</i>/St Alban's there is only the much smaller <a href="/wiki/River_Ver" title="River Ver">River Ver</a>), which some have taken as an indication that the actual martyrdom (or the more original version of the story about it) was located in <i><a href="/wiki/Londinium" title="Londinium">Londinium</a></i>.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Stained_glass_window,_Sandhurst_Church_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1397884.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6a/Stained_glass_window%2C_Sandhurst_Church_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1397884.jpg/220px-Stained_glass_window%2C_Sandhurst_Church_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1397884.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="333" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6a/Stained_glass_window%2C_Sandhurst_Church_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1397884.jpg/330px-Stained_glass_window%2C_Sandhurst_Church_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1397884.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6a/Stained_glass_window%2C_Sandhurst_Church_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1397884.jpg 2x" data-file-width="410" data-file-height="620" /></a><figcaption>Stained glass window depicting Saint Alban and Saint George, Patron Saint of England</figcaption></figure>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Cult">Cult</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Cult">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The hilltop located outside Verulamium eventually became the centre of the cult devoted to Alban. It has been claimed (but doubted by some) that a <i>memoria</i> over the execution point and holding the remains of St Alban may have existed at the site from c. 300, possibly earlier. There was certainly a cult centre of St Alban at Verulamium by the time of Bede c. 731, and the mention in Gildas strongly suggests that it was already in existence by the early sixth century. However, when and how the cult of Saint Alban originated is the subject of some debate: there is little textual or archaeological evidence that a cult of Saint Alban existed before Germanus of Auxerre visited the site in 429. In fact, one version of the <i>Passio Albani</i> says that Germanus did not know the name or story of Saint Alban before visiting the site, and Alban appeared to him in a dream to reveal his identity and martyrdom story.<sup id="cite_ref-Die_Legende_des_h._Albanus_-_Meyer_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Die_Legende_des_h._Albanus_-_Meyer-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> That can be interpreted as suggesting (see above: The Disputed Historicity) that the cult of Saint Alban did not exist before the arrival of Germanus. Germanus is said to have taken away dust from the site, which was still marked with Alban's blood.<sup id="cite_ref-Germanus,_Bishop_of_Auxerre_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Germanus,_Bishop_of_Auxerre-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">cult and veneration of saints</a> was still in its infancy at this time, and it has been suggested that Germanus had a hand in creating and promoting the cult of Saint Alban.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Shrine-of-st-alban.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Shrine-of-st-alban.jpg/220px-Shrine-of-st-alban.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="334" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Shrine-of-st-alban.jpg/330px-Shrine-of-st-alban.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Shrine-of-st-alban.jpg/440px-Shrine-of-st-alban.jpg 2x" data-file-width="685" data-file-height="1041" /></a><figcaption>Shrine of Saint Alban in <a href="/wiki/St_Albans_Cathedral" title="St Albans Cathedral">St Albans Cathedral</a></figcaption></figure><p> Gildas writing probably in the second quarter of the fifth century calls Saint Alban <i>Verolamiensis</i>, 'of Verulamium' in a passage that refers to the "graves and places where they suffered" of the early British martyrs. This suggests there was at least a shrine but quite possibly a church to him at <i>Verulamium</i> by then. Certainly, Bede (c. 720) mentions a church there, dedicated to him. <a href="/wiki/Offa_of_Mercia" title="Offa of Mercia">Offa of Mercia</a> established a Benedictine Abbey and monastery at the site c. 793, but the abbey was probably sacked and destroyed by the Danes c. 890. It was rebuilt by the Normans, with construction beginning in 1077. By the <a href="/wiki/High_Middle_Ages" title="High Middle Ages">High Middle Ages</a>, St Albans ranked as the premier abbey in England. The <a href="/wiki/St_Albans_Cathedral" title="St Albans Cathedral">abbey church</a> now serves as the <a href="/wiki/Cathedral" title="Cathedral">cathedral</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_St_Albans" title="Diocese of St Albans">Diocese of St Albans</a>, established in 1877.
</p><p>In a chapel east of the crossing and high altar are remains of the 14th-century marble shrine of St Alban.<sup id="cite_ref-saint_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-saint-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In June 2002 a scapula (shoulder blade), believed to be a <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relic</a> of St Alban, was presented to St Albans Cathedral and placed inside the saint's restored 13th-century shrine. The bone was given by the <a href="/wiki/St._Pantaleon%27s_Church,_Cologne" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Pantaleon&#39;s Church, Cologne">Church of St Pantaleon</a> in <a href="/wiki/Cologne" title="Cologne">Cologne</a>, Germany <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://web.archive.org/web/20070616122431/http://sobornost.org/archive/newsletter-2002_50.html">News</a>. St Pantaleon's, like St Albans Cathedral a former <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> abbey church that had a shrine dedicated to St Alban, has possessed remains believed to be those of St Alban since the 10th century. It is entirely possible that further relics were acquired by the church in the 16th century at the time of the <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Monasteries" class="mw-redirect" title="Dissolution of the Monasteries">Dissolution of the Monasteries</a> in England, when many such relics were smuggled abroad to prevent their destruction. St Albans Abbey was dissolved in 1539.
</p><p>The largest relic of St Alban in England is the thigh of the protomartyr preserved at <a href="/wiki/St_Michael%27s_Abbey,_Farnborough" title="St Michael&#39;s Abbey, Farnborough">St Michael's Benedictine Abbey</a>, <a href="/wiki/Farnborough,_Hampshire" title="Farnborough, Hampshire">Farnborough, Hampshire</a>, which was transferred from the St Pantaleon's reliquary in the 1950s.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="On_the_Continent">On the Continent</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: On the Continent">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>There has also been an extensive cult of Saint Alban on the Continent from an early date such as in Mainz, Cologne and Basel on the Rhine as well as a number of other localities in Switzerland and Italy and a notable concentration in the French Alpine regions and the Rhone Valley.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Sometimes, the 'Saint Alban' concerned is regarded as a separate figure, other times, he is alternatively called <i>Albinus</i> (and often identified with 6th c. bishop, Saint <a href="/wiki/Albinus_of_Angers" title="Albinus of Angers">Albinus of Angers</a>), and at other times he is identified with the British martyr.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Lancaster_Priory_glass_22.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/Lancaster_Priory_glass_22.jpg/130px-Lancaster_Priory_glass_22.jpg" decoding="async" width="130" height="416" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/Lancaster_Priory_glass_22.jpg/195px-Lancaster_Priory_glass_22.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/Lancaster_Priory_glass_22.jpg/260px-Lancaster_Priory_glass_22.jpg 2x" data-file-width="608" data-file-height="1944" /></a><figcaption>Stained-glass window in <a href="/wiki/Lancaster_Priory" title="Lancaster Priory">Lancaster Priory</a> designed by <a href="/wiki/Carl_Almquist" title="Carl Almquist">Carl Almquist</a></figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/wiki/Saint_Pantaleon%27s_Church,_Cologne" title="Saint Pantaleon&#39;s Church, Cologne">Saint Pantaleon's Church, Cologne</a> holds relics said to be those of the British martyr Alban (as noted above). In fact, although identified with the British martyr, he was locally known as <i>Albinus</i>. His relics were said to have been brought from Rome by Empress <a href="/wiki/Theophanu" title="Theophanu">Theophanu</a> and placed in St Pantaleon's church in about 984:<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> the relics were miraculously saved from destruction in an accident on the way at a place that a later version of 1502, was identified as <a href="/wiki/Silenen,_Switzerland" class="mw-redirect" title="Silenen, Switzerland">Silenen, Switzerland</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> The original record was in a 12th-century manuscript that alleged that the relics were actually those of the British martyr, having been delivered to <a href="/wiki/Ravenna" title="Ravenna">Ravenna</a> by Germanus himself and taken from there to Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> Another church at Cologne is known to have been dedicated to the British Alban from the 12th century.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Saint Alban of <a href="/wiki/Basel" title="Basel">Basel</a> is recorded in the Berne recension of the <i><a href="/wiki/Martyrologium_Hieronymianum" title="Martyrologium Hieronymianum">Martyrologium Hieronymianum</a></i> of circa 800: "<i>Basilea civitate sancti Albani martyris</i>", where he would appear to be an independent local figure, being celebrated on 24 August but later identified with the Saint Alban of Mainz.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>St <a href="/wiki/Alban_of_Mainz" title="Alban of Mainz">Alban of Mainz</a> is recorded from 756.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> He was regarded as a separate figure in sources from Raban Maur's early 9th century martyrology, including a 10th-century <i>Life</i> by Gozwin of 1060–2<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> However, Hippolyte Delehaye suggested that he very probably represents, in origin, a localised version of the British martyr since his feast date was recorded as June 21 in the <i>Martyrologium Hieronymianum</i> (just a day before that of the British one, who actually appears on the 21st and 22nd in early recensions).<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The story in Raban Maur associates Alban of Mainz with a martyred bishop, <a href="/wiki/Aureus_of_Mainz" title="Aureus of Mainz">Aureus of Mainz</a> and two other martyrs, Ursus and <a href="/wiki/Theonestus" class="mw-redirect" title="Theonestus">Theonestus</a><sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> the latter of whom is said to have originated on the Greek island of <a href="/wiki/Naxos" title="Naxos">Naxos</a>, together with Alban. 
A Saint Alban of <a href="/wiki/Burano" title="Burano">Burano</a> (near Altino, Italy), meanwhile was associated with one Domenicus in a legendary tale reminiscent of one told about Dionysus.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Alban is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Lesser_Festival_(Anglicanism)" title="Lesser Festival (Anglicanism)">Lesser Festival</a> on <a href="/wiki/June_22" title="June 22">22 June</a><sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup> and he continues to be venerated in the <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican</a>, <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox</a> <a href="/wiki/Communion_(Christian)" class="mw-redirect" title="Communion (Christian)">Communions</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Fellowship_of_Saint_Alban_and_Saint_Sergius" title="Fellowship of Saint Alban and Saint Sergius">Fellowship of Saint Alban and Saint Sergius</a> is also named in part after Alban.
</p><p>Every year, during the weekend closest to his feast day, St Albans Cathedral hosts the "Alban Pilgrimage", with huge puppets re-enacting the events of Alban's martyrdom around the city of <a href="/wiki/St_Albans" title="St Albans">St Albans</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Besides his abbey, churches in England dedicated to Saint Alban include the former <a href="/wiki/St_Alban,_Wood_Street" title="St Alban, Wood Street">St Alban, Wood Street</a> in the <a href="/wiki/City_of_London" title="City of London">City of London</a>, <a href="/wiki/St_Alban%27s_Church,_Holborn" title="St Alban&#39;s Church, Holborn">St Alban's Church</a> at <a href="/wiki/Holborn" title="Holborn">Holborn</a> in central <a href="/wiki/London" title="London">London</a>, ones in the London suburbs of <a href="/wiki/Teddington" title="Teddington">Teddington</a>, <a href="/wiki/Croydon" title="Croydon">Croydon</a>, Cheam and <a href="/wiki/Ilford" title="Ilford">Ilford</a>, one in <a href="/wiki/Westcliff-on-Sea" title="Westcliff-on-Sea">Westcliff-on-Sea</a> in <a href="/wiki/Essex" title="Essex">Essex</a>, others in <a href="/wiki/Kingston_upon_Hull" title="Kingston upon Hull">Hull</a> and <a href="/wiki/Withernwick" title="Withernwick">Withernwick</a> in the <a href="/wiki/East_Riding_of_Yorkshire" title="East Riding of Yorkshire">East Riding of Yorkshire</a>, one in <a href="/wiki/Swaythling" title="Swaythling">Swaythling, Southampton</a>, one in <a href="/wiki/Northampton" title="Northampton">Northampton</a>, one in a Norwich suburb, one in <a href="/wiki/Bristol" title="Bristol">Bristol</a>, one in <a href="/wiki/St_Alban%27s_Church,_Tattenhall" title="St Alban&#39;s Church, Tattenhall">Tattenhall</a>, <a href="/wiki/Cheshire" title="Cheshire">Cheshire</a> and another in <a href="/wiki/Macclesfield" title="Macclesfield">Macclesfield</a>, Cheshire.  There is also <a href="/wiki/St_Alban%27s_Church,_West_Leigh" title="St Alban&#39;s Church, West Leigh">St Alban's, West Leigh</a> near <a href="/wiki/Havant" title="Havant">Havant</a> in <a href="/wiki/Hampshire" title="Hampshire">Hampshire</a>, and the <a href="/wiki/St_Alban_the_Martyr,_Birmingham" title="St Alban the Martyr, Birmingham">St Alban the Martyr Parish Church</a> of <a href="/wiki/Highgate,_Birmingham" title="Highgate, Birmingham">Highgate, Birmingham</a> (including <a href="/wiki/Ark_St_Alban%27s_Academy" title="Ark St Alban&#39;s Academy">Ark St Alban's Academy</a>). and St Alban the Martyr Church, <a href="/wiki/Cowley,_Oxfordshire" title="Cowley, Oxfordshire">Cowley</a>, Oxford.  Finally, a church is dedicated to Saint Alban at <a href="/wiki/Earsdon" title="Earsdon">Earsdon</a> Village, Northumberland, which is the nearest one to Bede's <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Holy Island</a>. There is also a St Albans parish and church in <a href="/wiki/Splott" title="Splott">Splott</a>, Cardiff.
</p><p>St Alban is the Patron Saint of the Liberal Catholic Church worldwide.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Outside_Britain">Outside Britain</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Outside Britain">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Churches, festivals, and places dedicated to Saint Alban outside Britain include the following:
</p>

<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Denmark">Denmark</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Alban&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Denmark">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p><a href="/wiki/St._Alban%27s_Church,_Copenhagen" title="St. Alban&#39;s Church, Copenhagen">St Alban's Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Copenhagen" title="Copenhagen">Copenhagen</a>, Denmark, which is the city's only Anglican church. It was built to the design of Sir <a href="/wiki/Arthur_Blomfield" title="Arthur Blomfield">Arthur Blomfield</a> and consecrated in 1887.<sup id="cite_ref-51" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-51">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> The connection with Denmark goes back to the Middle Ages where a church dedicated to Saint Alban was built in <a href="/wiki/Odense" title="Odense">Odense</a>. Supposedly, the relics of the saint had been brought here, maybe as early as the ninth century. It was in that church that King <a href="/wiki/Canute_IV_of_Denmark" title="Canute IV of Denmark">Canute IV of Denmark</a> (Saint Canute) was murdered in 1086.<sup id="cite_ref-52" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-52">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup> The original church no longer exists, but the Roman Catholic parish church of Odense, <a href="/wiki/St._Alban%27s_Church,_Odense" title="St. Alban&#39;s Church, Odense">St Alban's Church</a>, was consecrated in 1908.
</p>

<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Albans_DC_church_in_fall.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/St._Albans_DC_church_in_fall.jpg/220px-St._Albans_DC_church_in_fall.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/St._Albans_DC_church_in_fall.jpg/330px-St._Albans_DC_church_in_fall.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/St._Albans_DC_church_in_fall.jpg/440px-St._Albans_DC_church_in_fall.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2048" data-file-height="1366" /></a><figcaption>St Alban's Parish, Washington DC</figcaption></figure>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Alban%27s_Tokyo_3.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/St._Alban%27s_Tokyo_3.jpg/220px-St._Alban%27s_Tokyo_3.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/St._Alban%27s_Tokyo_3.jpg/330px-St._Alban%27s_Tokyo_3.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/St._Alban%27s_Tokyo_3.jpg/440px-St._Alban%27s_Tokyo_3.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2048" data-file-height="1536" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/St._Andrew%27s_Cathedral,_Tokyo#St._Alban&#39;s_by_St._Andrew&#39;s" title="St. Andrew&#39;s Cathedral, Tokyo">St Alban's, Tokyo</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>St. Albans is the name of a community in the borough of <a href="/wiki/Queens" title="Queens">Queens</a> in New York City. In 1899, a year after Queens became part of New York City, the new post office for the 600 residents<sup id="cite_ref-54" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-54">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup>
was named St. Albans, after St Albans in Hertfordshire, England, which itself was named after Saint Alban. The name had been in use for the area since at least 1894 for the name of the school district,<sup id="cite_ref-55" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-55">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup>
and the <a href="/wiki/St._Albans_station_(LIRR)" title="St. Albans station (LIRR)">LIRR station was named St. Albans</a> when it opened in 1898.  A 1909 map also shows a St Albans Avenue and a St Albans Place in the area.<sup id="cite_ref-1909map_56-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1909map-56">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The parish church of <a href="/wiki/St._Alban%27s_Episcopal_Church_(Washington,_D.C.)" title="St. Alban&#39;s Episcopal Church (Washington, D.C.)">St Alban's Episcopal Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Washington,_D.C." title="Washington, D.C.">Washington, D.C.</a>, was erected on Mount Saint Alban in 1854 using a bequest from a young woman, Phoebe Nourse, who earned the money sewing. St Alban's went on to found five mission churches in Washington, four of which still maintain active congregations of their own.<sup id="cite_ref-57" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-57">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup>  <a href="/wiki/Washington_National_Cathedral" title="Washington National Cathedral">Washington National Cathedral</a>, a cathedral of the <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church</a> in Washington D.C., is located next to the parish church, which preceded the laying of the Cathedral's cornerstone by 53 years. The <a href="/wiki/St._Albans_School_(Washington,_D.C.)" title="St. Albans School (Washington, D.C.)">St Albans School for Boys</a>, which is affiliated with and was established in 1909 soon after the construction of the Cathedral began, is also named for the saint.
</p><p>In 1972, a chapel named after St. Alban was erected and later consecrated in the Sabino Catchment area of <a href="/wiki/Tucson,_Arizona" title="Tucson, Arizona">Tucson, Arizona</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-58" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-58">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup> The chapel and the congregation later became St. Alban's Church and Parish.  It was in this church that the second Anglican female priest, and first female priest in Arizona, was ordained.
</p><p>In 1928, St. Alban's Chapel, an Episcopalian church, was established on the campus of <a href="/wiki/Louisiana_State_University" title="Louisiana State University">Louisiana State University</a> in Baton Rouge, Louisiana.<sup id="cite_ref-59" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-59">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After undergoing several name changes, St. Albans is the name of a community West of <a href="/wiki/Charleston,_West_Virginia" title="Charleston, West Virginia">Charleston, West Virginia</a>, the capital of the state.
