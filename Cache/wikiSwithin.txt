<p><b>Swithun</b> (or <b>Swithin</b>; <a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a>&#58; <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">Swīþhūn</i></span>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Swithunus</i>; died 863) was an Anglo-Saxon <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Winchester" title="Bishop of Winchester">bishop of Winchester</a> and subsequently patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Winchester_Cathedral" title="Winchester Cathedral">Winchester Cathedral</a>.  His historical importance as bishop is overshadowed by his reputation for posthumous miracle-working.  According to tradition, if it rains on Saint Swithun's bridge (Winchester) on his feast day (15 July) it will continue for forty days.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Swithun&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>St. Swithun was <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Winchester" title="Bishop of Winchester">Bishop of Winchester</a> from his consecration on 30 October 852 until his death on 2 July 863.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> However, he is scarcely mentioned in any document of his own time. His death is entered in the Canterbury manuscript of the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> (MS F) under the year 861.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He is recorded as a  witness to nine charters, the earliest of which (S 308) is dated 854.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>More than a hundred years later, when <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">Dunstan</a> and <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester" title="Æthelwold of Winchester">Æthelwold of Winchester</a> were inaugurating their church reform, Swithun was adopted as patron of the restored church at Winchester, formerly dedicated to <a href="/wiki/St._Peter" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Peter">St. Peter</a> and <a href="/wiki/Paul_of_Tarsus" class="mw-redirect" title="Paul of Tarsus">St. Paul</a>. His body was transferred from its almost forgotten grave to Æthelwold's new basilica on 15 July 971; according to contemporary writers, numerous miracles preceded and followed the move.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_legend">In legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Swithun&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: In legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St-Swithin.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/St-Swithin.jpg/220px-St-Swithin.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/St-Swithin.jpg/330px-St-Swithin.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/St-Swithin.jpg/440px-St-Swithin.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1536" data-file-height="2048" /></a><figcaption>Swithun shown in the <a href="/wiki/Benedictional_of_St._%C3%86thelwold" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictional of St. Æthelwold">Benedictional of St. Æthelwold</a>, <a href="/wiki/Winchester" title="Winchester">Winchester</a>, 10th century. <a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>, London.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The revival of Swithun's fame gave rise to a mass of legendary literature. The so-called <i>Vita S. Swithuni</i> of <a href="/wiki/Lantfred" title="Lantfred">Lantfred</a> and <a href="/wiki/Wulfstan_the_Cantor" title="Wulfstan the Cantor">Wulfstan</a>, written about 1000, hardly contains any biographical fact; all that has in later years passed for authentic detail of Swithun's life is extracted from a late eleventh-century <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> ascribed to <a href="/wiki/Goscelin" title="Goscelin">Goscelin</a> of St. Bertin's, a monk who came over to England with <a href="/wiki/Herman_(bishop_of_Salisbury)" title="Herman (bishop of Salisbury)">Hermann</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Salisbury" title="Bishop of Salisbury">bishop of Salisbury</a> from 1058 to 1078. According to this writer Saint Swithun was born in the reign of <a href="/wiki/Egbert_of_Wessex" class="mw-redirect" title="Egbert of Wessex">Egbert of Wessex</a>, and was ordained priest by <a href="/wiki/Helmstan" title="Helmstan">Helmstan</a>, bishop of Winchester (838-c. 852). His fame reached the king's ears, and he appointed him tutor of his son, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwulf_of_Wessex" class="mw-redirect" title="Æthelwulf of Wessex">Æthelwulf</a> (alias Adulphus), and considered him one of his chief friends.<sup id="cite_ref-webster_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-webster-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>  However, <a href="/wiki/Michael_Lapidge" title="Michael Lapidge">Michael Lapidge</a> describes the work as "pure fiction" and shows that the attribution to Goscelin is false.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Under Æthelwulf, Swithun was appointed bishop of Winchester, to which <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_see" title="Episcopal see">see</a> he was consecrated by Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Ceolnoth" title="Ceolnoth">Ceolnoth</a>. In his new office he was known for his piety and his zeal in building new churches or restoring old ones. At his request Æthelwulf gave the tenth of his royal lands to the Church. Swithun made his diocesan journeys on foot; when he gave a banquet he invited the poor and not the rich. <a href="/wiki/William_of_Malmesbury" title="William of Malmesbury">William of Malmesbury</a> adds that, if Bishop <a href="/wiki/Eahlstan" title="Eahlstan">Ealhstan</a> of <a href="/wiki/Sherborne" title="Sherborne">Sherborne</a> was Æthelwulf's minister for temporal matters, Swithun was the minister for spiritual matters.<sup id="cite_ref-webster_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-webster-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Swithun's best-known miracle was his restoration on a bridge of a basket of eggs that workmen had maliciously broken. Of stories connected with Swithun the two most famous are those of the Winchester egg-woman and Queen Emma's ordeal. The former is to be found in the hagiography attributed to Goscelin, the latter in <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Rudborne" title="Thomas Rudborne">Thomas Rudborne</a>'s <i>Historia major</i> (15th century), a work which is also responsible for the story that Swithun accompanied <a href="/wiki/Alfred_the_Great" title="Alfred the Great">Alfred</a> on his visit to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> in the 850s. He died on 2 July 862. On his deathbed Swithun begged that he should be buried outside the north wall of his cathedral where passers-by should pass over his grave and raindrops from the eaves drop upon it.<sup id="cite_ref-webster_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-webster-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Swithun&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Wincath1-11S7-9724wiki.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Wincath1-11S7-9724wiki.jpg/220px-Wincath1-11S7-9724wiki.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Wincath1-11S7-9724wiki.jpg/330px-Wincath1-11S7-9724wiki.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Wincath1-11S7-9724wiki.jpg/440px-Wincath1-11S7-9724wiki.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1024" data-file-height="682" /></a><figcaption>St. Swithun's memorial shrine in the <a href="/wiki/Retrochoir" class="mw-redirect" title="Retrochoir">retrochoir</a> of <a href="/wiki/Winchester_Cathedral" title="Winchester Cathedral">Winchester Cathedral</a> where the saint's relics were originally kept.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Swithun's <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> in England is on 15 July and in Norway (and formerly in medieval Wales) on 2 July. He is also listed on 2 July in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a>. He was moved from his grave to an indoor <a href="/wiki/Shrine" title="Shrine">shrine</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Old_Minster,_Winchester" title="Old Minster, Winchester">Old Minster</a> at <a href="/wiki/Winchester" title="Winchester">Winchester</a> in 971. His body was probably later split between a number of smaller shrines. His head was certainly detached and, in the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>, taken to <a href="/wiki/Canterbury_Cathedral" title="Canterbury Cathedral">Canterbury Cathedral</a>. <a href="/wiki/Peterborough_Cathedral" title="Peterborough Cathedral">Peterborough Abbey</a> had an arm.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> His main shrine was transferred into the new <a href="/wiki/Norman_architecture" title="Norman architecture">Norman</a> cathedral at <a href="/wiki/Winchester" title="Winchester">Winchester</a> in 1093. He was installed on a 'feretory platform' above and behind the high altar. The <a href="/wiki/Retrochoir" class="mw-redirect" title="Retrochoir">retrochoir</a> was built in the early 13th century to accommodate the huge numbers of pilgrims wishing to visit his shrine and enter the 'holy hole' beneath him. His empty <a href="/wiki/Tomb" title="Tomb">tomb</a> in the ruins of the Old Minster was also popular with visitors. The shrine was only moved into the retrochoir itself in 1476. It was <a href="/wiki/Demolition" title="Demolition">demolished</a> in 1538 during the <a href="/wiki/English_Reformation" title="English Reformation">English Reformation</a>. A modern representation of it now stands on the site.
</p><p>The shrine of Swithun at Winchester was supposedly a site of numerous miracles in the Middle Ages. <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester" title="Æthelwold of Winchester">Æthelwold of Winchester</a> ordered that all monks were to stop whatever they were doing and head to the church to praise God every time that a miracle happened. A story exists that the monks at some point got so fed up with this, because they sometimes had to wake up and go to the church three or four times each night, that they decided to stop going. St. Swithun then appeared in a dream to someone (possibly two people) and warned them that if they stopped going to the church, then miracles would cease. This person (or persons) then warned the monks about the dream they had, and the monks then caved in and decided to go to the church each time a miracle happened again.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Swithun is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Lesser_Festival_(Anglicanism)" title="Lesser Festival (Anglicanism)">Lesser Festival</a> on <a href="/wiki/July_15" title="July 15">15 July</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage">Patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Swithun&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Swithun is regarded as one of the saints to whom one should pray in the event of drought.<sup id="cite_ref-farmers_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-farmers-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Swithun&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There are in excess of forty <a href="/wiki/Church_(building)" title="Church (building)">churches</a> dedicated to St Swithun, which can be found throughout the south of England, especially in <a href="/wiki/Hampshire" title="Hampshire">Hampshire</a> – see list <a href="/wiki/St_Swithun%27s_Church_(disambiguation)" class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" title="St Swithun&#39;s Church (disambiguation)">St Swithun's Church (disambiguation)</a>. An example is <a href="/wiki/St_Swithun%27s_Church,_Headbourne_Worthy" class="mw-redirect" title="St Swithun&#39;s Church, Headbourne Worthy">St Swithun's, Headbourne Worthy</a>, to the north of Winchester. This church is surrounded on three sides by a brook that flows from a spring in the village; the <a href="/wiki/Lych_gate" class="mw-redirect" title="Lych gate">lych gate</a> on the south side is also a bridge over the brook, which is unusual. Other churches dedicated to St Swithun can be found at <a href="/wiki/Walcot,_Bath" title="Walcot, Bath">Walcot</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-walcot_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-walcot-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/St_Swithin%27s_Church,_Lincoln" title="St Swithin&#39;s Church, Lincoln">Lincoln</a>, <a href="/wiki/St_Swithun%27s_Church,_Worcester" title="St Swithun&#39;s Church, Worcester">Worcester</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-worcester_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-worcester-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Cheswardine, Shropshire and western <a href="/wiki/Norway" title="Norway">Norway</a>, where <a href="/wiki/Stavanger_Cathedral" title="Stavanger Cathedral">Stavanger Cathedral</a> is dedicated to him. He is also commemorated at St Swithin's Lane in the <a href="/wiki/City_of_London" title="City of London">City of London</a> (site of the former church of <a href="/wiki/St_Swithin,_London_Stone" title="St Swithin, London Stone">St Swithin, London Stone</a>, demolished after wartime damage in 1962), <a href="/wiki/St_Swithun%27s_School,_Winchester" title="St Swithun&#39;s School, Winchester">St Swithun's School</a> for girls in Winchester and St Swithun's quadrangle in <a href="/wiki/Magdalen_College,_Oxford" title="Magdalen College, Oxford">Magdalen College, Oxford</a>. In <a href="/wiki/Stavanger" title="Stavanger">Stavanger</a>, Norway, several schools and institutions are named “St Svithun” after him.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Proverb">Proverb</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Swithun&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Proverb">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Swithun_Statue.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/St_Swithun_Statue.jpg/220px-St_Swithun_Statue.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/St_Swithun_Statue.jpg/330px-St_Swithun_Statue.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/St_Swithun_Statue.jpg/440px-St_Swithun_Statue.jpg 2x" data-file-width="480" data-file-height="640" /></a><figcaption>Statue of St. Swithun originally on the <a href="/wiki/Fa%C3%A7ade" title="Façade">façade</a> of <a href="/wiki/Winchester_Cathedral" title="Winchester Cathedral">Winchester Cathedral</a>; now housed in the <a href="/wiki/Crypt" title="Crypt">Crypt</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The name of Swithun is best known today for a <a href="/wiki/United_Kingdom" title="United Kingdom">British</a> <a href="/wiki/Weather_lore" title="Weather lore">weather lore</a> <a href="/wiki/Proverb" title="Proverb">proverb</a>, which says that if it rains on St. Swithun's day, 15 July, it will rain for forty days.
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><div class="poem">
<p>St. Swithun's day if thou dost rain<br />
For forty days it will remain<br />
St. Swithun's day if thou be fair<br />
For forty days 'twill rain nae mare
</p>
</div></blockquote>
<p>A Buckinghamshire variation has:
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"><blockquote class="templatequote"><div class="poem">
<p>If on St. Swithun's day it really pours<br />
You're better off to stay indoors
</p>
</div></blockquote>
<p>Swithun was initially buried out of doors, rather than in his cathedral, apparently at his own request. William of Malmesbury recorded that the bishop left instructions that his body should be buried outside the church, <i>ubi et pedibus praetereuntium et stillicidiis ex alto rorantibus esset obnoxius</i> [where it might be subject to the feet of passers-by and to the raindrops pouring from on high], which has been taken as indicating that the legend was already well known in the 12th century.
</p><p>In 971 it was decided to move his body to a new indoor shrine, and one theory traces the origin of the legend to a heavy shower by which, on the day of the move, the saint marked his displeasure towards those who were removing his remains. This story, however, cannot be traced further back than the 17th or 18th century. Also, it is at variance with the 10th century writers, who all agreed that the move took place in accordance with the saint's desire expressed in a vision. <a href="/wiki/James_Raine" title="James Raine">James Raine</a> suggested that the legend was derived from the tremendous downpour of rain that occurred, according to the <a href="/wiki/Durham,_England" title="Durham, England">Durham</a> chroniclers, on St. Swithun's Day, 1315.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/John_Earle_(professor)" title="John Earle (professor)">John Earle</a> suggests that the legend comes from a <a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">pagan</a> or possibly prehistoric day of <a href="/wiki/Augury" title="Augury">augury</a>. In <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>, <a href="/wiki/Medardus" title="Medardus">St. Medard</a> (8 June), <a href="/wiki/Urban_of_Langres" title="Urban of Langres">Urban of Langres</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Gervasius_and_Protasius" title="Gervasius and Protasius">St. Gervase and St. Protais</a> (19 June) are credited with an influence on the weather almost identical with that attributed to St. Swithun in England. In <a href="/wiki/Flanders" title="Flanders">Flanders</a>, there is <a href="/wiki/Godelieve" title="Godelieve">St. Godelieve</a> (6 July) and in Germany the <a href="/wiki/Seven_Sleepers" title="Seven Sleepers">Seven Sleepers</a>' Day (27 June). In Russia it is the day of <a href="/wiki/Sampson_the_Hospitable" title="Sampson the Hospitable">Sampson the Hospitable</a> (27 June <a href="/wiki/Old_style" class="mw-redirect" title="Old style">old style</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> There is a scientific basis to the weather pattern behind the legend of St. Swithun's day. Around the middle of July, the <a href="/wiki/Jet_stream" title="Jet stream">jet stream</a> settles into a pattern which, in the majority of years, holds reasonably steady until the end of August. When the jet stream lies north of the <a href="/wiki/British_Isles" title="British Isles">British Isles</a> then continental high pressure is able to move in; when it lies across or south of the British Isles, Arctic air and Atlantic weather systems predominate.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The most false that the prediction has been, according to the <a href="/wiki/Guinness_Book_of_Records" class="mw-redirect" title="Guinness Book of Records">Guinness Book of Records</a>, were in 1924 when 13.5 hours of sunshine in London were followed by 30 of the next 40 days being wet, and in 1913 when a 15-hour rainstorm was followed by 30 dry days out of 40.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
