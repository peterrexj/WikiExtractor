<p><b>Pope Nicholas V</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Nicholaus V</i>; <a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Niccolò V</i>; 13 November 1397 – 24 March 1455),<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> born <b>Tommaso Parentucelli</b>, was head of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> and ruler of the <a href="/wiki/Papal_States" title="Papal States">Papal States</a> from 6 March 1447 until his death in March 1455.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFilelfoRobin2009370_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilelfoRobin2009370-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Pope_Eugene_IV" title="Pope Eugene IV">Pope Eugene</a> made him a <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_(Catholic_Church)" title="Cardinal (Catholic Church)">cardinal</a> in 1446 after successful trips to Italy and Germany, and when Eugene died the next year, Parentucelli was elected in his place. He took his name Nicholas in memory of his obligations to <a href="/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_Albergati" title="Niccolò Albergati">Niccolò Albergati</a>.
</p><p>The pontificate of Nicholas saw the <a href="/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople" title="Fall of Constantinople">fall of Constantinople</a> to the <a href="/wiki/Ottoman_Turks" title="Ottoman Turks">Ottoman Turks</a> and the end of the <a href="/wiki/Hundred_Years_War" class="mw-redirect" title="Hundred Years War">Hundred Years War</a>. He responded by calling a <a href="/wiki/Crusade" class="mw-redirect" title="Crusade">crusade</a> against the Ottomans, which never materialized. By the <a href="/wiki/Concordat_of_Vienna" title="Concordat of Vienna">Concordat of Vienna</a> he secured the recognition of papal rights over bishoprics and benefices. He also brought about the submission of the last of the antipopes, <a href="/wiki/Felix_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Felix V">Felix V</a>, and the dissolution of the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Basel" class="mw-redirect" title="Synod of Basel">Synod of Basel</a>. A key figure in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Renaissance" title="Roman Renaissance">Roman Renaissance</a>, Nicholas sought to make Rome the home of literature and art. He strengthened fortifications, restored aqueducts, and rebuilt many churches. He ordered design plans for what would eventually be the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_St._Peter" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica of St. Peter">Basilica of St. Peter</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Nicholas_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Tommaso Parentucelli was born to Andreola Bosi of Fivizzano and the physician Bartolomeo Parentucelli in <a href="/wiki/Sarzana" title="Sarzana">Sarzana</a>, an important town in <a href="/wiki/Lunigiana" title="Lunigiana">Lunigiana</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGregoroviusHamilton1900106_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGregoroviusHamilton1900106-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The Lunigiana region had long been fought over by competing Tuscan, Ligurian and Milanese forces. Three years before Parentucelli's birth, the town was taken from the Florentines by the Genoese Republic. His father died while he was young. Parentucelli later became a tutor, in <a href="/wiki/Florence" title="Florence">Florence</a>, to the families of the <a href="/wiki/Strozzi_family" title="Strozzi family">Strozzi</a> and <a href="/wiki/Albizzi" title="Albizzi">Albizzi</a>, where he met the leading <a href="/wiki/Humanism" title="Humanism">humanist</a> scholars.<sup id="cite_ref-Scannell_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Scannell-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Parentucelli studied at <a href="/wiki/Bologna" title="Bologna">Bologna</a> and Florence, gaining a degree in <a href="/wiki/Theology" title="Theology">theology</a> in 1422.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHay1995164_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHay1995164-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Bishop <a href="/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_Albergati" title="Niccolò Albergati">Niccolò Albergati</a> was so awestruck with his capabilities that he took him into his service and gave him the chance to pursue his studies further by sending him on a tour through Germany, France and England.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He was able to collect books, for which he had an intellectual's passion, wherever he went. Some of them survive with his marginal annotations.<sup id="cite_ref-Scannell_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Scannell-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Parentucelli attended the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Florence" title="Council of Florence">Council of Florence</a><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollingsworth1995238_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollingsworth1995238-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and in 1444, when his patron died, he was appointed <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Bologna" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Bologna">Bishop of Bologna</a> in his place.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETerpstra199534_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETerpstra199534-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Civic disorders at <a href="/wiki/Bologna" title="Bologna">Bologna</a> were prolonged, so <a href="/wiki/Pope_Eugene_IV" title="Pope Eugene IV">Pope Eugene IV</a> soon named him as one of the legates sent to <a href="/wiki/Frankfurt_am_Main" class="mw-redirect" title="Frankfurt am Main">Frankfurt</a>. He was to assist in negotiating an understanding between the <a href="/wiki/Papal_States" title="Papal States">Papal States</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire" title="Holy Roman Empire">Holy Roman Empire</a>, regarding undercutting or at least containing the reforming decrees of the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Basel" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Basel">Council of Basel</a> (1431–1439).<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Papacy">Papacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Nicholas_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Papacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Parentucelli's successful diplomacy gained him the reward, on his return to Rome, of the title <a href="/wiki/Cardinal-Priest" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinal-Priest">Cardinal-Priest</a> of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Susanna" class="mw-redirect" title="Santa Susanna">Santa Susanna</a> in December 1446. At the <a href="/wiki/Papal_conclave,_1447" class="mw-redirect" title="Papal conclave, 1447">papal conclave</a> of 1447 he was elected <a href="/wiki/Pope" title="Pope">pope</a> in succession to Eugene IV on 6 March. He took the name Nicholas in honour of his early benefactor, <a href="/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_Albergati" title="Niccolò Albergati">Niccolò Albergati</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Scannell_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Scannell-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Medieval_Bull_(FindID_132051).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/Medieval_Bull_%28FindID_132051%29.jpg/220px-Medieval_Bull_%28FindID_132051%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/Medieval_Bull_%28FindID_132051%29.jpg/330px-Medieval_Bull_%28FindID_132051%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/Medieval_Bull_%28FindID_132051%29.jpg/440px-Medieval_Bull_%28FindID_132051%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2560" data-file-height="1708" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Medieval_Bull_(FindID_132051).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Papal_bulla" class="mw-redirect" title="Papal bulla">Papal bulla</a> of Nicholas V</div></div></div>
<p>In only eight years, his pontificate delivered important achievements in the political, scientific, and literary history of the world. Politically, he needed to repair relationships which had broken down in the pontificate of Eugene IV. He called the congress which produced the <a href="/wiki/Treaty_of_Lodi" title="Treaty of Lodi">Treaty of Lodi</a>, secured peace with <a href="/wiki/Charles_VII_of_France" title="Charles VII of France">Charles VII of France</a>, and concluded the Concordat of Vienna or <i>Aschaffenburg</i> (17 February 1448) with the German King, <a href="/wiki/Frederick_III,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Frederick III, Holy Roman Emperor">Frederick III</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Scannell_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Scannell-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> by which the decrees of the Council of Basel against papal <a href="/wiki/Annates" title="Annates">annates</a> and reservations were abrogated so far as Germany was concerned. In the following year he secured a still greater tactical triumph with the resignation of the <a href="/wiki/Antipope_Felix_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Antipope Felix V">Antipope Felix V</a> on 7 April and his own recognition by the rump of the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Florence" title="Council of Florence">Council of Basel</a> that assembled at <a href="/wiki/Lausanne" title="Lausanne">Lausanne</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1450, Nicholas held a <a href="/wiki/Jubilee_(Christian)" class="mw-redirect" title="Jubilee (Christian)">Jubilee</a> at Rome,<sup id="cite_ref-Scannell_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Scannell-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and the offerings of the numerous pilgrims who thronged to Rome gave him the means of furthering the cause of culture in Italy, which he had so much at heart. In March 1452 he crowned <a href="/wiki/Frederick_III,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Frederick III, Holy Roman Emperor">Frederick III</a> as <a href="/wiki/Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Holy Roman Emperor">Holy Roman Emperor</a> in <a href="/wiki/St._Peter%27s_Basilica" title="St. Peter&#39;s Basilica">St. Peter's</a>, in what was the last imperial coronation held in Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Within the city of Rome, Nicholas introduced the fresh spirit of the <a href="/wiki/Renaissance" title="Renaissance">Renaissance</a> both intellectually and architecturally. His plans were of embellishing the city with new monuments worthy of the capital of the Christian world.<sup id="cite_ref-Scannell_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Scannell-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> It was in recognition of this commitment to building that <a href="/wiki/Leon_Battista_Alberti" title="Leon Battista Alberti">Leon Battista Alberti</a> dedicated to Nicholas V his treatise <i><a href="/wiki/De_re_aedificatoria" title="De re aedificatoria">De re aedificatoria</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Rebuilding_Rome">Rebuilding Rome</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Nicholas_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Rebuilding Rome">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>His first care was practical, to reinforce the city's fortifications,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECheetham1983180_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECheetham1983180-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> cleaning and even paving some main streets and restoring the water supply. The end of ancient Rome is sometimes dated from the destruction of its magnificent array of <a href="/wiki/Roman_aqueduct" title="Roman aqueduct">aqueducts</a> by 6th-century invaders. In the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a> Romans depended for water on wells and cisterns, and the poor dipped their water from the yellow <a href="/wiki/Tiber" title="Tiber">Tiber</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Aqua_Virgo" title="Aqua Virgo">Aqua Virgo</a> <a href="/wiki/Aqueduct_(watercourse)" class="mw-redirect" title="Aqueduct (watercourse)">aqueduct</a>, originally constructed by <a href="/wiki/Marcus_Vipsanius_Agrippa" title="Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa">Agrippa</a>, was restored by Nicholas and emptied into a simple basin that Alberti designed, the predecessor of the <a href="/wiki/Trevi_Fountain" title="Trevi Fountain">Trevi Fountain</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He continued restoration of the major Roman basilicas, but also of many other Roman churches including <a href="/wiki/Santi_Apostoli,_Rome" title="Santi Apostoli, Rome">Sant' Apostoli</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sant%27Eusebio" title="Sant&#39;Eusebio">Sant' Eusebio</a>, <a href="/wiki/San_Lorenzo_fuori_le_Mura" class="mw-redirect" title="San Lorenzo fuori le Mura">San Lorenzo fuori le Mura</a>, <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_in_Trastevere" title="Santa Maria in Trastevere">Santa Maria in Trastevere</a>, <a href="/wiki/Santa_Prassede" title="Santa Prassede">Santa Prassede</a>, <a href="/wiki/San_Salvatore_in_Onda" title="San Salvatore in Onda">San Salvatore</a>, <a href="/wiki/Santo_Stefano_al_Monte_Celio" title="Santo Stefano al Monte Celio">Santo Stefano Rotondo</a>, <a href="/wiki/San_Teodoro,_Rome" title="San Teodoro, Rome">San Teodoro</a>, and especially <a href="/wiki/Santi_Celso_e_Giuliano" title="Santi Celso e Giuliano">San Celso</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollingsworth1995240_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollingsworth1995240-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> He rebuilt the <a href="/wiki/Ponte_Sant%27_Angelo" class="mw-redirect" title="Ponte Sant&#39; Angelo">Ponte Sant' Angelo</a> which had collapsed in 1450, and supported the redevelopment of the surrounding area as a prestigious business and residential district.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollingsworth1995241_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollingsworth1995241-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Arts_patron">Arts patron</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Nicholas_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Arts patron">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:II._Sixtus%C3%81tadjaSzentL%C5%91rincnekAz_Egyh%C3%A1zKincseitKJ.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f4/II._Sixtus%C3%81tadjaSzentL%C5%91rincnekAz_Egyh%C3%A1zKincseitKJ.jpg/220px-II._Sixtus%C3%81tadjaSzentL%C5%91rincnekAz_Egyh%C3%A1zKincseitKJ.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="292" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f4/II._Sixtus%C3%81tadjaSzentL%C5%91rincnekAz_Egyh%C3%A1zKincseitKJ.jpg/330px-II._Sixtus%C3%81tadjaSzentL%C5%91rincnekAz_Egyh%C3%A1zKincseitKJ.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f4/II._Sixtus%C3%81tadjaSzentL%C5%91rincnekAz_Egyh%C3%A1zKincseitKJ.jpg/440px-II._Sixtus%C3%81tadjaSzentL%C5%91rincnekAz_Egyh%C3%A1zKincseitKJ.jpg 2x" data-file-width="639" data-file-height="847" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:II._Sixtus%C3%81tadjaSzentL%C5%91rincnekAz_Egyh%C3%A1zKincseitKJ.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Fresco in the Niccoline Chapel depicting <a href="/wiki/Pope_Sixtus_II" title="Pope Sixtus II">Pope Sixtus II</a> with the physical features of Pope Nicholas V</div></div></div>
<p>Nicholas V's major focus was on establishing the Vatican as the official residence of the Papacy, replacing the Lateran Palace. He added a substantial new wing including <a href="/wiki/Niccoline_Chapel" title="Niccoline Chapel">a private chapel</a> to the <a href="/wiki/Vatican_Palace" class="mw-redirect" title="Vatican Palace">Vatican</a>, and&#160;&#8211;&#32;according to <a href="/wiki/Giannozzo_Manetti" title="Giannozzo Manetti">Giannozzo Manetti</a>, biographer of Nicholas&#160;&#8211;&#32;planned substantial changes to the <a href="/wiki/Borgo_(rione_of_Rome)" title="Borgo (rione of Rome)">Borgo</a> district. He also laid up 2,522 cartloads of marble from the dilapidated <a href="/wiki/Colosseum" title="Colosseum">Colosseum</a> for use in the later constructions.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEManetti1734_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEManetti1734-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Pope's contemporaries criticised his lavish expenditure on building: <a href="/wiki/Giannozzo_Manetti" title="Giannozzo Manetti">Manetti</a> drew parallels with the wealth and expenditure of Solomon, suggesting that Papal wealth was acceptable so long as it was expended to the glory of God and the good of the Church.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollingsworth1995243_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollingsworth1995243-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The decoration of the <a href="/wiki/Niccoline_Chapel" title="Niccoline Chapel">Niccoline Chapel</a> by <a href="/wiki/Fra_Angelico" title="Fra Angelico">Fra Angelico</a> demonstrated this message through its depictions of <a href="/wiki/St_Lawrence" class="mw-redirect" title="St Lawrence">St Lawrence</a> (martyred for refusing to hand to the Roman state the wealth of the Church) and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Stephen" title="Saint Stephen">St Stephen</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Under the generous patronage of Nicholas, <a href="/wiki/Humanism" title="Humanism">humanism</a> made rapid strides as well. The new humanist learning had been hitherto looked on with suspicion in Rome, a possible source of <a href="/wiki/Schism_(religion)" class="mw-redirect" title="Schism (religion)">schism</a> and <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heresy</a> from an unhealthy interest in <a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">paganism</a>. For Nicholas, humanism became a tool for the cultural aggrandizement of the Christian capital, and he sent emissaries to the East to attract Greek scholars after the <a href="/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople" title="Fall of Constantinople">fall of Constantinople</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDuffy1997181_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDuffy1997181-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> The pope also employed <a href="/wiki/Lorenzo_Valla" title="Lorenzo Valla">Lorenzo Valla</a> to translate Greek histories,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESider2005147_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESider2005147-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> pagan as well as Christian, into <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a>. This industry, coming just before the dawn of <a href="/wiki/Printing" title="Printing">printing</a>, contributed enormously to the sudden expansion of the intellectual horizon.
</p><p>Nicholas, with assistance from <a href="/wiki/Enoch_of_Ascoli" title="Enoch of Ascoli">Enoch of Ascoli</a> and <a href="/wiki/Giovanni_Tortelli" title="Giovanni Tortelli">Giovanni Tortelli</a>, founded a library of five thousand volumes, including manuscripts rescued from the <a href="/wiki/Ottoman_Empire" title="Ottoman Empire">Turks</a> after the fall of Constantinople.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> The Pope himself was a man of vast erudition, and his friend Aeneas Silvius Piccolomini, later <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_II" title="Pope Pius II">Pope Pius II</a>, said of him that "what he does not know is outside the range of human knowledge". A lifelong bibliophile, he treasured books: while the Vatican library was still being designed and planned, he kept the rarest books near to him in his bedroom, with the others in a room nearby. Often thinking fondly of his former work as a librarian, he once remarked, "I had more happiness in a day than now in a whole year."<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He was compelled, however, to add that the lustre of his pontificate would be forever dulled by the <a href="/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople" title="Fall of Constantinople">fall of Constantinople</a>, which the <a href="/wiki/Ottoman_Empire" title="Ottoman Empire">Turks</a> took in 1453. Unsuccessful in a campaign to unite Christian powers to come to the aid of Constantinople, just before that great citadel was conquered, Nicholas had ordered 10 papal ships to sail with ships from Genoa, Venice and Naples to defend the capital of the Eastern Roman Empire. However, the ancient capital fell before the ships could offer any aid. The Pope bitterly felt this catastrophe as a double blow to <a href="/wiki/Christendom" title="Christendom">Christendom</a> and to <a href="/wiki/Greek_literature" title="Greek literature">Greek letters</a>. "It is a second death", wrote Aeneas Silvius, "to Homer and <a href="/wiki/Plato" title="Plato">Plato</a>."<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Nicholas preached a <a href="/wiki/Crusade" class="mw-redirect" title="Crusade">crusade</a> and endeavoured to reconcile the mutual animosities of the Italian states, but without much success.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_6-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In undertaking these works, Nicholas was moved "to strengthen the weak faith of the populace by the greatness of that which it sees". The Roman populace, however, appreciated neither his motives nor their results, and in 1452 a formidable conspiracy for the overthrow of the papal government under the leadership of <a href="/wiki/Stefano_Porcari" title="Stefano Porcari">Stefano Porcari</a> was discovered and crushed. This revelation of disaffection, together with the fall of Constantinople in 1453, darkened the last years of Pope Nicholas. "As Thomas of Sarzana", he said, "I had more happiness in a day than now in a whole year".<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_6-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Slavery">Slavery</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Nicholas_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Slavery">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In late spring of 1452 Byzantine Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantine_XI" class="mw-redirect" title="Constantine XI">Constantine XI</a> wrote to Pope Nicholas for help against the impending siege by Ottoman Sultan <a href="/wiki/Mehmed_II" title="Mehmed II">Mehmed II</a>. Nicholas issued the bull <i><a href="/wiki/Dum_Diversas" title="Dum Diversas">Dum Diversas</a></i> (18 June 1452) authorizing King Alfonso V of Portugal to "attack, conquer, and subjugate Saracens, pagans and other enemies of Christ wherever they may be found". Issued less than a year before the fall of Constantinople, the bull may have been intended to begin another crusade against the <a href="/wiki/Ottoman_Empire" title="Ottoman Empire">Ottoman Empire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-sardar_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sardar-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Portuguese_Morocco.PNG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Portuguese_Morocco.PNG/220px-Portuguese_Morocco.PNG" decoding="async" width="220" height="189" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Portuguese_Morocco.PNG/330px-Portuguese_Morocco.PNG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Portuguese_Morocco.PNG/440px-Portuguese_Morocco.PNG 2x" data-file-width="586" data-file-height="504" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Portuguese_Morocco.PNG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Portuguese possessions in Morocco (1415–1769)</div></div></div> 
<p>Ownership of the <a href="/wiki/Canary_Islands" title="Canary Islands">Canary Islands</a> continued to be a source of dispute between Spain and Portugal and Nicholas was asked to settle the matter, ultimately in favor of the Portuguese.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStogre199265_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStogre199265-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> The geographical area of the concession given in the bull is not explicit, but historian <a href="/wiki/Richard_Raiswell" title="Richard Raiswell">Richard Raiswell</a> finds that it clearly refers to the recently discovered lands along the coast of West Africa.<sup id="cite_ref-Raiswell_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Raiswell-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Portuguese ventures were intended to compete with the Muslim trans-Sahara caravans, which played a key role in the highly profitable Muslim slave trade and also held a monopoly on West African gold and ivory.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Portuguese claimed territorial rights along the African coast by virtue of having invested time and treasure in discovering it; the Castilian claim was based on their being the heirs of the <a href="/wiki/Visigoths" title="Visigoths">Visigoths</a>. In 1454 a fleet of caravels from Seville and Cádiz traded along the African coast and upon their return, were intercepted by a Portuguese squadron. <a href="/wiki/Enrique_IV_of_Castile" class="mw-redirect" title="Enrique IV of Castile">Enrique IV of Castile</a> threatened war. Afonso V appealed to the Pope for moral support of Portugal's right to a monopoly of trade in lands she discovered.<sup id="cite_ref-Bown_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bown-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The papal bull <i><a href="/wiki/Romanus_Pontifex" title="Romanus Pontifex">Romanus Pontifex</a></i>, issued on 8 January 1455, endorsed Portuguese possession of Cuerta (which they already held), and the exclusive right to trade, navigation, and fishing in the discovered lands, and reaffirmed the previous <i>Dum Diversas</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-nyt-elliott-hughes-2019_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nyt-elliott-hughes-2019-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> It granted permission to Afonso and his heirs to "... make purchases and sales of any things and goods, and victuals whatsoever, as it may seem fit, with any Saracens and infidels in said regions; ... provided they be not iron instruments, wood used for construction, cordage, ships, and any kinds of armor."<sup id="cite_ref-Davenport_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Davenport-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The bull conferred exclusive trading rights to the Portuguese between Morocco and the Indies with the rights to conquer and convert the inhabitants.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> A significant concession given by Nicholas in a brief issued to King Alfonso in 1454 extended the rights granted to existing territories to all those that might be taken in the future.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Consistent with these broad aims, it allowed the Portuguese "to invade, search out, capture, vanquish, and subdue all Saracens and pagans whatsoever, and other enemies of Christ wheresoever placed, and the kingdoms, dukedoms, principalities, dominions, possessions, and all movable and immovable goods whatsoever held and possessed by them and to reduce their persons to perpetual slavery". However, together with a second reference to some who have already been enslaved, this has been used to suggest that Nicholas sanctioned the <a href="/wiki/Atlantic_slave_trade" title="Atlantic slave trade">purchase of black slaves</a> from "the infidel":<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> "... many Guineamen and other negroes, taken by force, and some by barter of unprohibited articles, or by other lawful contract of purchase, have been ... converted to the Catholic faith, and it is hoped, by the help of divine mercy, that if such progress be continued with them, either those peoples will be converted to the faith or at least the souls of many of them will be gained for Christ."<sup id="cite_ref-Davenport_27-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Davenport-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It is on this basis that it has been argued that collectively the two bulls issued by Nicholas gave the Portuguese the rights to <a href="/wiki/History_of_slavery" title="History of slavery">acquire slaves</a> along the African coast by force or trade.<sup id="cite_ref-nyt-elliott-hughes-2019_26-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nyt-elliott-hughes-2019-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> By dealing with local African chieftains and Muslim slave traders, the Portuguese sought to become key European players in the lucrative slave trade. The concessions given in them were confirmed by bulls issued by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Callixtus_III" title="Pope Callixtus III">Pope Callixtus III</a> (<i>Inter Caetera quae</i> in 1456), <a href="/wiki/Pope_Sixtus_IV" title="Pope Sixtus IV">Sixtus IV</a> (<i>Aeterni regis</i> in 1481), and they became the models for subsequent bulls issued by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Alexander_VI" title="Pope Alexander VI">Pope Alexander VI</a>: <i><a href="/wiki/Eximiae_devotionis" title="Eximiae devotionis">Eximiae devotionis</a></i> (3 May 1493),  <i><a href="/wiki/Inter_Caetera" class="mw-redirect" title="Inter Caetera">Inter Caetera</a></i> (4 May 1493) and <i>Dudum Siquidem</i> (23 September 1493), in which he conferred similar rights to Spain relating to the newly discovered lands in the Americas.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
