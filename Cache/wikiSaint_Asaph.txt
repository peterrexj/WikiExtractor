<p>Saint <b>Asaph</b> (or <b>Asaf</b>, <b>Asa</b>) was, in the second half of the 6th century, the first <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_St_Asaph" title="Bishop of St Asaph">Bishop of St Asaph</a>, i.e. bishop of the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Saint_Asaph" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Saint Asaph">diocese of Saint Asaph</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Asaph&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>No traditional <a href="/wiki/Welsh_language" title="Welsh language">Welsh</a> account devoted to the life of Asaph exists.  He is, though, well-attested to through place names. Local tradition points out many landmarks attested to him; his ash tree, his church, his well and his Valley. Many local names bear the "asa" associated with his name; Onnen Asa, Ffynnon Asa, Llanasa, Pantasa. All these sites are near <a href="/wiki/Holywell,_Flintshire" title="Holywell, Flintshire">Holywell</a> in <a href="/wiki/Tegeingl" title="Tegeingl">Tegeingl</a> (<a href="/wiki/Flintshire" title="Flintshire">Flintshire</a>), indicating probably that the saint may once have had a hermitage in that area.<sup id="cite_ref-pollen_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pollen-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The <i><a href="/wiki/Bonedd_y_Saint" title="Bonedd y Saint">Bonedd y Saint</a></i> tells us that he was a son of King <a href="/wiki/Sawyl_Penuchel" title="Sawyl Penuchel">Sawyl Penuchel</a> from the <i>Old North</i> or <a href="/wiki/Y_Gogledd_Hen" class="mw-redirect" title="Y Gogledd Hen">Yr Hen Ogledd</a>; his mother was said to be Gwenaseth, daughter of Rhufon Rhufoniog.<sup id="cite_ref-Rice_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Rice-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The want of a Welsh <i>Life</i>, however, is in part compensated for by <a href="/wiki/Jocelyn_of_Furness" title="Jocelyn of Furness">Jocelyn of Furness</a>'s <i>Life of St. Kentigern</i>, which tells the story of Cyndeyrn (Kentigern) alias <a href="/wiki/Saint_Mungo" title="Saint Mungo">Saint Mungo</a>, the founder of the <a href="/wiki/Bishops_and_Archbishops_of_Glasgow" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishops and Archbishops of Glasgow">Diocese of Glasgow</a>. During his exile (c. 545), Kentigern took himself to Wales and there founded the <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">Celtic Monastery</a> of <a href="/wiki/Llanelwy" class="mw-redirect" title="Llanelwy">Llanelwy</a> (the church on the <a href="/wiki/River_Elwy" title="River Elwy">River Elwy</a>), as the Welsh still call the city of <a href="/wiki/St_Asaph" title="St Asaph">St Asaph</a>. Llanelwy is among the best documented of Celtic monasteries: the church was described as built "of smoothed wood, after the fashion of the Britons, seeing that they could not yet build of stone". The 965 disciples, of whom Asaph was one, were divided into three groups: 300 of the unlettered farmed the outlying lands, 300 worked in the offices around the monastery, and 365 (the number corresponds to the days of the year) attended to the divine services. Of these the oldest assisted Kentigern in the government of the diocese, and the rest were subdivided into three choirs. "As soon as one choir had terminated its service in church, immediately another entering commenced it: and that again being concluded another entered to celebrate."<sup id="cite_ref-pollen_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pollen-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Kentigern would frequently pray standing in the icy cold river. On one occasion, having suffered very severely under this hardship, he sent the boy, Asaph, who was then attending him, to bring a brand of blazing wood to burn and warm him. Asaph instead brought him live coals in his apron, and the miracle revealed to Kentigern the sanctity of his disciple. So when the old man was recalled to <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Strathclyde" title="Kingdom of Strathclyde">Strathclyde</a>, after the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Arfderydd" title="Battle of Arfderydd">Battle of Arfderydd</a>, in 573 Asaph was consecrated bishop to succeed him, and became the first Welsh bishop of the see.<sup id="cite_ref-pollen_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pollen-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Asaph is said to have died in 596.<sup id="cite_ref-city_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-city-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Asaph&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In the 2004 edition of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a>, Asaph is listed under 1 May with the Latin name <i>Asáphi</i>. He is described simply as 'bishop and abbot of Elvae (Elwy) in Wales, after whom the see is named'.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Although the traditional date of his death is recognised as 1 May, the current <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> liturgical calendar for Wales<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> keeps his memorial on 5 May, the 1st being designated for <a href="/wiki/St_Joseph" class="mw-redirect" title="St Joseph">St Joseph</a> the Worker.
</p><p>The Concise Oxford Dictionary of the Christian Church<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> indicates that St Asaph's feast, though now celebrated on 5 May, was previously celebrated on 11 May.
</p><p>Asaph is also recognized as founder of the church of <a href="/wiki/Llanasa" title="Llanasa">Llanasa</a> in Flintshire.<sup id="cite_ref-Rice_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Rice-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There are streets in <a href="/wiki/Brockley" title="Brockley">Brockley</a>, <a href="/wiki/South_East_(London_sub_region)" class="mw-redirect" title="South East (London sub region)">South East London</a>; <a href="/wiki/Christchurch" title="Christchurch">Christchurch</a>, <a href="/wiki/New_Zealand" title="New Zealand">New Zealand</a>; <a href="/wiki/Bala_Cynwyd,_Pennsylvania" title="Bala Cynwyd, Pennsylvania">Bala Cynwyd, Pennsylvania</a>; and in <a href="/wiki/Alexandria,_Virginia" title="Alexandria, Virginia">Alexandria, Virginia</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Leominster,_Massachusetts" title="Leominster, Massachusetts">Leominster, Massachusetts</a> named in his honor.
</p><p>A hymn tune used with the Scottish paraphrase 'How bright these glorious spirits shine' is called St Asaph. It was first published in 1825 in a collection by the Edinburgh musician <a href="/wiki/Robert_Archibald_Smith" title="Robert Archibald Smith">Robert Archibald Smith</a>, and attributed by him to Giovanni Marie Giornovichi 1745–1804.
</p>
