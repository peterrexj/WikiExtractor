<p><b>Jutta of Kulmsee</b> (also called <i>Judith</i> and <i>Otta</i>.<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1200-May 5, 1264</span>),<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-448_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-448-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> was a German member of the <a href="/wiki/Third_Order_of_Saint_Francis" title="Third Order of Saint Francis">Third Order of Saint Francis</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Jutta_of_Kulmsee&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Jutta was born in <a href="/wiki/Sangerhausen" title="Sangerhausen">Sangerhausen, Germany</a>, southwest of <a href="/wiki/Eisleben" title="Eisleben">Eiseleben</a>, to the noble family of Sangerhausen, who were related to the <a href="/wiki/Duchy_of_Brunswick" title="Duchy of Brunswick">dukes of Brunswick</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-449_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-449-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> She is the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Prussia" title="Prussia">Prussia</a> and was a member of the <a href="/wiki/Third_Order_of_Saint_Francis" title="Third Order of Saint Francis">Third Order of Saint Francis</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-2392_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-2392-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-ellsberg-34_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ellsberg-34-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> She was called "the first Prussian <a href="/wiki/Anchorite" title="Anchorite">anchoress</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> There are few records of Jutta's life because anchoritic life was unusual outside of southwest Germany, although her biography appears in the <i><a href="/wiki/Acta_Sanctorum" title="Acta Sanctorum">Acta Sanctorum</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>According to scholar Michelle Sauer, Jutta's life "bore surprising resemblance" to <a href="/wiki/Dorothy_of_Montau" class="mw-redirect" title="Dorothy of Montau">Dorothy of Montau</a>, another Prussian anchoress who lived later, during the 14th century.<sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Jutta is represented in a grey habit with long sleeves, with a black strap or chord tied around her waist and neck, and is often depicted alongside Dorothea of Montau.<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-449_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-449-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> As Sauer put it, "Jutta claimed only three things could result in a close personal relationship with God: painful sickness, exile from home, and voluntary poverty".<sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Her feast day is May 5.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-2392_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-2392-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At the age of fifteen, Jutta married a nobleman with whom she had children.<sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> According to <i>The Franciscan Book of Saints</i>, edited by Marion A. Habig, "in the married state she was more intent upon virtue and the fear of God than upon worldly honor”.<sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Hagiographer Robert Ellsberg called their marriage "happy",<sup id="cite_ref-ellsberg-34_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ellsberg-34-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> although at first, Jutta's piety displeased her husband, but he eventually came to value it and they raised their children in the Christian faith.<sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He died while on a <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> to <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>, and Jutta raised their children alone.<sup id="cite_ref-ellsberg-34_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ellsberg-34-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When they reached a suitable age, each child entered the religious life,<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-449_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-449-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-ellsberg-34_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ellsberg-34-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> which left Jutta "free to pursue her heart’s desire".<sup id="cite_ref-ellsberg-34_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ellsberg-34-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Inspired by <a href="/wiki/Elizabeth_of_Hungary" title="Elizabeth of Hungary">Elizabeth of Hungary</a>, a princess who was also from Thuringia and who had renounced her royal status and embraced poverty, Jutta joined the Third Order of Francis and, after receiving permission from her confessor, gave all her belongings away to the poor, wore simple clothes, and became a wandering pilgrim,<sup id="cite_ref-ellsberg-34_6-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ellsberg-34-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> going barefoot in the summer and winter and caring for the poor and homeless.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> As the <i>Franciscan Book of Saints</i> put it, “she devoted herself entirely to the care of the sick, especially the lepers, and to the poor, whom she visited in their hovels and provided with all necessities. The crippled and the blind she led by the hand to her home and took care of their needs”.<sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Although her devotion was met with derision, others were "moved by her piety and the austerity she had exchanged for her previous privilege".<sup id="cite_ref-ellsberg-34_6-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ellsberg-34-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Franciscan Book of Saints</i> also states that "she recognized in the poor her Divine Lord, and deemed herself happy and highly honored that she could render them such services".<sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As hagiographer Alban Butler put it, she "received wonderful graces, for besides being favoured with many visions and revelations, she was given an infused understanding of the Holy Scriptures".<sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Christ appeared to her in visions during prayer<sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and told her, "All my treasures are yours, and yours are Mine", which encouraged her to continuing serving the poor.<sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He appeared to her, telling her to "Follow me",<sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and again when she was ill and close to death, giving her the choice of "entering into glory at the time, or of suffering still more out of love for Him".<sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> She chose to continue living and was able to become an anchoress.<sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1260,<sup id="cite_ref-habig-465_2-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-465-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-449_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-449-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> she "enclosed herself"<sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> in the forest near <a href="/wiki/Che%C5%82m%C5%BCa" title="Chełmża">Kulmsee</a> (modern-day <a href="/wiki/Che%C5%82m%C5%BCa" title="Chełmża">Chełmża</a>, a town in north-central Poland), where she was "sheltered"<sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> in a cell attached to a church by the <a href="/wiki/Teutonic_Order" title="Teutonic Order">Teutonic Order</a>, whose <a href="/wiki/Grand_Master_of_the_Teutonic_Order" title="Grand Master of the Teutonic Order">Grand Master</a>, <a href="/wiki/Anno_von_Sangershausen" title="Anno von Sangershausen">Anno von Sangerhausen</a>, was her relative.<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-449_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-449-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> She devoted herself for the rest of her life to praying for the conversion of the pagans and the newly converted in Prussia.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-sauer-1342_7-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sauer-1342-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Her Christian neighbors reported witnessing her "raised high in the air in the fervor of her devotion”,<sup id="cite_ref-habig-466_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-466-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> like she was upheld by angels.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Hagiographer Agnes Dunbar reports that Jutta miraculously walked across the pond to get to the church in Kulmsee.<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-449_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-449-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>She lived in her cell for four years and died of a fever in 1264. She was buried at the church at Kulmsee; even though her confessor Henry Heidenreich attempted to bury her quietly as she had requested and no announcement was made, more people gathered there than ever before and 13 priests were present at her funeral.<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-449_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-449-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-butler-240_4-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-240-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-habig-466_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-466-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> <i>The Franciscan Book of Saints</i> states that the church was "filled with a wonderful odor”<sup id="cite_ref-habig-466_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-466-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> and that a special chapel was built in her honor because of the many miracles that occurred there.<sup id="cite_ref-habig-466_8-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-habig-466-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The process for her canonization began 15 years after her death, in recognition of "her great renown for sanctity and the numerous miracles wrought at her tomb".<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-449_3-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-449-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Mechthild_of_Magdeburg" title="Mechthild of Magdeburg">Saint Mechthild of Magdeburg</a>, the 13th-century <a href="/wiki/Beguines_and_Beghards" title="Beguines and Beghards">Beguine</a> and the first Christian <a href="/wiki/Mysticism" title="Mysticism">mystic</a> to write in German, had a great admiration for Jutta.<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-449_3-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-449-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
