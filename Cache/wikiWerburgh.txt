<p><b>Werburgh</b> (also <i>Wærburh</i>, <i>Werburh</i>, <i>Werburga</i>, meaning "true city"; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Vereburga</i>;<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> c. AD 650 – 3 February 699/700) was an <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon">Anglo-Saxon</a> princess who became the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the city of <a href="/wiki/Chester" title="Chester">Chester</a> in <a href="/wiki/Cheshire" title="Cheshire">Cheshire</a>. Her feast day is 3 February.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Werburgh&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Werburgh was born at <a href="/wiki/Stone,_Staffordshire" title="Stone, Staffordshire">Stone</a> (now in <a href="/wiki/Staffordshire" title="Staffordshire">Staffordshire</a>), and was the daughter of King <a href="/wiki/Wulfhere_of_Mercia" title="Wulfhere of Mercia">Wulfhere of Mercia</a> (himself the Christian son of the <a href="/wiki/Celtic_polytheism" class="mw-redirect" title="Celtic polytheism">pagan</a> King <a href="/wiki/Penda_of_Mercia" title="Penda of Mercia">Penda of Mercia</a>) and his wife <a href="/wiki/Ermenilda_of_Ely" title="Ermenilda of Ely">St Ermenilda</a>, herself daughter of the <a href="/wiki/Eorcenberht_of_Kent" title="Eorcenberht of Kent">King of Kent</a>. She obtained her father's consent to enter the <a href="/wiki/Ely_Cathedral" title="Ely Cathedral">Abbey of Ely</a>, which had been founded by her great-aunt <a href="/wiki/Etheldreda" class="mw-redirect" title="Etheldreda">Etheldreda</a> (or Audrey), the first <a href="/wiki/Abbess_of_Ely" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbess of Ely">Abbess of Ely</a> and former queen of <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbria</a>, whose fame was widespread. Werburgh was trained at home by St <a href="/wiki/Chad_of_Mercia" title="Chad of Mercia">Chad</a><sup id="cite_ref-casanova_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-casanova-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> (afterwards Bishop of Lichfield), and by her mother; and in the cloister by her aunt and grandmother. Werburgh was a <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nun</a> for most of her life. During some of her life she was resident in <a href="/wiki/Weedon_Bec" title="Weedon Bec">Weedon Bec</a>, Northamptonshire.
</p><p>Werburgh was instrumental in <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a> reform across England. She eventually succeeded her mother Ermenilda, her grandmother <a href="/wiki/Seaxburh_of_Ely" title="Seaxburh of Ely">Seaxburh</a>, and great-aunt Etheldreda as fourth Abbess of Ely. She died on 3 February 700 and was buried at <a href="/wiki/Hanbury,_Staffordshire" title="Hanbury, Staffordshire">Hanbury</a> in Staffordshire.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Werburgh&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Chester_Cathedral_misericord_Hamilton_0172.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Chester_Cathedral_misericord_Hamilton_0172.JPG/250px-Chester_Cathedral_misericord_Hamilton_0172.JPG" decoding="async" width="250" height="167" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Chester_Cathedral_misericord_Hamilton_0172.JPG/375px-Chester_Cathedral_misericord_Hamilton_0172.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Chester_Cathedral_misericord_Hamilton_0172.JPG/500px-Chester_Cathedral_misericord_Hamilton_0172.JPG 2x" data-file-width="3480" data-file-height="2320" /></a><figcaption>Miracles of St Werburgh, including the resurrection of the goose, depicted on a <a href="/wiki/Misericord" title="Misericord">misericord</a> at <a href="/wiki/Chester_Cathedral" title="Chester Cathedral">Chester Cathedral</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>Following Werburgh's death, her brother <a href="/wiki/Coenred_of_Mercia" title="Coenred of Mercia">Coenred</a> became king of <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercia</a>. In 708 he decided to move his sister's remains to a more conspicuous place within the church at <a href="/wiki/Hanbury,_Staffordshire" title="Hanbury, Staffordshire">Hanbury</a>. When the tomb was opened, her body was found to be miraculously intact. This preservation was taken as a sign of divine favour. A year later Coenred had abdicated as king and taken holy orders, becoming a monk in Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-casanova_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-casanova-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> It was at this time that the most famous story about Werburgh appeared, according to which she restored a dead goose to life, as recounted by the medieval <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographer</a> <a href="/wiki/Goscelin" title="Goscelin">Goscelin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-stwerburghrc_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stwerburghrc-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> A stained glass window in the Church of St Peter and St Paul, <a href="/wiki/Weedon_Bec" title="Weedon Bec">Weedon Bec</a>, Northamptonshire relates to another tale in which she was said to have banished all the geese from the village.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The shrine of St Werberh remained at Hanbury until the threat from Danish <a href="/wiki/Norse_activity_in_the_British_Isles" class="mw-redirect" title="Norse activity in the British Isles">Viking raids</a> in the late 9th century prompted their relocation to within the walled city of Chester. A shrine to St Werberh was established at the Church of St Peter and St Paul (the site is now occupied by <a href="/wiki/Chester_Cathedral" title="Chester Cathedral">Chester Cathedral</a>). In 975, the Church of St Peter and St Paul was re-dedicated to St Werburgh and the Northumbrian saint <a href="/wiki/Oswald_of_Northumbria" title="Oswald of Northumbria">Oswald</a>. A monastery in the names of these two saints was attached to the church in the 11th century.
</p><p>By 1057 the Abbey church was rebuilt and further endowed by <a href="/wiki/Leofric_of_Mercia" class="mw-redirect" title="Leofric of Mercia">Leofric, Earl of Mercia</a>. By this time, Werburgh was regarded as the patron saint and protector of Chester. A miracle attributed to her was the unexpected withdrawal of the Welsh king <a href="/wiki/Gruffudd_ap_Llywelyn" class="mw-redirect" title="Gruffudd ap Llywelyn">Gruffudd ap Llywelyn</a> from besieging the city.
</p><p>Even after 1066 and the <a href="/wiki/Norman_conquest_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Norman conquest of England">Norman conquest of England</a>, the shrine of St Werburgh was a place of veneration. In 1093, <a href="/wiki/Hugh_d%27Avranches" class="mw-redirect" title="Hugh d&#39;Avranches">Hugh d'Avranches</a>, the second Norman <a href="/wiki/Earl_of_Chester" title="Earl of Chester">Earl of Chester</a>, presented gifts of property to the abbey and had the church enlarged and rebuilt. He also established a <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Saint_Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of Saint Benedict">Benedictine</a> monastery. Its monks came from <a href="/wiki/Bec_Abbey" title="Bec Abbey">Bec Abbey</a> in Normandy, which had provided the first two post-Conquest <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishops of Canterbury</a>: <a href="/wiki/Lanfranc" title="Lanfranc">Lanfranc</a> and <a href="/wiki/Anselm_of_Canterbury" title="Anselm of Canterbury">Anselm</a>. Like many other Anglo-Norman barons, Hugh d'Avranches entered the monastery shortly before he died and, in turn, was buried there. During the Middle Ages, the badge of a gaggle of geese was adopted as proof of having made a pilgrimage to the Shrine of St Werburgh.<sup id="cite_ref-stwerburghrc_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stwerburghrc-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1540 the <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Monasteries" class="mw-redirect" title="Dissolution of the Monasteries">dissolution</a> of the abbey led to the creation of <a href="/wiki/Chester_Cathedral" title="Chester Cathedral">Chester Cathedral</a>, which was rededicated to Christ and the Blessed Virgin Mary. Although an elaborate shrine had been constructed in the 14th century to Werburgh, this was broken up at the time of the Dissolution. Werburgh's relics were lost. Parts of the shrine's stonework that survived were reassembled in 1876. The shrine remains on display in the cathedral's Lady Chapel at the back of the main nave.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Today">Today</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Werburgh&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Today">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Stwerburghbadge.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b7/Stwerburghbadge.jpg/220px-Stwerburghbadge.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b7/Stwerburghbadge.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="300" data-file-height="225" /></a><figcaption>St Werburgh's pilgrimage badge, showing a gaggle of geese</figcaption></figure>
<p>St Werburgh remains the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Chester" title="Chester">Chester</a>. Her <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is 3 February.
</p><p>At least ten churches in England, and some overseas, are dedicated to St.Werburgh, including those in <a href="/wiki/St._Werburgh%27s_Church,_Dublin" title="St. Werburgh&#39;s Church, Dublin">Dublin</a>, <a href="/wiki/St._Werburgh%27s_Church,_Derby" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Werburgh&#39;s Church, Derby">Derby</a> and <a href="/wiki/St._Werburgh%27s_Church,_Spondon" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Werburgh&#39;s Church, Spondon">Spondon</a>.   The village of <a href="/wiki/Warburton,_Greater_Manchester" title="Warburton, Greater Manchester">Warburton</a> in Greater Manchester (previously Cheshire) is named after its parish church of St Werburgh, and a neighbourhood in <a href="/wiki/Bristol" title="Bristol">Bristol</a> is named <a href="/wiki/St_Werburghs" title="St Werburghs">St Werburghs</a> after <a href="/wiki/St_Werburgh%27s_Church,_Bristol" title="St Werburgh&#39;s Church, Bristol">its church</a> (now a climbing centre).  The <a href="/wiki/Manchester_Metrolink" title="Manchester Metrolink">Manchester Metrolink</a> line to <a href="/wiki/Didsbury" title="Didsbury">East Didsbury</a> has <a href="/wiki/St_Werburgh%27s_Road_Metrolink_station" class="mw-redirect" title="St Werburgh&#39;s Road Metrolink station">St Werburgh's Road</a>, at the crossroads with Wilbraham Road where there is a church dedicated to St Werburgh.
</p>
