<p><b>Josephine Margaret Bakhita</b>, <span class="noexcerpt nowraplinks" style="font-size:85%; font-weight:normal;"><a href="/wiki/Canossians" title="Canossians">F.D.C.C.</a></span> (ca. 1869 &#8211; 8 February 1947), was a <a href="/wiki/Sudan" title="Sudan">Sudanese</a>-<a href="/wiki/Italians" title="Italians">Italian</a> <a href="/wiki/Canossians" title="Canossians">Canossian</a> <a href="/wiki/Religious_sister" title="Religious sister">religious sister</a> who lived in <a href="/wiki/Italy" title="Italy">Italy</a> for 45 years, after having been a <a href="/wiki/Slave" class="mw-redirect" title="Slave">slave</a> in <a href="/wiki/Sudan" title="Sudan">Sudan</a>. In 2000, she was declared a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>, the first Black woman to receive the honor in the modern era.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josephine_Bakhita&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josephine_Bakhita&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>She was born around 1869 in <a href="/wiki/Darfur" title="Darfur">Darfur</a> (now in western Sudan) in the village of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Olgossa&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Olgossa (page does not exist)">Olgossa</a>, west of <a href="/wiki/Nyala,_Sudan" title="Nyala, Sudan">Nyala</a> and close to <a href="/w/index.php?title=Mount_Agilerei&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Mount Agilerei (page does not exist)">Mount Agilerei</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> She was one of the <a href="/wiki/Daju_people" title="Daju people">Daju people</a>;<sup id="cite_ref-davis_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-davis-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> her respected and reasonably prosperous father was brother of the village chief. She was surrounded by a loving family of three brothers and three sisters; as she says in her autobiography: "I lived a very happy and carefree life, without knowing what suffering was".<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Slavery">Slavery</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josephine_Bakhita&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Slavery">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 1877, when she was 7–8 years old, she was seized by <a href="/wiki/Arab_slave_trade" title="Arab slave trade">Arab slave traders</a>, who had abducted her elder sister two years earlier. She was forced to walk barefoot about 960 kilometres (600&#160;mi) to <a href="/wiki/El-Obeid" title="El-Obeid">El-Obeid</a> and was sold and bought twice before she arrived there. Over the course of twelve years (1877–1889) she was sold three more times and then she was finally given her freedom.
</p><p>'Bakhita' was not the name she received from her parents at birth. It is said that the trauma of her abduction caused her to forget her original name; she took one given to her by the slavers, <i>bakhīta</i> (بخيتة), <a href="/wiki/Arabic" title="Arabic">Arabic</a> for 'lucky' or 'fortunate'.<sup id="cite_ref-omalley32_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-omalley32-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> She was also <a href="/wiki/Forced_conversion" title="Forced conversion">forcibly converted</a> to <a href="/wiki/Islam" title="Islam">Islam</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Murchison,_p.7_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Murchison,_p.7-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In El-Obeid, Bakhita was bought by a rich Arab who used her as a maid for his two daughters. They treated her relatively well, until after offending one of her owner's sons, wherein the son lashed and kicked her so severely that she spent more than a month unable to move from her straw bed. Her fourth owner was a Turkish general, and she had to serve his mother-in-law and his wife, who were cruel to their slaves. Bakhita says: "During all the years I stayed in that house, I do not recall a day that passed without some wound or other. When a wound from the whip began to heal, other blows would pour down on me."<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>She once said that the most terrifying of all of her memories there was when she (along with other slaves) was marked by a process resembling both <a href="/wiki/Scarification" title="Scarification">scarification</a> and <a href="/wiki/Tattooing" class="mw-redirect" title="Tattooing">tattooing</a>, which was a traditional practice throughout Sudan.<sup id="cite_ref-burns53_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-burns53-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> As her mistress was watching her with a whip in her hand, a dish of white flour, a dish of salt and a razor were brought by a woman. She used the flour to draw patterns on her skin and then she cut deeply along the lines before filling the wounds with salt to ensure permanent scarring. A total of 114 intricate patterns were cut into her breasts, belly and into her right arm.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-afrol.com_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-afrol.com-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>By the end of 1882, El-Obeid came under the threat of an attack of <a href="/wiki/Mahdist_State" title="Mahdist State">Mahdist</a> revolutionaries.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The Turkish general began making preparations to return to his homeland and sold his slaves. In 1883, Bakhita was bought in <a href="/wiki/Khartoum" title="Khartoum">Khartoum</a> by the Italian <a href="/wiki/Vice_Consul" class="mw-redirect" title="Vice Consul">Vice Consul</a> Callisto Legnani, who did not beat or punish her.<sup id="cite_ref-cdc_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cdc-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Two years later, when Legnani himself had to return to Italy, Bakhita begged to go with him. At the end of 1884 they escaped from a besieged Khartoum with a friend, Augusto Michieli. They travelled a risky 650-kilometre (400&#160;mi) trip on camelback to <a href="/wiki/Suakin" title="Suakin">Suakin</a>, which was the largest port of Sudan. In March 1885 they left Suakin for Italy and arrived at the port of <a href="/wiki/Genoa" title="Genoa">Genoa</a> in April. They were met there by Augusto Michieli's wife, Maria Turina Michieli, to whom Legnani gave ownership of Bakhita. Her new owners took her to their family villa at Zianigo, near <a href="/wiki/Mirano" title="Mirano">Mirano</a>, Veneto, about 25&#160;km (16&#160;mi) west of <a href="/wiki/Venice" title="Venice">Venice</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-burns53_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-burns53-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> She lived there for three years and became nanny to the Michieli's daughter Alice, known as 'Mimmina', born in February 1886. The Michielis brought Bakhita with them back to the Sudan where they stayed for nine months before returning to Italy.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Conversion_to_Catholicism_and_freedom">Conversion to Catholicism and freedom</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josephine_Bakhita&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Conversion to Catholicism and freedom">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><a href="/wiki/Suakin" title="Suakin">Suakin</a> on the <a href="/wiki/Red_Sea" title="Red Sea">Red Sea</a> was besieged but remained in <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Egyptian_Sudan" title="Anglo-Egyptian Sudan">Anglo-Egyptian</a> hands. Augusto Michieli acquired a large hotel there and decided to sell his property in Italy and to move his family to Sudan permanently. Selling his house and lands took longer than expected. By the end of 1888, Turina Michieli wanted to see her husband in Sudan even though land transactions were unfinished. Since the villa in Zianigo was already sold, Bakhita and Mimmina needed a temporary place to stay while Micheli went to Sudan without them. On the advice of their business agent Illuminato Cecchini, on 29 November 1888, Michieli left both in the care of the <a href="/wiki/Canossians" title="Canossians">Canossian Sisters</a> in Venice. There, cared for and instructed by the Sisters, Bakhita encountered Christianity for the first time. Grateful to her teachers, she recalled, "Those holy mothers instructed me with heroic patience and introduced me to that God who from childhood I had felt in my heart without knowing who He was."<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Michieli returned to take her daughter and maid back to Suakin, Bakhita firmly refused to leave. For three days, Michieli tried to force the issue, finally appealing to the attorney general of the <a href="/wiki/King_of_Italy" title="King of Italy">King of Italy</a>; while the superior of the Institute for baptismal candidates (<a href="/wiki/Catechumenate" class="mw-redirect" title="Catechumenate">catechumenate</a>) that Bakhita attended contacted the <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_of_Venice" title="Patriarch of Venice">Patriarch of Venice</a> about her protegée's problem. On 29 November 1889, an Italian court ruled that because the British had outlawed slavery in Sudan before Bakhita's birth and because Italian law had never recognized slavery as legal, Bakhita had never legally been a slave.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> For the first time in her life, Bakhita found herself in control of her own destiny, and she chose to remain with the Canossians.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> On 9 January 1890, Bakhita was baptized with the names of 'Josephine Margaret' and 'Fortunata' (the Latin translation of the Arabic <i>Bakhita</i>). On the same day, she was also <a href="/wiki/Confirmation_in_the_Catholic_Church" title="Confirmation in the Catholic Church">confirmed</a> and received <a href="/wiki/Holy_Communion" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Communion">Holy Communion</a> from Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_X" title="Pope Pius X">Giuseppe Sarto</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_(Catholic_Church)" title="Cardinal (Catholic Church)">Cardinal</a> Patriarch of Venice and later Pope Pius X.<sup id="cite_ref-burns54_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-burns54-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Canossian_Sister">Canossian Sister</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josephine_Bakhita&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Canossian Sister">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Chiesa_della_Sacra_Famiglia_2.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/Chiesa_della_Sacra_Famiglia_2.jpg/220px-Chiesa_della_Sacra_Famiglia_2.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="330" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/Chiesa_della_Sacra_Famiglia_2.jpg/330px-Chiesa_della_Sacra_Famiglia_2.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/Chiesa_della_Sacra_Famiglia_2.jpg/440px-Chiesa_della_Sacra_Famiglia_2.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2592" data-file-height="3888" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Chiesa_della_Sacra_Famiglia_2.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Church of the Holy Family, <a href="/wiki/Schio" title="Schio">Schio</a></div></div></div>
<p>On 7 December 1893, Josephine Bakhita entered the <a href="/wiki/Novitiate" title="Novitiate">novitiate</a> of the Canossian Sisters and on 8 December 1896, she took her <a href="/wiki/Religious_vows" title="Religious vows">vows</a>, welcomed by Cardinal Sarto. In 1902 she was assigned to the Canossian convent at <a href="/wiki/Schio" title="Schio">Schio</a>, in the northern Italian province of <a href="/wiki/Vicenza" title="Vicenza">Vicenza</a>, where she spent the rest of her life. Her only extended time away was between 1935 and 1939, when she stayed at the Missionary Novitiate in <a href="/wiki/Vimercate" title="Vimercate">Vimercate</a> (<a href="/wiki/Milan" title="Milan">Milan</a>); mostly visiting other Canossian communities in Italy, talking about her experiences and helping to prepare young sisters for work in Africa.<sup id="cite_ref-burns54_20-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-burns54-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> A strong missionary drive animated her throughout her entire life – "her mind was always on God, and her heart in Africa".<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During her 42 years in Schio, Bakhita was employed as the cook, <a href="/wiki/Sacristan" title="Sacristan">sacristan</a>, and portress (doorkeeper) and was in frequent contact with the local community. Her gentleness, calming voice, and the ever-present smile became well known and Vicenzans still refer to her as <i>Sor Moretta</i> ("little brown sister") or <i>Madre Moretta</i> ("black mother"). Her special charisma and reputation for sanctity were noticed by her order; the first publication of her story (<i>Storia Meravigliosa</i> by Ida Zanolini) in 1931, made her famous throughout Italy.<sup id="cite_ref-davis_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-davis-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> During the <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">Second World War</a> (1939–1945) she shared the fears and hopes of the townspeople, who considered her a saint and felt protected by her presence. Bombs did not spare Schio, but the war passed without a single casualty. 
</p><p>Her last years were marked by pain and sickness. She used a wheelchair but she retained her cheerfulness, and if asked how she was, she would always smile and answer: "As the Master desires." In the extremity of her last hours, her mind was driven back to her youth in slavery and she cried out: "The chains are too tight, loosen them a little, please!" After a while, she came round again. Someone asked her, "How are you? Today is Saturday," probably hoping that this would cheer her because Saturday is the day of the week dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus" title="Mary, mother of Jesus">Mary, mother of Jesus</a>. Bakhita replied, "Yes, I am so happy: Our Lady... Our Lady!" These were her last audible words.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bakhita died at 8:10 PM on 8 February 1947. For three days, her body <a href="/wiki/Lying_in_repose" title="Lying in repose">lay in repose</a> while thousands of people arrived to pay their respects. Her remains were <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">translated</a> to the Church of the Holy Family of the Canossian convent of Schio in 1969.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy_and_canonization">Legacy and canonization</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josephine_Bakhita&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Legacy and canonization">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>A young student once asked Bakhita: "What would you do, if you were to meet your captors?" Without hesitation, she replied: "If I were to meet those who kidnapped me, and even those who tortured me, I would kneel and kiss their hands. For, if these things had not happened, I would not have been a Christian and a religious today".<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The petitions for her <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a> began immediately, and the process commenced by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_XXIII" title="Pope John XXIII">Pope John XXIII</a> in 1959, twelve years after her death. On 1 December 1978, <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> declared Josephine <a href="/wiki/Venerable" class="mw-redirect" title="Venerable">Venerable</a>, the first step towards canonization. On 17 May 1992, she was declared <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">Blessed</a> and given <a href="/wiki/February_8" title="February 8">8 February</a> as her <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a>. On 1 October 2000, she was canonized as Saint Josephine Bakhita. She is venerated as a modern African saint, and as a statement against the brutal history of slavery. She has been adopted as the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of modern Sudan and <a href="/wiki/Human_trafficking" title="Human trafficking">human trafficking</a> survivors. Caritas Bakhita House in London, which provides accommodation and support for women escaping human trafficking, is named in her honour.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bakhita's legacy is that transformation is possible through suffering. Her story of deliverance from physical slavery also symbolises all those who find meaning and inspiration in her life for their own deliverance from spiritual slavery.<sup id="cite_ref-afrol.com_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-afrol.com-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> In May 1992, news of her beatification was banned by Khartoum which Pope John Paul II visited nine months later.<sup id="cite_ref-Hutchison,_p.7_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hutchison,_p.7-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> On 10 February 1993, he solemnly honoured Bakhita on her own soil. "Rejoice, all of Africa! Bakhita has come back to you. The daughter of Sudan sold into slavery as a living piece of merchandise and yet still free. Free with the freedom of the saints."<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a>, on 30 November 2007, in the beginning of his second <a href="/wiki/Encyclical" title="Encyclical">encyclical</a> letter <i><a href="/wiki/Spe_Salvi" class="mw-redirect" title="Spe Salvi">Spe Salvi</a></i> ("In Hope We Were Saved"), relates her life story as an outstanding example of the Christian hope.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Josephine Margaret Bakhita is honored with a <a href="/wiki/Lesser_Feasts_and_Fasts" class="mw-redirect" title="Lesser Feasts and Fasts">Lesser Feast</a> on the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">liturgical calendar</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church in the United States of America</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-lff2018_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lff2018-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> also on 8 February.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
