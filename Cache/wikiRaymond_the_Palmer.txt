					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><p><b>Raymond of Piacenza</b> (1139/40 &#8211; 26 July 1200), called <b>the Palmer</b> or <b>Zanfogni</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> was a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Pilgrim" title="Pilgrim">pilgrim</a> and <a href="/wiki/Religious_(Catholicism)" class="mw-redirect" title="Religious (Catholicism)">religious</a> who practiced <a href="/wiki/Charity_(practice)" title="Charity (practice)">charity</a> to the poor and ill. Raymond's nickname, "the Palmer", derives from his pilgrimage to <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>, from which he brought back the customary <a href="/wiki/Palm_frond" class="mw-redirect" title="Palm frond">palm frond</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Such a pilgrim was called a <a href="/wiki/Palmer_(pilgrim)" title="Palmer (pilgrim)"><i>Palmarius</i> or <i>Palmerius</i></a> (<a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Palmario</i> or <span title="Italian-language text"><i lang="it">Palmerio</i></span>). His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is 26 July.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Raymond's life is known from a <a href="/wiki/Medieval_Latin" title="Medieval Latin">Latin</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">vita</a></i> written in 1212, only twelve years after his death, by a certain Rufinus,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> at the instigation of Raymond's son Gerard. This was kept in the library of San Raimondo di Piacenza, a <a href="/wiki/Cistercian" class="mw-redirect" title="Cistercian">Cistercian</a> convent, until 1525, when it was lent out to a <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican</a> friar to be translated into Italian. It was never seen again. The <a href="/wiki/Bollandist" title="Bollandist">Bollandist</a> <a href="/wiki/Peter_van_der_Bosch" title="Peter van der Bosch">Peter van der Bosch</a> translated the Italian back into <a href="/wiki/New_Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="New Latin language">Latin</a> for the <i><a href="/wiki/Acta_Sanctorum" title="Acta Sanctorum">Acta Sanctorum</a></i> (July, vol. VI, col. 645&#8211;57). This version has been translated into English by <a href="/wiki/Kenneth_Baxter_Wolf" title="Kenneth Baxter Wolf">Kenneth Baxter Wolf</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Childhood_and_married_life">Childhood and married life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Raymond_the_Palmer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Childhood and married life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Raymond was born at <a href="/wiki/Piacenza" title="Piacenza">Piacenza</a> to parents "neither illustrious in origin nor completely lowborn", i.e., of the burgeoning <a href="/wiki/Middle_class" title="Middle class">middle class</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He was raised by his mother until about the age of twelve, and never received a thorough education, being still "unlettered" at his death.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> At about twelve he was sent by his father to learn the family trade of <a href="/wiki/Shoemaking" title="Shoemaking">shoemaking</a> at a certain workshop.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> His father died when he was an adolescent (his biographer presumes that to be fourteen),<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> and after receiving the permission of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Hugh_(Bishop_of_Piacenza)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Hugh (Bishop of Piacenza) (page does not exist)">Bishop Hugh</a> (1155&#8211;66), Raymond and his mother went on a pilgrimage to Jerusalem.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Raymond was ill for much of the journey and almost died on the return trip by sea.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Raymond's mother died soon after their return to Italy but before they reached Piacenza. At Piacenza Raymond's relatives persuaded him to marry, which he did, and he returned to shoemaking to support his family.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> In his leisure hours, his biographer writes, he conversed with religious men and became wise enough to preach, on holidays, in houses and workshops. His fame soon grew and people flocked to hear him. His biographer refers to him as their "spiritual leader", though he notes that Raymond refused to contravene <a href="/wiki/Canon_law" title="Canon law">canon law</a> by preaching publicly.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Instead, he urged those of his listeners who wanted more to speak with a priest or a monk.<sup id="cite_ref-vauchez64_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vauchez64-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the space of one year all five of Raymond's children died, probably of an epidemic.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> He tried to persuade his wife to <a href="/wiki/Sexual_abstinence" title="Sexual abstinence">abstain from sexual relations</a> so that they could devote themselves more fully to God, but she refused, saying "If I wished to be a nun, I would follow this advice. But since you have married me, it seems right to me to behave like a married woman, not like a widow or a nun".<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> She bore him another child, a son named Gerard (Gerardo), whom Raymond secretly dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Brigid_of_Kildare" title="Brigid of Kildare">Brigid of Kildare</a> in her church at Piacenza.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Shortly after his wife was afflicted with an incurable disease, to which she eventually succumbed, a fact which is treated by his biographer as <a href="/wiki/Divine_Providence" class="mw-redirect" title="Divine Providence">Providence</a>. Raymond then took vow a <a href="/wiki/Celibacy" title="Celibacy">celibacy</a> and, leaving Gerard and all his possessions (including his house) with his parents-in-law, left on a series of pilgrimages, which he planned to perform for the rest of his life.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Pilgrimages">Pilgrimages</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Raymond_the_Palmer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Pilgrimages">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Raymond first followed the <a href="/wiki/Way_of_Saint_James" class="mw-redirect" title="Way of Saint James">Way of Saint James</a> to <a href="/wiki/Santiago_de_Compostela" title="Santiago de Compostela">Santiago de Compostela</a>, supporting himself by <a href="/wiki/Begging" title="Begging">begging</a>. From Compostela he went to <a href="/wiki/V%C3%A9zelay" title="Vézelay">Vézelay</a>, where, since the mid-eleventh century, the body of <a href="/wiki/Mary_Magdalene" title="Mary Magdalene">Mary Magdalene</a> was said to lie. According to a legend that developed there Mary had lived out her life in <a href="/wiki/Penance" title="Penance">penance</a> in the caves of the <a href="/wiki/Sainte-Baume" title="Sainte-Baume">Sainte-Baume</a> near <a href="/wiki/Marseille" title="Marseille">Marseille</a> after the <a href="/wiki/Ascension_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascension of Christ">ascension of Christ</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>  Raymond visited there and then hurried through <a href="/wiki/Provence" title="Provence">Provence</a>, where he visited the shrine of the <a href="/wiki/Three_Maries" class="mw-redirect" title="Three Maries">Three Maries</a> at <a href="/wiki/Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer" title="Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer">Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Camargue" title="Camargue">Camargue</a>. From there he visited the shrine of <a href="/wiki/Anthony_the_Great" title="Anthony the Great">Anthony the Great</a> at <a href="/wiki/Vienne" title="Vienne">Vienne</a> and that of <a href="/wiki/Bernard_of_Clairvaux" title="Bernard of Clairvaux">Bernard of Clairvaux</a>, either at <a href="/wiki/Ville-sous-la-Fert%C3%A9" title="Ville-sous-la-Ferté">Clairvaux</a><sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> or <a href="/w/index.php?title=Menthon&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Menthon (page does not exist)">Menthon</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-vauchez61_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vauchez61-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> He returned to Italy and visited the shrine of <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</a> at <a href="/wiki/Pavia" title="Pavia">Pavia</a>. He then proceeded to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>.
</p><p>While he was sleeping under a portico at the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint_Peter" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica of Saint Peter">Basilica of Saint Peter</a>, contemplating another pilgrimage to Jerusalem, he had a vision of Jesus Christ, who told him to return to Piacenza and "lead the rich to almsgiving, rival parties to peace, and those who have strayed&#8212;especially wayward women&#8212;to a proper way of life".<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> He claimed that Christ had told him to wear a sky-blue, knee-length garment with loose sleeves and no hood, and to always carry a cross over his shoulder. These thing he adopted in the <a href="/wiki/Val_di_Taro" title="Val di Taro">Val di Taro</a>, on his way back to Piacenza. He also wore a traveller's cap, perhaps as a reminder of his former pilgrim's life.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div style="float: right; margin: 1em; padding: 1em; width: 300px; border: solid 2px gray; background-color: lavender;">
<p>If I were to try to describe one by one all of blessed Raymond’s virtues, all of his labors, and each of his pious works, an entire year would not be sufficient for the task. So I think it will suffice to have briefly culled a few from them, for the glory of God and his saint and as an example to those living now as well as to those who will come in the future. So I will not mention how many times he came upon children here and there who had been abandoned and secretly cast aside; children whom he carried&#8212;often two at a time, one in each arm, lest they perish in their misery, all the while crying out of pity&#8212;back to his hospital to nurture with his care. Nor will I say how many sick people, especially foreigners and pilgrims, destitute and on the street without any resources, he carried on his own back.
</p>
&#8212;Master Rufino (<i>Rufinus magister</i>), Raymond's biographer<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup></div>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Spiritual_leadership_at_Piacenza">Spiritual leadership at Piacenza</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Raymond_the_Palmer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Spiritual leadership at Piacenza">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>At Piacenza Raymond, then thirty-eight years of age, received the support of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Theobald_(Bishop_of_Piacenza)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Theobald (Bishop of Piacenza) (page does not exist)">Bishop Theobald</a> (1167&#8211;92) and the canonry of the Twelve Apostles gave him a large building next to theirs, where he established a <a href="/wiki/Xenodochium" title="Xenodochium">xenodochium</a> (1178).<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> He set about gathering those who were to ashamed or to infirm to beg, and went through the streets calling out for <a href="/wiki/Alms" title="Alms">alms</a> from the rich. He soon attracted a large number of beggars and religiously motivated persons who helped him in his task. He established a domicile attached to the canonry for women, "which was somewhat better furnished and yet more closed off" than the original building, where he housed the men in need of shelter.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> He also began a ministry to <a href="/wiki/Prostitutes" class="mw-redirect" title="Prostitutes">prostitutes</a>, some of whom were convinced to marry and some to become nuns, while some continued in their prostitution.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Raymond also acted as advocate of the poor before the civic tribunal, which, Rufinus boasts, "often deferred to his judgment with regard to what ought be done".<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> He tried to stem in Piacenza the factionalism and party strife that was endemic in the cities of northern Italy, but in this he was largely unsuccessful. He eventually prophesied destruction for Piacenza: "Woe to you, seditious Piacenza! God has already prepared a scourge with which to beat you. You will be plundered and set on fire. You will lose your fortunes and your lives."<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> Rufinus wrote that this prophecy had been fulfilled since Raymond's death, but no fire and plundering is known from any other records.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Raymond tried to intervene to prevent a war between Piacenza and <a href="/wiki/Cremona" title="Cremona">Cremona</a>, but the Cremonese imprisoned him. He was eventually released because of his sanctity.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Raymond was also an opponent of knightly <a href="/wiki/Hastilude" title="Hastilude">hastiludes</a> (his biographer calls them "Trojan games ... [a] kind of gladiatorial contest [for "mounted men"] in which brawling, injury, and murder were commonplace"), which he sought to outlaw.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> He reputedly dragged the bishop and the city magistrate (or prefect) to these events to put a stop to them, since he himself could not convince the youths to stop. Influenced by his own spell in jail, he also began visiting prisons, and spoke on behalf of many prisoners whom he had converted, some of whom later went on to join the canonry of the Twelve Apostles.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Raymond's institutions also took in abandoned children, whom he is said to have gathered up himself. Raymond had a "keen sense of the social realities of poverty and marginality" and he once led a demonstration of beggars through the streets crying out for help from the rich.<sup id="cite_ref-vauchez61_20-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vauchez61-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> He was on good terms with the bishops of Piacenza throughout his life, though this did not prevent his criticising their inaction against factionalism.<sup id="cite_ref-vauchez64_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vauchez64-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_subsequent_miracles">Death and subsequent miracles</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Raymond_the_Palmer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Death and subsequent miracles">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Raymond died of a fever on 26 July 1200 at the age of sixty.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> He had called his son to his bedside, where he convinced him to take up the religious life. His body quickly drew throngs of visitors, and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Grimerio_(Bishop_of_Piacenza)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Grimerio (Bishop of Piacenza) (page does not exist)">Bishop Grimerio</a> buried him in the canonry, where his tomb attracted suppliants even from Cremona. The city established a Hospital of Saint Raymond (<i>Hospitale sancti Raymundi</i>) with the gifts made in his name.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> The aforementioned Cistercian convent of San Raimondo in Piacenza was also named after Raymond. His veneration was approved by a <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">papal bull</a> of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Martin_V" title="Pope Martin V">Martin V</a> in 1422.<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Between 1208 and 1247 many <a href="/wiki/Miracle" title="Miracle">miracles</a> were reportedly wrought through Raymond's intercession throughout <a href="/wiki/Lombardy" title="Lombardy">Lombardy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Rufinus records how a <a href="/wiki/German_people" class="mw-redirect" title="German people">German</a> living in the <a href="/wiki/Piedmont" title="Piedmont">Piedmont</a>, Ogerius by name, accidentally swallowed a bone during a Christmas meal, which lodged in his chest and caused him great pain. Informed by a friend returning from Piacenza of Raymond's tomb there, he vowed to visit it if he should be cured. Immediately he regurgitated the bone and brought it to Raymond's tomb, to be hung up there as a memorial of the miracle.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> In the county of <a href="/wiki/Lavagna" title="Lavagna">Lavagna</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Genoa" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Genoa">diocese of Genoa</a> a certain girl who was <a href="/wiki/Demonic_possession" class="mw-redirect" title="Demonic possession">possessed by a demon</a> taunted the exorcists with "Raymond expels [demons] easily", explaining to her parents, Sophia and Hugo, that Raymond was "a new saint among the people of Piacenza". Her parents brought her to his tomb and the demon was expelled.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> News of this spread throughout Lavagna until it reached a certain nobleman, Bernard de la Torre, and his wife Gelasia, who had a paralysed daughter named Mabilina. Gelasia prayed that if God would heal Mabilina she would send a wax statuette to adorn Raymond's tomb. Five days later Mabilina was walking; Gelasia fulfilled her vow.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Rufinus also tells of a locally famous Pavian woman, Berta, who was possessed and tormented by three demons named Tralinus, Capricius, and Carincius. Many tried to exorcise them by singing the so-called "Verses of Saint Maurice" to her, and her sister led her from church to church. Eventually she was brought to the tomb of Raymond, where she was almost instantaneously healed.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> Rufinus also describes how a man named, ironically, Gerald Vitalis from Ripa in the <a href="/wiki/County_of_Piacenza" title="County of Piacenza">county of Piacenza</a>, suffered from "a hernia such that the intestines, sunken into his abdominal cavity, swelled to the point that he was unable to walk or engage in any kind of work".<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> He was unconvinced, despite the entreaties of his wife, that Raymond was a saint, but he eventually agreed to accompany her to his tomb, where he was, over the course of a few days, healed. In thanks "he donated two measures of wine every year to the poor of Raymond's hospital, so that from that time on the caretakers of this same institution, accustomed to going about asking for donations of wine, could henceforth depend on a more stable supply."<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Rufinus also describes a <a href="/wiki/Republic_of_Venice" title="Republic of Venice">Venetian</a> woman named Maria, a <a href="/wiki/Hunchback" class="mw-redirect" title="Hunchback">hunchback</a> (though Rufinus avoids this term) so bent over that "you might even say that she was a <a href="/wiki/Quadruped" class="mw-redirect" title="Quadruped">quadruped</a>". After completing the laborious journey to Raymond's tomb and praying there, she was healed and could walk upright without a cane.<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> At about the same time there was in <i>Acquense</i> (the area around <a href="/wiki/Acqui_Terme" title="Acqui Terme">Acqui Terme</a>) a man named Lomellus, who "had to bind his bowels with an iron belt", and his wife, who had "been confined to bed for an entire year".<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> Lomellus promised God that if he were healed he would make a pilgrimage to Raymond's tomb. His belt promptly fell off and broke into pieces. His wife, who saw it, swore that she would accompany him and immediately she too was healed.
</p>
