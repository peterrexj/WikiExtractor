<p>The <b>Rising of the North</b> of 1569, also called the <b>Revolt of the Northern Earls</b> or <b>Northern Rebellion</b>, was an unsuccessful attempt by <a href="/wiki/Catholicism" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholicism">Catholic</a> nobles from <a href="/wiki/Northern_England" title="Northern England">Northern England</a> to depose Queen <a href="/wiki/Elizabeth_I_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Elizabeth I of England">Elizabeth I of England</a> and replace her with <a href="/wiki/Mary,_Queen_of_Scots" title="Mary, Queen of Scots">Mary, Queen of Scots</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background">Background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rising_of_the_North&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Elizabeth_I_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Elizabeth I of England">Elizabeth I</a> succeeded her half-sister <a href="/wiki/Mary_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Mary I">Mary I</a> as queen of England in 1558. Elizabeth's accession was disputed due to the questioned legitimacy of the marriage of her parents (<a href="/wiki/Henry_VIII" title="Henry VIII">Henry VIII</a> and <a href="/wiki/Anne_Boleyn" title="Anne Boleyn">Anne Boleyn</a>), and Elizabeth's own questioned legitimacy due to the <a href="/wiki/Second_Succession_Act" title="Second Succession Act">Act of Succession 1536</a>. Under Henry VIII and his advisor <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Cromwell" title="Thomas Cromwell">Thomas Cromwell</a>, power was gradually shifted from regional institutions to royal control. This course was encouraged by Elizabeth's counsellors such as <a href="/wiki/William_Cecil,_1st_Baron_Burghley" title="William Cecil, 1st Baron Burghley">William Cecil</a> and a policy of centralization was the approach favoured by Elizabeth herself at least in regards to the northern border region.
</p><p>Opponents of Elizabeth looked to <a href="/wiki/Mary,_Queen_of_Scots" title="Mary, Queen of Scots">Mary, Queen of Scots</a>, the descendant of Henry VIII's sister <a href="/wiki/Margaret_Tudor" title="Margaret Tudor">Margaret</a>. The claims were initially put forward by Mary's father-in-law, King <a href="/wiki/Henry_II_of_France" title="Henry II of France">Henry II of France</a>, and Mary upheld them after her return to Scotland in 1561.
</p><p>Many <a href="/wiki/English_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="English Catholic">English Catholics</a>, then a significant portion of the population, supported Mary's claim as a way to restore Roman Catholicism. This position was especially strong in <a href="/wiki/Northern_England" title="Northern England">Northern England</a>, where several powerful nobles were Roman Catholics; there had been similar risings against Henry VIII; the <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage_of_Grace" title="Pilgrimage of Grace">Pilgrimage of Grace</a> of 1536 and <a href="/wiki/Bigod%27s_Rebellion" class="mw-redirect" title="Bigod&#39;s Rebellion">Bigod's Rebellion</a> of 1537. Supporters of Mary hoped for aid from France (among Scots) and possibly Spain (among English). Mary's position was strengthened by the birth of her son, <a href="/wiki/James_I_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="James I of England">James</a>, in 1566 but weakened again when she was deposed in July 1567. Following this, she fled to England and at the time of the Rising was in the custody of the <a href="/wiki/George_Talbot,_6th_Earl_of_Shrewsbury" title="George Talbot, 6th Earl of Shrewsbury">Earl of Shrewsbury</a>, on Elizabeth's orders.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Rebellion_under_Northumberland_and_Westmorland">Rebellion under Northumberland and Westmorland</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rising_of_the_North&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Rebellion under Northumberland and Westmorland">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1066479718">.mw-parser-output .infobox-subbox{padding:0;border:none;margin:-3px;width:auto;min-width:100%;font-size:100%;clear:none;float:none;background-color:transparent}.mw-parser-output .infobox-3cols-child{margin:auto}.mw-parser-output .infobox .navbar{font-size:100%}body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-header,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-subheader,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-above,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-title,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-image,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-full-data,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-below{text-align:center}</style><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div class="honorific-prefix" style="display:inline;font-size: 77%; font-weight: normal;">Blessed</div><br /><div style="display:inline;" class="fn">Thomas Plumtree</div></th></tr><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-header" style="background-color:gold;">Priest, Rector, Chaplain to the Rising of the North</th></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Born</th><td class="infobox-data">c. 1520<br /><a href="/wiki/Lincolnshire" title="Lincolnshire">Lincolnshire</a>, England</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Died</th><td class="infobox-data">4 January 1570 (aged 49 - <p>The rebellion was led by <a href="/wiki/Charles_Neville,_6th_Earl_of_Westmorland" title="Charles Neville, 6th Earl of Westmorland">Charles Neville, 6th Earl of Westmorland</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Percy,_7th_Earl_of_Northumberland" title="Thomas Percy, 7th Earl of Northumberland">Thomas Percy, 7th Earl of Northumberland</a>. Seven hundred soldiers assembled at <a href="/wiki/Brancepeth_Castle" title="Brancepeth Castle">Brancepeth Castle</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> In November 1569 Westmorland and Northumberland occupied <a href="/wiki/Durham,_England" title="Durham, England">Durham</a>. <b>Thomas Plumtree</b> (see right) celebrated <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">Mass</a> in <a href="/wiki/Durham_Cathedral" title="Durham Cathedral">Durham Cathedral</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> From Durham, the rebels marched south to <a href="/wiki/Bramham_Moor" class="mw-redirect" title="Bramham Moor">Bramham Moor</a>, while Elizabeth struggled to raise forces sufficient to confront them. But, hearing of a large force being raised by the <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Radclyffe,_3rd_Earl_of_Sussex" title="Thomas Radclyffe, 3rd Earl of Sussex">Earl of Sussex</a>, the rebels abandoned plans to besiege <a href="/wiki/York" title="York">York</a>, and captured <a href="/wiki/Barnard_Castle_(castle)" title="Barnard Castle (castle)">Barnard Castle</a> instead. They proceeded to Clifford Moor, but found little popular support. Sussex marched out from York on 13 December 1569 with 10,000 men against the rebels' 6,000,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and was followed by 12,000 men under <a href="/wiki/Edward_Clinton,_1st_Earl_of_Lincoln" title="Edward Clinton, 1st Earl of Lincoln">Baron Clinton</a>. The rebel earls retreated northward and finally dispersed their forces, fleeing into Scotland.
</p>
<h2><span id="Leonard_Dacre.27s_resistance"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Leonard_Dacre's_resistance">Leonard Dacre's resistance</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rising_of_the_North&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Leonard Dacre&#039;s resistance">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>A questionable role in the rebellion was played by <a href="/wiki/Leonard_Dacre" title="Leonard Dacre">Leonard Dacre</a>, an early sympathiser of Mary. At the outbreak of the rebellion, he travelled to Elizabeth's court at Windsor to claim the heritage of his young nephew, the 5th Baron Dacre. After the latter's untimely death in 1569, this had descended to his sisters, all married to sons of <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Howard,_4th_Duke_of_Norfolk" title="Thomas Howard, 4th Duke of Norfolk">Thomas Howard, 4th Duke of Norfolk</a>. Dacre returned to Northern England, ostensibly a faithful partisan of Elizabeth, but his intentions remain unclear.
</p><p>After the retreat of the rebels, he seized <a href="/wiki/Greystoke_Castle" title="Greystoke Castle">Greystoke Castle</a> and fortified his own <a href="/wiki/Naworth_Castle" title="Naworth Castle">Naworth Castle</a>, where he gathered 3,000 Cumbrian troops and tried to keep up the appearance of good relations with the Queen. He held out against a siege of the royal army under <a href="/wiki/Henry_Carey,_1st_Baron_Hunsdon" title="Henry Carey, 1st Baron Hunsdon">Baron Hunsdon</a> but then attacked the retreating army at <a href="/wiki/Gelt_River" class="mw-redirect" title="Gelt River">Gelt River</a>. Though Hunsdon was outnumbered, he charged Dacre's foot with his cavalry, killing 300–400 and capturing 200–300 men. Dacre escaped via Scotland to <a href="/wiki/Brussels" title="Brussels">Brussels</a>, where he died in exile.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Reprisals">Reprisals</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rising_of_the_North&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Reprisals">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Some of the rebels escaped into Scotland. <a href="/wiki/Regent_Mar" class="mw-redirect" title="Regent Mar">Regent Mar</a> wrote that <a href="/wiki/Alexander_Home,_5th_Lord_Home" title="Alexander Home, 5th Lord Home">Agnes Gray, Lady Home</a>, had been a busy worker to receive the rebels.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Two of the leaders, the Earls of Northumberland and Westmorland, had fled into Scotland. Northumberland was captured by <a href="/wiki/James_Douglas,_4th_Earl_of_Morton" title="James Douglas, 4th Earl of Morton">James Douglas, 4th Earl of Morton</a>, and turned over to Elizabeth in 1572, who had him beheaded at York. After having been hidden at <a href="/wiki/Ferniehirst_Castle" title="Ferniehirst Castle">Ferniehirst Castle</a>, Westmorland escaped to Flanders, where he died impoverished. His family lost their ancestral homes and his wife, <a href="/wiki/Jane_Howard,_Countess_of_Westmorland" title="Jane Howard, Countess of Westmorland">Jane Howard</a>, also fled to the Continent. She lived the rest of her life under house arrest. Her brother, the Duke of Norfolk, was first imprisoned, then pardoned. He was imprisoned again following the <a href="/wiki/Ridolfi_plot" title="Ridolfi plot">Ridolfi plot</a> in 1571 and finally executed in 1572. Norfolk's treason charges included "comforting and relieving of the English rebels that stirred the Rebellion in the North since they have fled out of the realm."<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Altogether, 600 supporters of Mary were executed, while many others fled into exile.
</p><p>Queen Elizabeth declared martial law, exacting terrible retribution on the ordinary folk of the Yorkshire Dales, despite the lack of any popular support for the Earls' Rising, with her demand for at least 700 executions. The victims of this purge were, as a contemporary account said "wholly of the meanest sort of people", so that hardly a village escaped the sight of a public hanging.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1570, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_V" title="Pope Pius V">Pope Pius V</a> had tried to aid the rebellion by excommunicating Elizabeth and declaring her deposed in the <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">papal bull</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Regnans_in_Excelsis" title="Regnans in Excelsis">Regnans in Excelsis</a></i>, but the document did not arrive until the rebellion had been suppressed. The bull gave Elizabeth more reason to view Catholics with suspicion. It inspired conspiracies to assassinate her, starting with the Ridolfi plot.
</p><p>In 1587, Elizabeth brought <a href="/wiki/Mary,_Queen_of_Scots" title="Mary, Queen of Scots">Mary, Queen of Scots</a>, to trial for <a href="/wiki/Treason" title="Treason">treason</a>; she was convicted by the court and executed.
</p>
