font-weight: normal;">Saint</div><br /><div style="display:inline;" class="fn">Euphrosyne of Alexandria</div></th></tr><tr><td colspan="2" class="infobox-image"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria.jpg" class="image"><img alt="Saint Euphrosyne of Alexandria.jpg" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Saint_Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria.jpg/220px-Saint_Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="354" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Saint_Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria.jpg/330px-Saint_Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Saint_Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria.jpg/440px-Saint_Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria.jpg 2x" data-file-width="570" data-file-height="917" /></a><div class="infobox-caption">Fresco of Saint Euphrosyne in a Greek Orthodox Church</div></td></tr><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-header" style="background-color:gold;">Virgin</th></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Born</th><td class="infobox-data">410<br /><a href="/wiki/Alexandria" title="Alexandria">Alexandria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Died</th><td class="infobox-data">470</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Venerated&#160;in</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a><br /><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a><br /><a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Feast</a></th><td class="infobox-data">September 25, January 16</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p><b>Euphrosyne of Alexandria</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Ἁγία Εὐφροσύνη</span> tr. "good cheer"<i>,</i> 410–470),<sup id="cite_ref-swan-832_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-832-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-kirsch2_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch2-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> also called <b>Euphrosynē,</b><sup id="cite_ref-oca2_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca2-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> was a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> who disguised herself as a male to enter a <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> and live, for 38 years, as an <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetic</a>. Her <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a> is celebrated on September 25 by the <a href="/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church" title="Greek Orthodox Church">Greek Orthodox Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church</a>, as well as <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Rite_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine Rite Catholic">Byzantine Rite Catholics</a>, and January 16 by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>. Euphrosyne was born to a wealthy family in <a href="/wiki/Alexandria" title="Alexandria">Alexandria</a>; her father Paphnutius was a devout Christian and her mother died when Euphrosyne was twelve. When she was 18, her father wanted her to marry, so she escaped, disguised as a man, and entered the same monastery he often visited for spiritual counsel. She spent most of her years as a monk in seclusion because her beauty tempted the other monks. During the final year of her life, Euphrosyne became her father's spiritual director, comforting his grief over losing his only daughter. Eventually, she revealed her identity to him and they reconciled. After she died, he entered her monastery and became an ascetic himself, living in her cell until he died ten years later. 
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Euphrosyne was born in 410,<sup id="cite_ref-swan-83_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-83-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> into a "rich and illustrious"<sup id="cite_ref-antiochen_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-antiochen-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> family in Alexandria, the only daughter of Paphnutius, "a deeply believing and pious Christian".<sup id="cite_ref-oca_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/wiki/Johann_Peter_Kirsch" title="Johann Peter Kirsch">Johann Peter Kirsch</a> in the <i><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia" title="Catholic Encyclopedia">Catholic Encyclopedia</a></i>, "Her story belongs to that group of legends which relate how Christian <a href="/wiki/Virginity" title="Virginity">virgins</a>, in order to more successfully to lead the life of <a href="/wiki/Celibacy" title="Celibacy">celibacy</a> and <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a> to which they had dedicated themselves, put on male attire and passed for men".<sup id="cite_ref-kirsch_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Paphnutius and his wife were having difficulty having children, so he went to a local <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a>, which he visited often, and requested that the <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a>, who was his spiritual advisor, and <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monks</a> pray for them; Euphrosyne was born shortly afterwards.<sup id="cite_ref-oca_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-294_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-294-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> She was baptized at the age of seven, educated in the scriptures, and was well known for her wisdom and love of learning.<sup id="cite_ref-swan-83_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-83-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-294_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-294-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Euphrosyne was twelve, her mother died and her father raised her alone. When she was 18, she had many suitors, so her father chose the most noble and wealthiest for her to marry. They visited the monastery together to receive a blessing from the abbot for her marriage, which he did, but the visit inspired Euphrosyne to enter the <a href="/wiki/Monasticism" title="Monasticism">monastic</a> life.<sup id="cite_ref-swan-83_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-83-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-antiochen_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-antiochen-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-294_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-294-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-clark-196_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-196-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> As writer David Clark put it, she was "unwilling to allow her gender to be a barrier to adopting this lifestyle for herself".<sup id="cite_ref-clark-196_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-196-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> A year later, the abbot sent a monk to Paphnutius' home to invite him to the anniversary celebration of the abbot's ordination; she met with the monk, and admitted to him her wish to become an <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetic</a>, despite her fears of disobeying her father.<sup id="cite_ref-clark-196_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-196-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The monk advised her to disguise herself as a man "to escape her impending marriage".<sup id="cite_ref-clark-197_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-197-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> She sent a servant to bring another monk to her, a <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a> from <a href="/wiki/Wadi_El_Natrun" title="Wadi El Natrun">Scete</a>, who gave her the same advice. At her request, the monk shaved her head and invested her as a monk.<sup id="cite_ref-clark-197_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-197-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> When her father left home for another spiritual retreat, Euphrosyne took advantage of his absence and decided to join a monastery, the same one her father visited, instead of a convent, because she was afraid that her father would find her. She disguised herself as a man, claiming to be a <a href="/wiki/Eunuch" title="Eunuch">eunuch</a>; the abbot did not recognize her, and welcomed her into the monastery. Euphrosyne took the name Smaragdus, and lived there as a monk for 38 years, until her death in about 470.<sup id="cite_ref-swan-83_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-83-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-kirsch_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-oca_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-clark-197_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-197-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Euphrosyne, as Smaragdus, impressed the abbot with "the rapid strides which she made toward a perfect ascetic life",<sup id="cite_ref-kirsch_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> but as writer Laura Swan put it, "Dissension arose in the community over Euphrosyne's beauty, and the same abbot ordered her into seclusion".<sup id="cite_ref-swan-83_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-83-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Smaragdus moved deeper into the desert to a solitary cell, reciting his prayers alone, without the rest of the community, and as Swan also said, grew to love "the intense solitude",<sup id="cite_ref-swan-84_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-84-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> eventually only seeing his spiritual director and the abbot.<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-294_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-294-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Clark, in his chapter about Euphrosyne in his book <i>Between Medieval Men: Male Friendship and Desire in Early Medieval English Literature</i>, compares her story with the story of <a href="/wiki/Joseph_(Genesis)" title="Joseph (Genesis)">Joseph</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old Testament</a>, which also includes themes of disguise and secret identities.<sup id="cite_ref-clark-201_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-201-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Clark, who considered Smaragdus' fellow monks' discomfort with him another "interesting gender dynamic"<sup id="cite_ref-clark-195_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-195-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> and discusses "the complex and contradictory gender dynamic"<sup id="cite_ref-clark-195_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-195-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> in Euphrosyne's story, also compares Euphrosyne with <a href="/wiki/Eugenia_of_Rome" title="Eugenia of Rome">Eugenia of Rome</a>, a 3rd-century saint who also disguised herself as a man, because they share a "similar dynamic".<sup id="cite_ref-clark-195_14-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-195-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Clark also says that Eugenia and Euphrosyne's stories, which both include the aid of servants and the use of disguise to escape into a life of religious seclusion, "are typical of tales of lovers thwarting unwanted marriages. However, here the lover is Christ, and the aim is not conjugal bliss but the celibate life".<sup id="cite_ref-clark-195_14-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-195-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;note 1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_legacy">Death and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Death and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria_(Menologion_of_Basil_II).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg/220px-Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="155" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg/330px-Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg/440px-Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="545" data-file-height="385" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria_(Menologion_of_Basil_II).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>A dying Euphrosyne reveals herself to her father, miniature from the <i><a href="/wiki/Menologion_of_Basil_II" title="Menologion of Basil II">Menologion of Basil II</a></i></div></div></div>
<p><br />
Euphrosyne's father Paphnutius went to the monastery "for solace for his grief"<sup id="cite_ref-swan-84_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-84-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> over the loss of his only daughter; the abbot sent Euphrosyne to provide him with spiritual direction and comfort, but Paphnutius did not recognize her because she covered her face with a veil and never revealed her identity.<sup id="cite_ref-swan-84_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-84-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> He received "helpful advice and comforting exhortation"<sup id="cite_ref-kirsch_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> from her anyway and returned to meet with her several times, becoming, as Clark put it, "the spiritual father to her own biological father",<sup id="cite_ref-clark-196_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-196-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  Eventually, in the last year before her death, she revealed to Paphnutius her secret; they reconciled, and she requested that he tell no one and that he prepare her body for burial. After she died, Paphnutius distributed all his wealth to the poor and to the monastery, and became a monk himself, living in his daughter's cell for ten years, until he died and was buried beside Euphrosyne.<sup id="cite_ref-kirsch_7-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-oca_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-swan-84_11-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-84-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Clark stated that Paphutius' actions was another instance of the theme of gender reversal in Euphrosyne's story, and a reworking and complication of the issues of physical and spiritual fatherhood<sup id="cite_ref-clark-196_9-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-196-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> revealed in the "reversal of the father-daughter relationship".<sup id="cite_ref-clark-201_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-201-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Euphrosyne's tomb "became a place of prayer with miracles attributed to her".<sup id="cite_ref-swan-84_11-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-84-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Her <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a> is celebrated on September 25 by the <a href="/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church" title="Greek Orthodox Church">Greek Orthodox Church</a> and January 16 by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-kirsch_7-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kirsch-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-antiochen_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-antiochen-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> According to Swan, an early version of Euphrosyne's life was written in <a href="/wiki/Iambic_pentameter" title="Iambic pentameter">iambic pentameter</a> and another one was written in <a href="/wiki/Prose" title="Prose">prose</a> form.<sup id="cite_ref-swan-84_11-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swan-84-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> According to Clark, an account of her life, written in Old English, also exists.<sup id="cite_ref-clark-195_14-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-clark-195-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 2022, Euphrosyne was officially added to the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church liturgical calendar</a> with a feast day on 25 September.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
