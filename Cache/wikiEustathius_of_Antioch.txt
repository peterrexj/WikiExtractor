<p><b>Eustathius of Antioch</b>, sometimes surnamed <b>the Great</b>, was a Christian <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a> and <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_of_Antioch" title="Patriarch of Antioch">archbishop of Antioch</a> in the 4th century. His feast day in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> is <a href="/wiki/February_21_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics)" title="February 21 (Eastern Orthodox liturgics)">February 21</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eustathius_of_Antioch&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was a native of <a href="/wiki/Side,_Turkey" title="Side, Turkey">Side</a> in <a href="/wiki/Pamphylia" title="Pamphylia">Pamphylia</a>. About 320 he was bishop of <a href="/wiki/Aleppo" title="Aleppo">Beroea</a>, and he became <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_of_Antioch" title="Patriarch of Antioch">patriarch of Antioch</a> shortly before the <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Nicaea" title="First Council of Nicaea">Council of Nicaea</a> in 325. In that assembly he distinguished himself zealously against the <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arians</a>, though the <i>Allocutio ad Imperatorem</i> with which he has been credited is probably not by him.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His anti-Arian polemic against <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Nicomedia" title="Eusebius of Nicomedia">Eusebius of Nicomedia</a> made him unpopular among his fellow bishops in the East, and a synod convened at Antioch in 330 deposed him for adultery,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> which was confirmed by the emperor.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the dispute with Eustathius of Antioch, who opposed the growing influence of Origen and his practice of an allegorical exegesis of scripture, seeing in his theology the roots of Arianism, Eusebius, an admirer of <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>, was reproached by Eustathius for deviating from the Nicene faith, who was charged in turn with <a href="/wiki/Sabellianism" title="Sabellianism">Sabellianism</a>. Eustathius was accused, condemned, and deposed at a synod in Antioch. The people of Antioch rebelled against this action,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> while the anti-Eustathians proposed Eusebius as the new bishop, but he declined. He was banished to <a href="/wiki/Traianoupoli" title="Traianoupoli">Trajanopolis</a> in <a href="/wiki/Thrace" title="Thrace">Thrace</a>, where he died, probably about 337, though possibly not until 370.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The people of Antioch, who loved and revered their patriarch, became indignant at the injustice done to him and were ready to take up arms in his defence. But Eustathius kept them in check, exhorted them to remain true to the orthodox faith and humbly left for his place of exile, accompanied by a large body of his clergy. His adherents in Antioch formed a separate community by the name of "Eustathians" and refused to acknowledge the bishops set over them by the Arians. When, after the death of Eustathius, St. Meletius became Bishop of Antioch in 360 by the united vote of the Arians and the orthodox, the Eustathians would not recognize him, even after his election was approved by the Synod of Alexandria in 362. Their intransigent attitude gave rise to two factions among the orthodox, the so-called Meletian Schism, which lasted till the second decade of the fifth century.<sup id="cite_ref-ott_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ott-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The only complete work by Eustathius is the <i>De Engastrimytho contra Origenem.</i><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Ps.Eustathius,_Commentary_on_the_Hexameron" class="mw-redirect" title="Ps.Eustathius, Commentary on the Hexameron">Commentary on the Hexameron</a> attributed to him in the manuscripts is too late to be authentic.
</p>
