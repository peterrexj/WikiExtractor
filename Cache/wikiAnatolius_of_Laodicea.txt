<p><b>Anatolius of Laodicea</b> (early 3rd century – July 3, 283<sup id="cite_ref-:0_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>), also known as <b>Anatolius of Alexandria</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> was a <a href="/wiki/Syria" title="Syria">Syro-</a><a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egyptian</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">Bishop</a> of <a href="/wiki/Latakia" title="Latakia">Laodicea</a> on the <a href="/wiki/Mediterranean" class="mw-redirect" title="Mediterranean">Mediterranean</a> coast of <a href="/wiki/Syria_(Roman_province)" class="mw-redirect" title="Syria (Roman province)">Roman Syria</a> in AD 268. He was not only one of the foremost scholars of his day in the physical sciences, as well as in <a href="/wiki/Aristotelian_philosophy" class="mw-redirect" title="Aristotelian philosophy">Aristotelian</a> and <a href="/wiki/Platonism" title="Platonism">platonic philosophy</a>, but also a renowned <a href="/wiki/Computus" class="mw-redirect" title="Computus">computist</a> and teacher of the <a href="/wiki/Neoplatonism" title="Neoplatonism">neoplatonic</a> <a href="/wiki/Philosopher" title="Philosopher">philosopher</a> <a href="/wiki/Iamblichus" title="Iamblichus">Iamblichus</a>.
</p><p>Anatolius is recognized as a saint by both the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a>, like the one of his namesake <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_Anatolius_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Patriarch Anatolius of Constantinople">Saint Anatolius of Constantinople</a>, is celebrated on 3 July.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anatolius_of_Laodicea&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Anatolius was born and raised in <a href="/wiki/Alexandria" title="Alexandria">Alexandria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ptolemaic_Egypt" class="mw-redirect" title="Ptolemaic Egypt">Egypt</a>, during the early 3rd century. Prior to becoming one of the great lights of the Church, Anatolius enjoyed considerable prestige at Alexandria while working as a <a href="/wiki/Summus_Senator" title="Summus Senator">senator</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea" class="mw-redirect" title="Eusebius of Caesarea">Eusebius of Caesarea</a>, he was credited with a rich knowledge of <a href="/wiki/Arithmetic" title="Arithmetic">arithmetic</a>, <a href="/wiki/Geometry" title="Geometry">geometry</a>, <a href="/wiki/Physics" title="Physics">physics</a>, <a href="/wiki/Rhetoric" title="Rhetoric">rhetoric</a>, <a href="/wiki/Dialectic" title="Dialectic">dialectic</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Astronomy" title="Astronomy">astronomy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Eusebius states that Anatolius was deemed worthy to maintain the school of the Aristotelian succession in Alexandria.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Pagan" class="mw-redirect" title="Pagan">pagan</a> <a href="/wiki/Philosopher" title="Philosopher">philosopher</a> <a href="/wiki/Iamblichus" title="Iamblichus">Iamblichus</a> also studied among his disciples.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There are fragments of ten books on arithmetic written by him; it's a mostly complete work known to us by the name <i>Introduction to Arithmetic</i>. This work seems to have been copied by the author of the curious writing entitled <i>Theologoumena arithmetica,</i> a Neoplatonic treatise heavily influenced by Pythagoreanism, uncertainly attributed to lamblichus ─ though not written in his style, it is a discussion of each of the first ten natural numerals that mixes accounts of formal arithmetical properties with mystical philosophical analysis.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The character of its writing may be illustrated by the following quotation from it attributed to Anatolius: "[Four] is called the "just number", due to the square being equal to its perimeter (i.e 4x4 = 4+4+4+4); of the numbers lesser than four, the perimeter of the square is greater than the area, while of those greater, the perimeter is lesser than the area."<sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There is also a treatise on time of the Paschal celebration.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> His famous 19-year Paschal cycle has survived in seven different complete medieval manuscripts of the Latin text <i>De ratione paschali</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A story is told by Eusebius of the way in which Anatolius broke up a rebellion in a part of Alexandria known then as Bruchium. It was held by the forces of <a href="/wiki/Zenobia" title="Zenobia">Zenobia</a>, and being strictly beleaguered by the Romans was in a state of starvation. Anatolius, who was living in Bruchium at the time, made arrangements with the besiegers to receive all the women and children, as well as the elderly, continuing at the same time to let as many as wished profit by the means of escaping. It broke up the defense and the rebels surrendered.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In going to <a href="/wiki/Latakia" title="Latakia">Laodicea</a> he was welcomed by the people and made bishop. Whether his friend Eusebius had died, or whether they both occupied the see together, is a matter of much discussion. The question is treated at length in the <a href="/wiki/Bollandists" class="mw-redirect" title="Bollandists">Bollandists</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Anatolius_as_the_pioneer_for_the_mainstream_medieval_computus">Anatolius as the pioneer for the mainstream medieval computus</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anatolius_of_Laodicea&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Anatolius as the pioneer for the mainstream medieval computus">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Around AD 260 Anatolius invented the first <a href="/wiki/Metonic" class="mw-redirect" title="Metonic">Metonic</a> 19-year lunar cycle (which 19-year periodic sequence of dates of the Paschal full moon must not be confused with the Metonic cycle, of which it is an application in the Julian calendar).<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Therefore, Anatolius can be considered to be the founder of the new Alexandrian <a href="/wiki/Computus" class="mw-redirect" title="Computus">computus paschalis</a> which half a century after began with the active construction of the second version of the Metonic 19-year lunar cycle, which itself or a close variant of which ultimately would prevail throughout <a href="/wiki/Christendom" title="Christendom">Christendom</a> for a long time (until the year 1582, when the <a href="/wiki/Julian_calendar" title="Julian calendar">Julian calendar</a> was replaced with the <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_calendar" title="Gregorian calendar">Gregorian calendar</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> The seventeen-centuries-old enigma of his famous 19-year Paschal cycle (not to be confused with the <a href="/wiki/Paschal_cycle" title="Paschal cycle">Paschal cycle</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>) was recently completely resolved by the Irish scholars Daniel Mc Carthy and Aidan Breen.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The dates of the ultimately resulting classical Alexandrian 19-year lunar cycle have recently appeared to be advanced by about two days over the dates of Anatolius’ 19-year lunar cycle.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> The former began somewhere in the 4th century, as evidenced by Ethiopic copies of Alexandrian tables covering three lunar cycles from 310/11 to 367/68.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> The dates are secured by the inclusion of an indiction column, and the included Easter dates are consistent with the later dates of <a href="/wiki/Dionysius_Exiguus" title="Dionysius Exiguus">Dionysius Exiguus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>. The classical Alexandrian 19-year lunar cycle itself or a close variant of it was added to the festal letters of <a href="/wiki/Athanasius_of_Alexandria" title="Athanasius of Alexandria">Athanasius</a> during the late 4th century;<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> it was used by <a href="/wiki/Annianus_of_Alexandria" title="Annianus of Alexandria">Annianus</a> in his 532-year tables during the early 5th century,<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> was fully enumerated in the subsequent 532-year Ethiopic tables,<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> and was adopted by bishop <a href="/wiki/Cyril_of_Alexandria" title="Cyril of Alexandria">Cyril of Alexandria</a> (without any mention of a 532-year table).<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>However, the Metonic 19-year lunar cycle which was added to Athanasius’ Festal Letters was a one which had 6 April instead of 5 April.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> Furthermore, <a href="/wiki/Otto_Neugebauer" class="mw-redirect" title="Otto Neugebauer">Otto Neugebauer</a> (1899-1990), according to himself, was in the dark about the date of compilation of the whole 7980-year framework (based on the classical Alexandrian 19-year lunar cycle) created by Alexandrian computists.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> Considering that only around AD 400 Annianus obtained his classical Alexandrian version of the Metonic 19-year lunar cycle by adapting <a href="/wiki/Pope_Theophilus_I_of_Alexandria" class="mw-redirect" title="Pope Theophilus I of Alexandria">Theophilus</a>’ 19-year lunar cycle by moving its saltus 1 year forward by replacing its date 6 April by 5 April,<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> we may conclude that the compilation in question dates from the fifth century. Of course, this does not exclude that the (in principle tentative) version constructed in the first quarter of the fourth century could be, by chance, equal to Annianus’ ultimately definitive one. In any case, this possible equality cannot be assured in the way Neugebauer tried to prove it, because the particular Metonic 19-year lunar cycle of the not-dated manuscript concerning AD 311-369 he staged,<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> could have been obtained afterwards by simply extrapolating from the fifth century 7980-year framework. Moreover, Neugebauer not only ignored the difference between the classical Alexandrian and the Festal Index 19-year lunar cycle, but also kept us guessing about, at least refrained from showing, the (crucial) position of the saltus in the particular Metonic 19-year lunar cycle in question.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Anatolius describes the vernal equinox as a section of the first zodiacal sign from March 22 to 25. Although he defines March 22 as the Sun's entry into the first sign, he never defines it as his equinox, but as Ptolemy's equinox.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> This does not exclude that in reality he used Ptolemy's equinox as the theoretical lower limit date for all of his dates of Paschal full moon. As a matter of fact, the corresponding de facto lower limit date (necessarily being either 22 or 23 March) is not 22 but 23 March, which easily can be derived from Anatolius’ Paschal table.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> In contrast, he used not only Ptolemy's but also the Roman equinox, March 25, in his Paschal table.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
