<p><b>Alix Le Clerc</b> (2 February 1576 – 9 January 1622), known as <b>Mother Alix</b>, was the founder of the <a href="https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chanoinesses_de_Saint-Augustin_de_la_Congr%C3%A9gation_Notre-Dame" class="extiw" title="fr:Chanoinesses de Saint-Augustin de la Congrégation Notre-Dame">Canonesses of St. Augustine of the Congregation of Our Lady</a> (French: <i lang="fr">Notre-Dame</i>), a <a href="/wiki/Religious_order" title="Religious order">religious order</a> founded to provide education to girls, especially those living in poverty. They opened Schools of Our Lady throughout Europe. Offshoots of this order brought its mission and spirit around the globe. Le Clerc was <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> by the Catholic Church in 1947.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alix_Le_Clerc&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alix_Le_Clerc&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Alix (the local form of Alice) Le Clerc was born on 2 February 1576 into a wealthy family in <a href="/wiki/Remiremont" title="Remiremont">Remiremont</a> in the independent <a href="/wiki/Duchy_of_Lorraine" title="Duchy of Lorraine">Duchy of Lorraine</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-nds_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nds-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> part of the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire" title="Holy Roman Empire">Holy Roman Empire</a>. She was a vivacious girl who loved music and dancing. She would spend her evenings partying with her young friends. When she was about 18, her family moved to <a href="/wiki/Mattaincourt" title="Mattaincourt">Mattaincourt</a>, a manufacturing center.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Conversion">Conversion</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alix_Le_Clerc&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Conversion">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Three years later, a sudden illness confined her to her bed. While there, her only reading material was a <a href="/wiki/Christian_devotional_literature" title="Christian devotional literature">devotional book</a>. From the reading and reflection she was able to do while recuperating from her illness, Le Clerc began to feel the need for a change in her life. She approached the pastor of the town, <a href="/wiki/Dom_(title)" class="mw-redirect" title="Dom (title)">Dom</a> <a href="/wiki/Peter_Fourier" title="Peter Fourier">Peter Fourier</a>, with whom she shared this growing conviction about the need for a new direction in her life,<sup id="cite_ref-nds_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nds-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> but that none of the religious orders appealed to her.
</p><p>A purported vision of <a href="/wiki/Mary,_the_mother_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Mary, the mother of Jesus">Our Lady</a> answered her questioning and gave her the direction she sought, as she felt called to care for the daughters of the poor of the region, who had little or no access to education.<sup id="cite_ref-nds_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nds-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Supported in this by Fourier, who himself had seen the desperate need for this among the rural populace of his parish, Le Clerc resolved to commit her life to this goal. She was joined in this enterprise by four of her friends, with whom she established a community where they could follow lives of simplicity, prayer, and respecting the presence of God in each girl whom they would receive for instruction.<sup id="cite_ref-CEF_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CEF-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Foundress">Foundress</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alix_Le_Clerc&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Foundress">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>On Christmas Day 1597, Le Clerc and her companions made <a href="/wiki/Religious_vows" title="Religious vows">private vows</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Parish_church" title="Parish church">parish church</a> to Fourier. The small community opened their first school the following July in <a href="/wiki/Poussay" title="Poussay">Poussay</a>, where they offered free education to the girls of the duchy. Expansion of their work developed quickly, with communities being opened in Mattaincourt (1599), <a href="/wiki/Saint-Mihiel" title="Saint-Mihiel">Saint-Mihiel</a> (1602), <a href="/wiki/Nancy,_France" title="Nancy, France">Nancy</a> (1603), <a href="/wiki/Pont-%C3%A0-Mousson" title="Pont-à-Mousson">Pont-à-Mousson</a> (1604), <a href="/wiki/Verdun" title="Verdun">Verdun</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Saint-Nicolas-de-Port" title="Saint-Nicolas-de-Port">Saint-Nicolas-de-Port</a> (1605). All the schools took the name of <i>Notre-Dame</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-CRSA_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CRSA-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Le Clerc established herself in Nancy, capital of the duchy, and devoted herself to the care of the girls who came to the schools of the new <a href="/wiki/Religious_congregation" title="Religious congregation">congregation</a>. At the same time, working through major obstacles, she and Fourier developed constitutions for the new congregation through which the communities could be legally recognized by the Church and the State.<sup id="cite_ref-CEF_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CEF-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The vision Le Clerc and Fourier had was one in which schools would give a free education to all, poor and rich, and all girls would be welcome, regardless of whether they were Catholic or Protestant. Additionally, the other needs of their locales would be answered, with visits to the sick and poor. They encountered resistance to this open form of life from the hierarchy, who did not look favorably on their teaching outside a cloister.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> In consultation with the first Sisters, especially Le Clerc, the final form of the constitutions which Fourier wrote took an innovative answer to this by allowing two ways of life to those women who wished to follow the goals of the congregation. In keeping with ancient practice, each community would be autonomous, subject to the local <a href="/wiki/Bishop_(Catholicism)" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop (Catholicism)">bishop</a>, and would each have to seek this formal recognition on its own from the local religious authorities.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> The houses were to be of two forms, all following the <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_St._Augustine" class="mw-redirect" title="Rule of St. Augustine">Rule of St. Augustine</a>, as well as the constitutions:<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<ul><li>Those monasteries whose members who would take public vows (canonesses) and would observe full monastic enclosure, wearing the habit of the congregation.</li>
<li>Those monasteries whose members would take private vows (Daughters/Sisters (French: <i lang="fr">Filles</i>) of the congregation) and would be free to leave the monastery with the approval of the Superiors of the house for any legitimate purpose, such as going to <a href="/wiki/Confession_(religion)" title="Confession (religion)">Confession</a>, participating in <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">Mass</a> when unable to do so in the monastery, or participating in works of charity. They would not wear the <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">religious habit</a> of the Congregation, but instead one developed for that community.</li></ul>
<p>The first approval for the Constitutions came on 6 March 1617 from the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Toul" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Toul">Bishop of Toul</a>, in whose territory Nancy then lay, as a result of which that became the first monastery of the congregation. Le Clerc and the members of that community <a href="/wiki/Profession_(religious)" class="mw-redirect" title="Profession (religious)">professed</a> public vows on 2 December 1618, at which time she took the <a href="/wiki/Religious_name" title="Religious name">religious name</a> of <b>Teresa of Jesus</b>, after the great <a href="/wiki/Carmelite" class="mw-redirect" title="Carmelite">Carmelite</a> foundress. Immediately following the ceremony, Fourier met with the assembled Superiors of the various houses and distributed copies of the approved constitutions for their study and observance. Shortly after that, the canonesses of Nancy held their first formal elections and Le Clerc was elected the <a href="/wiki/Prioress" class="mw-redirect" title="Prioress">prioress</a> of the community.<sup id="cite_ref-CRSA_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CRSA-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Le Clerc oversaw the development of the congregation as the various houses, each in their own turn, became formally recognized. For the rest of her life, she led the development of the spiritual and practical aspects of the lives of the canonesses in the various monasteries. She would visit each new community, to instill in them the spirit of their founding, saying to them, <i>Que Dieu soit votre amour entier!</i> (May God be your only love!), reflecting the deep spiritual life she maintained in the midst of her responsibilities in the congregation.<sup id="cite_ref-CRSA_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CRSA-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_veneration">Death and veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alix_Le_Clerc&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Death and veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Le Clerc died on 9 January 1622 at the monastery in Nancy. She was buried in the cemetery of the monastery in a lead coffin.
</p><p>The cause for her canonization was begun in the latter part of the century, but proceeded slowly. The monastery in Nancy was destroyed during the upheavals of the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>, and the traces of the grave were lost. With the re-establishment of Catholic institutions in France in the early 19th-century, the cause was taken up again, but faced the difficulty of there being no remains, normally required during the process. Various efforts were made by a number of priests to find Le Clerc's remains in the precincts of the former cloister of the monastery over the next century, without success.<sup id="cite_ref-CRSA_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CRSA-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Despite this obstacle, the <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Holy See</a> decided to proceed with the beatification of Mother Teresa of Jesus. This was done by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII" title="Pope Pius XII">Pope Pius XII</a> on 4 May 1947.<sup id="cite_ref-CEF_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CEF-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Discovery_of_remains">Discovery of remains</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alix_Le_Clerc&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Discovery of remains">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Not long after this declaration of her holiness by the Catholic Church, in 1950 a group of young students in Nancy was exploring the basement of a building in the city and found a lead coffin buried nearly 5 feet (1.5 meters) below the ground. By 1960, the remains were conclusively identified as those of Le Clerc, and were placed for veneration in the chapel of the Notre Dame School of the city. A special chapel was eventually built for the remains in the cathedral and they were transferred there on 14 October 2007, where they are available for <a href="/wiki/Veneration" title="Veneration">veneration</a> by the public.<sup id="cite_ref-CRSA_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CRSA-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alix_Le_Clerc&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The congregation spread throughout France, into which the duchy was forcibly absorbed in the 1630s. Within thirty years of Le Clerc's death, the monastery which had been established in <a href="/wiki/Troyes" title="Troyes">Troyes</a> was instrumental in the extension of her vision to the New World. Through a connection with the governor of <a href="/wiki/Fort_Ville-Marie" title="Fort Ville-Marie">Fort Ville-Marie</a> in the colony of <a href="/wiki/New_France" title="New France">New France</a>, the canonesses had offered to go there to educate its children, but the governor felt that the colony was unable to support a cloistered community of teachers at that stage of its development. Instead, they recruited <a href="/wiki/Marguerite_Bourgeoys" title="Marguerite Bourgeoys">Marguerite Bourgeoys</a>, the president of a <a href="/wiki/Sodality_(Catholic_Church)" class="mw-redirect" title="Sodality (Catholic Church)">sodality</a> attached to the community, to bring this service to the colony. She went there in 1653, and within five years her work there led to the founding of the <a href="/wiki/Congregation_of_Notre_Dame_of_Montreal" title="Congregation of Notre Dame of Montreal">Congregation of Notre Dame of Montreal</a>, an unenclosed institute of religious sisters with the same goal of free education for the poor. Today, they have 1,150 Sisters serving worldwide.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The congregation had also spread to other regions of Europe by the time it faced a century of upheaval, starting with the French Revolution, which closed many of their houses. In central Europe, communities were scattered, moving back and forth between Germany (founded in 1640) and Bohemia. Out of this chaos, <a href="/wiki/Theresa_Gerhardinger" class="mw-redirect" title="Theresa Gerhardinger">Theresa Gerhardinger</a>, a former student of the suppressed monastery in Stadtamhof, came to found the <a href="/wiki/School_Sisters_of_Notre_Dame" title="School Sisters of Notre Dame">School Sisters of Notre Dame</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Bavaria" title="Kingdom of Bavaria">Kingdom of Bavaria</a> in 1833. It currently has 3,500 members working in over 30 countries around the world.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At the time of Fourier's canonization in 1897, thirty monasteries of the congregation still functioned in Europe. Over the next decades, the congregation expanded to South America, Africa, and Asia, and they now serve in 43 nations. Their mission has expanded to include work for human rights, such as the protection of the rights of migrants and the promotion of justice for developing nations. The <a href="/wiki/General_Chapter" class="mw-redirect" title="General Chapter">General Chapter</a> of 2008 formally recognized the many groups of alumni and associates of the congregation which had sprung up around the world as full partners in the heritage of Fourier and Le Clerc.<sup id="cite_ref-CRSA_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CRSA-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
