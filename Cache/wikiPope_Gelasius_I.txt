<p><b>Pope Gelasius I</b> was the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Rome">bishop of Rome</a> from 1 March 492 to his death on 19 November 496.<sup id="cite_ref-ce-gelasius_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-gelasius-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Gelasius was a prolific author whose style placed him on the cusp between <a href="/wiki/Late_Antiquity" class="mw-redirect" title="Late Antiquity">Late Antiquity</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages" title="Early Middle Ages">Early Middle Ages</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ullman_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ullman-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Some scholars have argued that his predecessor <a href="/wiki/Felix_III" class="mw-redirect" title="Felix III">Felix III</a> may have employed him to draft papal documents,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> although this is not certain.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During his pontificate he called for strict <a href="/wiki/Catholic_orthodoxy" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic orthodoxy">Catholic orthodoxy</a>, more assertively demanded obedience to papal authority, and, consequently, increased the tension between the Western and Eastern Churches. Surprisingly, he also had cordial relations with the <a href="/wiki/Ostrogothic_Kingdom" title="Ostrogothic Kingdom">Ostrogoths</a>, who were <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arians</a> (i.e. <a href="/wiki/Nontrinitarianism" title="Nontrinitarianism">Non-trinitarian Christians</a>), and therefore perceived as <a href="/wiki/Heresy_in_Christianity" title="Heresy in Christianity">heretics</a> from the perspective of <a href="/wiki/Nicene_Christianity" class="mw-redirect" title="Nicene Christianity">Nicene Christians</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The feast of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Valentine" title="Saint Valentine">Saint Valentine</a> of February 14 was first established in 496 by Pope Gelasius, who included Valentine among all those "... whose names are justly reverenced among men, but whose acts are known only to God."
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Place_of_birth">Place of birth</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Gelasius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Place of birth">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There is some confusion regarding where Gelasius was born: according to the <i><a href="/wiki/Liber_Pontificalis" title="Liber Pontificalis">Liber Pontificalis</a></i> he was born in <a href="/wiki/Africa_(Roman_province)" title="Africa (Roman province)">Africa</a> ("<i>natione Afer</i>"), while in a letter addressed to the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Emperor">Roman Emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Anastasius_I_Dicorus" title="Anastasius I Dicorus">Anastasius</a> he stated that he was "born a Roman" ("<i>Romanus natus</i>").<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> J. Conant opined that the latter assertion probably merely denotes that he was born in <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Africa" title="Diocese of Africa">Roman Africa</a> before the <a href="/wiki/Vandals" title="Vandals">Vandals</a> invaded it.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Ramsgate_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ramsgate-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Acacian_schism" title="Acacian schism">Acacian schism</a></div>
<p>The papal election of Gelasius on 1 March 492 was a gesture of continuity: Gelasius inherited the conflicts of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Felix_III" title="Pope Felix III">Pope Felix III</a> with <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Roman_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Roman Emperor">Eastern Roman Emperor</a> Anastasius and the <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Patriarch of Constantinople">patriarch of Constantinople</a> and exacerbated them by insisting on the obliteration of the name of the deceased <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_Acacius_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Patriarch Acacius of Constantinople">Patriarch Acacius of Constantinople</a> from the <a href="/wiki/Diptych" title="Diptych">diptychs</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> in spite of every ecumenical gesture by the contemporaneous Patriarch <a href="/wiki/Euphemius_of_Constantinople" title="Euphemius of Constantinople">Euphemius</a> (<i>q. v.</i> for details of the <a href="/wiki/Acacian_schism" title="Acacian schism">Acacian schism</a>).
</p><p>The split with the Emperor and the Patriarch of Constantinople was inevitable, from the Western view, because they adopted the <a href="/wiki/Monophysite" class="mw-redirect" title="Monophysite">Monophysite</a> <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heresy</a> of <a href="/wiki/Jesus_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesus Christ">Jesus Christ</a> having only a Divine nature. Gelasius authored the book <i>De duabus in Christo naturis</i> (<i>On the dual nature of Christ</i>), which described <a href="/wiki/Catholic_theology" title="Catholic theology">Catholic doctrine</a> in the matter. Thus Gelasius, for all the conservative Latinity of his style of writing, was on the cusp of <a href="/wiki/Late_Antiquity" class="mw-redirect" title="Late Antiquity">Late Antiquity</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages" title="Early Middle Ages">Early Middle Ages</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ullman_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ullman-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During the Acacian schism, Gelasius advocated the primacy of the <a href="/wiki/See_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="See of Rome">See of Rome</a> over the universal Church, both East and West, and he presented this doctrine in terms that became the model for successive popes, who also claimed <a href="/wiki/Papal_supremacy" title="Papal supremacy">papal supremacy</a> because of their succession to the <a href="/wiki/Supreme_Pontiff" class="mw-redirect" title="Supreme Pontiff">papacy</a> from the first supreme pontiff, <a href="/wiki/Peter_the_Apostle" class="mw-redirect" title="Peter the Apostle">Peter the Apostle</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2013)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>In 494, Gelasius authored the very influential letter <i><a href="/wiki/Duo_sunt" class="mw-redirect" title="Duo sunt">Duo sunt</a></i> to Anastasius on the subject of the relation of Church and state, which letter had political impact for almost a millennium after.<sup id="cite_ref-Fordham_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fordham-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Suppression_of_the_Lupercalia">Suppression of the Lupercalia</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Gelasius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Suppression of the Lupercalia">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Lupercalia#History" title="Lupercalia">Lupercalia §&#160;History</a></div>
<p>Closer to home, after a long contest Gelasius finally suppressed the <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Roman" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Roman">ancient Roman</a> festival of the <a href="/wiki/Lupercalia" title="Lupercalia">Lupercalia</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Ramsgate_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ramsgate-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> which had persisted for several generations among a nominally Christian population. Gelasius' letter to the Senator Andromachus treated the primary contentions of the controversy and incidentally provided some details of the festival, which combined <a href="/wiki/Fertility" title="Fertility">fertility</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ritual_purification" title="Ritual purification">purification</a>, that might have been lost otherwise. Although the Lupercalia was a festival of purification, which had given its name "<i>dies februatus</i>", from "<i>februare</i>" ("to purify"), to the month of February, it was unrelated to the Feast of the Purification of the <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Blessed Virgin Mary">Blessed Virgin Mary</a>, also commonly denominated "<a href="/wiki/Candlemas" title="Candlemas">Candlemas</a>", which latter feast commemorates the fulfillment of the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Family" title="Holy Family">Holy Family</a>'s ceremonial obligations pursuant to Mosaic law 40 days after the birth of the first son. In the instance of the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Family" title="Holy Family">Holy Family</a>, that occurred 40 days after <a href="/wiki/Christmas" title="Christmas">Christmas</a>, <i>scire licet</i>, on 2 February.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Gelasius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After a brief yet dynamic ministry, Gelasius died on 19 November AD 496. His feast day is 21 November, the anniversary of his <a href="/wiki/Interment" class="mw-redirect" title="Interment">interment</a>, not his death.<sup id="cite_ref-ce-gelasius_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-gelasius-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Gelasius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Couronnement_d%27un_prince_-_Sacramentaire_de_Charles_le_Chauve_Lat1141_f2v.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Couronnement_d%27un_prince_-_Sacramentaire_de_Charles_le_Chauve_Lat1141_f2v.jpg/300px-Couronnement_d%27un_prince_-_Sacramentaire_de_Charles_le_Chauve_Lat1141_f2v.jpg" decoding="async" width="300" height="388" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Couronnement_d%27un_prince_-_Sacramentaire_de_Charles_le_Chauve_Lat1141_f2v.jpg/450px-Couronnement_d%27un_prince_-_Sacramentaire_de_Charles_le_Chauve_Lat1141_f2v.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Couronnement_d%27un_prince_-_Sacramentaire_de_Charles_le_Chauve_Lat1141_f2v.jpg/600px-Couronnement_d%27un_prince_-_Sacramentaire_de_Charles_le_Chauve_Lat1141_f2v.jpg 2x" data-file-width="934" data-file-height="1209" /></a><figcaption>Image of c. AD 870 featuring the coronation of <a href="/wiki/Charles_the_Bald" title="Charles the Bald">Charles the Bald</a>, flanked by Gelasius I and <a href="/wiki/Gregory_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Gregory the Great">Gregory the Great</a>. Gelasius' writings gave him a high status with posterity.</figcaption></figure> 
<p>Gelasius was one of the most prolific authors of the early <a href="/wiki/Bishops_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishops of Rome">bishops of Rome</a>. Over 100 Gelasian letters survive, although 49 of these are fragmentary, some as short as several lines.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Additionally, 6 treatises are extant that bear the name of Gelasius. According to <a href="/wiki/Cassiodorus" title="Cassiodorus">Cassiodorus</a>, the reputation of Gelasius attracted to his name other works not by him. Although his dogmatic letters connected to the Acacian Schism were widely circulated in late antiquity (and have been the focus of much scholarly interest), the majority of Gelasius' letters were in fact concerned with the administration of the church of suburbicarian Italy.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Decretum_Gelasianum"><i>Decretum Gelasianum</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Gelasius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Decretum Gelasianum">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Decretum_Gelasianum" class="mw-redirect" title="Decretum Gelasianum">Decretum Gelasianum</a></div>
<p>The most famous of pseudo-Gelasian works is the list <i>De libris recipiendis et non recipiendis</i> ("On books to be received and not to be received"), also denominated the <i><a href="/wiki/Decretum_Gelasianum" class="mw-redirect" title="Decretum Gelasianum">Decretum Gelasianum</a></i>, which is believed to be connected to the pressure for orthodoxy during his pontificate and intended to be read as a decretal by Gelasius on the canonical and apocryphal books, which internal evidence reveals to be of later date. Thus the determination of the <a href="/wiki/The_Canon_of_Scripture" class="mw-redirect" title="The Canon of Scripture">canon of Sacred Scripture</a> has traditionally been attributed to Gelasius.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Gelasian_Sacramentary"><i>Gelasian Sacramentary</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Gelasius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Gelasian Sacramentary">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Gelasian_Sacramentary" title="Gelasian Sacramentary">Gelasian Sacramentary</a></div>
<p>In the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Catholic">Latin Catholic</a> tradition, the pseudo <i>Gelasian Sacramentary</i> is in fact a liturgical book that was derived from Roman sources and transcribed, with inclusion of native Gallican liturgical elements, near <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a> in the middle of the 8th century AD. While including the texts of some prayers that Gelasius composed, he was not a principal author or compiler of the book. The manuscript (Vatican, Vatican Library, Reg. lat. 316 + Paris, National Library, ms. lat. 7193, fol. 41–56) is actually titled the <i>Liber sacramentorum Romanae ecclesiae</i> (<i>Book of Sacraments of the Roman Church</i>). The attribution to Gelasius is premised in part at least on the chronicle of the <a href="/wiki/Supreme_Pontiff" class="mw-redirect" title="Supreme Pontiff">Supreme Pontiffs</a> that is denominated the <i>Liber Pontificalis</i>, which states of Gelasius that he "<i>fecit etiam et sacramentorum praefationes et orationes cauto sermone et epistulas fidei delimato sermone multas</i>" ("he also made prefaces to the sacraments and prayers in careful language and many epistles in polished language regarding the faith").<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> An old tradition linked the book to Gelasius, apparently based on the ascription of <a href="/wiki/Walafrid_Strabo" title="Walafrid Strabo">Walafrid Strabo</a> to him of what evidently is this book.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Gelasius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Figurengruppe_Schloss_Stainz_Hl_Gelasius.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/Figurengruppe_Schloss_Stainz_Hl_Gelasius.jpg/170px-Figurengruppe_Schloss_Stainz_Hl_Gelasius.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="253" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/Figurengruppe_Schloss_Stainz_Hl_Gelasius.jpg/255px-Figurengruppe_Schloss_Stainz_Hl_Gelasius.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/Figurengruppe_Schloss_Stainz_Hl_Gelasius.jpg/340px-Figurengruppe_Schloss_Stainz_Hl_Gelasius.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1944" data-file-height="2896" /></a><figcaption>Statue of Gelasius I, <a href="/wiki/Schloss_Stainz" title="Schloss Stainz">Schloss Stainz</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Giuseppe_Maria_Tomasi" title="Giuseppe Maria Tomasi">Giuseppe Maria Tomasi</a> quoted a portion of a missal that was attributed to Gelasius in the Mass that was entitled 'Contra obloquentes' and published it. The section read: "Grant, We beseech Thee, O Lord, that we do not trouble ourselves about the contradiction of spurious minds, but once that very wickedness has been spurned let us pray that you suffer us neither to be frightened by the unjust criticisms, nor to be attracted to the insidious flatteries, but rather to love that which Thou dost command ...". In 1751, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XIV" title="Pope Benedict XIV">Pope Benedict XIV</a> published this quotation within his Apostolic Constitution "<i>Providas</i>" that attacked freemasonry.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
