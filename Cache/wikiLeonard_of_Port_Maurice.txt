<p><b>Leonard of Port Maurice</b>, <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Friars_Minor" title="Order of Friars Minor">O.F.M.</a>, (<a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">San Leonardo da Porto Maurizio</i>; 20 December 1676 – 26 November 1751) was an Italian <a href="/wiki/Franciscan" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan">Franciscan</a> preacher and <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a> writer.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonard_of_Port_Maurice&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Leonard was born 19 December 1676, the son of Domenico Casanova and Anna Maria Benza. He was given the name Paul Jerome Casanova.<sup id="cite_ref-cna_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cna-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Leonard's father was a ship captain whose family lived in Port Maurice on the northwestern coast of Italy. At 13, Leonard went to Rome to live with his uncle Agostino and study at the Jesuit Roman College. He was a good student and destined for a career in medicine. In 1697, however, he joined the Friars Minor.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> When he decided against medicine, his uncle disowned him.<sup id="cite_ref-cna_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cna-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> On 2 October 1697, he received the habit and took the name Brother Leonard, after a relative who had been kind to him.<sup id="cite_ref-osv_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-osv-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> After making his novitiate at <a href="/wiki/Ponticelli" title="Ponticelli">Ponticelli</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Sabina_(region)" title="Sabina (region)">Sabine mountains</a>, he completed his studies at St. Bonaventura on the Palatine at Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-bihl_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bihl-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After his ordination he remained there as lector (professor), and expected to be sent on the <a href="/wiki/Christianity_in_China" title="Christianity in China">Chinese missions</a>. After ordination Leonard contracted a bleeding ulcer and was sent to his hometown <sup id="cite_ref-foley_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> where there was a monastery of the <a href="/wiki/Franciscan_Observants" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan Observants">Franciscan Observants</a> (1704). After four years he was restored to health, and began to preach in Porto Maurizio and the happiness
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mission_work">Mission work</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonard_of_Port_Maurice&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Mission work">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>When <a href="/wiki/Cosimo_III_de%27_Medici" class="mw-redirect" title="Cosimo III de&#39; Medici">Cosimo III de' Medici</a> handed over the monastery del Monte (on <a href="/wiki/San_Miniato" title="San Miniato">San Miniato</a> near <a href="/wiki/Florence" title="Florence">Florence</a>, also called Monte alle Croci) to the members of the Riformella, Leonard was sent hither under the auspices and by desire of Cosimo III, and began shortly to hold missions among the people of Tuscany. His colleagues and he practiced austerities and penances during these missions. In 1710 he founded the <a href="/wiki/Convento_dell%27Incontro" title="Convento dell&#39;Incontro">monastery of Icontro</a>, on a peak in the mountains about four miles from Florence, whither he and his assistants could retire from time to time after missions, and devote themselves to spiritual renewal.<sup id="cite_ref-bihl_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bihl-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Alphonsus_Liguori" title="Alphonsus Liguori">Alphonsus Liguori</a> called Leonard "the great missionary of the 18th century".<sup id="cite_ref-foley_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He attracted large crowds and was invited to visit and preach in many places.<sup id="cite_ref-cna_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cna-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Leonard spent over forty years preaching retreats, Lenten sermons and parish missions throughout Italy. His missions lasted 15 to 18 days, and he often stayed an additional week to hear confessions.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1720 he crossed the borders of <a href="/wiki/Tuscany" title="Tuscany">Tuscany</a> and held his celebrated missions in Central and Southern Italy. Everywhere Leonard made conversions, and was very often obliged both in cities and country districts to preach in the open, as the churches could not contain the thousands who came to listen.<sup id="cite_ref-bihl_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bihl-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_XII" title="Pope Clement XII">Pope Clement XII</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XIV" title="Pope Benedict XIV">Pope Benedict XIV</a> called him to Rome; the latter especially held him in high esteem both as a preacher and as a propagandist, and exacted a promise that he would come to Rome to die. Pope Benedict XIV appointed him to several complex diplomatic assignments. In Genoa and Corsica, in Lucca and Spoleto the citizens expected a bejeweled cardinal to represent the intentions of the pope. Instead, they were confronted by a humble, shoeless, muddy friar to confound their hostility and pride.<sup id="cite_ref-passionist_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-passionist-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>For a time, Leonard was the spiritual director of <a href="/wiki/Maria_Clementina_Sobieska" title="Maria Clementina Sobieska">Maria Clementina Sobieska</a> of Poland, the wife of <a href="/wiki/James_Francis_Edward_Stuart" title="James Francis Edward Stuart">James Stuart, the Old Pretender</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-osv_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-osv-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Leonard founded many pious societies and <a href="/wiki/Confraternity" title="Confraternity">confraternities</a>, and exerted himself to spread devotion to the <a href="/wiki/Sacred_Heart_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Sacred Heart of Jesus">Sacred Heart of Jesus</a>, and the perpetual adoration of the <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Sacrament" class="mw-redirect" title="Blessed Sacrament">Blessed Sacrament</a>. He also began to insist that the concept of the <a href="/wiki/Immaculate_Conception" title="Immaculate Conception">Immaculate Conception</a> of Mary be defined as a dogma of the faith.<sup id="cite_ref-passionist_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-passionist-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Franciscans had been the custodians of the Holy Sites in the Holy Land, including of the "Way of the Cross", since 1343. Though many saints were devoted to the <a href="/wiki/Stations_of_the_Cross" title="Stations of the Cross">Stations of the Cross</a>, few if any did more to promote them than Leonard. As a Franciscan priest, he preached the Way of the Cross at missions for forty-three years and reportedly set up stations in 571 locations throughout Italy, including the Colosseum in Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-osv_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-osv-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>From May to November, 1744, he preached in <a href="/wiki/Corsica" title="Corsica">Corsica</a>, which at that time belonged to the <a href="/wiki/Republic_of_Genoa" title="Republic of Genoa">Republic of Genoa</a> and which was torn by party strife. In November, 1751, when he was preaching to the <a href="/wiki/Bologna" title="Bologna">Bolognese</a>, Benedict XIV called him to Rome, as already there were indications of his rapidly approaching end. The strain of his missionary labors and his mortifications had completely exhausted his body. He arrived on the evening of 26 November 1751, at his beloved monastery of St. Bonaventura on the Palatine, and expired on the same night at eleven o'clock at the age of seventy-four.<sup id="cite_ref-bihl_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bihl-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonard_of_Port_Maurice&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_VI" title="Pope Pius VI">Pope Pius VI</a> pronounced his <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatification</a> on 19 June 1796, and <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_IX" title="Pope Pius IX">Pope Pius IX</a> his <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a> on 29 June 1867.<sup id="cite_ref-passionist_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-passionist-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The Franciscan Order celebrates his feast on 26 November, but outside this Order it is often celebrated on 27 November.
</p><p>The partly incorrupt body of Leonard is kept in the high altar of the church of St. Bonaventure monastery in Rome, where he died.<sup id="cite_ref-bihl_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bihl-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St._Leonard%27s_Church,_Boston" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Leonard&#39;s Church, Boston">St. Leonard's Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Boston" title="Boston">Boston</a>, founded in 1873, is the first Roman Catholic Church in New England built by Italian immigrants. The church is located in the historic <a href="/wiki/North_End,_Boston" title="North End, Boston">North End</a> of Boston at the corner of Hanover and Prince Streets on Boston's <a href="/wiki/Freedom_Trail" title="Freedom Trail">Freedom Trail</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonard_of_Port_Maurice&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The numerous writings of the saint consist of sermons, letters, ascetic treatises, and books of devotion for the use of the faithful and of priests, especially missionaries. The <i>Diary</i> (<i>Diario</i>) of his missions is written by the friar Diego da Firenze. A treasure for asceticism and homiletics, many of his writings have been translated into the most diverse languages and often republished: for example his <i>Via Sacra spianata ed illuminata</i> <sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> (the Way of the Cross simplified and explained), <i>Il Tesoro Nascosto</i> (on the Holy Mass); his celebrated <i>Proponimenti</i>, or resolutions for the attainment of higher Christian perfection. A complete edition of his works appeared first at Rome in thirteen octavo volumes (1853–84), <i>Collezione completa delle opere di B. Leonardo da Porto Maurizio</i>. Then another in five octavo volumes, <i>Opere complete di S. Leonardo di Porto Maurizio</i> (Venice, 1868–9). In English, German, etc., only single works have been issued, but a French translation of the entire set has appeared: <i>Œuvres complètes de S. Léonard de Port-Maurice</i> (8 vols., Paris and Tournai, 1858), and <i>Sermons de S. Léonard de Port-Maurice</i> (3 vols., Paris).
</p>
