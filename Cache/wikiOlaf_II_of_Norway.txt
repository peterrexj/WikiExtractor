<p><b>Olaf II Haraldsson</b> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;995</span> – 29 July 1030), later known as <b>Saint Olaf</b>, was <a href="/wiki/List_of_Norwegian_monarchs" title="List of Norwegian monarchs">King of Norway</a> from 1015 to 1028. Son of <a href="/wiki/Harald_Grenske" title="Harald Grenske">Harald Grenske</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Petty_king" class="mw-redirect" title="Petty king">petty king</a> in <a href="/wiki/Vestfold" title="Vestfold">Vestfold</a>, Norway,<sup id="cite_ref-nyg_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nyg-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> he was posthumously given the title <i><a href="/wiki/Rex_Perpetuus_Norvegiae" title="Rex Perpetuus Norvegiae">Rex Perpetuus Norvegiae</a></i> (English: <span lang="en">Eternal/Perpetual King of Norway</span>) and <a href="/wiki/Canonised" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonised">canonised</a> at <a href="/wiki/Nidaros" title="Nidaros">Nidaros</a> (<a href="/wiki/Trondheim" title="Trondheim">Trondheim</a>) by Bishop <a href="/wiki/Grimketel" title="Grimketel">Grimketel</a>, one year after his death in the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Stiklestad" title="Battle of Stiklestad">Battle of Stiklestad</a> on 29 July 1030. His remains were enshrined in <a href="/wiki/Nidaros_Cathedral" title="Nidaros Cathedral">Nidaros Cathedral</a>, built over his burial site. His sainthood encouraged the widespread adoption of <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a> by <a href="/wiki/Scandinavia" title="Scandinavia">Scandinavia</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Vikings" title="Vikings">Vikings</a>/<a href="/wiki/Norsemen" title="Norsemen">Norsemen</a>.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Pope_Alexander_III" title="Pope Alexander III">Pope Alexander III</a> confirmed Olaf's local canonisation in 1164, making him a recognised saint of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> and started to be known as <i>Rex Perpetuus Norvegiae</i> – <i>eternal king of Norway</i>. Following the <a href="/wiki/Reformation" title="Reformation">Reformation</a>, he was a commemorated historical figure among some members of the <a href="/wiki/Lutheranism" title="Lutheranism">Lutheran</a> and Anglican Communions.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The saga of Olav Haraldsson and the legend of Olaf the Saint became central to a national identity. Especially during the period of <a href="/wiki/Norwegian_romantic_nationalism" title="Norwegian romantic nationalism">romantic nationalism</a>, Olaf was a symbol of Norwegian independence and pride. Saint Olaf is symbolised by the axe in <a href="/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_Norway" title="Coat of arms of Norway">Norway's coat of arms</a> and <a href="/wiki/Olsok" title="Olsok">Olsok</a> (29 July) is still his day of celebration. Many Christian institutions with Scandinavian links as well as Norway's <a href="/wiki/Order_of_St._Olav" title="Order of St. Olav">Order of St. Olav</a> are named after him.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Name">Name</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Name">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Olaf's <a href="/wiki/Old_Norse" title="Old Norse">Old Norse</a> name is <i>Óláfr Haraldsson</i> <small></small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="non-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA" title="Help:IPA">[ˈoːlɑːvz̠ ˈhɑrɑldsˌson]</a></span>. During his lifetime he was known as Olaf "the fat" or "the stout"<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> or simply as Olaf "the big" (<i>Ólafr digri</i> <small></small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="non-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA" title="Help:IPA">[ˈdiɣre]</a></span>; Modern Norwegian <i>Olav Digre</i>).<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He was also called Olaf 'the Lawbreaker' for his many brutal ways of converting the Norwegian populace. In Norway today,<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items" title="Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers"><span title="The time period mentioned near this tag is ambiguous. (May 2023)">when?</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> he is commonly called <i>Olav den hellige</i> (<a href="/wiki/Bokm%C3%A5l" title="Bokmål">Bokmål</a>; Olaf the Holy) or <i>Heilag-Olav</i> (<a href="/wiki/Nynorsk" title="Nynorsk">Nynorsk</a>; the Holy Olaf) in honour of his sainthood.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Olaf Haraldsson had the given name <i>Óláfr</i> in <a href="/wiki/Old_Norse" title="Old Norse">Old Norse</a> (etymology: <i>Anu-</i> "forefather", -laibaR <i>—</i>"heir"). <i>Olav</i> is the modern equivalent in <a href="/wiki/Norwegian_language" title="Norwegian language">Norwegian</a>, formerly often spelt <i>Olaf</i>. His name in <a href="/wiki/Icelandic_language" title="Icelandic language">Icelandic</a> is <i>Ólafur</i> <small></small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="is-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Icelandic" title="Help:IPA/Icelandic">[ˈouːlaːvʏr̥]</a></span>, in <a href="/wiki/Faroese_language" title="Faroese language">Faroese</a> <i>Ólavur</i>, in <a href="/wiki/Danish_language" title="Danish language">Danish</a> <i>Olav</i>, in <a href="/wiki/Swedish_language" title="Swedish language">Swedish</a> <i>Olof</i>, and in <a href="/wiki/Finnish_language" title="Finnish language">Finnish</a> <i>Olavi</i>. Olave was the traditional spelling in England, preserved in the name of medieval churches dedicated to him. Other names, such as <i>Óláfr hinn helgi</i>, <i>Olavus rex</i>, and Olaf are used interchangeably (see the <i><a href="/wiki/Heimskringla" title="Heimskringla">Heimskringla</a></i> of <a href="/wiki/Snorri_Sturluson" title="Snorri Sturluson">Snorri Sturluson</a>). He is sometimes called <i><a href="/wiki/Rex_Perpetuus_Norvegiae" title="Rex Perpetuus Norvegiae">Rex Perpetuus Norvegiae</a></i> (English: <span lang="en">"Norway's Eternal King"</span>), a designation which goes back to the 13th century.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background">Background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>St. Olaf is attested having been born in <a href="/wiki/Ringerike_(traditional_district)" title="Ringerike (traditional district)">Ringerike</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-socc_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-socc-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> yet Ringerike must not be conflated with the modern notion of the district named after the legendary Ringerike of <a href="/wiki/Ivar_Vidfamne" title="Ivar Vidfamne">Ivar Vidfamne</a> and Sigurd Hring, which may be regarded as the confederation of five petty kingdoms conferring with the five kings that established Olaf Haraldson as their High King at Hringsakri according to Saint Olafs Saga, King Hrœrekr, King Guðrøðr, King Hring and two others of less certain identity. Olaf Haraldsson did not become King of Norway until the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Nesjar" title="Battle of Nesjar">Battle of Nesjar</a>. St. Olaf was the son of <a href="/wiki/%C3%85sta_Gudbrandsdatter" title="Åsta Gudbrandsdatter">Åsta Gudbrandsdatter</a> and <a href="/wiki/Harald_Grenske" title="Harald Grenske">Harald Grenske</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Petty_king" class="mw-redirect" title="Petty king">petty king</a> in <a href="/wiki/Vestfold" title="Vestfold">Vestfold</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-nyg_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nyg-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> whom later Icelandic sagas would describe as a great-great-grandchild of <a href="/wiki/Harald_Fairhair" title="Harald Fairhair">Harald Fairhair</a> (who had unified Norway as one Kingdom, establishing a feudalist structure with the kingship far less dependent of local rulers, earls, herses and the so called petty kings). Harald Grenski died when Åsta Gudbrandsdatter was pregnant with Olaf. He was the son of <a href="/wiki/Gudr%C3%B8d_Bj%C3%B8rnsson" title="Gudrød Bjørnsson">Gudrød Bjørnsson</a>, King <a href="/wiki/Bj%C3%B8rn_Farmann" title="Bjørn Farmann">Bjørn Farmann</a> in Vestfold, that is, son of King Harald I Fairhair Halvdansson. Åsta later married <a href="/wiki/Sigurd_Syr" title="Sigurd Syr">Sigurd Syr</a>, with whom she had other children, including <a href="/wiki/Harald_Hardrada" title="Harald Hardrada">Harald Hardrada</a>, who later reigned as king of Norway.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> The petty kings of Ringerike seem to have had some claims to the High Kingship of the Commonwealth of Uppsala posing a possible threat to the royal <a href="/wiki/House_of_Muns%C3%B6" title="House of Munsö">House of Munsö</a> under King Olof Skötkonung. The <a href="/wiki/Earls_of_Lade" title="Earls of Lade">Earls of Hlaðir</a> and the petty kings of Hringerike had been in conflict since at least King Harald Fairhair assumed power of all of Norway.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Saga_sources_for_Olaf_Haraldsson">Saga sources for Olaf Haraldsson</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Saga sources for Olaf Haraldsson">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Many texts have information about Olaf Haraldsson.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The oldest is the <i>Glælognskviða</i> or "Sea-Calm Poem", composed by <a href="/wiki/%C3%9E%C3%B3rarinn_loftunga" title="Þórarinn loftunga">Þórarinn loftunga</a>, an Icelander. It praises Olaf and mentions some of the famous miracles attributed to him. The Norwegian synoptic histories also mention Olaf. These include the <span title="Icelandic-language text"><i lang="is"><a href="/wiki/%C3%81grip_af_N%C3%B3regskonungas%C3%B6gum" title="Ágrip af Nóregskonungasögum">Ágrip af Nóregskonungasögum</a></i></span> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1190</span>), the <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_Norwegiae" class="mw-redirect" title="Historia Norwegiae">Historia Norwegiae</a></i> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1160</span>–1175) and a Latin text, <i>Historia de Antiquitate Regum Norwagiensium</i> by <a href="/wiki/Theodoric_the_Monk" title="Theodoric the Monk">Theodoric the Monk</a> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1177</span>–1188).<sup id="cite_ref-autogenerated1_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated1-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Icelanders also wrote extensively about Olaf and there are several Icelandic sagas about him, including <i><a href="/wiki/Fagrskinna" title="Fagrskinna">Fagrskinna</a></i> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1220</span>) and <i><a href="/wiki/Morkinskinna" title="Morkinskinna">Morkinskinna</a></i> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1225–1235</span>). <i><a href="/wiki/Heimskringla" title="Heimskringla">Heimskringla</a></i> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1225</span>), by <a href="/wiki/Snorri_Sturluson" title="Snorri Sturluson">Snorri Sturluson</a>, largely bases its account of Olaf on the earlier <i><a href="/wiki/Fagrskinna" title="Fagrskinna">Fagrskinna</a></i>. The <i><a href="/wiki/Oldest_Saga_of_St._Olaf" title="Oldest Saga of St. Olaf">Oldest Saga of St. Olaf</a></i> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1200</span>) is important to scholars for its constant use of <a href="/wiki/Skald" title="Skald">skaldic</a> verses, many of which are attributed to Olaf himself.<sup id="cite_ref-autogenerated1_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated1-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Finally, many <a href="/wiki/Hagiographic" class="mw-redirect" title="Hagiographic">hagiographic</a> sources describe St. Olaf, but these focus mostly on miracles attributed to him and cannot be used to accurately recreate his life. A notable one is <i><a href="/wiki/Passio_Olavi" title="Passio Olavi">The Passion and the Miracles of the Blessed Olafr</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Reign">Reign</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Reign">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<p>A widely used account of Olaf's life is found in <i><a href="/wiki/Heimskringla" title="Heimskringla">Heimskringla</a></i> from <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1225</span>. Although its facts are dubious, the saga recounts Olaf's deeds as follows:
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Norway_1020_AD.png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Norway_1020_AD.png/170px-Norway_1020_AD.png" decoding="async" width="170" height="300" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Norway_1020_AD.png/255px-Norway_1020_AD.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Norway_1020_AD.png/340px-Norway_1020_AD.png 2x" data-file-width="1134" data-file-height="2002" /></a><figcaption>Norway in 1020</figcaption></figure><p> In 1008, Olaf landed on the <a href="/wiki/Estonia" title="Estonia">Estonian</a> island of <a href="/wiki/Saaremaa" title="Saaremaa">Saaremaa</a> (Osilia). The <a href="/wiki/Osilians" class="mw-redirect" title="Osilians">Osilians</a>, taken by surprise, had at first agreed to Olaf's demands, but then gathered an army during the negotiations and attacked the Norwegians. Olaf nevertheless won the battle.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It is said that Olaf participated alongside fellow Viking <a href="/wiki/Thorkell_the_Tall" title="Thorkell the Tall">Thorkell the Tall</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Siege_of_Canterbury" title="Siege of Canterbury">siege of Canterbury</a> in 1011.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Olaf sailed to the southern coast of <a href="/wiki/Finland" title="Finland">Finland</a> sometime in 1008.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> The journey resulted in the <a href="/wiki/Battle_at_Herdaler" title="Battle at Herdaler">Battle at Herdaler</a>, where Olaf and his men were ambushed in the woods. Olaf lost many men but made it back to his boats. He ordered his ships to depart despite a rising storm. The Finns pursued them and made the same progress on land as Olaf and his men made on water. Despite these events they survived. The exact location of the battle is uncertain and the Finnish equivalent of Herdaler is unknown. It has been suggested that it could be in <a href="/wiki/Uusimaa_(historical_province)" title="Uusimaa (historical province)">Uusimaa</a>.
</p><p>As a teenager Olaf went to the <a href="/wiki/Baltic_region" title="Baltic region">Baltic</a>, then to <a href="/wiki/Denmark" title="Denmark">Denmark</a> and later to <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a>. Skaldic poetry suggests he led a successful seaborne attack that took down <a href="/wiki/London_Bridge" title="London Bridge">London Bridge</a>, though Anglo-Saxon sources do not confirm this. This may have been in 1014, restoring London and the English throne to <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_the_Unready" title="Æthelred the Unready">Æthelred the Unready</a> and removing Cnut.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> According to Snorri's <a href="/wiki/Heimskringla" title="Heimskringla">Heimskringla</a>, the attack happened soon after the death of <a href="/wiki/Sweyn_Forkbeard" title="Sweyn Forkbeard">Sweyn Forkbeard</a> with the city being held by Danish forces. Snorri's account claims that Olaf assisted Æthelred in driving the Danes out of England. Olaf is also said by Snorri to have aided the sons of Æthelred after his death. Olaf is said to have won battles but been unable to assist Æthelred's sons in driving Cnut out. After this, he set his sights on Norway.
</p><p>Olaf saw it as his calling to unite Norway into one kingdom, as <a href="/wiki/Harald_Fairhair" title="Harald Fairhair">Harald Fairhair</a> had largely succeeded in doing. On the way home he wintered with Duke <a href="/wiki/Richard_II_of_Normandy" class="mw-redirect" title="Richard II of Normandy">Richard II of Normandy</a>. <a href="/wiki/Norsemen" title="Norsemen">Norsemen</a> had conquered this region in 881. Richard was himself an ardent Christian, and the <a href="/wiki/Normans" title="Normans">Normans</a> had also previously converted to Christianity. Before leaving, Olaf was baptised in <a href="/wiki/Rouen" title="Rouen">Rouen</a><sup id="cite_ref-socc_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-socc-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> in the pre-Romanesque <a href="/wiki/Rouen_Cathedral" title="Rouen Cathedral">Notre-Dame Cathedral</a> by Richard's brother <a href="/wiki/Robert_II_(archbishop_of_Rouen)" title="Robert II (archbishop of Rouen)">Robert the Dane</a>, archbishop of Normandy.
</p><p>Olaf returned to Norway in 1015 and declared himself king, obtaining the support of the five <a href="/wiki/Petty_kingdom" title="Petty kingdom">petty kings</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Uplands,_Norway" title="Uplands, Norway">Norwegian Uplands</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEReeves191162_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEReeves191162-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> In 1016 at the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Nesjar" title="Battle of Nesjar">Battle of Nesjar</a> he defeated <a href="/wiki/Sveinn_H%C3%A1konarson" class="mw-redirect" title="Sveinn Hákonarson">Earl Sweyn</a>, one of the <a href="/wiki/Earls_of_Lade" title="Earls of Lade">earls of Lade</a> and hitherto the <i>de facto</i> ruler of Norway. He founded the town of Borg, later known as <a href="/wiki/Sarpsborg" title="Sarpsborg">Sarpsborg</a>, by the waterfall <a href="/wiki/Sarpsfossen" class="mw-redirect" title="Sarpsfossen">Sarpsfossen</a> in <a href="/wiki/%C3%98stfold" title="Østfold">Østfold</a> county. Within a few years he had won more power than any of his predecessors on the throne had enjoyed.
</p><p>Olaf annihilated the petty kings of the douth, subdued the aristocracy, asserted his <a href="/wiki/Suzerainty" title="Suzerainty">suzerainty</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Orkney_Islands" class="mw-redirect" title="Orkney Islands">Orkney Islands</a>, and conducted a successful raid on Denmark.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEReeves191162_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEReeves191162-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>  He made peace with King <a href="/wiki/Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung" title="Olof Skötkonung">Olof Skötkonung</a> of <a href="/wiki/Sweden" title="Sweden">Sweden</a> through <a href="/wiki/%C3%9Eorgn%C3%BDr_the_Lawspeaker" class="mw-redirect" title="Þorgnýr the Lawspeaker">Þorgnýr the Lawspeaker</a>, and was for some time engaged to Olof's daughter, <a href="/wiki/Ingegerd_Olofsdotter" class="mw-redirect" title="Ingegerd Olofsdotter">Ingegerd</a>, though without Olof's approval. In 1019 Olaf married <a href="/wiki/Astrid_Olofsdotter_of_Sweden" title="Astrid Olofsdotter of Sweden">Astrid Olofsdotter</a>, King Olof's illegitimate daughter and the half-sister of his former fiancée. The union produced a daughter, <a href="/wiki/Wulfhild_of_Norway" title="Wulfhild of Norway">Wulfhild</a>, who married <a href="/wiki/Ordulf,_Duke_of_Saxony" title="Ordulf, Duke of Saxony">Ordulf, Duke of Saxony</a> in 1042.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Olaf's success was short-lived. In 1026 he lost the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_the_Helge%C3%A5" class="mw-redirect" title="Battle of the Helgeå">Battle of the Helgeå</a>,<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> and in 1029 the Norwegian nobles, seething with discontent, supported the invasion of King <a href="/wiki/Cnut_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Cnut the Great">Cnut the Great</a> of Denmark. Olaf was driven into exile in <a href="/wiki/Kievan_Rus" class="mw-redirect" title="Kievan Rus">Kievan Rus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEReeves191162_18-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEReeves191162-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-socc_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-socc-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He stayed for some time in the Swedish province of <a href="/wiki/History_of_Nericia" class="mw-redirect" title="History of Nericia">Nerike</a>, where, according to local legend, he baptised many locals. In 1029, King Cnut's Norwegian regent, Jarl <a href="/wiki/H%C3%A5kon_Eiriksson" class="mw-redirect" title="Håkon Eiriksson">Håkon Eiriksson</a>, was lost at sea and Olaf seized the opportunity to win back the kingdom. Given military and logistical support by the Swedish king <a href="/wiki/Anund_Jacob" title="Anund Jacob">Anund Jacob</a> he tried to bypass the formidable "Øresundfleet" of the Danish king by traveling across the <a href="/wiki/J%C3%A4mtland" title="Jämtland">Jämtland</a>-mountains to take Nidaros, the Norwegian capital at the time, in 1030. However, Olaf was killed in <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Stiklestad" title="Battle of Stiklestad">Battle of Stiklestad</a>, where some of his own subjects from central and northern Norway took arms against him. The exact position of Saint Olaf's grave in Nidaros has been unknown since 1568, due to the effects of the <a href="/wiki/Reformation_in_Denmark%E2%80%93Norway_and_Holstein" title="Reformation in Denmark–Norway and Holstein">Lutheran iconoclasm</a> in 1536–37.
</p><p>King Cnut, though distracted by the task of governing England, ruled Norway for five years after Stiklestad, with his son <a href="/wiki/Svein_Knutsson" title="Svein Knutsson">Svein</a> and Svein's mother <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfgifu_of_Northampton" title="Ælfgifu of Northampton">Ælfgifu</a> (known as <i>Álfífa</i> in Old Norse sources) as regents. But their regency was unpopular, and when Olaf's illegitimate son <a href="/wiki/Magnus_I_of_Norway" class="mw-redirect" title="Magnus I of Norway">Magnus</a> ('the Good') laid claim to the Norwegian throne, Svein and Ælfgifu were forced to flee.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Christianising">Christianising</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Christianising">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Bischofstab_Norwegen_1375-1400.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="St. Olaf with his axe, depicted on an ivory crozier" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Bischofstab_Norwegen_1375-1400.JPG/170px-Bischofstab_Norwegen_1375-1400.JPG" decoding="async" width="170" height="227" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Bischofstab_Norwegen_1375-1400.JPG/255px-Bischofstab_Norwegen_1375-1400.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Bischofstab_Norwegen_1375-1400.JPG/340px-Bischofstab_Norwegen_1375-1400.JPG 2x" data-file-width="2389" data-file-height="3188" /></a><figcaption>St. Olaf with his axe on a <a href="/wiki/Crozier" class="mw-redirect" title="Crozier">bishop's crozier</a>, walrus ivory, Norway c. 1375–1400</figcaption></figure>
<p>Olaf has traditionally been seen as leading the <a href="/wiki/Christianization_of_Scandinavia" title="Christianization of Scandinavia">Christianisation</a> of Norway, but most scholars of the period now believe that Olaf had little to do with the process. Olaf brought with him <a href="/wiki/Grimketel" title="Grimketel">Grimketel</a>, who is usually credited with helping him create episcopal sees and further organising the Norwegian church, but Grimketel was only a member of Olaf's household and no permanent sees were created until c.&#160;1100. Also, Olaf and Grimketel most likely did not introduce new ecclesiastical laws to Norway; these were ascribed to Olaf at a later date. Olaf most likely did try to bring Christianity to the interior of Norway, where it was less prevalent.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Questions have also been raised about the nature of Olaf's Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Modern historians generally agree<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> that Olaf was inclined to violence and brutality, and note that earlier scholars often neglected this side of his character. It seems that, like many Scandinavian kings, Olaf used his Christianity to gain more power for the monarchy and centralise control in Norway. The skaldic verses attributed to Olaf do not speak of Christianity at all, but use <a href="/wiki/Norse_paganism" class="mw-redirect" title="Norse paganism">pagan</a> references to describe romantic relationships.<sup id="cite_ref-autogenerated1_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated1-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Sadism_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Sadism-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In his book <i>The Conversion of Scandinavia</i>, Anders Winroth argues that there was a "long process of assimilation, in which the Scandinavians adopted, one by one and over time, individual Christian practices."<sup id="cite_ref-autogenerated2_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated2-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Winroth does not claim that Olaf was not Christian, but argues that we cannot think of any Scandinavians as fully converting as portrayed in the later hagiographies or sagas. Olaf himself is portrayed in later sources as a saintly miracle-working figure to help support this quick view of conversion for Norway, but the historical Olaf did not act this way, as seen especially in the skaldic verses attributed to him.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Sainthood">Sainthood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Sainthood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<p>Olaf swiftly became Norway's patron saint; Bishop <a href="/wiki/Grimketel" title="Grimketel">Grimketel</a> performed his canonisation only a year after his death.<sup id="cite_ref-beat_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-beat-26">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> The cult of Olaf unified the country and consolidated the Christianisation of Norway. He is also recognized as the patron saint of the <a href="/wiki/Faroe_Islands" title="Faroe Islands">Faroe Islands</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Owing to Olaf's later status as Norway's patron saint, and to his importance in later medieval historiography and in Norwegian folklore, it is difficult to assess the historical Olaf's character. Judging from the bare outlines of known historical facts, he appears to have been a fairly unsuccessful ruler, whose power was based on an alliance with the much more powerful King <a href="/wiki/Cnut_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Cnut the Great">Cnut the Great</a>; who was driven into exile when he claimed power of his own; and whose attempt at a reconquest was swiftly crushed.
</p><p>This calls for an explanation of the status he gained after his death. Three factors are important: the later myth surrounding his role in the Christianisation of Norway, the various dynastic relationships among the ruling families, and the need for legitimisation in a later period.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Conversion_of_Norway">Conversion of Norway</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Conversion of Norway">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Olaf Haraldsson and <a href="/wiki/Olaf_Tryggvason" title="Olaf Tryggvason">Olaf Tryggvason</a> (Olaf Haraldsson's <a href="/wiki/Godparent" title="Godparent">godfather</a>) are both traditionally regarded as the driving forces behind Norway's final conversion to Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> But large stone crosses and other Christian symbols suggest that at least Norway's coastal areas were deeply influenced by Christianity long before Olaf's time; with one exception, all of Norway's rulers since <a href="/wiki/Haakon_I_of_Norway" class="mw-redirect" title="Haakon I of Norway">Håkon the Good</a> (c. 920–961) were Christians (however Håkon later reverted to Heathenism as an apostate), as was Olaf's main opponent, <a href="/wiki/Cnut_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Cnut the Great">Cnut the Great</a>. What seems clear is that Olaf made efforts to establish a church organization on a broader scale than before, among other things by importing bishops from <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a>, <a href="/wiki/Normandy" title="Normandy">Normandy</a> and <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a>, and that he tried to enforce Christianity in the inland areas, which had the least communication with the rest of Europe, and which economically were more strongly based on agriculture, so that the inclination to hold on to the former fertility cult was stronger than in the more diversified and expansive western parts of Norway.
</p><p>Many believe Olaf introduced Christian law into Norway in 1024, based upon the <a href="/wiki/Kuli_stone" class="mw-redirect" title="Kuli stone">Kuli stone</a>, but this stone is hard to interpret.<sup id="cite_ref-autogenerated2_24-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated2-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> The codification of Christianity as the legal religion of Norway was attributed to Olaf, and his legal arrangements for the Church of Norway came to stand so high in the Norwegian people's and clergy's eyes that when <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_VII" title="Pope Gregory VII">Pope Gregory VII</a> attempted to make clerical celibacy binding on the priests of Western Europe in 1074–75, Norwegians largely ignored it, since there was no mention of clerical celibacy in Olaf's legal code for their church. Only after Norway was made a metropolitan province with its own archbishop in 1153—making the Norwegian church, on the one hand, more independent of its king, but on the other hand, more directly responsible to the Pope—did <a href="/wiki/Canon_law_(Catholic_Church)" class="mw-redirect" title="Canon law (Catholic Church)">canon law</a> gain a greater prominence in the life and jurisdiction of the Norwegian church.
</p>
<h3><span id="Olaf.27s_dynasty"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Olaf's_dynasty">Olaf's dynasty</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Olaf&#039;s dynasty">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>For various reasons, most importantly the death of King Cnut the Great in 1035 but perhaps also a certain discontent among Norwegian nobles with Danish rule in the years after Olaf's death in 1030, Olaf's illegitimate son with the concubine Alvhild, <a href="/wiki/Magnus_I_of_Norway" class="mw-redirect" title="Magnus I of Norway">Magnus the Good</a>, assumed power in Norway, and eventually also in Denmark. Numerous Danish churches were dedicated to Olaf during his reign, and the sagas give glimpses of the young king's efforts to promote the cult of his deceased father. This became typical of Scandinavian monarchies. In pagan times, Scandinavian kings derived their right to rule from their claims of descent from the Norse god <a href="/wiki/Odin" title="Odin">Odin</a>, or in the case of the kings of the Swedes at <a href="/wiki/Old_Uppsala" class="mw-redirect" title="Old Uppsala">Old Uppsala</a>, from <a href="/wiki/Freyr" title="Freyr">Freyr</a>. In Christian times this legitimation of a dynasty's right to rule and its national prestige was based on its descent from a saintly king. Thus the kings of Norway promoted the cult of St. Olaf, the kings of Sweden the cult of <a href="/wiki/St._Erik" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Erik">St. Erik</a> and the kings of Denmark the cult of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Canute" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Canute">Saint Canute</a>, just as in England the Norman and <a href="/wiki/Plantagenet" class="mw-redirect" title="Plantagenet">Plantagenet</a> kings promoted the cult of <a href="/wiki/St._Edward_the_Confessor" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Edward the Confessor">St. Edward the Confessor</a> at <a href="/wiki/Westminster_Abbey" title="Westminster Abbey">Westminster Abbey</a>, their coronation church.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Saint_Olaf">Saint Olaf</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Saint Olaf">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Liturgical_cult">Liturgical cult</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Liturgical cult">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>

<p><a href="/wiki/Sigrid_Undset" title="Sigrid Undset">Sigrid Undset</a> noted that Olaf was baptised in <a href="/wiki/Rouen" title="Rouen">Rouen</a>, the capital of <a href="/wiki/Normandy" title="Normandy">Normandy</a>, and suggested that Olaf may have used priests of Norman descent for his missionaries. Normans were somewhat familiar with the culture of the people they were to convert and in some cases may have been able to understand the language. Among the bishops Olaf is known to have brought with him from England was <a href="/wiki/Grimketel" title="Grimketel">Grimketel</a> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Grimcillus</i>). He was probably the only one of the missionary bishops left in the country at the time of Olaf's death, and he stood behind the <a href="/wiki/Translation" title="Translation">translation</a> and <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatification</a> of Olaf on 3 August 1031.<sup id="cite_ref-beat_26-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-beat-26">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> Grimketel later became the first bishop of <a href="/wiki/Sigtuna" title="Sigtuna">Sigtuna</a> in Sweden.
</p><p>At this time, local bishops and their people recognised and proclaimed a person a saint, and a formal <a href="/wiki/Canonisation" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonisation">canonisation</a> procedure through the papal <a href="/wiki/Curia" title="Curia">curia</a> was not customary; in Olaf's case, this did not happen until 1888. But Olaf II died before the <a href="/wiki/East-West_Schism" class="mw-redirect" title="East-West Schism">East-West Schism</a> and a strict <a href="/wiki/Roman_Rite" title="Roman Rite">Roman Rite</a> was not well-established in Scandinavia at the time. He is also venerated in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Grimketel was later appointed bishop in the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Selsey" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Selsey">diocese of Selsey</a> in southeastern England. This is probably why the earliest traces of a liturgical cult of Olaf are found in England. An <a href="/wiki/Liturgy_of_the_Hours" title="Liturgy of the Hours">office</a>, or prayer service, for Olaf is found in the so-called <i><a href="/wiki/Leofric,_Earl_of_Mercia" title="Leofric, Earl of Mercia">Leofric collectar</a></i> (c. 1050), which Bishop <a href="/wiki/Leofric_(bishop)" title="Leofric (bishop)">Leofric</a> of <a href="/wiki/Exeter" title="Exeter">Exeter</a> bequeathed in his last will and testament to <a href="/wiki/Exeter_Cathedral" title="Exeter Cathedral">Exeter Cathedral</a>. This English cult seems to have been short-lived.
</p><p>Writing around 1070, <a href="/wiki/Adam_of_Bremen" title="Adam of Bremen">Adam of Bremen</a> mentions pilgrimage to <a href="/wiki/St._Olav%27s_shrine" title="St. Olav&#39;s shrine">St. Olaf's shrine</a> in <a href="/wiki/Nidaros" title="Nidaros">Nidaros</a>, but this is the only firm trace we have of a cult of St. Olaf in Norway before the mid-12th century. By this time he was also being called <i>Norway's Eternal King</i>. In 1152/3, Nidaros was separated from Lund as the <a href="/wiki/Archbishopric_of_Nidaros" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishopric of Nidaros">archbishopric of Nidaros</a>. It is likely that whatever formal or informal veneration of Olaf as a saint may have existed in Nidaros before that was emphasised and formalised on this occasion.
</p><p>Miracles performed by St. Olaf appear for the first time in <a href="/wiki/%C3%9E%C3%B3rarinn_loftunga" title="Þórarinn loftunga">Þórarinn loftunga</a>'s skaldic poem <i>Glælognskviða</i>, or "Sea-Calm Poem", from about 1030–34.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> One is the killing and throwing onto a mountain of a sea serpent still visible on the cliffside.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup><sup class="noprint Inline-Template noprint Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research#Primary,_secondary_and_tertiary_sources" title="Wikipedia:No original research"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable secondary sources. (March 2017)">non-primary source needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> Another took place on the day of his death, when a blind man regained his sight after rubbing his eyes with hands stained with Olaf's blood.
</p><p>The texts used for the liturgical celebration of St. Olaf during most of the Middle Ages were probably compiled or written by <a href="/wiki/Eystein_Erlendsson" class="mw-redirect" title="Eystein Erlendsson">Eystein Erlendsson</a>, the second <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Nidaros" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Nidaros">Archbishop of Nidaros</a> (1161–1189).<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> The nine miracles reported in <i>Glælognskviða</i> form the core of the catalogue of miracles in this office.
</p><p>St. Olaf was widely popular throughout Scandinavia. Numerous churches in Norway, Sweden, and Iceland were dedicated to him. His presence was even felt in Finland and many travelled from all over the Norse world in order to visit his shrine.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> Apart from the early traces of a cult in England, there are only scattered references to him outside the Nordic area.
</p><p>Several churches in England were dedicated to him (often as <i>St Olave</i>); the name was presumably popular with Scandinavian immigrants. <a href="/wiki/St_Olave%27s_Church,_York" title="St Olave&#39;s Church, York">St Olave's Church, York</a>, is referred to in the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> for 1055<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> as the place of burial of its founder, <a href="/wiki/Earl_Siward" class="mw-redirect" title="Earl Siward">Earl Siward</a>. This is generally accepted to be the earliest datable church foundation dedicated to Olaf and is further evidence of a cult of St. Olaf in the early 1050s in England. <a href="/wiki/St_Olave_Hart_Street" class="mw-redirect" title="St Olave Hart Street">St Olave Hart Street</a> in the <a href="/wiki/City_of_London" title="City of London">City of London</a> is the burial place of <a href="/wiki/Samuel_Pepys" title="Samuel Pepys">Samuel Pepys</a> and his wife. <a href="/wiki/St_Olave%27s_Church,_Southwark" title="St Olave&#39;s Church, Southwark">Another St. Olave's Church</a> south of <a href="/wiki/London_Bridge" title="London Bridge">London Bridge</a> gave its name to <a href="/wiki/Tooley_Street" title="Tooley Street">Tooley Street</a> and to the <i>St Olave's <a href="/wiki/Poor_Law" class="mw-redirect" title="Poor Law">Poor Law</a> Union</i>, later the <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_Borough_of_Bermondsey" title="Metropolitan Borough of Bermondsey">Metropolitan Borough of Bermondsey</a>: its workhouse in <a href="/wiki/Rotherhithe" title="Rotherhithe">Rotherhithe</a> became <a href="/wiki/St_Olave%27s_Hospital" title="St Olave&#39;s Hospital">St Olave's Hospital</a> and then an old people's home a few hundred metres from <i>St Olav's Church</i>, which is the <a href="/wiki/Scandinavian_churches_in_London" class="mw-redirect" title="Scandinavian churches in London">Norwegian Church in London</a>. It also led to the naming of <a href="/wiki/St_Olave%27s_Grammar_School" title="St Olave&#39;s Grammar School">St Olave's Grammar School</a>, which was established in 1571 and was in Tooley Street until 1968, when it moved to <a href="/wiki/Orpington" title="Orpington">Orpington</a>, Kent. The village of <a href="/wiki/St_Olaves" title="St Olaves">St Olaves</a> in Norfolk bears the name as it is the location of the remains of a 13th-century Augustinian priory dedicated to Olaf.
</p><p>St. Olaf was also, together with the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Mother of God</a>, the patron saint of the chapel of the <a href="/wiki/Varangians" title="Varangians">Varangians</a>, the Scandinavian warriors who served as the bodyguard of the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine</a> emperor. This church is believed to have been near the church of <a href="/wiki/Hagia_Irene" title="Hagia Irene">Hagia Irene</a> in Constantinople. The icon of the <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nicopeia.jpg" class="extiw" title="commons:File:Nicopeia.jpg">Madonna Nicopeia</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> presently in <a href="/wiki/St._Mark%27s_Basilica" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Mark&#39;s Basilica">St. Mark's Basilica</a> in Venice, which is believed to have been traditionally carried into combat by the Byzantine military forces, is believed to have been kept in this chapel in times of peace. Thus St. Olaf was also the last saint venerated by both the Western and Eastern churches before the <a href="/wiki/East%E2%80%93West_Schism" title="East–West Schism">Great Schism</a>.
</p><p>The basilica of <a href="/wiki/Sant%27Ambrogio_e_Carlo_al_Corso" class="mw-redirect" title="Sant&#39;Ambrogio e Carlo al Corso">Sant'Ambrogio e Carlo al Corso</a> in Rome has a Chapel of St Olav. Its altarpiece contains a painting of the saint, shown as a martyr king defeating a dragon, representing victory over his pagan past. It was originally a gift presented to Pope <a href="/wiki/Leo_XIII" class="mw-redirect" title="Leo XIII">Leo XIII</a> in 1893 for the golden jubilee of his ordination as a bishop by Norwegian nobleman and <a href="/wiki/Papal_Gentlemen" class="mw-redirect" title="Papal Gentlemen">papal chamberlain</a> Baron <a href="/wiki/Wilhelm_Wedel-Jarlsberg" title="Wilhelm Wedel-Jarlsberg">Wilhelm Wedel-Jarlsberg</a>. The chapel was restored in 1980 and reinaugurated by Bishop <a href="/wiki/John_Willem_Gran" title="John Willem Gran">John Willem Gran</a>, bishop of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Oslo" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of Oslo">Roman Catholic Diocese of Oslo</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a>, there used to be a shrine of St. Olaf in <a href="/wiki/Koblenz" title="Koblenz">Koblenz</a>. It was founded in 1463 or 1464 by <a href="/wiki/Henrik_Kalteisen" title="Henrik Kalteisen">Heinrich Kalteisen</a> at his retirement home, the <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican</a> Monastery in the <i>Altstadt</i> ("Old City") neighborhood of Koblenz. He was the <a href="/wiki/Archdiocese_of_Nidaros" class="mw-redirect" title="Archdiocese of Nidaros">Archbishop of Nidaros</a> in Norway from 1452 to 1458. When he died in 1464, he was buried in front of the shrine's altar.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> However, the shrine did not last: the Dominican Monastery was secularized in 1802 and bulldozed in 1955. Only the <i>Rokokoportal</i> ("<a href="/wiki/Rococo" title="Rococo">Rococo</a> Portal"), built in 1754, remains to mark the spot.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the <a href="/wiki/Faroe_Islands" title="Faroe Islands">Faroe Islands</a>, the day of St. Olaf's death is celebrated as <a href="/wiki/%C3%93lavs%C3%B8ka" title="Ólavsøka">Ólavsøka</a>, a nation-wide holiday.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Recently the <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> route to <a href="/wiki/Nidaros_Cathedral" title="Nidaros Cathedral">Nidaros Cathedral</a>, the site of St. Olaf's tomb, has been reinstated. The route is known as The Pilgrim's Way (<i>Pilegrimsleden</i>). The main route, approximately 640&#160;km long, starts in the ancient part of <a href="/wiki/Oslo" title="Oslo">Oslo</a> and heads north, along <a href="/wiki/Lake_Mjosa" class="mw-redirect" title="Lake Mjosa">Lake Mjosa</a>, up the <a href="/wiki/Gudbrandsdal" class="mw-redirect" title="Gudbrandsdal">Gudbrandsdal</a> Valley, over <a href="/wiki/Dovrefjell" title="Dovrefjell">Dovrefjell</a> and down the <a href="/wiki/Orkdal" title="Orkdal">Orkdal</a> Valley, ending at Nidaros Cathedral in <a href="/wiki/Trondheim" title="Trondheim">Trondheim</a>. A Pilgrim's Office in Oslo gives advice to pilgrims, and a Pilgrim Centre in Trondheim, under the aegis of the Cathedral, awards certificates to pilgrims when they complete their journeys. However, the relics are no longer exposed in the cathedral, and it is not sure where exactly in the cathedral crypt his remains are buried.
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Folklore">Folklore</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Folklore">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>For centuries, Olaf figured in folk traditions as a slayer of <a href="/wiki/Troll" title="Troll">trolls</a> and giants, and as a protector against malicious forces. He was said to have healing power, which attracted people to his shrine, and various springs were claimed to have sprung forth where he or his body had been.<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> Around the 12th century, folk traditions and iconography of Olaf absorbed elements of the gods <a href="/wiki/Thor" title="Thor">Thor</a> and <a href="/wiki/Freyr" title="Freyr">Freyr</a> from <a href="/wiki/Norse_mythology" title="Norse mythology">Norse mythology</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> Like Freyr, he became associated with fertility, which led to his adoption as a <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> by farmers, fishermen, sailors and merchants of the <a href="/wiki/Hanseatic_League" title="Hanseatic League">Hanseatic League</a>, who turned to him for good yield and protection. From Thor, he inherited the quick temper, physical strength and merits of a giant-slayer.<sup id="cite_ref-medievalfolklore_45-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-medievalfolklore-45">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Popular tradition also made marks in the ecclesiastical material. Early depictions of Olaf portray him as clean-shaven, but after 1200 he appears with a red beard, which may have been absorbed from Thor. The <i><a href="/wiki/Passio_Olavi" title="Passio Olavi">Passio a miracule beati Olavi</a></i>, the official record of Olaf's miracles, contains an episode where Olaf helps a man escape from the <i><a href="/wiki/Hulder" title="Hulder">huldrefolk</a></i>, the "hidden people" of <a href="/wiki/Scandinavian_folklore" class="mw-redirect" title="Scandinavian folklore">Norwegian folklore</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-medievalfolklore_45-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-medievalfolklore-45">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="In_Normandy">In Normandy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olaf_II_of_Norway&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: In Normandy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>In <a href="/wiki/Normandy" title="Normandy">Normandy</a>, Saint Olaf represents an important figure and was chosen unofficially as the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Normans" title="Normans">Normans</a>, this term mainly designating the inhabitants of continental Normandy and of <a href="/wiki/Channel_Islands" title="Channel Islands">channel islands</a>, but also on a larger scale the inhabitants of the former <a href="/wiki/Vikings" title="Vikings">Viking</a> territories, namely the Scandinavian countries and, even more so, <a href="/wiki/Norway" title="Norway">Norway</a>. This choice can be explained by the time in which Olaf lived and when the exchanges between Normandy and the Scandinavian countries were common. There were also many kinships between the inhabitants of the brand new Norman state, as illustrated by the choice of Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Robert_II_(archbishop_of_Rouen)" title="Robert II (archbishop of Rouen)">Robert II</a> for the baptism of Olaf.
</p><p>The <a href="https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drapeau_normand#Drapeaux_à_croix_scandinave_(ou_croix_de_Saint_Olaf)" class="extiw" title="fr:Drapeau normand">normand flag</a> with a Scandinavian cross, which recalls the Scandinavian origins of Normandy, has been baptized "Cross of Saint Olav" (or "Cross of Saint Olaf") in honor of the saint.
</p><p>Baptized in <a href="/wiki/Rouen" title="Rouen">Rouen</a> by the brother of a Duke of Normandy, a church in Rouen is dedicated to Saint-Olaf.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> The Norwegian Saint-Olav Church was built in 1926, rue Duguay-Trouin, near the home of the Scandinavian sailors. The Norwegian Seamen's Mission wanted to build a Lutheran place of worship for visiting sailors.
</p><p>A bone from Saint Olaf's arm is kept as a relic in the crypt of <a href="/wiki/Rouen_Cathedral" title="Rouen Cathedral">Rouen Cathedral</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-france3-regions.francetvinfo.fr_47-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-france3-regions.francetvinfo.fr-47">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 2014 the city and the diocese of Rouen celebrated the millennium of the baptism of Saint Olav with the Norwegian representatives of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Evangelical_Lutheran_Church_of_Norway" class="mw-redirect" title="Evangelical Lutheran Church of Norway">Evangelical Lutheran Church of Norway</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-france3-regions.francetvinfo.fr_47-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-france3-regions.francetvinfo.fr-47">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In Normandy, <a href="/wiki/July_29" title="July 29">July 29</a> is the occasion of local cultural festivals which generally highlight the Norse heritage of <a href="/wiki/Normandy" title="Normandy">Normandy</a>. In some Norman parishes, <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">Mass</a> is celebrated on this day in honor of the saint and to mark the historical links that unite <a href="/wiki/Normandy" title="Normandy">Normandy</a> and <a href="/wiki/Scandinavia" title="Scandinavia">Scandinavia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
