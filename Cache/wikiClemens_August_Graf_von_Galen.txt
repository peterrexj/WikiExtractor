<p><b>Clemens Augustinus Emmanuel Joseph Pius Anthonius Hubertus Marie Graf<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> von Galen</b> (16 March 1878&#160;– 22 March 1946), better known as <i>Clemens August Graf von Galen</i>, was a German <a href="/wiki/Count" title="Count">count</a>, <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Diocese_of_M%C3%BCnster" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic Diocese of Münster">Bishop of Münster</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_(Catholicism)" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinal (Catholicism)">cardinal</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>. During <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">World War II</a>, Galen led <a href="/wiki/Nazi_euthanasia_and_the_Catholic_Church" title="Nazi euthanasia and the Catholic Church">Catholic protests against Nazi euthanasia</a> and denounced <a href="/wiki/Gestapo" title="Gestapo">Gestapo</a> lawlessness and the <a href="/wiki/Nazi_persecution_of_the_Catholic_Church_in_Germany" title="Nazi persecution of the Catholic Church in Germany">persecution of the Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Nazi_Germany" title="Nazi Germany">Nazi Germany</a>. He was appointed a cardinal by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII" title="Pope Pius XII">Pope Pius XII</a> in 1946, shortly before his death, and was <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a> in 2005.
</p><p>Born into the German aristocracy, Galen received part of his education in <a href="/wiki/Austria-Hungary" title="Austria-Hungary">Austria-Hungary</a> from the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">Jesuits</a> at <a href="/wiki/Stella_Matutina_(Jesuit_school)" title="Stella Matutina (Jesuit school)">Stella Matutina</a> in the town of <a href="/wiki/Feldkirch,_Vorarlberg" title="Feldkirch, Vorarlberg">Feldkirch</a>. After his ordination he worked in <a href="/wiki/Berlin" title="Berlin">Berlin</a> at St. Matthias. He intensely disliked the liberal values of the <a href="/wiki/Weimar_Republic" title="Weimar Republic">Weimar Republic</a> and opposed individualism, socialism, and democracy. A staunch German monarchist, nationalist, and patriot, he considered the <a href="/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles" title="Treaty of Versailles">Treaty of Versailles</a> unjust and viewed <a href="/wiki/Bolshevism" title="Bolshevism">Bolshevism</a> as a threat to Germany and the Church. He espoused the <a href="/wiki/Stab-in-the-back_myth" title="Stab-in-the-back myth">stab-in-the-back theory</a>: that the German military was defeated in 1918 only because it had been undermined by defeatist elements on the home front. He expressed his opposition to <a href="/wiki/Secularism" title="Secularism">secularism</a> in his book <i>Die Pest des Laizismus und ihre Erscheinungsformen</i> (The Plague of Laicism and its Forms of Expression) (1932).<sup id="cite_ref-Krieg_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Krieg-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> After serving in Berlin parishes from 1906 to 1929, he became the pastor of <a href="/wiki/M%C3%BCnster" title="Münster">Münster</a>'s St. Lamberti Church, where he was noted for his political conservatism before being appointed Bishop of Münster in 1933.
</p><p>Galen began to criticize <a href="/wiki/Hitler" class="mw-redirect" title="Hitler">Hitler</a>'s movement in 1934. He condemned the Nazi "worship of race" in a pastoral letter on 29 January 1934, and assumed responsibility for the publication of a collection of essays which fiercely criticized Nazi ideologist <a href="/wiki/Alfred_Rosenberg" title="Alfred Rosenberg">Alfred Rosenberg</a> and defended the teachings of the Catholic Church. He was an outspoken critic of certain Nazi policies and helped draft <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XI" title="Pope Pius XI">Pope Pius XI</a>'s 1937 anti-Nazi encyclical <i><a href="/wiki/Mit_brennender_Sorge" title="Mit brennender Sorge">Mit brennender Sorge</a></i>. In 1941, von Galen delivered three sermons in which he denounced the arrest of Jesuits, the confiscation of church property, Nazi attacks on the Church, and in the third, fiercely condemned the state-approved <a href="/wiki/Aktion_T4" title="Aktion T4">mass killing in the involuntary euthanasia programme of persons with mental or physical defects</a> (<i>Aktion T4</i>).<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The sermons were illegally circulated in print, inspiring some <a href="/wiki/German_resistance_to_Nazism" title="German resistance to Nazism">German Resistance</a> groups, including the <a href="/wiki/White_Rose" title="White Rose">White Rose</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Anton_Gill_p.188_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Anton_Gill_p.188-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Following this, in September 1943, another condemnation was read at the order of von Galen and other bishops from all Catholic pulpits in the diocese of Münster and across <a href="/wiki/Nazi_Germany" title="Nazi Germany">Nazi Germany</a>, denouncing the killing of "the innocent and defenceless mentally handicapped and mentally ill, the incurably infirm and fatally wounded, innocent hostages and disarmed prisoners of war and criminal offenders, people of a foreign race or descent".<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_years">Early years</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Clemens_August_Graf_von_Galen&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early years">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Burg_Dinklage_Wikipedia.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Burg_Dinklage_Wikipedia.JPG/220px-Burg_Dinklage_Wikipedia.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Burg_Dinklage_Wikipedia.JPG/330px-Burg_Dinklage_Wikipedia.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Burg_Dinklage_Wikipedia.JPG/440px-Burg_Dinklage_Wikipedia.JPG 2x" data-file-width="3872" data-file-height="2592" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Burg_Dinklage_Wikipedia.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Galen was one of thirteen children born to an old aristocratic family in Burg Dinklage.</div></div></div>
<p>Galen belonged to one of the oldest and most distinguished <a href="/wiki/German_nobility" title="German nobility">noble families</a> of <a href="/wiki/Westphalia" title="Westphalia">Westphalia</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Until 1890, Clemens August and his brother Franz were tutored at home. At a time when the Jesuits were still not permitted in Münster, he received his main schooling at a Jesuit School, <a href="/wiki/Stella_Matutina_(Jesuit_school)" title="Stella Matutina (Jesuit school)">Stella Matutina</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Vorarlberg" title="Vorarlberg">Vorarlberg</a>, Austria, where only <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> was spoken. He was not an easy student to teach, and his Jesuit superior wrote to his parents: "<a href="/wiki/Infallibility" title="Infallibility">Infallibility</a> is the main problem with Clemens, who under no circumstance will admit that he may be wrong. It is always his teachers and educators who are wrong.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Clemens_August_von_Galen_and_siblings_(1884).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/f/f6/Clemens_August_von_Galen_and_siblings_%281884%29.jpg/220px-Clemens_August_von_Galen_and_siblings_%281884%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="237" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/f/f6/Clemens_August_von_Galen_and_siblings_%281884%29.jpg/330px-Clemens_August_von_Galen_and_siblings_%281884%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/f/f6/Clemens_August_von_Galen_and_siblings_%281884%29.jpg/440px-Clemens_August_von_Galen_and_siblings_%281884%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="765" data-file-height="825" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Clemens_August_von_Galen_and_siblings_(1884).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Clemens August (third from left) at age six</div></div></div>
<p>Because <a href="/wiki/Prussia" title="Prussia">Prussia</a> did not recognize the Stella Matutina academy, Clemens returned home in 1894 to attend a public school in <a href="/wiki/Vechta" title="Vechta">Vechta</a> and by 1896 both Clemens and Franz had passed the examinations that qualified them to attend a university. Upon graduation, his fellow students wrote in his yearbook: "Clemens doesn't make love or go drinking, he does not like worldly deceit." In 1896 he went to study at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Fribourg" title="University of Fribourg">Catholic University of</a> <a href="/wiki/Fribourg" title="Fribourg">Freiburg</a>, which had been established in 1886 by the <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominicans</a>, where he encountered the writings of <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas" title="Thomas Aquinas">Thomas Aquinas</a>. In 1897 he began to study a variety of topics, including literature, history, and philosophy. One of his teachers was history professor and noted biblical archaeologist <a href="/wiki/Johann_Peter_Kirsch" title="Johann Peter Kirsch">Johann Peter Kirsch</a>. Following their first winter semester at Freiburg, Clemens and Franz visited Rome for three months. At the end of the visit he told Franz that he had decided to become a priest though he was unsure whether to become a contemplative <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> or a <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> In 1899 he met <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIII" title="Pope Leo XIII">Pope Leo XIII</a> in a private audience. He studied at the Theological Faculty and Convent in <a href="/wiki/Innsbruck" title="Innsbruck">Innsbruck</a>, founded in 1669 by the Jesuits, where scholastic philosophy was emphasized, and new concepts and ideas avoided. Galen left Innsbruck in 1903 to enter the <a href="/wiki/Seminary" title="Seminary">seminary</a> in <a href="/wiki/M%C3%BCnster" title="Münster">Münster</a> and was ordained a priest on 28 May 1904 by Bishop Hermann Dingelstadt.<sup id="cite_ref-bio_-_La_Santa_Sede_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bio_-_La_Santa_Sede-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> At first he worked for a family member, the Auxiliary <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_M%C3%BCnster" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Münster">Bishop of Münster</a>, as Chaplain.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Soon he moved to Berlin, where he worked as parish priest at St. Matthias.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Berlin_.281906.E2.80.931929.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Berlin_(1906–1929)">Berlin (1906–1929)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Clemens_August_Graf_von_Galen&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Berlin (1906–1929)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Galen arrived in Berlin on 23 April 1906 and stayed until 16 April 1929. Germany's capital contained districts of Protestant elites, a Catholic community composed of primarily working-class people and a Jewish community of both middle-class and poorer immigrants. It was a booming commercial and cultural metropolis at the time he arrived — its population increased from 900,000 in 1871 to slightly less than 4 million by 1920. Religion did not bring the community together — "religion and fears of a loss of religious belief came to be a major source of internal division."<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> For the working class, Catholicism and <a href="/wiki/Social_Democracy" class="mw-redirect" title="Social Democracy">Social Democracy</a> competed for allegiance. In this atmosphere, Galen sought to be an energetic and idealistic leader of his parish. He made visits to the sick and poor, became president of the Catholic Young Men's Association, gave religious instruction in the schools, and for his efforts he was named <i>Papa Galen</i> by the parishioners he served. A commanding presence (6&#160;feet 7&#160;inches (2.01&#160;m) tall) — his rooms were furnished simply, he wore unpretentious clothing, and he spoke plainly — he did not like the theatre, secular music (except for military marches), or literature. His only reported vice, which he refused to give up, was smoking his pipes.<sup id="cite_ref-Griech-Polelle,_p._17_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Griech-Polelle,_p._17-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Clemens_August_von_Galen_(1899).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/e/eb/Clemens_August_von_Galen_%281899%29.jpg/220px-Clemens_August_von_Galen_%281899%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="301" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/e/eb/Clemens_August_von_Galen_%281899%29.jpg/330px-Clemens_August_von_Galen_%281899%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/e/eb/Clemens_August_von_Galen_%281899%29.jpg/440px-Clemens_August_von_Galen_%281899%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="785" data-file-height="1073" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Clemens_August_von_Galen_(1899).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Clemens August von Galen in 1899 after a hunt</div></div></div> 
<p>During the <a href="/wiki/First_World_War" class="mw-redirect" title="First World War">First World War</a>, Galen volunteered for military service in order to demonstrate his loyalty to the <a href="/wiki/Wilhelm_II,_German_Emperor" title="Wilhelm II, German Emperor">Kaiser</a>. As parish priest, he encouraged his parishioners to serve their country willingly. In August 1917 he visited the front lines in France and found the optimistic morale of the troops uplifting. "Feelings of German nationalism, apparently, could triumph over concern for the violations of the sanctity of human life in war."<sup id="cite_ref-Griech-Polelle,_p._18_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Griech-Polelle,_p._18-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> In 1916 and 1917 he welcomed reports that the German military had a plan to colonize Eastern Europe, stating that German Catholics should be moved into the area, especially <a href="/wiki/Lithuania" title="Lithuania">Lithuania</a>, with the goal not of expelling the Lithuanians, but educating them to <i>think</i> and <i>feel</i> as Germans.<sup id="cite_ref-Griech-Polelle,_p._18_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Griech-Polelle,_p._18-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Following the German surrender in November 1918, Galen, still in Berlin, worked to create soup kitchens, aid societies, and clothing drives to deal with immediate problems of hunger and poverty. He feared the lower classes would embrace radicalism and anarchy. Galen deplored the fall of the monarchy and was suspicious of the new <a href="/wiki/Weimar_Republic" title="Weimar Republic">Weimar democracy</a>, believing that "the revolutionary ideas of 1918 had caused considerable damage to Catholic Christianity."<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> He believed the <a href="/wiki/Stab-in-the-back_myth" title="Stab-in-the-back myth">stab-in-the-back myth</a>, which held that the German Army hadn't been defeated in battle but by being undermined by defeatist elements on the home front<sup id="cite_ref-Griech-Polelle20_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Griech-Polelle20-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> and, as did most Germans, considered the <a href="/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles" title="Treaty of Versailles">Treaty of Versailles</a> unjust.
</p><p>Throughout the Weimar years he remained on the right of German politics. He often criticized the <a href="/wiki/Centre_Party_(Germany)" title="Centre Party (Germany)">Centre Party</a> for being too left-wing.<sup id="cite_ref-Griech-Polelle20_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Griech-Polelle20-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Galen openly supported the Protestant <a href="/wiki/Paul_von_Hindenburg" title="Paul von Hindenburg">Paul von Hindenburg</a> against the Centre Party's candidate, <a href="/wiki/Wilhelm_Marx" title="Wilhelm Marx">Wilhelm Marx</a>, in the presidential elections of 1925. Galen was known as a fierce anti-Communist (he later supported the battle by the <a href="/wiki/Axis_powers" title="Axis powers">Axis powers</a> on the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Front_(World_War_II)" title="Eastern Front (World War II)">Eastern Front</a> against <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Stalin" title="Joseph Stalin">Joseph Stalin</a>'s regime in the <a href="/wiki/Soviet_Union" title="Soviet Union">Soviet Union</a><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>). His views on Communism were largely formed as a consequence of the <a href="/wiki/Stalinism" title="Stalinism">Stalinization</a> and <a href="/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians_in_the_Soviet_Union" title="Persecution of Christians in the Soviet Union">relentless persecution of Christians within the Soviet Union after 1918</a>, during which virtually all Catholic bishops were either killed or forced underground. He also expressed his opposition to modernity in his 1932 publication,  <i>Die Pest des Laizismus und ihre Erscheinungsformen</i> (<i>The Plague of Laicism and its Forms of Expression</i>).<sup id="cite_ref-Krieg_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Krieg-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="M.C3.BCnster"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Münster">Münster</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Clemens_August_Graf_von_Galen&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Münster">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Galen became the pastor of
<a href="/wiki/St_Lambert%27s_Church,_M%C3%BCnster" title="St Lambert&#39;s Church, Münster">St Lambert's Church, Münster</a>, where he initially upset some parishioners with his political conservatism. At a meeting in Münster of the Association of Catholic Academicians in June 1933, Galen spoke against those scholars who had criticised the Nazi government and called for "a just and objective evaluation of [Hitler's] new political movement".<sup id="cite_ref-Krieg_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Krieg-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> In 1933, Galen was elected bishop of Münster, although he was not the popular candidate to succeed the previous bishop, <a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Poggenburg" class="extiw" title="de:Johannes Poggenburg">Johannes Poggenburg</a>, and was selected only after other candidates had declined to be nominated and despite a protest from the Papal Nuncio <a href="/wiki/Cesare_Orsenigo" title="Cesare Orsenigo">Cesare Orsenigo</a>, who reported that Galen was bossy and paternalistic in his public utterances.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> In October 1933, Galen wrote approvingly of the Nazis' efforts to "eradicate" the "open propaganda for godlessness and immorality".<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Galen was named bishop by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XI" title="Pope Pius XI">Pope Pius XI</a> on 5 September 1933. On 28 October, he was consecrated as bishop in Münster's cathedral by Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Karl_Joseph_Schulte" title="Karl Joseph Schulte">Karl Joseph Schulte</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-bio_-_La_Santa_Sede_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bio_-_La_Santa_Sede-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> He chose as his motto "Nec laudibus nec timore", a phrase from the liturgy used for a bishop's consecration when the consecrating bishop prays that the new bishop be overcome "neither by flattery nor by fear".<sup id="cite_ref-order_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-order-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> As bishop, Galen campaigned against the <a href="/wiki/Totalitarianism" title="Totalitarianism">totalitarian</a> approach of the Nazi Party in national education, appealing to parents to insist on Catholic teaching in schools. Citing the recently agreed-upon <a href="/wiki/Reichskonkordat" title="Reichskonkordat">Reichskonkordat</a> assurance that the Church had the right to determine its own religious instruction, he successfully forced the Nazis to permit continued Catholic instruction in Catholic schools. It was one of the first instances where the <i><a href="/wiki/Reichskonkordat" title="Reichskonkordat">Reichskonkordat</a></i> was used by the Church against the government, which was one of the intentions of Pope Pius XI.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1933, when the Nazi school superintendent of Münster issued a decree that religious instruction be combined with discussion of the "demoralising power" of the "people of Israel", Galen refused, writing that such interference in the school curriculum was a breach of the Concordat and that he feared children would be confused as to their "obligation to act with charity to all men" and as to the historical mission of the people of Israel.<sup id="cite_ref-Hamerow139_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hamerow139-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> Galen often protested against violations of the Concordat to Hitler directly. In 1936, when the Nazis removed crucifixes from schools, Galen's protest led to a public demonstration. Together with Munich's <a href="/wiki/Michael_von_Faulhaber" title="Michael von Faulhaber">Cardinal Faulhaber</a> and Berlin's <a href="/wiki/Konrad_von_Preysing" title="Konrad von Preysing">Bishop Preysing</a>, Galen helped to draft Pope Pius XI's anti-Nazi encyclical <i><a href="/wiki/Mit_brennender_Sorge" title="Mit brennender Sorge">Mit brennender Sorge</a></i> (<i>With Burning Concern</i>) of 1937.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Honourable_Defeat_p.59_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Honourable_Defeat_p.59-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1934, Bishop Galen began to attack the racial ideology of the Nazi regime, partly poking fun at it, partly critiquing its ideological basis as presented by the Nazi ideologist <a href="/wiki/Alfred_Rosenberg" title="Alfred Rosenberg">Alfred Rosenberg</a>. He declared it unacceptable to argue that Jewish authorship of the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old Testament</a> diminished its authority, or that morality and virtue were in any way derived from the perceived usefulness of a particular race.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> In January 1934, he criticized Nazi racial policy in a sermon and, in subsequent homilies, equated unquestioning loyalty to the Reich with "slavery". He spoke against Hitler's theory of the purity of German blood.<sup id="cite_ref-Honourable_Defeat_p.59_24-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Honourable_Defeat_p.59-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Bishop Galen also derided the neo-pagan theories of Rosenberg in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Myth_of_the_Twentieth_Century" title="The Myth of the Twentieth Century">The Myth of the Twentieth Century</a></i> as perhaps no more than "an occasion for laughter in the educated world", but warned that Rosenberg's "immense importance lies in the acceptance of his basic notions as the authentic philosophy of National Socialism and in his almost unlimited power in the field of German education. Herr Rosenberg must be taken seriously if the German situation is to be understood."<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In retaliation, two senior SS officers visited Galen to pressure him into endorsing Rosenberg's doctrines publicly, threatening the confiscation of Church property and an anti-Catholic propaganda campaign. One of them was the future SS General <a href="/wiki/J%C3%BCrgen_Stroop" title="Jürgen Stroop">Jürgen Stroop</a>, who later recalled, "Bishop von Galen was a great gentleman, a true aristocrat, a Renaissance prince of the Church. He welcomed us politely but with reserve."<sup id="cite_ref-Moz56_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Moz56-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Galen began by commending Stroop's mother for her devout Catholicism, then categorically refused to accept or praise Rosenberg's doctrines of euthanizing or forcibly sterilizing disabled people. He denounced the Nazis for trying to introduce <a href="/wiki/Germanic_neo-paganism" class="mw-redirect" title="Germanic neo-paganism">Germanic neo-paganism</a> into his diocese. He scoffed at marriage ceremonies and funerals conducted before altars dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Odin" title="Odin">Wotan</a>, surprising Stroop, who had attended such a ceremony only days before. Galen closed by assuring the officers that the Church would remain loyal to the state in all lawful matters. He expressed his deep love for Germany and reminded them that he had been the first bishop to publicly acknowledge the new regime.<sup id="cite_ref-Moz56_27-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Moz56-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> In Stroop's view, Galen's German patriotism "was tainted by <a href="/wiki/Papist" class="mw-redirect" title="Papist">Papist</a> ideals, which have been harmful to Germany for centuries. Besides, the Archbishop's orders came from outside the Fatherland, a fact which disturbed us. We all know that despite its diverse factions, the Catholic Church is a world community, which sticks together when the chips are down."<sup id="cite_ref-Moz56_27-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Moz56-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In June 1935 he delivered a sermon that connected the heresy of the <a href="/wiki/Anabaptist" class="mw-redirect" title="Anabaptist">Anabaptists</a> to the "sins of the Jews".  He told his audience that "whoever does not listen to the Church is a heathen and officially is a sinner". He described how "the Israelites debased the Savior", and how people who resisted Jesus as the Christ appeared on the "side of the blinded Jews". He equated the rejection of Christianity with rejection of worldly authority, leading to anarchy and chaos. He pointed to the Russians also as among those who had not respected God-given authority. Galen did not protest the antisemitic 1935 <a href="/wiki/Nuremberg_Laws" title="Nuremberg Laws">Nuremberg Laws</a>, or the <a href="/wiki/Kristallnacht" title="Kristallnacht">Kristallnacht</a> pogrom of 1938. Until his death, he refused to admit that referring to Jews as "degenerate", "rejected", and "lost" or labeling anarchy or liberalism as "Jewish", in any way aided the Nazi regime or and its racist antisemitism.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>By late 1935, Galen was urging a joint pastoral letter from the German bishops to protest about an "underground war" against the church.<sup id="cite_ref-Hamerow139_22-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hamerow139-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> By early 1937, the church hierarchy in Germany, which had initially attempted to co-operate with the Nazi government, had become highly disillusioned. In March, Pope Pius XI issued the encyclical <i><a href="/wiki/Mit_brennender_Sorge" title="Mit brennender Sorge">Mit brennender Sorge</a></i> (<i>With Burning Concern</i>), accusing the Nazi government of violating the 1933 Concordat and of sowing the "tales of suspicion, discord, hatred, calumny, of secret and open fundamental hostility to Christ and His Church".<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Galen was part of the five-member commission that prepared the papal encyclical. The Nazis responded with an intensification of their campaign against the Catholic Church.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> There were mass arrests of clergy and church publishing houses were expropriated, followed by widely spread abuse allegations and staged morality trials against members of religious orders and priests.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1941 Galen welcomed the German war against the USSR as a positive development<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> as he had rallied also to the cause of Germany when Hitler invaded Poland, offering a patriotic benediction.<sup id="cite_ref-Hamerow262_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hamerow262-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Euthanasia">Euthanasia</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Clemens_August_Graf_von_Galen&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Euthanasia">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Coat_of_arms_of_Clemens_August_von_Galen.svg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/Coat_of_arms_of_Clemens_August_von_Galen.svg/200px-Coat_of_arms_of_Clemens_August_von_Galen.svg.png" decoding="async" width="200" height="212" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/Coat_of_arms_of_Clemens_August_von_Galen.svg/300px-Coat_of_arms_of_Clemens_August_von_Galen.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/Coat_of_arms_of_Clemens_August_von_Galen.svg/400px-Coat_of_arms_of_Clemens_August_von_Galen.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="1148" data-file-height="1218" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Coat_of_arms_of_Clemens_August_von_Galen.svg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Coat of Arms of Cardinal von Galen</div></div></div>
<p>While the Nazi <a href="/wiki/Final_Solution" title="Final Solution">extermination of Jewish people</a> took place primarily on Polish territory, the <a href="/wiki/Euthanasia_in_Nazi_Germany" class="mw-redirect" title="Euthanasia in Nazi Germany">murder of people with disabilities (viewed by the Nazi regime as "invalid" individuals)</a> became public knowledge because it took place on German soil and interfered directly in Catholic and Protestant welfare institutions. Church leaders who opposed it – chiefly Bishop Galen and <a href="/wiki/Theophil_Wurm" title="Theophil Wurm">Theophil Wurm</a>, the Lutheran Bishop of Württemberg – were able to rouse widespread public opposition.<sup id="cite_ref-Peter_Hoffmann_p.24_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Peter_Hoffmann_p.24-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> The regime initiated its euthanasia program in 1939.<sup id="cite_ref-ReferenceA_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ReferenceA-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> It targeted people with dementia, cognitive/mental disabilities, mental illness, epileptic, physical disabilities, children with <a href="/wiki/Down%27s_Syndrome" class="mw-redirect" title="Down&#39;s Syndrome">Down's Syndrome</a> and people with similar afflictions.<sup id="cite_ref-Anton_Gill_p.60_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Anton_Gill_p.60-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> The programme systematically murdered more than 70,000 people between September 1939 and August 1941.<sup id="cite_ref-ReferenceA_35-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ReferenceA-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> After 1941 the killing continued unofficially, with the total number of deaths estimated at 200,000.<sup id="cite_ref-Burleigh_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burleigh-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1941, with the <i>Wehrmacht</i> still marching on Moscow, Galen, despite his long-time nationalist sympathies, denounced the lawlessness of the Gestapo, the confiscations of church properties, and the Nazi euthanasia programme.<sup id="cite_ref-ReferenceC_38-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ReferenceC-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> He attacked the Gestapo for converting church properties to their own purposes – including use as cinemas and brothels.<sup id="cite_ref-Honourable_Defeat_p.60_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Honourable_Defeat_p.60-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> He protested the mistreatment of Catholics in Germany: the arrests and imprisonment without legal process, the <a href="/wiki/Suppression_of_monasteries" title="Suppression of monasteries">suppression of monasteries</a>, and the expulsion of religious orders. But his sermons went further than defending the church; he spoke of a moral danger to Germany from the regime's violations of basic human rights: "the right to life, to inviolability, and to freedom is an indispensable part of any moral social order", he said – and any government that punishes without court proceedings "undermines its own authority and respect for its sovereignty within the conscience of its citizens".<sup id="cite_ref-Hamerow289_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hamerow289-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> Galen said that it was the duty of Christians to resist the taking of human life, even if it meant losing their own lives.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hitler's order for the <i><a href="/wiki/Aktion_T4" title="Aktion T4">Aktion T4</a></i> Euthanasia Programme was dated 1 September 1939, the day Germany invaded Poland. As word of the programme spread, protest grew, until finally, Galen delivered his famous August 1941 sermons denouncing the programme as "murder".<sup id="cite_ref-Hamerow289_40-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hamerow289-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> On 3 August 1941, in one of his series of denunciations, Galen declared:<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>"Thou shalt not kill." God engraved this commandment on the souls of men long before any penal code... God has engraved these <a href="/wiki/Ten_Commandments" title="Ten Commandments">commandments</a> in our hearts... They are the unchangeable and fundamental truths of our social life... Where in Germany and where, here, is obedience to the precepts of God? [...] As for the first commandment, "Thou shalt not have strange gods before me," instead of the One, True, Eternal God, men have created at the dictates of their whim, their own gods to adore: Nature, the State, the Nation, or the Race.</p></blockquote>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="1941_sermons">1941 sermons</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Clemens_August_Graf_von_Galen&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: 1941 sermons">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Galen's three powerful sermons of July and August 1941 earned him the nickname of the "Lion of Münster". The sermons were printed and distributed illegally.<sup id="cite_ref-Honourable_Defeat_p.60_39-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Honourable_Defeat_p.60-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> Hitler wanted to have Galen removed as a bishop, but Goebbels told him this would result in the loss of the loyalty of the population of <a href="/wiki/Westphalia" title="Westphalia">Westphalia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Honourable_Defeat_p.60_39-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Honourable_Defeat_p.60-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> The sermons protested against Nazi policies such as Gestapo terror, <a href="/wiki/Euthanasia" title="Euthanasia">euthanasia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Forced_sterilization" class="mw-redirect" title="Forced sterilization">forced sterilization</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Concentration_camp" class="mw-redirect" title="Concentration camp">concentration camps</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Cardinal_Ratzinger_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cardinal_Ratzinger-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> His attacks on the Nazis were so severe that Nazi official <a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Tie%C3%9Fler" class="extiw" title="de:Walter Tießler">Walter Tiessler</a> proposed in a letter to <a href="/wiki/Martin_Bormann" title="Martin Bormann">Martin Bormann</a> that Galen should be executed.<sup id="cite_ref-Cardinal_Ratzinger_43-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cardinal_Ratzinger-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 13 July 1941, Galen attacked the regime for its <a href="/wiki/Gestapo" title="Gestapo">Gestapo</a> tactics of <a href="/wiki/State_terrorism" title="State terrorism">terror</a>, including disappearances without trial, the closure of Catholic institutions without any stated justifications, and the resultant fear imposed on all Germans. The Gestapo, he argued, reduced even the most decent and loyal citizens to fear of ending up in a cellar prison or a concentration camp. Even though the country was at war, Galen rejected the notion that his speech undermined German solidarity or unity. Quoting Pope Pius XII's  <i>Opus Justitiae Pax and Justitia fundamentum Regnorum</i>, Galen noted that "Peace is the work of Justice and Justice, the basis for dominion", then attacked the Third Reich for undermining justice, the belief in justice and for reducing the German people to a state of permanent fear, even cowardice. He concluded: "As a German, as a decent citizen, I demand Justice".<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In a second sermon on 20 July 1941, Galen said that all written protests against the Nazi hostilities had proved to be useless. The confiscation of religious institutions continued unabated. Members of religious orders were still being deported or jailed. He asked his listeners to be patient and to endure, and said that the German people were being destroyed not by the Allied bombing from the outside, but from negative forces within.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 3 August 1941, Galen's third sermon described the continued desecration of Catholic churches, the closing and confiscation of convents and monasteries, and the deportation of mentally ill people to undisclosed destinations, while a notice was sent to family members stating that the person in question had died. This is murder, he exclaimed, unlawful by divine and German law, a rejection of the laws of God. He said he had forwarded his evidence to the State Attorney. "These are people, our brothers and sisters; maybe their life is unproductive, but productivity is not a justification for killing." If that were indeed a justification for execution, he reasoned, everybody would have to be afraid to even go to a doctor for fear of what might be discovered. The social fabric would be affected. Galen then remarked that a regime which can do away with the <a href="/wiki/Thou_shalt_not_kill" title="Thou shalt not kill">Fifth Commandment</a> ("Thou shalt not kill.") can destroy the other commandments as well.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> Galen went on to raise the question of whether permanently injured German soldiers would fall under the programme as well.
</p><p>Thousands of copies of the sermons were circulated throughout Germany.<sup id="cite_ref-Hamerow289_40-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hamerow289-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> The resulting local protests in Germany broke the secrecy that had surrounded the euthanasia programme known as <i>Aktion T4</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup> The local Nazi Gauleiter was furious and demanded Galen's immediate arrest. <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Goebbels" title="Joseph Goebbels">Joseph Goebbels</a> and party pragmatists preferred to wait until the end of hostilities to avoid undermining German morale in a heavily Catholic area.<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup> A year later, the euthanasia programme was still active, but the regime was conducting it in greater secrecy.
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/Robert_Jay_Lifton" title="Robert Jay Lifton">Robert Jay Lifton</a>, "[t]his powerful, populist sermon was immediately reproduced and distributed throughout Germany — indeed, it was dropped among German troops by British <a href="/wiki/Royal_Air_Force" title="Royal Air Force">Royal Air Force</a> flyers. Galen's sermon probably had a greater impact than any other one statement in consolidating anti-'euthanasia' sentiment."<sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Howard_K._Smith" title="Howard K. Smith">Howard K. Smith</a> called Galen "heroic", writing that the movement he represented was so widespread that the Nazi government could not arrest the bishop.<sup id="cite_ref-smith1942_50-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-smith1942-50">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Ian_Kershaw" title="Ian Kershaw">Ian Kershaw</a> called Galen's "open attack" on the government's euthanasia programme in 1941 a "vigorous denunciation of Nazi inhumanity and barbarism".<sup id="cite_ref-51" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-51">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/wiki/Anton_Gill" title="Anton Gill">Anton Gill</a>, "Galen used his condemnation of this appalling policy to draw wider conclusions about the nature of the Nazi state."<sup id="cite_ref-Anton_Gill_p.60_36-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Anton_Gill_p.60-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The sermons inspired various people in the <a href="/wiki/German_resistance_to_Nazism" title="German resistance to Nazism">German Resistance</a>. The <a href="/wiki/L%C3%BCbeck_martyrs" title="Lübeck martyrs">Lübeck martyrs</a> distributed von Galen's sermons.<sup id="cite_ref-Eduard_Müller_52-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Eduard_Müller-52">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup> The sermons influenced the <a href="/wiki/Scholl_siblings" class="mw-redirect" title="Scholl siblings">Scholl siblings</a> in founding the <a href="/wiki/White_Rose" title="White Rose">White Rose</a> pacifist student resistance group.<sup id="cite_ref-Anton_Gill_p.188_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Anton_Gill_p.188-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> One of von Galen's sermons of 1941 was the group's first pamphlet.<sup id="cite_ref-53" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-53">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> Generalmajor <a href="/wiki/Hans_Oster" title="Hans Oster">Hans Oster</a>, a devout Lutheran and a leading member of the German Resistance, once said of Galen:<sup id="cite_ref-54" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-54">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"/><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>He's a man of courage and conviction. And what resolution in his sermons! There should be a handful of such people in all our churches, and at least two handfuls in the <a href="/wiki/Wehrmacht" title="Wehrmacht">Wehrmacht</a>. If there were, Germany would look quite different!</p></blockquote>
<p>Galen suffered virtual house arrest from 1941 until the end of the war. Documents suggest the Nazis intended to hang him at the end of the war.<sup id="cite_ref-ReferenceC_38-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ReferenceC-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> In a <a href="/wiki/Hitler%27s_Table_Talk" title="Hitler&#39;s Table Talk">Table Talk</a> from 1942, Hitler said: "The fact that I remain silent in public over Church affairs is not in the least misunderstood by the sly foxes of the Catholic Church, and I am quite sure that a man like Bishop von Galen knows full well that after the war I shall extract retribution to the last farthing".<sup id="cite_ref-55" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-55">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Despite Galen's opposition to Nazism and its racial theories, he nonetheless believed Germany was the last bulwark against the spread of atheist <a href="/wiki/Bolshevism" title="Bolshevism">Bolshevism</a>. Parts of a sermon he gave in 1943 are said to have been used by the Nazis to aid in the enlistment of Dutch men to voluntarily join the <a href="/wiki/Waffen_SS" class="mw-redirect" title="Waffen SS">Waffen SS</a> against the Soviet Union.<sup id="cite_ref-56" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-56">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup> Galen feared that German Catholics were being relegated to second-class status in Hitler's Germany and believed Hitler was missing the point that the Catholic Church and the state could be aligned against Bolshevism.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2019)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> Although von Galen boldly spoke out against Nazi policies and the euthanasia programme,  historian Beth A. Griech-Polelle wrote that Galen remained silent on other issues such as the roundup, deportation and mass murder of Jews.<sup id="cite_ref-57" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-57">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup> German historian <a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim_Kuropka" class="extiw" title="de:Joachim Kuropka">Joachim Kuropka</a> dismissed this allegation as a "misjudgment".<sup id="cite_ref-Tagungsbericht_58-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Tagungsbericht-58">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup> Kuropka, referring to <a href="/w/index.php?title=Wilhelm_Damberg&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Wilhelm Damberg (page does not exist)">Wilhelm Damberg</a>'s discovery which in his opinion had not received enough attention so far, pointed out that the diocesan leadership in Münster had instructed all its pastors in June 1938 to recommend a brochure against anti-Semitism titled "The Nathanael Question of Our Days" ("Die Nathanaelfrage unserer Tage") to all faithful to read.<sup id="cite_ref-Tagungsbericht_58-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Tagungsbericht-58">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup> Kuropka also emphasized Galen's cordial personal relationship with Münster town rabbi <a href="/w/index.php?title=Fritz_Steinthal&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Fritz Steinthal (page does not exist)">Fritz Steinthal</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Tagungsbericht_58-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Tagungsbericht-58">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Kuropka, while there was no evidence in church archives for the rabbi's statement made from memory that after the <a href="/wiki/Kristallnacht" title="Kristallnacht">Kristallnacht</a>, by order of Galen, prayers for the Jews were said in all the churches of the Diocese of Münster, Kuropka was able to cite confirmatory evidence from Rhineland Gestapo files. Kuropka emphasized the uniqueness of the brochure distribution and the prayer campaign in Galen's diocese of Münster. However, like other bishops, according to Kuropka, Galen missed the right time to "escape into the public eye" on the question of the persecution of the Jews, for which Galen later reportedly blamed himself by his own admission.
<sup id="cite_ref-Tagungsbericht_58-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Tagungsbericht-58">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Apart from official pronouncements on the subject by the Pope and by German church bodies, Galen himself denounced Nazi racism on multiple occasions,<sup id="cite_ref-59" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-59">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup> and he was partly responsible for the German bishops' conference condemnation of racial persecution in the 1943 pastoral letter <i>Dekalog-Hirtenbrief</i>. After the war, Münster <a href="/wiki/Rabbi" title="Rabbi">rabbi</a> Fritz Steinthal recorded Galen's support after <a href="/wiki/Kristallnacht" title="Kristallnacht">Kristallnacht</a>, while expressing his firm conviction as rabbi that most Catholics in his city of Münster were horrified by the pogrom and in fact feared that they would be the next victims.<sup id="cite_ref-60" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-60">&#91;60&#93;</a></sup> During a commemoration in 2012, Jewish Holocaust survivor and witness Hans Kaufmann of Münster reminded of the fact that von Galen had offered a helping hand to Steinthal after the 1938 Kristallnacht, but deplored that other Jewish victims in Münster did not receive much aid from neighbours the day after.<sup id="cite_ref-61" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-61">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While not as explicit and not as effective as the vocal German episcopate's 1941 protests, in September 1943, von Galen and his fellow bishops in Germany drafted another condemnation of Nazi racial persecution and ordered it to be read from all pulpits in the diocese of Münster and across <a href="/wiki/Greater_Germanic_Reich" title="Greater Germanic Reich">Germany</a>, therein denouncing the killing of <i>"the innocent and defenceless mentally handicapped and mentally ill, the incurably infirm and fatally wounded, innocent hostages and disarmed prisoners of war and criminal offenders, people of foreign race or descent"</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-62" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-62">&#91;62&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In his history of the German Resistance, Theodore S. Hamerow characterised the resistance approach of Galen as "trying to influence the Third Reich from within". While some clergymen refused ever to feign support for the regime, in the Church's conflict with the State over ecclesiastical autonomy, the Catholic hierarchy adopted a strategy of "seeming acceptance of the Third Reich", by couching their criticisms as motivated merely by a desire to "point out mistakes that some of its overzealous followers committed" in order to strengthen the government.<sup id="cite_ref-Hamerow198_63-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hamerow198-63">&#91;63&#93;</a></sup> Thus when Bishop Galen delivered his famous 1941 denunciations of Nazi euthanasia and the lawlessness of the Gestapo, he also said that the Church had never sought the "overthrow of the Reich government".<sup id="cite_ref-Graml,_Mommsen_p.225_64-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Graml,_Mommsen_p.225-64">&#91;64&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Post-war_positions">Post-war positions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Clemens_August_Graf_von_Galen&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Post-war positions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After the war, Galen protested against the mistreatment of the German population by Allied occupation forces. On 13 April 1945, he raised a protest with American military authorities against the mass rape of German women by Red Army soldiers as well as against the plundering of German homes, factories, research centres, firms and offices by American and British troops.<sup id="cite_ref-65" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-65">&#91;65&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-66" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-66">&#91;66&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In a joint interview with British officials, Galen told the international press that "just as I fought against Nazi injustices, I will fight any injustice, no matter where it comes from".<sup id="cite_ref-67" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-67">&#91;67&#93;</a></sup> He repeated these claims in a sermon on 1 July 1945, which was copied and illegally distributed throughout occupied Germany. The British authorities ordered him to renounce the sermon immediately, but the bishop refused.<sup id="cite_ref-68" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-68">&#91;68&#93;</a></sup> In the face of his resistance and broad popularity, they allowed him free speech without any censorship. In an interview with Swiss media, Galen demanded punishment for Nazi criminals but humane treatment for the millions of German prisoners of war who had not committed any crimes and who were being denied contact with their relatives by the British. He criticized the British dismissal of Germans from public service without investigation and trial.<sup id="cite_ref-69" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-69">&#91;69&#93;</a></sup> He forcefully condemned the <a href="/wiki/Expulsion_of_Germans_after_World_War_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Expulsion of Germans after World War II">expulsion of German civilians</a> from <a href="/wiki/Historical_Eastern_Germany" class="mw-redirect" title="Historical Eastern Germany">former German provinces and territories in the east</a> annexed by <a href="/wiki/People%27s_Republic_of_Poland" class="mw-redirect" title="People&#39;s Republic of Poland">communist Poland</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Soviet_Union" title="Soviet Union">Soviet Union</a>.
</p><p>A paper from the <a href="/wiki/British_Foreign_Office" class="mw-redirect" title="British Foreign Office">British Foreign Office</a> called Galen <i>"the most outstanding personality among the clergy in the British zone... Statuesque in appearance and uncompromising in discussion, this oak-bottomed old aristocrat... is a German nationalist through and through."</i><sup id="cite_ref-70" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-70">&#91;70&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When SS-General <a href="/wiki/Kurt_Meyer_(Panzermeyer)" class="mw-redirect" title="Kurt Meyer (Panzermeyer)">Kurt Meyer</a>, accused of complicity in the shooting of eighteen Canadian prisoners of war, was sentenced to death, Galen pleaded for his life to be spared: "According to what has been reported to me, General Kurt Meyer was sentenced to death because his subordinates committed crimes he didn't arrange and of which he did not approve. As a proponent of Christian legal opinion, which states that you are only responsible for your own deeds, I support the plea for clemency for General Meyer and pledge for a pardon." On second review, a Canadian general, finding only "a mass of circumstantial evidence", commuted Meyer's death sentence to imprisonment. Meyer served nine years in British and Canadian military prisons.<sup id="cite_ref-71" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-71">&#91;71&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="College_of_Cardinals">College of Cardinals</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Clemens_August_Graf_von_Galen&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: College of Cardinals">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Unexpectedly, at Christmas 1945 it became known that <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII" title="Pope Pius XII">Pope Pius XII</a> would appoint three new German cardinals: Bishop Clemens August von Galen, Bishop <a href="/wiki/Konrad_von_Preysing" title="Konrad von Preysing">Konrad von Preysing</a> of Berlin, and Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Josef_Frings" title="Josef Frings">Josef Frings</a> of Cologne. Despite numerous British obstacles and denial of air travel, Galen arrived in Rome 5 February 1946.<sup id="cite_ref-72" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-72">&#91;72&#93;</a></sup> Generous American cardinals financed his Roman stay, as German money was not in demand. He had become famous and popular, so after the pope had placed the red hat on his head with the words: 'God bless you, God bless Germany', Saint Peter's Basilica for minutes thundered in a "triumphant applause" for Galen.<sup id="cite_ref-73" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-73">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, he visited the German POW camps in <a href="/wiki/Taranto" title="Taranto">Taranto</a> and told the German <a href="/wiki/Wehrmacht" title="Wehrmacht">Wehrmacht</a> soldiers that he would take care of their release, and that the Pope himself was working on the release of POWs. He took a large number of comforting personal messages to their worried families.<sup id="cite_ref-74" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-74">&#91;74&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After receiving the red hat, Galen went to see <a href="/wiki/Pascalina_Lehnert" title="Pascalina Lehnert">Madre Pascalina</a>, the faithful servant of the Pope. He told her how the Pope had quoted long passages from Galen's 1941 sermons from memory and how the Pope thanked him for his courage. Galen told the Pope, "Yes, Holy Father, but many of my very best priests died in concentration camps, because they distributed my sermons." Pius replied that he was always aware that thousands of innocent persons would have been sent to certain death if he as pope had protested. They talked about the old days in Berlin, and Galen declared: "for nothing in the world would I want to have missed those two hours, not even for the red hat."<sup id="cite_ref-75" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-75">&#91;75&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_beatification">Death and beatification</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Clemens_August_Graf_von_Galen&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Death and beatification">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Following his return from the wearisome travel to <a href="/wiki/Vatican_City" title="Vatican City">Vatican City</a>, the new cardinal was celebrated enthusiastically in his native <a href="/wiki/Westphalia" title="Westphalia">Westphalia</a> and in his destroyed city of <a href="/wiki/M%C3%BCnster" title="Münster">Münster</a>, which still lay completely in ruins as a result of the air raids. He died a few days after his return from Rome in the St. Franziskus Hospital of Münster due to an appendix infection diagnosed too late. His last words were:<sup id="cite_ref-76" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-76">&#91;76&#93;</a></sup> "Yes, Yes, as God wills it. May God reward you for it. May God protect the dear fatherland. Go on working for Him... oh, you dear Saviour!" He was buried in the family crypt of the Galen family in the destroyed Cathedral of Münster.
</p><p>The cause for <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatification</a> was requested by his successor, Bishop Michael Keller of Münster and began under Pope Pius XII in 1956. It was concluded positively in November 2004 under <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a>. Clemens August Graf von Galen was beatified on 9 October 2005 outside <a href="/wiki/St._Peter%27s_Basilica" title="St. Peter&#39;s Basilica">St. Peter's Basilica</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a>, the 47th anniversary of the death of Pope Pius (1958).
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Terminology_note">Terminology note</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Clemens_August_Graf_von_Galen&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Terminology note">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<ul><li><i><a href="/wiki/Graf" title="Graf">Graf</a></i> is a German title rendered as <a href="/wiki/Count" title="Count">Count</a> in English, not a first or middle name. The use of <i>von</i> before the family name <i>Galen</i> is indicative of this status. The noble particle (or preposition) <i>von</i> is traditionally dropped in prose when using the family name without the given name or the title <i>Graf</i>. – If <i>Graf</i> is recognized as a title of non-reigning nobility (and not as a mere part of the name as officially the case after 1919 in Germany), then it is surpassed by the title of Cardinal and thus not used together with it.</li></ul>
