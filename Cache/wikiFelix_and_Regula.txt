<p><b>Felix and Regula</b> are <a href="/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Coptic Orthodox">Coptic Orthodox</a> and <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> saints, together with their servant <b>Exuperantius</b>, and are the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saints</a> of <a href="/wiki/Z%C3%BCrich" title="Zürich">Zürich</a>, their <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> being 11 September in the Gregorian calendar,<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>  celebrated on the same day using the Julian calendar (which is the 24th of September Gregorian), the 14th of Tout in the Coptic Calendar. 
</p><p>Felix and Regula were siblings, beheaded in the third century, along with their loyal servant Exuperantius, for converting to Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> 
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Felix_and_Regula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Felix and Regula were siblings, and members of the <a href="/wiki/Theban_legion" class="mw-redirect" title="Theban legion">Theban legion</a> under <a href="/wiki/Saint_Maurice" title="Saint Maurice">Saint Maurice</a>, stationed in <a href="/wiki/Agaunum" title="Agaunum">Agaunum</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Valais" title="Valais">Valais</a>. When the legion was to be executed in 286, they fled, reaching Zürich via <a href="/wiki/Glarus" title="Glarus">Glarus</a> before they were caught, tried and executed. After <a href="/wiki/Decapitation" title="Decapitation">decapitation</a>, they miraculously stood to their feet, <a href="/wiki/Cephalophore" title="Cephalophore">picked up their own heads</a>, walked forty paces uphill, and prayed before lying down in death. They were buried on the spot where they lay down, on the hilltop which would become the site of the <a href="/wiki/Grossm%C3%BCnster" title="Grossmünster">Grossmünster</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The story was revealed in a dream to a monk called Florentius. 
It largely contributed to the massive conversion of the inhabitants of these regions to Christianity and had such an impact on Zurich that these three saints still appear on the seal of Zurich today.
</p><p>In the 9th century, there was a small monastery at the location, outside the settlement of Zürich which was situated on the left side of the <a href="/wiki/Limmat" title="Limmat">Limmat</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Grossm%C3%BCnster" title="Grossmünster">Grossmünster</a> was built on their graves from ca. 1100, while at the site of their execution stands the  <a href="/wiki/Wasserkirche" title="Wasserkirche">Wasserkirche</a>. From the 13th century, images of the saints were used in official seals of the city and on coins. On the saints' feast day, their relics were carried in procession between the Grossmünster and the <a href="/wiki/Fraum%C3%BCnster" title="Fraumünster">Fraumünster</a>, and the two monasteries vied for possession of the relics, which attracted enough pilgrims to make Zürich the most important pilgrimage site in the bishopric of <a href="/wiki/Konstanz" title="Konstanz">Konstanz</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Knabenschiessen" title="Knabenschiessen">Knabenschiessen</a> of Zürich originates with the feast day of the saints on 11 September, which came to be the "national holiday" of the early modern <a href="/wiki/Republic_of_Z%C3%BCrich" class="mw-redirect" title="Republic of Zürich">Republic of Zürich</a>.
</p><p>With the dissolution of the monasteries by <a href="/wiki/Huldrych_Zwingli" title="Huldrych Zwingli">Huldrych Zwingli</a> in 1524, their possessions were confiscated and the graves of the martyrs were opened. There are conflicting versions of what happened then. <a href="/wiki/Heinrich_Bullinger" title="Heinrich Bullinger">Heinrich Bullinger</a> claims that the graves were empty save for a few bone fragments, which were piously buried in the common graveyard outside the church. The Catholics, on the other hand, claimed that the reformers were planning to throw the relics of the saints into the river, and that a courageous man of <a href="/wiki/Canton_of_Uri" title="Canton of Uri">Uri</a> (who happened to be exiled from Uri, and by his action earned <a href="/wiki/Amnesty" title="Amnesty">amnesty</a>) stole the relics from the church and carried them to <a href="/wiki/Andermatt" title="Andermatt">Andermatt</a>, where the two skulls of Felix and Regula can be seen to this day, while the remaining relics were returned to Zürich in 1950, to the newly built Catholic church <i>St. Felix und Regula</i>. The skulls have been <a href="/wiki/Carbon_14" class="mw-redirect" title="Carbon 14">Carbon 14</a> dated, and while one dates to the Middle Ages, the other is in fact composed of fragments of two separate skulls, of which one is medieval, and the other could indeed date to Roman times.
</p>
