					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><p><b>Pavel Djidjov</b> (19 July 1919 &#8211; 3 October 1952) was a <a href="/wiki/Bulgarian_people" class="mw-redirect" title="Bulgarian people">Bulgarian</a> <a href="/wiki/Theologian" class="mw-redirect" title="Theologian">theologian</a> who was executed after a show trial and <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> in 2002 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a>.
</p>
<h2><span id="Education.2C_career"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Education,_career">Education, career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pavel_Djidjov&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Education, career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Pavel Djidjov was born to a <a href="/wiki/Latin_Church" title="Latin Church">Latin rite</a> <a href="/wiki/Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic">Catholic</a> family in <a href="/wiki/Plovdiv" title="Plovdiv">Plovdiv</a>. He was baptized on 2 August 1919 and given the name Joseph. He took the name Pavel (Paul) when he entered the <a href="/wiki/Assumptionists" title="Assumptionists">Assumptionist</a> novitiate in <a href="/wiki/Nozeroy" title="Nozeroy">Nozeroy</a>, France, in October 1938.
</p><p>To achieve his childhood ambition of attaining <a href="/wiki/Priesthood" class="mw-redirect" title="Priesthood">priesthood</a>, he entered the Assumptionist-sponsored St. Augustine College in Plovdiv, where he was considered a good student, especially in mathematics. After his novitiate, he went on to study theology in Lormoy, France, near Paris, during <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">World War II</a>. For health reasons he returned to Bulgaria in 1942 to complete his theological studies and was ordained on 26 January 1945. He later continued his studies in economics and social sciences. His first assignment was as a teacher at the Assumptionist high school in <a href="/wiki/Varna,_Bulgaria" title="Varna, Bulgaria">Varna</a>, where he was closely watched by the secret police. Not long thereafter he was named to be the treasurer at St. Augustine College in Plovdiv and served there until the institution was closed by the Communists in 1948.
</p><p>He was particularly appreciated by students and others for his piety, sense of humor, deep faith, ecumenical spirit, and bravery before the Communist regime. Often he risked personal safety by defending the rights of the Church in public and visiting political prisoners.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Arrest_and_trial">Arrest and trial</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pavel_Djidjov&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Arrest and trial">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>As a Soviet satellite, Bulgaria suffered from the wave of <a href="/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians_in_Warsaw_Pact_countries" class="mw-redirect" title="Persecution of Christians in Warsaw Pact countries">anti-Church legislation</a> that swept the bloc in the years after World War II (e.g. the arrest of Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Aloysius_Stepinac" title="Aloysius Stepinac">Aloysius Stepinac</a> in <a href="/wiki/Yugoslavia" title="Yugoslavia">Yugoslavia</a> in 1946, of Cardinal <a href="/wiki/J%C3%B3zsef_Mindszenty" title="József Mindszenty">József Mindszenty</a> in <a href="/wiki/Hungary" title="Hungary">Hungary</a> in 1948, of Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Josef_Beran" title="Josef Beran">Josef Beran</a> in <a href="/wiki/Czechoslovakia" title="Czechoslovakia">Czechoslovakia</a> in 1950, and of Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Stefan_Wyszy%C5%84ski" title="Stefan Wyszyński">Stefan Wyszyński</a> in <a href="/wiki/Poland" title="Poland">Poland</a> in 1953). Already as a student in 1945, Djidjov had engaged in polemics with Communist students and professors and was placed on a blacklist.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A month before his arrest in July 1952, he wrote to the Assumptionist procurator in Rome, Fr. Rémi Kokel, concerning a colleague they hadn't heard from in a long time: "We are distressed, having heard nothing of Fr. Josaphat (Chichkov) for six months now. There will be three trials within a week against Catholic priests. The first one is already over: death penalty for a diocesan priest of the Latin rite. Tomorrow it will be the trial of a diocesan priest of the Slavonic rite, in Sofia; the day after tomorrow, that of a Capuchin. And these will not be the last ones, surely. May God’s will be done."<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (July 2009)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> One month later he, too, was imprisoned.
</p><p>Besides Djidjov, two other Assumptionist brothers were imprisoned: Fr. Joseph Chichkov and Fr. Kamen Vitchev. The latter followed the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Rite" title="Byzantine Rite">Byzantine Rite</a>, whereas the others followed the <a href="/wiki/Latin_liturgical_rites" title="Latin liturgical rites">Latin Rite</a>—in their martyrdom, they united the two main Catholic rites,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> a point reiterated by Pope John Paul II in 2002.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> All three had been schooled in <a href="/wiki/Belgium" title="Belgium">Belgium</a> and France, and were accused of spying for the French government.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> After what international organizations universally considered a show trial, which began on 29 September 1952, and ended with a guilty verdict and a death sentence on 3 October, the three, together with the <a href="/wiki/Passionist" class="mw-redirect" title="Passionist">Passionist</a> bishop Eugene Bossilkov,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> were shot to death in prison,<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> without public notice, at 11:30 PM, on 11 November 1952. Their bodies were buried in a communal grave and have not been recovered.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Fr. Pavel Djidjov was declared a <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a> for the faith and <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> by Pope John Paul II in Plovdiv on 26 May 2002.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The pope delivered a homily on the occasion, during his <a href="/wiki/List_of_pastoral_visits_of_Pope_John_Paul_II_outside_Italy" class="mw-redirect" title="List of pastoral visits of Pope John Paul II outside Italy">2002 visit</a> to Bulgaria. Later, he commented that the Eucharistic celebration during which he beatified Djidjov, Vitchev, and Chichkov was "the high point of my brief but intense visit in Bulgaria."<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 28 July 2010 the Bulgarian parliament passed a law officially rehabilitating all of those who had been condemned by the People's Republic of Bulgaria in 1952, including Fr. Djidjov.
</p>
