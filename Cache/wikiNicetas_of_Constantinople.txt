<p>
<b>Saint Nicetas the Patrician</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Νικήτας Πατρίκιος</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek" title="Romanization of Greek">romanized</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Greek-language romanization"><i lang="el-Latn">Niketas Patrikios</i></span>; 761/62 – 6 October 836) was a <a href="/wiki/Byzantine" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine">Byzantine</a> monk and a fervent opponent of <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm" title="Byzantine Iconoclasm">Byzantine Iconoclasm</a>. He is usually identified with <b>Nicetas Monomachos</b> (Νικήτας Μονομάχος), a <a href="/wiki/Eunuch" title="Eunuch">eunuch</a> official and general from <a href="/wiki/Paphlagonia" title="Paphlagonia">Paphlagonia</a> active at the turn of the 9th century.
</p><p>He is honoured as a saint and a <a href="/wiki/Confessor_of_the_Faith" title="Confessor of the Faith">Confessor of the Faith</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>. His feast day is on 13 October.<sup id="cite_ref-EHW1_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW1-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_career">Early life and career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicetas_the_Patrician&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life and career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nicetas was born in <a href="/wiki/Paphlagonia" title="Paphlagonia">Paphlagonia</a> in 761/62, and his parents were probably named Gregory and Anna. Later tradition held that he was a descendant of <a href="/wiki/Theodora_(wife_of_Theophilos)" title="Theodora (wife of Theophilos)">Empress Theodora</a>, the wife of <a href="/wiki/Theophilos_(emperor)" title="Theophilos (emperor)">Theophilos</a> (<span style="white-space:nowrap;"><abbr title="reigned">r.</abbr>&#8201;829–842</span>). This is clearly impossible, but some sort of relation cannot be excluded. Another tradition records that he was also a relative of Empress <a href="/wiki/Irene_of_Athens" title="Irene of Athens">Irene of Athens</a> (<span style="white-space:nowrap;"><abbr title="reigned">r.</abbr>&#8201;797–802</span>).<sup id="cite_ref-EHW1_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW1-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Lilie_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lilie-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-PmbZ_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-PmbZ-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> According to his <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a>, he was <a href="/wiki/Castration" title="Castration">castrated</a> by his parents at a young age, received a good education and was sent to <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> at age 17 (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;778</span>), where he entered the service of the imperial court.<sup id="cite_ref-EHW1_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW1-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-TM313,315_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TM313,315-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> According to the same source, Nicetas distinguished himself among the court eunuchs and came to the attention of Empress-mother Irene, who handled the Empire's affairs as regent after 780. Irene promoted him because of his ability and because of their ties of kinship. In 787, Nicetas is even said to have represented the Empress at the <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Nicaea" title="Second Council of Nicaea">Second Council of Nicaea</a>, although given Nicetas' youth this is likely to be an invention by his hagiographer.<sup id="cite_ref-Lilie_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lilie-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-PmbZ_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-PmbZ-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-TM313,315_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TM313,315-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-EHW2_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW2-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Shortly after, he was promoted to the rank of <i><a href="/wiki/Patrikios" class="mw-redirect" title="Patrikios">patrikios</a></i>, and was sent to <a href="/wiki/Sicily" title="Sicily">Sicily</a> as the governor (<i><a href="/wiki/Strategos" title="Strategos">strategos</a></i>) of the local <a href="/wiki/Theme_(Byzantine_district)" title="Theme (Byzantine district)">theme</a>. On account of this information, he is usually identified with the <i>patrikios</i> and <i>strategos</i> of Sicily Nicetas, who in 797 sent an embassy to <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a>, as well as with the <i>patrikios</i> Nicetas Monomachos, who brought the hand of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Euphemia" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Euphemia">Saint Euphemia</a> from Constantinople <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;796</span> and built a church in Sicily to house the holy relic. Nicetas' tenure as governor of Sicily is therefore placed <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;797</span>, and ended before 799, when a certain Michael was governor of the <a href="/wiki/Theme_of_Sicily" class="mw-redirect" title="Theme of Sicily">Theme of Sicily</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-PmbZ_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-PmbZ-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-EHW2_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW2-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> If "Monomachos" represents a family name rather than a sobriquet (it means "single combatant"), Nicetas would be the first attested member of the <a href="/wiki/Monomachos_family" class="mw-redirect" title="Monomachos family">Monomachos family</a>, which rose to prominence in the 11th century, with several of its members becoming high-ranking functionaries, and which also produced an emperor, <a href="/wiki/Constantine_IX_Monomachos" title="Constantine IX Monomachos">Constantine IX Monomachos</a> (<span style="white-space:nowrap;"><abbr title="reigned">r.</abbr>&#8201;1042–1055</span>).<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Almost nothing is known of Nicetas' activities in the decade after the deposition of Irene in 802.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> According to his hagiography, he wanted to retire to a monastery, but was prohibited from doing so by Emperor <a href="/wiki/Nikephoros_I" title="Nikephoros I">Nikephoros I</a> (<span style="white-space:nowrap;"><abbr title="reigned">r.</abbr>&#8201;802–811</span>) and his son <a href="/wiki/Staurakios" title="Staurakios">Staurakios</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-TM313,315_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TM313,315-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-EHW2_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW2-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> However, he has been tentatively identified with a number of people of the same name and rank mentioned in the chronicles: thus he may be the <i>patrikios</i> Nicetas who owned the house where the <a href="/wiki/Gastria_Monastery" class="mw-redirect" title="Gastria Monastery">Gastria Monastery</a> was later built,<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> or the <i>patrikios</i> Nicetas who was one of the founders of the Church of St. Stephen in <a href="/wiki/Trigleia" class="mw-redirect" title="Trigleia">Trigleia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bithynia" title="Bithynia">Bithynia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> He is also frequently equated with the admiral Nicetas who in 807–808 led the Byzantine fleet in its reoccupation of <a href="/wiki/Dalmatia" title="Dalmatia">Dalmatia</a> and <a href="/wiki/Venice" title="Venice">Venice</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-EHW2_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW2-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> who in turn is sometimes identified with the Nicetas who was <a href="/wiki/General_Logothete" class="mw-redirect" title="General Logothete">General Logothete</a> in 808–811.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastic_life">Monastic life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicetas_the_Patrician&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Monastic life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>With the accession of <a href="/wiki/Michael_I_Rhangabe" class="mw-redirect" title="Michael I Rhangabe">Michael I Rhangabe</a> (<span style="white-space:nowrap;"><abbr title="reigned">r.</abbr>&#8201;811–813</span>) to the throne, Nicetas was at last able to receive <a href="/wiki/Tonsure" title="Tonsure">tonsure</a> (late 811). Indeed, the new emperor encouraged him in this endeavour, served as his sponsor, and gave him the convent of Chrysonike near the <a href="/wiki/Golden_Gate_(Constantinople)" class="mw-redirect" title="Golden Gate (Constantinople)">Golden Gate</a>, where Nicetas retired.<sup id="cite_ref-TM313,315_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TM313,315-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Nicetas remained in the monastery as its <i><a href="/wiki/Hegumenos" class="mw-redirect" title="Hegumenos">hegumenos</a></i> (<a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a>) until late 815, when the second phase of the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm" title="Byzantine Iconoclasm">Byzantine Iconoclasm</a> began under the auspices of <a href="/wiki/Leo_V_the_Armenian" title="Leo V the Armenian">Leo V the Armenian</a> (<span style="white-space:nowrap;"><abbr title="reigned">r.</abbr>&#8201;813–820</span>). Refusing to acknowledge the Emperor's iconoclast policies, Nicetas left the capital for one of its suburbs. He was accused at one point of sheltering an icon, but he suffered no punishment except for the confiscation of the image and his confinement to house arrest.<sup id="cite_ref-EHW3_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW3-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Nothing is known of his life during the reign of <a href="/wiki/Michael_II_the_Amorian" class="mw-redirect" title="Michael II the Amorian">Michael II the Amorian</a> (<span style="white-space:nowrap;"><abbr title="reigned">r.</abbr>&#8201;820–829</span>),<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> but in the early reign of Michael's son and successor Theophilos, the persecution of iconophiles intensified, and monks in particular became the targets of the emperor's iconoclast zeal. Despite his probable family connection to Theophilos' empress, Nicetas was ordered to accept communion with the iconoclast patriarch, <a href="/wiki/Antony_I_of_Constantinople" title="Antony I of Constantinople">Antony Kassymatas</a>, or face exile. Nicetas chose the latter, and with a handful of disciples and other like-minded monks he fled to Bithynia. He spent the next few years moving from locality to locality around the coast of the <a href="/wiki/Sea_of_Marmara" title="Sea of Marmara">Sea of Marmara</a> to evade harassment from iconoclast officials, before finally settling in the villages of Zouloupas and then Katesia, where he died on 6 October 836.<sup id="cite_ref-EHW3_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW3-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> According to the sources, Nicetas specialized in "healing men tormented by sexual desires" (K. Ringrose).<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Hagiographic_sources">Hagiographic sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicetas_the_Patrician&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Hagiographic sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The main sources on Nicetas are his hagiography and the <i><a href="/wiki/Synaxarion" class="mw-redirect" title="Synaxarion">synaxaria</a></i>. The hagiography survives in a 12th-century manuscript, now located in the <a href="/wiki/National_Library_of_Greece" title="National Library of Greece">National Library of Greece</a> at <a href="/wiki/Athens" title="Athens">Athens</a>. It was formerly attributed to <a href="/wiki/Nicetas_of_Medikion" title="Nicetas of Medikion">Nicetas of Medikion</a>, but was written by an anonymous monk of the Monastery of Asomaton at Katesia, founded by Nicetas, sometime shortly after Nicetas' death. The account was based on the notes of Nicetas' namesake nephew and disciple, who succeeded him as the monastery's abbot.<sup id="cite_ref-EHW4_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EHW4-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
