<p><b>Saint Guthlac of Crowland</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a>&#58; <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">Gūðlāc</i></span>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Guthlacus</i>; 674 – 3 April 714 CE) was a Christian <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a> and <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> from <a href="/wiki/Lincolnshire" title="Lincolnshire">Lincolnshire</a> in England. He is particularly venerated in <a href="/wiki/The_Fens" title="The Fens">the Fens</a> of eastern England.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Guthlac_of_Crowland&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<p>Guthlac was the son of Penwalh or Penwald, a noble of the English kingdom of <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercia</a>, and his wife Tette. His sister is also venerated as St <a href="/wiki/Pega" title="Pega">Pega</a>. As a young man, Guthlac fought in the army of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_of_Mercia" title="Æthelred of Mercia">Æthelred of Mercia</a>. He subsequently became a monk at <a href="/wiki/Repton" title="Repton">Repton Monastery</a> in <a href="/wiki/Derbyshire" title="Derbyshire">Derbyshire</a> at the age of 24, under the abbess there, Repton being a double monastery. Two years later he sought to live the life of a <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a>, and moved out to the island of <a href="/wiki/Crowland" title="Crowland">Croyland, now called Crowland</a>, on <a href="/wiki/St_Bartholomew%27s_Day" class="mw-redirect" title="St Bartholomew&#39;s Day">St Bartholomew's Day</a>, 699. His early biographer Felix asserts that Guthlac could understand the <i>strimulentes loquelas</i> ("sibilant speech")<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> of British-speaking demons who haunted him there, only because Guthlac had spent some time in exile among <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Britons" title="Celtic Britons">Celtic Britons</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Guthlac built a small <a href="/wiki/Oratory_(worship)" title="Oratory (worship)">oratory</a> and <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">cells</a> in the side of a plundered <a href="/wiki/Tumulus" title="Tumulus">barrow</a> on the island. There he lived until his death on 11 April 714. Felix, writing within living memory of Guthlac, described his hermit's life:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>Now there was in the said island a mound built of clods of earth which greedy comers to the waste had dug open, in the hope of finding treasure there; in the side of this there seemed to be a sort of cistern, and in this Guthlac the man of blessed memory began to dwell, after building a hut over it. From the time when he first inhabited this hermitage this was his unalterable rule of life: namely to wear neither wool nor linen garments nor any other sort of soft material, but he spent the whole of his solitary life wearing garments made of skins. So great indeed was the abstinence of his daily life that from the time when he began to inhabit the desert he ate no food of any kind except that after sunset he took a scrap of barley bread and a small cup of muddy water. For when the sun reached its western limits, then he thankfully tasted some little provision for the needs of this mortal life.</p></blockquote>
<p>Guthlac suffered from <a href="/wiki/Fever" title="Fever">ague</a> and <a href="/wiki/Marsh_fever" class="mw-redirect" title="Marsh fever">marsh fever</a>.
</p><p>Guthlac's pious and holy ascetic life became the talk of the land, and many people visited Guthlac during his life to seek spiritual guidance from him. He gave sanctuary to <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelbald_of_Mercia" title="Æthelbald of Mercia">Æthelbald</a>, future king of <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercia</a>, who was fleeing from his cousin <a href="/wiki/Ceolred_of_Mercia" title="Ceolred of Mercia">Ceolred</a>. Guthlac predicted that Æthelbald would become king, and Æthelbald promised to build him an <a href="/wiki/Abbey" title="Abbey">abbey</a> if his prophecy became true. Æthelbald indeed became king, and even though Guthlac had died two years before, he kept his word and started to build <a href="/wiki/Crowland_Abbey" title="Crowland Abbey">Crowland Abbey</a> on St Bartholomew's Day, 716. Guthlac's feast day is celebrated on 11 April.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Guthlac-Bartholemew.png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0c/Guthlac-Bartholemew.png/220px-Guthlac-Bartholemew.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="198" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0c/Guthlac-Bartholemew.png/330px-Guthlac-Bartholemew.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0c/Guthlac-Bartholemew.png/440px-Guthlac-Bartholemew.png 2x" data-file-width="3400" data-file-height="3060" /></a><figcaption>St Guthlac, tormented by demons, is handed a <a href="/wiki/Scourge" title="Scourge">scourge</a> by <a href="/wiki/St_Bartholomew" class="mw-redirect" title="St Bartholomew">St Bartholomew</a>, <i>Guthlac Roll</i>, 1210, <a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a></figcaption></figure>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St.Guthlac%27s_Cross_-_geograph.org.uk_-_593623.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8d/St.Guthlac%27s_Cross_-_geograph.org.uk_-_593623.jpg/120px-St.Guthlac%27s_Cross_-_geograph.org.uk_-_593623.jpg" decoding="async" width="120" height="180" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8d/St.Guthlac%27s_Cross_-_geograph.org.uk_-_593623.jpg/180px-St.Guthlac%27s_Cross_-_geograph.org.uk_-_593623.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8d/St.Guthlac%27s_Cross_-_geograph.org.uk_-_593623.jpg/240px-St.Guthlac%27s_Cross_-_geograph.org.uk_-_593623.jpg 2x" data-file-width="427" data-file-height="640" /></a><figcaption>St Guthlac's cross from c 1200, inscribed <i>Hanc Petra Guthlac ...</i>, marked the boundary of Crowland Abbey</figcaption></figure>
<p>The 8th-century Latin <i>Vita sancti Guthlaci</i>, written by Felix, describes the entry of the demons into Guthlac's cell:<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>They were ferocious in appearance, terrible in shape with great heads, long necks, thin faces, yellow complexions, filthy beards, shaggy ears, wild foreheads, fierce eyes, foul mouths, horses' teeth, throats vomiting flames, twisted jaws, thick lips, strident voices, singed hair, fat cheeks, pigeons breasts, scabby thighs, knotty knees, crooked legs, swollen ankles, splay feet, spreading mouths, raucous cries. For they grew so terrible to hear with their mighty shriekings that they filled almost the whole intervening space between earth and heaven with their discordant bellowings.</p></blockquote>
<p>Felix records Guthlac's foreknowledge of his own death, conversing with angels in his last days. At the moment of death a sweet nectar-like odour emanated from his mouth, as his soul departed from his body in a beam of light while the angels sang. Guthlac had requested a lead coffin and linen winding sheet from Ecgburh, Abbess of <a href="/wiki/Repton_Abbey" title="Repton Abbey">Repton Abbey</a>, so that his funeral rites could be performed by his sister <a href="/wiki/Pega" title="Pega">Pega</a>. Arriving the day after his death, she found the island of Crowland filled with the scent of ambrosia. She buried the body on the mound after three days of prayer. A year later Pega had a divine calling to move the tomb and relics to a nearby chapel: Guthlac's body is said to have been discovered uncorrupted, his shroud shining with light. Subsequently Guthlac appeared in a miraculous vision to <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelbald_of_Mercia" title="Æthelbald of Mercia">Æthelbald</a>, prophesying he would be future <a href="/wiki/King_of_Mercia" class="mw-redirect" title="King of Mercia">King of Mercia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The cult of Guthlac continued amongst a monastic community at Crowland, with the eventual foundation of <a href="/wiki/Crowland_Abbey" title="Crowland Abbey">Crowland Abbey</a> as a <a href="/wiki/Benedictine_Order" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine Order">Benedictine Order</a> in 971. A series of fires at the abbey mean that few records survive from before the 12th century. It is known that in 1136 the remains of Guthlac were moved once more, and that finally in 1196 his shrine was placed above the main altar.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_accounts">Other accounts</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Guthlac_of_Crowland&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Other accounts">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>A short Old English sermon (<a href="/wiki/Vercelli_Book" title="Vercelli Book">Vercelli</a> XXIII) and a longer prose translation into Old English are both based on Felix's <i>Vita</i>. There are also two poems in Old English known as <i><a href="/wiki/Guthlac_poems_A_and_B" title="Guthlac poems A and B">Guthlac A</a></i> and <i><a href="/wiki/Guthlac_poems_A_and_B" title="Guthlac poems A and B">Guthlac B</a></i>, part of the tenth century <a href="/wiki/Exeter_Book" title="Exeter Book">Exeter Book</a>, the oldest surviving collection of English poetry. The relationship of <i>Guthlac A</i> to Felix's <i>Vita</i> is debated, but <i>Guthlac B</i> is based on Felix's account of the saint's death.
</p><p><span class="anchor" id="Guthlac_Roll"></span>
The story of Guthlac is told pictorially in the <i>Guthlac Roll</i>, a set of detailed illustrations of the early 13th century. This is held in the <a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>, with copies on display in Crowland Abbey.
</p><p>Another account, also dating from after the <a href="/wiki/Norman_conquest_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Norman conquest of England">Norman Conquest</a>, was included in the <i>Ecclesiastical History</i> of <a href="/wiki/Orderic_Vitalis" title="Orderic Vitalis">Orderic Vitalis</a>, which like the <i>Guthlac Roll</i> was commissioned by the Abbot of Crowland Abbey. At a time when it was being challenged by the crown, the Abbey relied significantly on the cult of Guthlac, which made it a place of pilgrimage and healing. That is reflected in a shift in the emphasis from the earlier accounts of Felix and others. The post-conquest accounts portray him as a defender of the church rather than a saintly ascetic; instead of dwelling in an ancient burial mound, they depict Guthlac overseeing the building of a brick and stone chapel on the site of the abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Yorkshire village of <a href="/wiki/Golcar" title="Golcar">Golcar</a> on the outskirts of Huddersfield is named after St Guthlac, who preached in the area during the 8th century. The name of the village is recorded in the <a href="/wiki/Domesday_Book" title="Domesday Book">Domesday Book</a> as Goullakarres.
</p><p>It has been proposed that Shakespeare drew on a lost play based on St Guthlac when writing <i><a href="/wiki/The_Tempest" title="The Tempest">The Tempest</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>For a discussion of the name “Guthlac” see Paul Cavill's essay "The naming of Guthlac".<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
