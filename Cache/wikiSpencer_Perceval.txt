<p><b>Spencer Perceval</b> <span class="noexcerpt nowraplinks" style="font-size:85%; font-weight:normal;"><a href="/wiki/King%27s_Counsel" title="King&#39;s Counsel">KC</a></span> (1 November 1762 – 11 May 1812) was a British statesman and barrister who served as <a href="/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom" title="Prime Minister of the United Kingdom">Prime Minister of the United Kingdom</a> from October 1809 until <a href="/wiki/Assassination_of_Spencer_Perceval" title="Assassination of Spencer Perceval">his assassination</a> in May 1812. He is the only British prime minister to have been assassinated, and the only <a href="/wiki/Solicitor_General_for_England_and_Wales" title="Solicitor General for England and Wales">solicitor-general</a> or <a href="/wiki/Attorney_General_for_England_and_Wales" title="Attorney General for England and Wales">attorney-general</a> to have become prime minister.
</p><p>The younger son of an <a href="/wiki/Peerage_of_Ireland" title="Peerage of Ireland">Anglo-Irish earl</a>, Perceval was educated at <a href="/wiki/Harrow_School" title="Harrow School">Harrow School</a> and <a href="/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge" title="Trinity College, Cambridge">Trinity College, Cambridge</a>. He studied law at <a href="/wiki/Lincoln%27s_Inn" title="Lincoln&#39;s Inn">Lincoln's Inn</a>, practised as a <a href="/wiki/Barrister" title="Barrister">barrister</a> on the Midland circuit, and in 1796 became a <a href="/wiki/King%27s_Counsel" title="King&#39;s Counsel">King's Counsel</a>. He entered politics at age 33 as a <a href="/wiki/Member_of_Parliament_(United_Kingdom)" title="Member of Parliament (United Kingdom)">member of Parliament</a> (MP) for <a href="/wiki/Northampton" title="Northampton">Northampton</a>. A follower of <a href="/wiki/William_Pitt_the_Younger" title="William Pitt the Younger">William Pitt the Younger</a>, Perceval always described himself as a "friend of Mr. Pitt", rather than a Tory. Perceval was opposed to <a href="/wiki/Catholic_emancipation" title="Catholic emancipation">Catholic emancipation</a> and reform of <a href="/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom" title="Parliament of the United Kingdom">Parliament</a>; he supported <a href="/wiki/United_Kingdom_in_the_Napoleonic_Wars" title="United Kingdom in the Napoleonic Wars">the war against Napoleon</a> and the abolition of the <a href="/wiki/Atlantic_slave_trade" title="Atlantic slave trade">Atlantic slave trade</a>. He was opposed to hunting, gambling and adultery; he did not drink as much as most MPs at the time, gave generously to charity, and enjoyed spending time with his thirteen children.
</p><p>After a late entry into politics, his rise to power was rapid; he was appointed as <a href="/wiki/Solicitor_General_for_England_and_Wales" title="Solicitor General for England and Wales">Solicitor General</a> and then <a href="/wiki/Attorney_General_for_England_and_Wales" title="Attorney General for England and Wales">Attorney General for England and Wales</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Addington_ministry" title="Addington ministry">Addington ministry</a>, <a href="/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer" title="Chancellor of the Exchequer">Chancellor of the Exchequer</a> and <a href="/wiki/Leader_of_the_House_of_Commons" title="Leader of the House of Commons">Leader of the House of Commons</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Second_Portland_ministry" title="Second Portland ministry">second Portland ministry</a>, and then became prime minister in 1809. At the head of a weak government, Perceval faced a number of crises during his term in office, including an inquiry into the <a href="/wiki/Walcheren_Campaign" title="Walcheren Campaign">Walcheren expedition</a>, the mental illness and incapacity of <a href="/wiki/King_George_III" class="mw-redirect" title="King George III">King George III</a>, economic depression, and <a href="/wiki/Luddite" title="Luddite">Luddite</a> riots. He overcame those crises, successfully pursued the <a href="/wiki/Peninsular_War" title="Peninsular War">Peninsular War</a> in the face of opposition defeatism, and won the support of the <a href="/wiki/King_George_IV" class="mw-redirect" title="King George IV">Prince Regent</a>. His position was stronger by early 1812, when, in the lobby of the <a href="/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom" title="House of Commons of the United Kingdom">House of Commons</a>, he was assassinated by <a href="/wiki/John_Bellingham" title="John Bellingham">John Bellingham</a>, a merchant with a grievance against Perceval’s government. Bellingham was hanged one week later.
</p><p>Perceval had four older brothers who survived to adulthood. Through expiry of their <a href="/wiki/Male_primogeniture" class="mw-redirect" title="Male primogeniture">male-line, male heirs</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Earl_of_Egmont" title="Earl of Egmont">earldom of Egmont</a> passed to one of his great-grandsons in the early twentieth century and became extinct in 2011.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Childhood_and_education">Childhood and education</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Childhood and education">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:WrenCambridge.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/WrenCambridge.JPG/170px-WrenCambridge.JPG" decoding="async" width="170" height="128" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/WrenCambridge.JPG/255px-WrenCambridge.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/WrenCambridge.JPG/340px-WrenCambridge.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1496" data-file-height="1122" /></a><figcaption>Perceval studied at <a href="/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge" title="Trinity College, Cambridge">Trinity College, Cambridge</a> (pictured)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Perceval was born in <a href="/wiki/South_Audley_Street" title="South Audley Street">Audley Square</a>, <a href="/wiki/Mayfair" title="Mayfair">Mayfair</a>, London the seventh son of <a href="/wiki/John_Perceval,_2nd_Earl_of_Egmont" title="John Perceval, 2nd Earl of Egmont">John Perceval, 2nd Earl of Egmont</a>; he was the second son of the Earl's second marriage. His mother, Catherine Compton, <a href="/wiki/Earl_of_Egmont" title="Earl of Egmont">Baroness Arden</a>, was a granddaughter of the <a href="/wiki/George_Compton,_4th_Earl_of_Northampton" title="George Compton, 4th Earl of Northampton">4th Earl of Northampton</a>. Spencer was a Compton family name; Catherine Compton's great uncle <a href="/wiki/Spencer_Compton,_1st_Earl_of_Wilmington" title="Spencer Compton, 1st Earl of Wilmington">Spencer Compton, 1st Earl of Wilmington</a>, had been prime minister.<sup id="cite_ref-burke_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-burke-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His father, a political advisor to <a href="/wiki/Frederick,_Prince_of_Wales" title="Frederick, Prince of Wales">Frederick, Prince of Wales</a> and King George III, served briefly in the Cabinet as <a href="/wiki/British_Admiralty" class="mw-redirect" title="British Admiralty">First Lord of the Admiralty</a>.  Perceval's early childhood was spent at <a href="/wiki/Charlton,_London" title="Charlton, London">Charlton</a> House, which his father had taken to be near <a href="/wiki/Woolwich_Dockyard" title="Woolwich Dockyard">Woolwich Dockyard</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETreherne190919_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETreherne190919-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Perceval's father died when he was eight. Perceval went to <a href="/wiki/Harrow_School" title="Harrow School">Harrow School</a>, where he was a disciplined and hard-working pupil. It was at Harrow that he developed an interest in <a href="/wiki/Evangelical_Anglicanism" title="Evangelical Anglicanism">evangelical Anglicanism</a> and formed what was to be a lifelong friendship with <a href="/wiki/Dudley_Ryder,_1st_Earl_of_Harrowby" title="Dudley Ryder, 1st Earl of Harrowby">Dudley Ryder</a>. After five years at Harrow, he followed his older brother <a href="/wiki/Charles_Perceval,_2nd_Baron_Arden" title="Charles Perceval, 2nd Baron Arden">Charles</a> to <a href="/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge" title="Trinity College, Cambridge">Trinity College</a>, <a href="/wiki/University_of_Cambridge" title="University of Cambridge">Cambridge</a>. There he won the declamation prize in English and graduated in 1782.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legal_career_and_marriage">Legal career and marriage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legal career and marriage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Portrait_of_Mrs._Spencer_Perceval_by_Elisabeth-Louise_Vig%C3%A9e_Le_Brun.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Portrait_of_Mrs._Spencer_Perceval_by_Elisabeth-Louise_Vig%C3%A9e_Le_Brun.jpg/170px-Portrait_of_Mrs._Spencer_Perceval_by_Elisabeth-Louise_Vig%C3%A9e_Le_Brun.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="219" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Portrait_of_Mrs._Spencer_Perceval_by_Elisabeth-Louise_Vig%C3%A9e_Le_Brun.jpg/255px-Portrait_of_Mrs._Spencer_Perceval_by_Elisabeth-Louise_Vig%C3%A9e_Le_Brun.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Portrait_of_Mrs._Spencer_Perceval_by_Elisabeth-Louise_Vig%C3%A9e_Le_Brun.jpg/340px-Portrait_of_Mrs._Spencer_Perceval_by_Elisabeth-Louise_Vig%C3%A9e_Le_Brun.jpg 2x" data-file-width="750" data-file-height="965" /></a><figcaption>Jane Perceval (née Wilson) by <a href="/wiki/Vig%C3%A9e_Le_Brun" class="mw-redirect" title="Vigée Le Brun">Vigée Le Brun</a>, 1804</figcaption></figure>
<p>As the second son of a second marriage, and with an allowance of only £200 a year (equivalent to £28,000&#32;in 2021), Perceval faced the prospect of having to make his own way in life. (Under <a href="/wiki/Primogeniture" title="Primogeniture">primogeniture</a>, the first son inherited land and title.) He chose the law as a profession, studied at <a href="/wiki/Lincoln%27s_Inn" title="Lincoln&#39;s Inn">Lincoln's Inn</a>, and was called to the bar in 1786. Perceval's mother had died in 1783. Perceval and his brother Charles, now Lord Arden, rented a house in Charlton, where they fell in love with two sisters who were living in the Percevals' old childhood home. The sisters' father, <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Spencer_Wilson" title="Thomas Spencer Wilson">Sir Thomas Spencer Wilson</a>, approved of the match between his eldest daughter Margaretta and Lord Arden, who was wealthy and already a Member of Parliament and a Lord of the Admiralty. Perceval, who was at that time an impecunious barrister on the Midland Circuit, was told to wait until the younger daughter, Jane, came of age in three years' time. When Jane reached 21, in 1790, Perceval's career was still not prospering, and Sir Thomas still opposed the marriage.
</p><p>The couple eloped and married by special licence in <a href="/wiki/East_Grinstead" title="East Grinstead">East Grinstead</a>. They set up home together in lodgings over a carpet shop in Bedford Row,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEIremonger197047_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEIremonger197047-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> later moving to <a href="/wiki/Lindsey_House,_Lincoln%27s_Inn_Fields" title="Lindsey House, Lincoln&#39;s Inn Fields">Lindsey House</a>, <a href="/wiki/Lincoln%27s_Inn_Fields" title="Lincoln&#39;s Inn Fields">Lincoln's Inn Fields</a>. They had thirteen children together.
</p><p>Perceval's family connections obtained a number of positions for him: Deputy Recorder of Northampton, and commissioner of bankrupts in 1790; surveyor of the Maltings and clerk of the irons in the mint– a <a href="/wiki/Sinecure" title="Sinecure">sinecure</a> worth £119 a year&#160;– in 1791; and counsel to the Board of Admiralty in 1794. He acted as junior counsel for the Crown in the prosecutions of <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Paine" title="Thomas Paine">Thomas Paine</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Trial_in_absentia" title="Trial in absentia">in absentia</a></i> for <a href="/wiki/Seditious_libel" title="Seditious libel">seditious libel</a> (1792), and <a href="/wiki/John_Horne_Tooke" title="John Horne Tooke">John Horne Tooke</a> for high treason (1794). Perceval joined the London and Westminster Light Horse Volunteers in 1794 when the country was under threat of invasion by France and served with them until 1803.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThorne1986_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorne1986-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Perceval wrote anonymous pamphlets in favour of the <a href="/wiki/Impeachment_of_Warren_Hastings" title="Impeachment of Warren Hastings">impeachment of Warren Hastings</a>, and in defence of public order against sedition. These pamphlets brought him to the attention of <a href="/wiki/William_Pitt_the_Younger" title="William Pitt the Younger">William Pitt the Younger</a>, and in 1795 he was offered the appointment of <a href="/wiki/Chief_Secretary_for_Ireland" title="Chief Secretary for Ireland">Chief Secretary for Ireland</a>. He declined the offer.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThorne1986_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorne1986-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He could earn more as a barrister and needed the money to support his growing family. In 1796 he became a <a href="/wiki/King%27s_Counsel" title="King&#39;s Counsel">King's Counsel</a> and had an income of about £1,000 a year (equivalent to £105,000&#32;in 2021).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Perceval was 33 when he became a KC, making him one of the youngest ever.
</p>
<h2><span id="Early_political_career:_1796.E2.80.931801"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_political_career:_1796–1801">Early political career: 1796–1801</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Early political career: 1796–1801">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1796, Perceval's uncle, the <a href="/wiki/Spencer_Compton,_8th_Earl_of_Northampton" title="Spencer Compton, 8th Earl of Northampton">8th Earl of Northampton</a>, died. Perceval's cousin <a href="/wiki/Charles_Compton,_1st_Marquess_of_Northampton" title="Charles Compton, 1st Marquess of Northampton">Charles Compton</a>, who was MP for <a href="/wiki/Northampton_(UK_Parliament_constituency)" title="Northampton (UK Parliament constituency)">Northampton</a>, succeeded to the earldom and took his place in the <a href="/wiki/House_of_Lords" title="House of Lords">House of Lords</a>. Perceval was invited to stand for election in his place. In the May by-election he was elected unopposed, but weeks later had to defend his seat in a fiercely contested general election. Northampton had an electorate of about one thousand – every male householder not in receipt of poor relief had a vote – and the town had a strong radical tradition. Perceval stood for the <a href="/wiki/Castle_Ashby" title="Castle Ashby">Castle Ashby</a> interest, <a href="/wiki/Edward_Bouverie_(senior)" title="Edward Bouverie (senior)">Edward Bouverie</a> for the <a href="/wiki/Whigs_(British_political_party)" title="Whigs (British political party)">Whigs</a>, and William Walcot for the corporation. After a disputed count, Perceval and Bouverie were returned. Perceval represented Northampton until his death 16 years later, and is the only MP for Northampton to have held the office of prime minister. 1796 was his first and last contested election; in the general elections of 1802, 1806 and 1807, Perceval and Bouverie were returned unopposed.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray196333_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray196333-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Perceval took his seat in the House of Commons in September 1796, his political views were already formed. "He was for the constitution and Pitt; he was against <a href="/wiki/Charles_James_Fox" title="Charles James Fox">Fox</a> and France", wrote his biographer Denis Gray.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray196335_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray196335-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> During the 1796–1797 session, he made several speeches, always reading from notes. His public speaking skills had been sharpened at the Crown and Rolls debating society when he was a law student. After taking his seat in the House of Commons, Perceval continued with his legal practice, as MPs did not receive a salary, and the House only sat for a part of the year. During the Parliamentary recess of the summer of 1797, he was senior counsel for the Crown in the prosecution of John Binns for sedition. Binns, who was defended by <a href="/wiki/Samuel_Romilly" title="Samuel Romilly">Samuel Romilly</a>, was found not guilty. The fees from his legal practice allowed Perceval to take out a lease on a country house, <a href="/wiki/Belsize_House" title="Belsize House">Belsize House</a> in Hampstead.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It was during the next session of Parliament, in January 1798, that Perceval established his reputation as a debater&#160;– and his prospects as a future minister&#160;– with a speech in support of the Assessed Taxes Bill (a bill to increase the taxes on houses, windows, male servants, horses and carriages, in order to finance the war against France). He used the occasion to mount an attack on <a href="/wiki/Charles_James_Fox" title="Charles James Fox">Charles Fox</a> and his demands for reform. Pitt described the speech as one of the best he had ever heard, and later that year Perceval was appointed to the post of Solicitor to the Ordnance.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Solicitor_and_attorney_general:_1801.E2.80.931806"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Solicitor_and_attorney_general:_1801–1806">Solicitor and attorney general: 1801–1806</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Solicitor and attorney general: 1801–1806">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Pitt resigned in 1801 when the King and Cabinet opposed his bill for Catholic emancipation. As Perceval shared the King's views on Catholic emancipation, he did not feel obliged to follow Pitt into opposition. His career continued to prosper during Henry Addington's administration. He was appointed solicitor general in 1801 and attorney general the following year. Perceval did not agree with Addington's general policies (especially on foreign policy), and confined himself to speeches on legal issues. He was retained in the position of attorney general when Addington resigned, and Pitt formed his second ministry in 1804. As attorney general, Perceval was involved with the prosecution of radicals <a href="/wiki/Edward_Despard" title="Edward Despard">Edward Despard</a> and <a href="/wiki/William_Cobbett" title="William Cobbett">William Cobbett</a>, but was also responsible for more liberal decisions on trade unions, and for improving the conditions of convicts <a href="/wiki/Penal_transportation" title="Penal transportation">transported</a> to <a href="/wiki/New_South_Wales" title="New South Wales">New South Wales</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Pitt died, in January 1806, Perceval was an emblem bearer at his funeral. Although he had little money to spare (by now he had eleven children), he contributed £1,000 towards a fund to pay off Pitt's debts. He resigned as attorney general, refusing to serve in <a href="/wiki/William_Grenville,_1st_Baron_Grenville" title="William Grenville, 1st Baron Grenville">Lord Grenville</a>'s ministry of "all the talents", as it included Fox. Instead he became the leader of the Pittite opposition in the House of Commons.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During his period in opposition, Perceval used his legal skills to defend <a href="/wiki/Caroline_of_Brunswick" title="Caroline of Brunswick">Princess Caroline</a>, the estranged wife of the Prince of Wales, during the "<a href="/wiki/Delicate_investigation" class="mw-redirect" title="Delicate investigation">delicate investigation</a>". The Princess had been accused of giving birth to an illegitimate child, and the Prince of Wales ordered an inquiry, hoping to obtain evidence for a divorce. The government inquiry found that the main accusation was untrue (the child in question had been adopted by the Princess), but it was critical of the behaviour of the Princess. The opposition sprang to her defence and Perceval became her advisor, drafting a 156-page letter to King George III in her support. Known as <em><i>The Book</i></em>, it was described by Perceval's biographer as "the last and greatest production of his legal career".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray196383_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray196383-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> When the King refused to let Caroline return to court, Perceval threatened publication of <i>The Book</i>, but Grenville's ministry fell&#160;– again over a difference of opinion with the King on the Catholic question&#160;– before <i>The Book</i> could be distributed. As a member of the new government, Perceval drafted a cabinet minute acquitting Caroline on all charges and recommending her return to court. He had a bonfire of <i>The Book</i> at Lindsey House, and large sums of government money were spent on buying back stray copies. A few remained at large and <i>The Book</i> was published soon after his death.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer:_1807.E2.80.931809"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer:_1807–1809">Chancellor of the Exchequer: 1807–1809</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Chancellor of the Exchequer: 1807–1809">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Appointment">Appointment</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Appointment">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Microcosm_of_London_Plate_021_-_House_of_Commons_(colour).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Microcosm_of_London_Plate_021_-_House_of_Commons_%28colour%29.jpg/220px-Microcosm_of_London_Plate_021_-_House_of_Commons_%28colour%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Microcosm_of_London_Plate_021_-_House_of_Commons_%28colour%29.jpg/330px-Microcosm_of_London_Plate_021_-_House_of_Commons_%28colour%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Microcosm_of_London_Plate_021_-_House_of_Commons_%28colour%29.jpg/440px-Microcosm_of_London_Plate_021_-_House_of_Commons_%28colour%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1792" data-file-height="1340" /></a><figcaption>Engraving of the British House of Commons, 1808</figcaption></figure>
<p>On the resignation of Grenville, the Duke of Portland put together a ministry of Pittites and asked Perceval to become Chancellor of the Exchequer and Leader of the House of Commons. Perceval would have preferred to remain attorney general or become <a href="/wiki/Home_Secretary" title="Home Secretary">Home Secretary</a>, and pleaded ignorance of financial affairs. He agreed to take the position when the salary (smaller than that of the Home Office) was augmented by the <a href="/wiki/Duchy_of_Lancaster" title="Duchy of Lancaster">Duchy of Lancaster</a>. <a href="/wiki/Robert_Jenkinson,_2nd_Earl_of_Liverpool" title="Robert Jenkinson, 2nd Earl of Liverpool">Lord Hawkesbury</a> (later Liverpool) recommended Perceval to the King by explaining that he came from an old English family and shared the King's views on the Catholic question.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Perceval's youngest child, Ernest Augustus, was born soon after Perceval became chancellor (Princess Caroline was godmother). Jane Perceval became ill after the birth and the family moved out of the damp and draughty Belsize House, spending a few months in <a href="/wiki/John_Shore,_1st_Baron_Teignmouth" title="John Shore, 1st Baron Teignmouth">Lord Teignmouth</a>'s house in <a href="/wiki/Clapham" title="Clapham">Clapham</a> before finding a suitable country house in <a href="/wiki/Ealing" title="Ealing">Ealing</a>. Elm Grove was a 16th-century house that had been the home of the Bishop of Durham;<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENeaves1971_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENeaves1971-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Perceval paid £7,500 for it in 1808 (borrowing from his brother Lord Arden and the trustees of Jane's dowry), and the Perceval family's long association with Ealing began. Meanwhile, in town, Perceval had moved from Lindsey House into <a href="/wiki/10_Downing_Street" title="10 Downing Street">10 Downing Street</a>, when the Duke of Portland moved back to <a href="/wiki/Burlington_House" title="Burlington House">Burlington House</a> shortly after becoming prime minister.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Trade.2C_taxation_and_financial_reform"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Trade,_taxation_and_financial_reform">Trade, taxation and financial reform</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Trade, taxation and financial reform">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>One of Perceval's first tasks in Cabinet was to expand the <a href="/wiki/Orders_in_Council_(1807)" title="Orders in Council (1807)">Orders in Council</a> that had been brought in by the previous administration and were designed to restrict the trade of neutral countries with France, in retaliation to Napoleon's embargo on British trade. He was also responsible for ensuring that <a href="/wiki/William_Wilberforce" title="William Wilberforce">Wilberforce</a>'s bill on the abolition of the slave trade, which had still not passed its final stages in the House of Lords when Grenville's ministry fell, would not "fall between the two ministries" and be rejected in a snap division.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963100_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963100-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Perceval was one of the founding members of the African Institute, which was set up in April 1807 to safeguard the Abolition of the Slave Trade Act.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As Chancellor of the Exchequer, Perceval had to raise money to finance the war against Napoleon. This he managed to do in his budgets of 1808 and 1809 without increasing taxes, by raising loans at reasonable rates and making economies. As leader of the House of Commons, he had to deal with a strong opposition, which challenged the government over the conduct of the war, Catholic emancipation, corruption, and Parliamentary reform. Perceval successfully defended the commander-in-chief of the army, the <a href="/wiki/Prince_Frederick,_Duke_of_York_and_Albany" title="Prince Frederick, Duke of York and Albany">Duke of York</a>, against charges of corruption when the Duke's ex-mistress Mary Anne Clarke claimed to have sold army commissions with his knowledge. Although Parliament voted to acquit the Duke of the main charge, his conduct was criticised, and he accepted Perceval's advice to resign. (He was re-instated in 1811).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Prime_Minister:_1809-1812">Prime Minister: 1809-1812</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Prime Minister: 1809-1812">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Fall_of_Portland_and_appointment">Fall of Portland and appointment</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Fall of Portland and appointment">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Portland's ministry contained three future prime-ministers&#160;– Perceval, <a href="/wiki/Robert_Jenkinson,_2nd_Earl_of_Liverpool" title="Robert Jenkinson, 2nd Earl of Liverpool">Lord Hawkesbury</a> and <a href="/wiki/George_Canning" title="George Canning">George Canning</a>&#160;– as well as another two of the 19th-century's great statesmen: <a href="/wiki/John_Scott,_1st_Earl_of_Eldon" title="John Scott, 1st Earl of Eldon">Lord Eldon</a> and <a href="/wiki/Robert_Stewart,_Viscount_Castlereagh" title="Robert Stewart, Viscount Castlereagh">Lord Castlereagh</a>. But Portland was not a strong leader and his health was failing. The country was plunged into political crisis in the summer of 1809 as Canning schemed against Castlereagh, and the Duke of Portland resigned following a stroke. Negotiations began to find a new prime minister: Canning wanted to be either prime minister or nothing, Perceval was prepared to serve under a third person, but not Canning. The remnants of the cabinet decided to invite <a href="/wiki/Charles_Grey,_2nd_Earl_Grey" title="Charles Grey, 2nd Earl Grey">Lord Grey</a> and Lord Grenville to form "an extended and combined administration"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963236_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963236-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> in which Perceval was hoping for the home secretaryship. But Grenville and Grey refused to enter into negotiations, and the King accepted the Cabinet's recommendation of Perceval for his new prime minister.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Perceval kissed the King's hands on 4 October and set about forming his Cabinet, a task made more difficult by the fact that Castlereagh and Canning had ruled themselves out of consideration by fighting a duel (which Perceval had tried to prevent). Having received five refusals for the office, he had to serve as his own Chancellor of the Exchequer&#160;– characteristically declining to accept the salary.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Government_and_Regency">Government and Regency <span class="anchor" id="Premiership"></span></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Government and Regency">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Perceval_ministry" title="Perceval ministry">Perceval ministry</a></div>
<p>The new ministry was not expected to last. It was especially weak in the Commons, where Perceval had only one cabinet member– Home Secretary <a href="/wiki/Richard_Ryder_(politician,_born_1766)" title="Richard Ryder (politician, born 1766)">Richard Ryder</a>&#160;– and had to rely on the support of backbenchers in debate. In the first week of the new Parliamentary session in January 1810 the government lost four divisions, one on a motion for an inquiry into the <a href="/wiki/Walcheren_Expedition" class="mw-redirect" title="Walcheren Expedition">Walcheren Expedition</a> (in which, the previous summer, a military force intending to seize <a href="/wiki/Antwerp" title="Antwerp">Antwerp</a> had instead withdrawn after losing many men to an epidemic on the island of Walcheren off the Dutch coast) and three on the composition of the finance committee. The government survived the inquiry into the Walcheren Expedition at the cost of the resignation of the expedition's leader <a href="/wiki/John_Pitt,_2nd_Earl_of_Chatham" title="John Pitt, 2nd Earl of Chatham">Lord Chatham</a>. The radical MP <a href="/wiki/Francis_Burdett" title="Francis Burdett">Sir Francis Burdett</a> was committed to the <a href="/wiki/Tower_of_London" title="Tower of London">Tower of London</a> for having published a letter in William Cobbett's <i><a href="/wiki/Political_Register" title="Political Register">Political Register</a></i> denouncing the government's exclusion of the press from the inquiry. It took three days, owing to various blunders, to execute the warrant for Burdett's arrest. The mob took to the streets in support of Burdett, troops were called out, and there were fatal casualties. As Chancellor, Perceval continued to find the funds to finance <a href="/wiki/Arthur_Wellesley,_1st_Duke_of_Wellington" title="Arthur Wellesley, 1st Duke of Wellington">Wellington</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Peninsular_War" title="Peninsular War">campaign in the Iberian Peninsula</a>, whilst contracting a lower debt than his predecessors or successors.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Bodleian_Libraries,_Sketch_for_a_prime_minister_or-_how_to_purchase_a_peace.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Bodleian_Libraries%2C_Sketch_for_a_prime_minister_or-_how_to_purchase_a_peace.jpg/170px-Bodleian_Libraries%2C_Sketch_for_a_prime_minister_or-_how_to_purchase_a_peace.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="237" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Bodleian_Libraries%2C_Sketch_for_a_prime_minister_or-_how_to_purchase_a_peace.jpg/255px-Bodleian_Libraries%2C_Sketch_for_a_prime_minister_or-_how_to_purchase_a_peace.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Bodleian_Libraries%2C_Sketch_for_a_prime_minister_or-_how_to_purchase_a_peace.jpg/340px-Bodleian_Libraries%2C_Sketch_for_a_prime_minister_or-_how_to_purchase_a_peace.jpg 2x" data-file-width="716" data-file-height="1000" /></a><figcaption><i>Sketch for a Prime Minister</i> by <a href="/wiki/Samuel_De_Wilde" title="Samuel De Wilde">Samuel De Wilde</a> in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Satirist" title="The Satirist">The Satirist</a></i>, 1811</figcaption></figure>
<p>King George III had celebrated his Golden Jubilee in 1809; by the following autumn he was showing signs of a return of the illness that had led to the threat of a Regency in 1788. The prospect of a Regency was not attractive to Perceval, as the Prince of Wales was known to favour Whigs and disliked Perceval for the part he had played in the "delicate investigation". Twice Parliament was adjourned in November 1810, as doctors gave optimistic reports about the King's chances of a return to health. In December select committees of the Lords and Commons heard evidence from the doctors, and Perceval finally wrote to the Prince of Wales on 19 December saying that he planned the next day to introduce a regency bill. As with Pitt's bill in 1788, there would be restrictions: the regent's powers to create peers and award offices and pensions would be restricted for 12 months, <a href="/wiki/Charlotte_of_Mecklenburg-Strelitz" title="Charlotte of Mecklenburg-Strelitz">the Queen</a> would be responsible for the care of the King, and the King's private property would be looked after by trustees.
</p><p>The Prince of Wales, supported by the Opposition, objected to the restrictions, but Perceval steered the bill through Parliament. Everyone had expected the Regent to change his ministers but, surprisingly, he chose to retain his old enemy Perceval. The official reason given by the Regent was that he did not wish to do anything to aggravate his father's illness. The King <a href="/wiki/Royal_assent" title="Royal assent">assented</a> to the Regency Bill on 5 February, the Regent took the royal oath the following day and Parliament formally opened for the 1811 session. The session was largely taken up with problems in Ireland, economic depression and the bullion controversy in England (a bill was passed to make bank notes legal tender), and military operations in the peninsula.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When the restrictions on the Regency expired in February 1812, the King still showed no signs of recovery, and the Prince Regent decided, after an unsuccessful attempt to persuade Grey and Grenville to join the government, to retain Perceval and his ministers. <a href="/wiki/Richard_Wellesley,_1st_Marquess_Wellesley" title="Richard Wellesley, 1st Marquess Wellesley">Richard Wellesley, 1st Marquess Wellesley</a>, after intrigues with the Prince Regent, resigned as foreign secretary and was replaced by Castlereagh. The opposition meanwhile was mounting an attack on the Orders in Council, which had caused a crisis in relations with America and were widely blamed for depression and unemployment in England. Rioting had broken out in the Midlands and North, and been harshly repressed. <a href="/wiki/Henry_Brougham,_1st_Baron_Brougham_and_Vaux" title="Henry Brougham, 1st Baron Brougham and Vaux">Henry Brougham</a>'s motion for a select committee was defeated in the Commons, but, under continuing pressure from manufacturers, the government agreed to set up a Committee of the Whole House to consider the Orders in Council and their impact on trade and manufacture. The committee began its examination of witnesses in early May 1812.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Assassination">Assassination</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Assassination">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Assassination_of_Spencer_Perceval" title="Assassination of Spencer Perceval">Assassination of Spencer Perceval</a></div>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Murder_of_Spencer_Perceval.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="19th-century stipple engraving" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/Murder_of_Spencer_Perceval.jpg/220px-Murder_of_Spencer_Perceval.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/Murder_of_Spencer_Perceval.jpg/330px-Murder_of_Spencer_Perceval.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/Murder_of_Spencer_Perceval.jpg/440px-Murder_of_Spencer_Perceval.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1024" data-file-height="768" /></a><figcaption>Artist's impression of Perceval's assassination, 11 May 1812</figcaption></figure>
<p>At 5:15&#160;pm, on the evening of 11 May 1812, Perceval was on his way to attend the inquiry into the Orders in Council. As he entered the lobby of the House of Commons, a man stepped forward, drew a pistol and shot him in the chest. Perceval fell to the floor, after uttering something that was variously heard as "murder" and "oh my God".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGillen1972_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGillen1972-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> They were his last words. By the time he had been carried into an adjoining room and propped up on a table with his feet on two chairs, he was senseless, although there was still a faint pulse. When a surgeon arrived a few minutes later, the pulse had stopped, and Perceval was declared dead.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At first it was feared that the shot might signal the start of an uprising, but it soon became apparent that the assassin&#160;– who had made no attempt to escape&#160;– was a man with an obsessive grievance against the Government and had acted alone. The assassin, <a href="/wiki/John_Bellingham" title="John Bellingham">John Bellingham</a>, was a merchant who believed he had been unjustly imprisoned in Russia and was entitled to compensation from the government, but all his petitions had been rejected.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGillen1972_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGillen1972-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Perceval's body was laid on a sofa in the speaker's drawing room and removed to Number 10 in the early hours of 12 May. That same morning an inquest was held at the Cat and Bagpipes public house on the corner of Downing Street and a verdict of wilful murder was returned.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963_6-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Perceval left a widow and twelve children aged between three and twenty, and there were soon rumours that he had not left them well provided for. He had just £106 5s 1d in the bank when he died.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolithoPeel1967_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolithoPeel1967-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> A few days after his death, Parliament voted to settle £50,000 on Perceval's children, with additional annuities for his widow and eldest son.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGillen197229_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGillen197229-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Jane Perceval married Lieutenant-Colonel <a href="/wiki/Henry_William_Carr" title="Henry William Carr">Sir Henry Carr</a>, brother of the Reverend <a href="/wiki/Robert_James_Carr" class="mw-redirect" title="Robert James Carr">Robert James Carr</a>, then Vicar of Brighton, in 1815 and was widowed again six years later. She died aged 74 in 1844.
</p><p>Perceval was buried on 16 May 1812 in the Egmont vault at <a href="/wiki/St_Luke%27s_Church,_Charlton" title="St Luke&#39;s Church, Charlton">St Luke's Church, Charlton</a>, London. At his widow's request, it was a private funeral. Lord Eldon, Lord Liverpool, <a href="/wiki/Dudley_Ryder,_1st_Earl_of_Harrowby" title="Dudley Ryder, 1st Earl of Harrowby">Lord Harrowby</a> and Richard Ryder were the pall-bearers. The previous day, Bellingham had been tried, and, refusing to enter a plea of insanity, was found guilty and sentenced to death. He was executed by <a href="/wiki/Hanging" title="Hanging">hanging</a> on 18 May.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGillen1972_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGillen1972-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Perceval was a small, slight, and very pale man, who usually dressed in black. Lord Eldon called him "Little P".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEIremonger197043_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEIremonger197043-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> He never sat for a full-sized portrait; likenesses are either miniatures or are based on a death mask by <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Nollekens" title="Joseph Nollekens">Joseph Nollekens</a>. Perceval was the last British prime minister to wear a <a href="/wiki/Wig" title="Wig">powdered wig tied in a queue</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Culottes" title="Culottes">knee-breeches</a> according to the <a href="/wiki/1775%E2%80%9395_in_Western_fashion#Men&#39;s_fashion" class="mw-redirect" title="1775–95 in Western fashion">old-fashioned style of the 18th century</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEIremonger1970_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEIremonger1970-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> He is sometimes referred to as one of Britain's forgotten prime ministers, remembered only for the manner of his death.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEIremonger197043_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEIremonger197043-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Although not considered an inspirational leader, he is generally seen as a devout, industrious, principled man who at the head of a weak government steered the country through difficult times. A contemporary MP <a href="/wiki/Henry_Grattan" title="Henry Grattan">Henry Grattan</a>, used a naval analogy to describe Perceval: "He is not a ship-of-the-line, but he carries many guns, is tight-built and is out in all weathers".<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Perceval's modern biographer, Denis Gray, described him as "a herald of the Victorians".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray1963121_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray1963121-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Memorial_to_death_of_Spencer_Perceval,_Westminster_Abbey.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/Memorial_to_death_of_Spencer_Perceval%2C_Westminster_Abbey.jpg/220px-Memorial_to_death_of_Spencer_Perceval%2C_Westminster_Abbey.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="124" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/Memorial_to_death_of_Spencer_Perceval%2C_Westminster_Abbey.jpg/330px-Memorial_to_death_of_Spencer_Perceval%2C_Westminster_Abbey.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/Memorial_to_death_of_Spencer_Perceval%2C_Westminster_Abbey.jpg/440px-Memorial_to_death_of_Spencer_Perceval%2C_Westminster_Abbey.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2747" data-file-height="1543" /></a><figcaption>Memorial to Perceval in Westminster Abbey</figcaption></figure>
<p>Perceval was mourned by many; Lord Chief Justice <a href="/wiki/James_Mansfield" title="James Mansfield">Sir James Mansfield</a> wept during his summing up to the jury at Bellingham's trial.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGillen1972_13-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGillen1972-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> However, in some quarters he was unpopular and in <a href="/wiki/Nottingham" title="Nottingham">Nottingham</a> the crowds that gathered following his assassination were in a more cheerful mood.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGillen197215_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGillen197215-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Public monuments to Perceval were erected in Northampton, Lincoln's Inn and in <a href="/wiki/Westminster_Abbey" title="Westminster Abbey">Westminster Abbey</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> The memorial in Westminster Abbey, by the sculptor <a href="/wiki/Richard_Westmacott" title="Richard Westmacott">Richard Westmacott</a>, has an effigy of the dead Perceval with mourning figures representing Truth, Temperance and Power in front of a relief depicting the aftermath of the assassination in the House of Commons.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> Four biographies have been published: a book on his life and administration by Charles Verulam Williams, which appeared soon after his death; his grandson Spencer Walpole's biography in 1894; Philip Treherne's short biography in 1909; Denis Gray's 500-page political biography in 1963. In addition, there are three books about his assassination, one by <a href="/wiki/Mollie_Gillen" title="Mollie Gillen">Mollie Gillen</a>, one by David Hanrahan, and the latest by <a href="/wiki/Andro_Linklater" title="Andro Linklater">Andro Linklater</a> entitled <i>Why Spencer Perceval Had to Die</i>.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Spencer_Perceval_622.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6e/Spencer_Perceval_622.jpg/170px-Spencer_Perceval_622.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="250" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6e/Spencer_Perceval_622.jpg/255px-Spencer_Perceval_622.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6e/Spencer_Perceval_622.jpg/340px-Spencer_Perceval_622.jpg 2x" data-file-width="448" data-file-height="659" /></a><figcaption>Marble bust (based on Perceval's <a href="/wiki/Death_mask" title="Death mask">death mask</a> by <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Nollekens" title="Joseph Nollekens">Joseph Nollekens</a>) at <a href="/wiki/Pitzhanger_Manor" title="Pitzhanger Manor">Pitzhanger Manor</a>, London</figcaption></figure>
<p>Perceval's assassination inspired poems such as <i>Universal sympathy on the martyr'd statesman</i> (1812):
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><div class="poem">
<p>Such was his private, such his public life,<br />
That all who differ'd in polemic strife,<br />
Or varied in opinion with his plan,<br />
Agreed with one accord to love the man.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray196327_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray196327-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
</div></blockquote>
<p>One of Perceval's most noted critics, especially on the question of Catholic emancipation, was the cleric <a href="/wiki/Sydney_Smith" title="Sydney Smith">Sydney Smith</a>. In <i>Peter Plymley's Letters</i> Smith writes:
</p>
<blockquote><p>If I lived at Hampstead upon stewed meats and claret; if I walked to church every Sunday before eleven young gentlemen of my own begetting, with their faces washed, and their hair pleasingly combed; if the Almighty had blessed me with every earthly comfort&#8211;how awfully would I pause before I sent forth the flame and the sword over the cabins of the poor, brave, generous, open-hearted peasants of Ireland!</p></blockquote>
<p>American historian <a href="/wiki/Henry_Adams" title="Henry Adams">Henry Adams</a> suggested that it was this picture of Perceval that stayed in the minds of Liberals for a whole generation.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAdams1986966_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAdams1986966-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In July 2014, a memorial plaque was unveiled in St Stephen's Hall of the Houses of Parliament, close to where he was killed. The plaque had been proposed by <a href="/wiki/Michael_Ellis_(British_politician)" title="Michael Ellis (British politician)">Michael Ellis</a>, <a href="/wiki/Conservative_Party_(UK)" title="Conservative Party (UK)">Conservative</a> MP for <a href="/wiki/Northampton_North_(UK_Parliament_constituency)" title="Northampton North (UK Parliament constituency)">Northampton North</a> (parts of which Perceval once represented).<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In streets in <a href="/wiki/Northampton" title="Northampton">Northampton</a> and <a href="/wiki/Northamptonshire" title="Northamptonshire">Northamptonshire</a> his name is memorialised as it is by the main streets set back behind two sides of <a href="/wiki/Northampton_Square" title="Northampton Square">Northampton Square</a>, <a href="/wiki/London" title="London">London</a>: Spencer and Percival Streets.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Family">Family</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Spencer_Perceval&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Family">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Spencer and Jane Perceval had thirteen children, of whom twelve survived to adulthood.<sup id="cite_ref-burke_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-burke-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Four of the daughters never married, and lived together all their lives. During their mother's life, they lived with her in Elm Grove, <a href="/wiki/Ealing" title="Ealing">Ealing</a>;<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> after her death, the sisters moved to nearby <a href="/wiki/Pitzhanger_Manor" title="Pitzhanger Manor">Pitzhanger Manor House</a>, while their brother Spencer took over Elm Grove. <a href="/wiki/Cousin_marriage" title="Cousin marriage">Cousin marriage</a> was common: the remaining two daughters and two of the sons took this path.
</p>
<ol><li>Jane (1791–1824) married her cousin Edward Perceval, son of Lord Arden, in 1821 and lived in <a href="/wiki/Felpham" title="Felpham">Felpham</a>, Sussex. She died three years after marrying, apparently in childbirth.</li>
<li>Frances (1792–1877) lived with three unmarried sisters.</li>
<li>Maria (1794–1877) lived with her three unmarried sisters.</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Spencer_Perceval_(junior)" title="Spencer Perceval (junior)">Spencer</a> (1795–1859) was, like his father, educated at Harrow and Trinity College, Cambridge. After Perceval's assassination, Spencer junior was voted an annuity of £1000 (equivalent to £71,000&#32;in 2021), free legal training at Lincoln's Inn and a tellership of the Exchequer, all of which left him financially secure. He became a Member of Parliament at the age of 22 and in 1821 married Anna, a daughter of the chief of the <a href="/wiki/Clan_Macleod" class="mw-redirect" title="Clan Macleod">clan Macleod</a>, with whom he had eleven children. He joined the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Apostolic_Church" title="Catholic Apostolic Church">Catholic Apostolic Church</a> and was created an apostle in 1833. He served as a <a href="/wiki/Commissioners_in_Lunacy" title="Commissioners in Lunacy">metropolitan lunacy commissioner</a>.</li>
<li>Charles (born and died 1796)</li>
<li>Frederick James (1797–1861) was the only one of Perceval's sons not to go to Harrow. Due to his fragile health he was sent to school at <a href="/wiki/Rottingdean" title="Rottingdean">Rottingdean</a>. He married for the first time in 1827, spent some time in <a href="/wiki/Ghent" title="Ghent">Ghent</a>, Belgium, was a director of the <a href="/wiki/Clerical_Medical" title="Clerical Medical">Clerical, Medical and General Life Assurance Society</a> and a justice of the peace for Middlesex and for Kent, but generally led a quiet and retired life. Widowed in 1843, he married for the second time the following year. A grandson, Frederick Joseph Trevelyan Perceval, who was a Canadian rancher, became the 10th <i>de jure</i> <a href="/wiki/Earl_of_Egmont" title="Earl of Egmont">Earl of Egmont</a> (he did not claim the title) and was the father of the 11th earl.<sup id="cite_ref-burke_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-burke-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Rev. Henry (1799–1885) was educated at Harrow, where he was the only Perceval to become head of school. He went to <a href="/wiki/Brasenose_College,_Oxford" title="Brasenose College, Oxford">Brasenose College, Oxford</a>. In 1826 he married his cousin Catherine Drummond. For 46 years Henry was the rector of <a href="/wiki/Elmley_Lovett" title="Elmley Lovett">Elmley Lovett</a> in Worcestershire.</li>
<li>Dudley Montague (1800–1856) was educated at Harrow and <a href="/wiki/Christ_Church,_Oxford" title="Christ Church, Oxford">Christ Church, Oxford</a>. Like his brother Spencer, he was given free legal training at Lincoln's Inn but was not called to the bar. He spent two years as an administrator at the Cape of Good Hope, where he married a daughter of Gen. Sir <a href="/wiki/Richard_Bourke" title="Richard Bourke">Richard Bourke</a>, future <a href="/wiki/Governor_of_New_South_Wales" title="Governor of New South Wales">Governor of New South Wales</a>, in 1827. Back in England he obtained a treasury post and defended his father's reputation after it was attacked in <a href="/wiki/Charles_James_Napier" title="Charles James Napier">Napier's</a> history of the Peninsular War. In 1853 he stood unsuccessfully against <a href="/wiki/William_Ewart_Gladstone" title="William Ewart Gladstone">William Gladstone</a> in the election for an MP to represent <a href="/wiki/Oxford_University_(UK_Parliament_constituency)" title="Oxford University (UK Parliament constituency)">Oxford University</a>.</li>
<li>Isabella (1801–1886) married her cousin <a href="/wiki/Spencer_Horatio_Walpole" title="Spencer Horatio Walpole">Spencer Horatio Walpole</a> in 1835 and was the only one of Perceval's daughters to have children. Her husband was a lawyer who became an MP in 1846 and served as Home Secretary. They lived in the Hall on Ealing Green, next-door to Isabella's four unmarried sisters.</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/John_Thomas_Perceval" title="John Thomas Perceval">John Thomas</a> (1803–1876) was educated at Harrow. After a three-year career as an officer in the <a href="/wiki/Grenadier_Guards" title="Grenadier Guards">Grenadier Guards</a> and a term at Oxford University, he spent three years in asylums and became a campaigner for reform of the Lunacy Laws. In 1832, just after his release from an asylum, he married a cheesemonger's daughter.</li>
<li>Louisa (1804–1891) lived with her three unmarried sisters.</li>
<li>Frederica (1805–1900) lived with her three unmarried sisters. In her will she left money to build All Saints Church, Ealing, in memory of her father (he was born on All Saints Day). It is also known as the Spencer Perceval Memorial Church.</li>
<li>Ernest Augustus (1807–1896) was educated at Harrow. He spent nine years in the 15th Hussars, seven of them as a captain. In 1830, he married his cousin Beatrice Trevelyan, daughter of Sir John Trevelyan, 5th Baronet. The couple settled in Somerset and raised a large family, including antiquary <a href="/wiki/Spencer_George_Perceval" title="Spencer George Perceval">Spencer George Perceval</a>. Ernest served as private secretary to the Home Office on three occasions.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGault2010_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGault2010-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup></li></ol>
