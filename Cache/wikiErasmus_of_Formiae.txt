<p><b>Erasmus of Formia</b>, also known as <b>Saint Elmo</b> (died <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;303</span>), was a <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyrs" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian martyrs">martyr</a>. He is venerated as the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Sailors" class="mw-redirect" title="Sailors">sailors</a> and <a href="/wiki/Abdominal_pain" title="Abdominal pain">abdominal pain</a>. Erasmus or Elmo is also one of the <a href="/wiki/Fourteen_Holy_Helpers" title="Fourteen Holy Helpers">Fourteen Holy Helpers</a>, saintly figures of Christian tradition who are venerated especially as <a href="/wiki/Intercession" title="Intercession">intercessors</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Documentation_of_his_life">Documentation of his life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Erasmus_of_Formia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Documentation of his life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <i>Acts of Saint Elmo</i> were partly compiled from legends that confuse him with a Syrian bishop Erasmus of Antioch. <a href="/wiki/Jacobus_de_Voragine" title="Jacobus de Voragine">Jacobus de Voragine</a> in the <i><a href="/wiki/Golden_Legend" title="Golden Legend">Golden Legend</a></i> credited him as a bishop at <a href="/wiki/Formia" title="Formia">Formia</a> over all the Italian <a href="/wiki/Campania" title="Campania">Campania</a>, as a hermit on <a href="/wiki/Mount_Lebanon" title="Mount Lebanon">Mount Lebanon</a>, and a martyr in the <a href="/wiki/Diocletianic_Persecution" title="Diocletianic Persecution">Diocletianic Persecution</a>. There appears to be no historical basis for his <i>passio</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-Lanzi_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lanzi-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Account_of_life_and_martyrdom">Account of life and martyrdom</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Erasmus_of_Formia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Account of life and martyrdom">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Erasmus was Bishop of Formia, Italy. During the persecution against Christians under the emperors <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a> (284–305) and <a href="/wiki/Maximian" title="Maximian">Maximian Hercules</a> (286–305), he left his diocese and went to Mount Libanus, where he hid for seven years. However, an <a href="/wiki/Angel" title="Angel">angel</a> is said to have appeared to him, and counseled him to return to his city.<sup id="cite_ref-oca_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On the way, he encountered some soldiers who questioned him. Erasmus admitted that he was a Christian and they brought him to trial at Antioch before the emperor Diocletian. After suffering terrible tortures, he was bound with chains and thrown into prison, but an angel appeared and helped him escape.<sup id="cite_ref-oca_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He passed through <a href="/wiki/Lycia" title="Lycia">Lycia</a>, where he raised up the son of an illustrious citizen. This resulted in a number of baptisms, which drew the attention of the <a href="/wiki/Western_Roman_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Western Roman Emperor">Western Roman Emperor</a> Maximian who, according to Voragine, was "much worse than was Diocletian." Maximian ordered his arrest and Erasmus continued to confess his faith. They forced him to go to a temple of the idol, but along Erasmus's route all the idols fell and were destroyed, and from the temple there came fire which fell upon many of the pagans.<sup id="cite_ref-oca_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oca-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>These actions angered the emperor, who had Erasmus enclosed in a barrel full of protruding spikes and rolled down a hill. An angel healed him from these wounds.
</p><p>When he was recaptured, he was brought before the emperor and beaten and whipped, then coated with pitch and set alight (as Christians had been in <a href="/wiki/Nero" title="Nero">Nero</a>'s games), and still he survived. Thrown into prison with the intention of letting him die of starvation, Erasmus managed to escape.
</p><p>He was recaptured and tortured in the Roman province of <a href="/wiki/Illyria" title="Illyria">Illyricum</a>, after boldly preaching and converting numerous pagans to Christianity. Finally, according to this version of his death, his abdomen was slit open and his intestines wound around a <a href="/wiki/Windlass" title="Windlass">windlass</a>. This version may have developed from interpreting an icon that showed him with a windlass, signifying his patronage of sailors.<sup id="cite_ref-columbia_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-columbia-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration_and_patronage">Veneration and patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Erasmus_of_Formia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration and patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Erasmus may have become the patron of sailors because he is said to have continued preaching even after a thunderbolt struck the ground beside him. This prompted sailors, who were in danger from sudden storms and lightning, to claim his prayers. The electrical discharges at the mastheads of ships were read as a sign of his protection and came to be called "<a href="/wiki/St._Elmo%27s_Fire" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Elmo&#39;s Fire">Saint Elmo's Fire</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Gregory_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Gregory the Great">Pope Gregory the Great</a> recorded in the 6th century that the relics of Erasmus were preserved in the cathedral of Formia. When the old <i><a href="/wiki/Formia" title="Formia">Formiae</a></i> was razed by the <a href="/wiki/Saracen" title="Saracen">Saracens</a> in 842, the cult of Erasmus was moved to <a href="/wiki/Gaeta" title="Gaeta">Gaeta</a>. He is currently the patron of Gaeta, <a href="/wiki/Santeramo_in_Colle" title="Santeramo in Colle">Santeramo in Colle</a> and <a href="/wiki/Formia" title="Formia">Formia</a>.
</p><p>There is an altar to Erasmus in the north transept of St. Peter's Basilica.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> A copy of Nicolas Poussin's <i>Martyrdom of St Erasmus</i> serves as the <a href="/wiki/Altarpiece" title="Altarpiece">altarpiece</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-columbia_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-columbia-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Besides his patronage of <a href="/wiki/Sailors" class="mw-redirect" title="Sailors">mariners</a>, Erasmus is invoked against <a href="/wiki/Colic" title="Colic">colic</a> in children, abdominal pain, intestinal ailments and diseases, cramps and the pain of women in labour, as well as cattle pests.
</p>
