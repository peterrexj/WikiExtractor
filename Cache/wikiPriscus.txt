<p><b>Priscus of Panium</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;p&#39; in &#39;pie&#39;">p</span><span title="&#39;r&#39; in &#39;rye&#39;">r</span><span title="/ɪ/: &#39;i&#39; in &#39;kit&#39;">ɪ</span><span title="&#39;s&#39; in &#39;sigh&#39;">s</span><span title="&#39;k&#39; in &#39;kind&#39;">k</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;s&#39; in &#39;sigh&#39;">s</span></span>/</a></span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Πρίσκος</span>; 410s AD/420s AD-after 472 AD) was a 5th-century <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Roman_Empire" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Roman Empire">Eastern Roman</a> diplomat and <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Greeks" title="Byzantine Greeks">Greek</a> historian and rhetorician (or <a href="/wiki/Sophist_(dialogue)" title="Sophist (dialogue)">sophist</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Chisholm_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Chisholm-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Byzantine_History_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Byzantine_History-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Priscus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Priscus was born in <a href="/wiki/Panion" title="Panion">Panion</a> (located in <a href="/wiki/Thrace" title="Thrace">Thrace</a>) between 410 and 420 AD.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Chisholm_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Chisholm-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> In 448/449 AD, he accompanied <a href="/wiki/Maximinus_(diplomat)" title="Maximinus (diplomat)">Maximinus</a>, the head of the Byzantine <a href="/wiki/Embassy" class="mw-redirect" title="Embassy">embassy</a> representing Emperor <a href="/wiki/Theodosius_II" title="Theodosius II">Theodosius II</a> (r. 402–450), on a <a href="/wiki/Diplomacy" title="Diplomacy">diplomatic mission</a> to the court of <a href="/wiki/Attila_the_Hun" class="mw-redirect" title="Attila the Hun">Attila the Hun</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Chisholm_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Chisholm-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Byzantine_History_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Byzantine_History-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> While there, he met and conversed with a <a href="/wiki/Greeks" title="Greeks">Greek</a> <a href="/wiki/Merchant" title="Merchant">merchant</a>, dressed in "Scythian" (or Hunnic) fashion, who was captured eight years earlier (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;441–442</span>) when the city of <a href="/wiki/Viminacium" title="Viminacium">Viminacium</a> (located on the <a href="/wiki/Danube" title="Danube">Danube</a> east of modern-day <a href="/wiki/Belgrade" title="Belgrade">Belgrade</a>) was sacked by the <a href="/wiki/Huns" title="Huns">Huns</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Jones_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jones-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The trader explained to Priscus that after the sack of Viminacium, he was a slave of <a href="/wiki/Onegesius" title="Onegesius">Onegesius</a>, a Hunnic nobleman, but obtained his freedom and chose to settle among the Huns.<sup id="cite_ref-Jones_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jones-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Priscus ultimately engaged in a <a href="/wiki/Debate" title="Debate">debate</a> with the Greek <a href="/wiki/Defector" class="mw-redirect" title="Defector">defector</a> regarding the qualities of life and <a href="/wiki/Justice" title="Justice">justice</a> in both the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine Empire</a> and in <a href="/wiki/Barbarians" class="mw-redirect" title="Barbarians">barbarian</a> kingdoms.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After an interlude in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, Priscus traveled to <a href="/wiki/Alexandria" title="Alexandria">Alexandria</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Thebaid" title="Thebaid">Thebaid</a> in <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Chisholm_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Chisholm-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He last appeared in the East, circa 456, attached to the staff of Euphemios as Emperor <a href="/wiki/Marcian" title="Marcian">Marcian</a>'s (r. 450–457) <a href="/wiki/Magister_officiorum" title="Magister officiorum">magister officiorum</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He died after 472 AD.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="History_of_Byzantium">History of Byzantium</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Priscus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: History of Byzantium">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Priscus was the author of an eight-volume historical work, written in Greek, entitled the <i>History of Byzantium</i> (Greek: Ἱστορία Βυζαντιακή), which was probably not the original title name.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Chisholm_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Chisholm-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The <i>History</i> probably covered the period from the accession of Attila the Hun to the accession of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Zeno_(emperor)" title="Zeno (emperor)">Zeno</a> (r. 474–475), or from 433 up until 474 AD.<sup id="cite_ref-Chisholm_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Chisholm-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Priscus's work currently survives in fragments and was very influential in the Byzantine Empire.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The <i>History</i> was used in the <i><a href="/wiki/Excerpta_de_Legationibus" class="mw-redirect" title="Excerpta de Legationibus">Excerpta de Legationibus</a></i> of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantine_VII_Porphyrogenitus" class="mw-redirect" title="Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus">Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus</a> (r. 913–959), as well as by authors such as <a href="/wiki/Evagrius_Scholasticus" title="Evagrius Scholasticus">Evagrius Scholasticus</a>, <a href="/wiki/Cassiodorus" title="Cassiodorus">Cassiodorus</a>, <a href="/wiki/Jordanes" title="Jordanes">Jordanes</a>, and the author of the <a href="/wiki/Suda" title="Suda">Souda</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_1-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Priscus's writing style is straightforward and his work is regarded as a reliable contemporary account of Attila the Hun, his court, and the reception of the Roman ambassadors.<sup id="cite_ref-Chisholm_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Chisholm-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He is considered a "classicizing" historian to the extent that his work, though written during the <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> era, is almost completely secular and relies on a style and word-choice that are part of an historiographical tradition dating back to the fifth century BC.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Priscus_account_of_a_dinner_with_Attila_the_Hun">Priscus account of a dinner with Attila the Hun</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Priscus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Priscus account of a dinner with Attila the Hun">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Priscus recounted the story of a dinner with Attila the Hun which took place at one of Attila's many houses. This house was said to be greater than the rest (having been made for celebration) due to it being constructed of decorative polished wood, with little thought to making any part of the place for defense. The dinner was at three o’clock; Priscus entered the house bearing gifts to Attila's wife; her name was Kreka and she had three sons. Priscus and the embassy of Eastern Romans were placed at the end of the table farthest from Attila but still in his presence; this was meant to show that he was greater than the Roman guests, and that Attila considered his people to be more important than Priscus and the Roman embassy. As Priscus and the Eastern Roman embassy stood, they followed the cultural tradition of being served tea from the cupbearers; they were to pray and have a drink before having a seat at the table. The seats were arranged parallel to the walls; Attila sat on the middle couch. The right side of Attila was reserved for his honored Chiefs, and everyone else including Priscus and the Roman embassies sat on the left. After being seated, everyone raised a glass to pledge one another with wine. Once the cupbearers left another attendant came in with a platter of meat, followed by bread and other foods of the time. All of the food was served onto plates of silver and gold. Priscus also notes that Attila didn't use any silver or gold plates but instead used a cup made of wood; also, his attire was not very grand. Once the first round was finished, they stood and drank again to the health of Attila. When evening arrived torches were lit and songs that were about Attila's victories were sung.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERobinson1905_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobinson1905-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Priscus_in_fiction">Priscus in fiction</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Priscus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Priscus in fiction">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Priscus is an important character in <i><a href="/wiki/Slave_of_the_Huns" title="Slave of the Huns">Slave of the Huns</a></i> by Geza Gardonyi.  He is depicted as a kindly master and scholar, and part of the novel is based on his account of his visit to Attila.
</p>
