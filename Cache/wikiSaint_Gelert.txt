<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1066479718">.mw-parser-output .infobox-subbox{padding:0;border:none;margin:-3px;width:auto;min-width:100%;font-size:100%;clear:none;float:none;background-color:transparent}.mw-parser-output .infobox-3cols-child{margin:auto}.mw-parser-output .infobox .navbar{font-size:100%}body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-header,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-subheader,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-above,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-title,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-image,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-full-data,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-below{text-align:center}</style><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div style="display:inline;" class="fn">Gelert / Celer</div></th></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Born</th><td class="infobox-data">7th century<br /><a href="/wiki/Wales" title="Wales">Wales</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Venerated&#160;in</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a><br /><a href="/wiki/Anglican_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican Church">Anglican Church</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><span class="nowrap">Major <a href="/wiki/Shrine" title="Shrine">shrine</a></span></th><td class="infobox-data">Chapel of St Celer</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Feast</a></th><td class="infobox-data">June 29<sup id="cite_ref-chapel_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chapel-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p><b>Saint Gelert</b>, also known as <b>Celer</b>, <b>Celert</b><sup id="cite_ref-jenk_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jenk-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> or <b>Kellarth</b><sup id="cite_ref-rhys_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rhys-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-jacobs_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jacobs-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> (see below), was an early <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">Celtic</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>. Several locations in <a href="/wiki/Wales" title="Wales">Wales</a> are believed to bear his name. They include <a href="/wiki/Beddgelert" title="Beddgelert">Beddgelert</a> ("Gelert's grave") and the surrounding Gelert Valley and <a href="/wiki/Llangeler" title="Llangeler">Llangeler</a> ("Gelert's church")<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> where there is a church dedicated to him.  Through the promotional efforts of an innkeeper in the early 1790s,<sup id="cite_ref-SNOPES_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SNOPES-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> St. Gelert, the human, has become much conflated with the legend of a saintly dog putatively from the same region, <a href="/wiki/Gelert" title="Gelert">Gelert</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-WEIR_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WEIR-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-DATA_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DATA-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Name">Name</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Gelert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Name">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The name "Gelert" is a <a href="/wiki/Welsh_language" title="Welsh language">cymricized</a> variant of Celert or Cilert<sup id="cite_ref-jenk_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jenk-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> (also written <i>Cylart</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-jacobs_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jacobs-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> <i>Kelert</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-rhys_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rhys-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-jacobs_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jacobs-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> <i>Kilart</i>, or <i>Kylart</i><sup id="cite_ref-rhys_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rhys-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>) and Kellarth (also written <i>Kelarth</i><sup id="cite_ref-rhys_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rhys-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> or <i>Kełłarth</i><sup id="cite_ref-rhys_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rhys-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>). It is also spelled Geler<sup id="cite_ref-dunn_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunn-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> or Celer,<sup id="cite_ref-dunn_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunn-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-etym_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-etym-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> although this probably represents a misunderstanding of Celtic <a href="/wiki/Alveolar_plosive" class="mw-redirect" title="Alveolar plosive">alveolar plosives</a> and <a href="/wiki/Dental_fricative" title="Dental fricative">dental fricatives</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-etym_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-etym-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> and is sometimes even <a href="/wiki/Germanic_languages" title="Germanic languages">teutonized</a> to Killhart, Kilhart, or <a href="/wiki/Gerard_Sagredo" class="mw-redirect" title="Gerard Sagredo">Gellert</a>. It is of unknown meaning or origin.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Gelert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gelert was a <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a> in the late 7th century<sup id="cite_ref-chapel_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chapel-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> who lived in a cave near what is now known as the Holy Well of St Celer<sup id="cite_ref-chapel_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chapel-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> near <a href="/wiki/Llandysul" title="Llandysul">Llandysul</a>. In the <a href="/wiki/Dark_Ages_(historiography)" title="Dark Ages (historiography)">Dark Ages</a>, pilgrims would travel to the well for healing by Gelert. Eventually a chapel dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Mary_(mother_of_Jesus)" class="mw-redirect" title="Mary (mother of Jesus)">St Mary</a> (called "Capel Mair") was erected over the well, of which the ruins still remain.<sup id="cite_ref-chapel_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chapel-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> It is believed that Gelert was at some time a missionary, evangelizing in <a href="/wiki/Llangeler" title="Llangeler">Llangeler</a> and <a href="/wiki/Beddgelert" title="Beddgelert">Beddgelert</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-dunn_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunn-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> According to modern historical belief, he was martyred in Beddgelert, although this is a misconception simply based on the town name's meaning ("Gelert's grave").<sup id="cite_ref-dunn_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunn-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> It is believed, however, that Gelert was <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyred</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-chapel_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chapel-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Conflation">Conflation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Gelert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Conflation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Local Welsh legend more often identifies <a href="/wiki/Gelert" title="Gelert">Gelert</a> as a dog rather than a human.  Unlike the dog-saint <a href="/wiki/St._Guinefort" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Guinefort">St. Guinefort</a>, who was in fact an actual dog 'sainted' via folk belief for his reputed protection of children, St. Gelert was a man whose human identity has been overshadowed by <a href="/wiki/Huckster" title="Huckster">hucksterism</a> about a mythical <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyred</a> dog.  According to folklore promoted by an innkeeper in Beddgelert, Gelert the dog was a <a href="/wiki/Irish_wolfhound" class="mw-redirect" title="Irish wolfhound">wolfhound</a> unjustly killed by his owner, Prince <a href="/wiki/Llywelyn_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Llywelyn the Great">Llywelyn the Great</a>, when found with bloody maws near the empty cradle of Llywelyn's son.  When the scene was investigated, the body of a wolf<sup id="cite_ref-GREYFRIARS_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-GREYFRIARS-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-SNAKE_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SNAKE-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> was found, which the dog Gelert had killed to save the baby's life.
</p><p>One modern writer offers this concise explanation of Beddgelert's connection to the "obscure, early-medieval, local saint":
</p>
<blockquote><p>The Welsh dog-hero/saint Gelert, associated with Prince <a href="/wiki/Llywelyn_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Llywelyn the Great">Llywelyn the Great</a> (1173-1240), is, however, a romantic fiction of the late 18th century derived from a 5th century Indian Buddhist work, the <i>Pancha Tantra</i>. The story gained wide currency in Europe [and] the Middle East. The heraldic <i><a href="/wiki/John_Rous_(historian)" title="John Rous (historian)">Rous Roll</a></i> of the 15th century, for example, depicted the arms of <a href="/wiki/Wales" title="Wales">Wales</a> as a helmet on which stand a dog and a cradle. But it was finally applied specifically by a hotelier to the village of Beddgelert, named after an obscure, early-mediæval, local saint. To reinforce the story further, he erected a megalith, Gelert's Bed. The 'new' story became the subject of a poem by <a href="/wiki/William_Robert_Spencer" title="William Robert Spencer">W.R. Spencer</a> which <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Haydn" title="Joseph Haydn">Joseph Haydn</a> set to music.<sup id="cite_ref-GREYFRIARS_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-GREYFRIARS-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Such is the stuff of nationalist legend — and this is one of the more benign examples.<sup id="cite_ref-WEIR_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WEIR-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>The innkeeper who popularized the dog's story was David Pritchard, who came to the area either around 1793 or 1801, depending on the source.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-GRAVE_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-GRAVE-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>  The poem by Spencer is widely available online.<sup id="cite_ref-GRAVE_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-GRAVE-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-POEM_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-POEM-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>  Haydn set the poem to the tune of the Welsh air <i>Eryri Wen</i>, which means "White Snowdon&#32;&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Sic" title="Sic">sic</a>?</i>&#93;", a reference to <a href="/wiki/Snowdon" title="Snowdon">Snowdon</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>  In the <i><a href="/wiki/Panchatantra" title="Panchatantra">Panchatantra</a>,</i> which dates back to oral traditions prior to 300 B.C.E., the villain is a snake, and the role of the dog is filled by a <a href="/wiki/Mongoose" title="Mongoose">mongoose</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-SNAKE_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SNAKE-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>  <a href="/wiki/Aesop" title="Aesop">Aesop</a> included a dog-and-snake version.<sup id="cite_ref-SNOPES_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SNOPES-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>  An early Welsh version of the tale appears in the <a href="/wiki/Mabinogion" title="Mabinogion">Mabinogion</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-DATA_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DATA-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Other cultures provide other substitutions; even <a href="/wiki/Disney" class="mw-redirect" title="Disney">Disney</a> added a non-fatal version of the legend to <i><a href="/wiki/Lady_and_the_Tramp" title="Lady and the Tramp">Lady and the Tramp</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-SNOPES_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SNOPES-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
