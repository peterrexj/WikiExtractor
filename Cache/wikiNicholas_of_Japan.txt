<p><b>Saint Nicholas (Kasatkin), <a href="/wiki/Equal-to-apostles" title="Equal-to-apostles">Equal-to-the-Apostles</a>, <a href="/wiki/Orthodox_Church_in_Japan" title="Orthodox Church in Japan">Archbishop of Japan</a></b>,<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> born <b>Ivan Dmitrovich Kasatkin</b> (<a href="/wiki/Russian_language" title="Russian language">Russian</a>: <span lang="ru">Иван Дмитриевич Касаткин</span>; 13 August&#160;&#91;<a href="/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates" title="Old Style and New Style dates">O.S.</a> 1 August&#93;&#160;1836 &#8211; 16 February 1912) was a <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Russian Orthodox">Russian Orthodox</a> <a href="/wiki/Priest" title="Priest">priest</a>, <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a>. He introduced the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Orthodox">Eastern Orthodox</a> Church to <a href="/wiki/Japan" title="Japan">Japan</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-praa_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-praa-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The Orthodox cathedral of Tokyo (<a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop" title="Metropolitan bishop">metropolitan</a> <a href="/wiki/Diocese" title="Diocese">diocese</a> of Japan), <a href="/wiki/Tokyo_Resurrection_Cathedral" class="mw-redirect" title="Tokyo Resurrection Cathedral">Tokyo Resurrection Cathedral</a>, was informally named after him as <i>Nikorai-do</i>, first by the local community, and today nationwide, in remembrance of his work.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicholas_of_Japan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nicholas was born in the <a href="/wiki/Smolensk" title="Smolensk">Smolensk</a> prefecture in the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Empire" title="Russian Empire">Russian Empire</a> to Dimitry Kasatkin, a Russian Orthodox <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a>. His mother died when he was five years old. In 1857, he entered the <a href="/wiki/Theological_Academy_in_Saint_Petersburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Theological Academy in Saint Petersburg">Theological Academy in Saint Petersburg</a>.
</p><p>On 24 June 1860, he was tonsured with the name Nicholas by the Academy Rector, Bishop Nectarius Nadezhdin. Nicholas was ordained deacon on 29 July the same year; the following day, on the altar day of the Academy church<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> (the <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> of the Holy <a href="/wiki/Twelve_Apostles" class="mw-redirect" title="Twelve Apostles">Apostles</a>, according to the Julian Calendar), he was ordained to the holy priesthood.<sup id="cite_ref-nicholas-equal_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nicholas-equal-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Japan">Japan</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicholas_of_Japan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Japan">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On 2 July 1861, Nicholas landed at <a href="/wiki/Hakodate" title="Hakodate">Hakodate</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hokkaid%C5%8D" class="mw-redirect" title="Hokkaidō">Hokkaidō</a>, Japan, as a priest attached to the chapel of the Russian consulate in Hakodate. He had volunteered for the appointment to this duty, attracted since the day he noticed a poster calling for a priest for this chapel when he was a seminary student. After he arrived at the consulate, he studied <a href="/wiki/Japanese_language" title="Japanese language">Japanese</a> and quickly gained mastery of the language. He studied Buddhism during the first eight years of his time in Japan, when, in his words, he “strove with all diligence to study Japanese history, religion, and the spirit of the Japanese people”.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While at the consulate chapel, he converted three Japanese, one of whom, a former <a href="/wiki/Samurai" title="Samurai">samurai</a> and <a href="/wiki/Kannushi" title="Kannushi">Shinto priest</a> named Sawabe Takuma, had originally come to his home to kill him. After conversion, Sawabe became one of the first Japanese Orthodox priests. In 1870, Nicholas was made an <a href="/wiki/Archimandrite" title="Archimandrite">archimandrite</a> and moved to <a href="/wiki/Tokyo" title="Tokyo">Tokyo</a>, and began an extensive missionary effort. He bought property on a height in <a href="/wiki/Kanda,_Tokyo" title="Kanda, Tokyo">Kanda</a> <a href="/wiki/Surugadai" title="Surugadai">Surugadai</a> for his headquarters which later became the site of the see of the Orthodox <a href="/wiki/Archbishop" title="Archbishop">Archbishop</a> of Japan. Under his leadership, by 1870 the Orthodox community numbered more than 4,000 people, and by 1912 about 33,000 people and 266 Orthodox communities.
</p><p>Nicholas was consecrated bishop on 30 March 1880, as Bishop of <a href="/wiki/Tallinn" title="Tallinn">Revel</a>, auxiliary to the Archdiocese of <a href="/wiki/Riga" title="Riga">Riga</a>. While Nicholas never visited the city, the parish of Revel supported his Japanese mission financially. In the Eastern Orthodox Church tradition, bishops sign with their see, but Nicholas made his habit to sign as "Episcop (Bishop) Nicholai", without mentioning Revel.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He presided over the consecration of the <a href="/wiki/Tokyo_Resurrection_Cathedral" class="mw-redirect" title="Tokyo Resurrection Cathedral">Tokyo Resurrection Cathedral</a> in 1891, and was elevated to the dignity of Archbishop of All Japan by the Russian Orthodox Holy Synod on April 6, 1907.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Russo-Japanese_War">Russo-Japanese War</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicholas_of_Japan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Russo-Japanese War">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>During the <a href="/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War" title="Russo-Japanese War">Russo-Japanese War</a>, Nicholas stayed in Japan. Those days were very difficult for him. His love for the land of his birth conflicted with his duty as the bishop of Japan to support his faithful and to pray for the <a href="/wiki/Emperor_Meiji" title="Emperor Meiji">Japanese Emperor</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Army" title="Imperial Japanese Army">Imperial Japanese Army</a> and <a href="/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Navy" title="Imperial Japanese Navy">Navy</a>: in the orthodox liturgy at that time, priests had to explicitly pray not only benediction on the sovereign and his army, but also for the defeat of his enemies in the intercession. Nicholas therefore did not participate in any public services during the war; instead, he encouraged his Japanese faithful to both pray for and to contribute to the Army and Navy. Some encouraged him to go back to Russia, but he refused and worked eagerly for Japanese faithful and Russian <a href="/wiki/Prisoner_of_war" title="Prisoner of war">prisoners of war</a>. In a letter on the conditions of a camp in Hamadera, <a href="/wiki/Osaka" title="Osaka">Osaka</a>, Nicholas wrote of his astonishment at the Russians soldiers' illiteracy: <i>nine of ten captives could not read.</i>
</p><p>Nicholas sent priests and teachers to camps to educate and care for the captives. His attitude and manners impressed not only the Orthodox faithful but also non-Christians.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Character">Character</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicholas_of_Japan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Character">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Even <a href="/wiki/Emperor_Meiji" title="Emperor Meiji">Emperor Meiji</a> was impressed with his character, especially his diplomatic efforts between the Russian Imperial Household and the Japanese government. When the Russian Tsar <a href="/wiki/Nicholas_II_of_Russia" title="Nicholas II of Russia">Nicholas II</a> was the Tsarevich under <a href="/wiki/Alexander_III_of_Russia" title="Alexander III of Russia">Alexander III</a>, the young Nicholas II visited Japan and was injured during the <a href="/wiki/%C5%8Ctsu_Incident" class="mw-redirect" title="Ōtsu Incident">Ōtsu Incident</a> by a Japanese policeman. Bishop Nicholas made a great effort to resolve this incident.
</p><p>Nicholas's study of Japanese was fruitful, allowing him to translate all liturgy books and many parts of the <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">Bible</a> including the whole of the <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a> and <a href="/wiki/Psalms" title="Psalms">Psalms</a>, most of the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Genesis" title="Book of Genesis">Book of Genesis</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Isaiah" title="Book of Isaiah">Book of Isaiah</a> with help from a Japanese Christian and scholar Nakai Tsugumaro who ran a <i><a href="/wiki/Kanbun" title="Kanbun">kanbun</a></i> private school Kaitokudo in <a href="/wiki/Osaka" title="Osaka">Osaka</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> His translations are still used in the liturgy of Japanese Orthodox Church. Being fond of church singing, Kasatkin made a significant contribution in introducing this art to the Japanese.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He also established a theological seminary, primary schools for boys and girls, a library, shelter and other agencies.
</p><p>Nicholas wrote a diary in Russian for years, recording the pastoral life of early Orthodox Church of Japan as well as his thought and observation of <a href="/wiki/Meiji_period" class="mw-redirect" title="Meiji period">Meiji era Japan</a>. His diary was believed to have been burned and lost in <a href="/wiki/1923_Great_Kant%C5%8D_earthquake" title="1923 Great Kantō earthquake">Great Kantō earthquake</a> of 1923, but rediscovered by Kennosuke Nakamura, a Russian literary researcher, and published in 2004 as <i>Dnevniki Sviatogo Nikolaia Iaponskogo</i> (5 vols. Saint Petersburg: Giperion, 2004). Nakamura translated the whole diary into Japanese and published with his commentary in 2007.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Nicholas offered an integral study of Buddhism in his work, “Japan from the point of view of Christian mission”, published in 1869. This was the first description of <a href="/wiki/Buddhism_in_Japan" title="Buddhism in Japan">Japanese Buddhism</a> accessible to the Russian language reader.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicholas_of_Japan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nicholas was the first saint of the <a href="/wiki/Japanese_Orthodox_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Japanese Orthodox Church">Japanese Orthodox Church</a>. After his death, his body was buried in Tokyo Metropolitan <a href="/wiki/Yanaka_Cemetery" title="Yanaka Cemetery">Yanaka Cemetery</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Ueno,_Tokyo" class="mw-redirect" title="Ueno, Tokyo">Ueno</a>. In 1970, he was canonized as 'Equal-to-the-Apostles, Archbishop of Japan, St Nicholas'. His feast day is 3 February (Old-style), 16 February (New-style). The Russian Orthodox Church and the Japanese Orthodox Church celebrate this feast nationwide on the old-style date.
</p><p>There is a church which commemorates him in <a href="/wiki/Maebashi,_Gunma" class="mw-redirect" title="Maebashi, Gunma">Maebashi, Gunma</a>, built in 1974. There is also an Eastern Orthodox Church dedicated to Saint Nicholas of Japan in Moscow.<sup id="cite_ref-praa_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-praa-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
