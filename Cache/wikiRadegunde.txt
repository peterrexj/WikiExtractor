<p><b>Radegund</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Radegundis</i>; also spelled <i>Rhadegund, Radegonde, or Radigund</i>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 520 – 13 August 587) was a <a href="/wiki/Thuringii" title="Thuringii">Thuringian</a> princess and <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frankish</a> queen, who founded the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Cross_Abbey_(Poitiers)" title="Holy Cross Abbey (Poitiers)">Abbey of the Holy Cross</a> at <a href="/wiki/Poitiers" title="Poitiers">Poitiers</a>. She is the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of several churches in France and England and of <a href="/wiki/Jesus_College,_Cambridge" title="Jesus College, Cambridge">Jesus College, Cambridge</a> (whose full name is "The College of the Blessed Virgin Mary, Saint John the Evangelist <i>and the glorious Virgin Saint Radegund</i>, near Cambridge").

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Radegund&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:%C3%89glise_Sainte_Radegonde,_Poitiers.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/%C3%89glise_Sainte_Radegonde%2C_Poitiers.jpg/220px-%C3%89glise_Sainte_Radegonde%2C_Poitiers.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="330" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/%C3%89glise_Sainte_Radegonde%2C_Poitiers.jpg/330px-%C3%89glise_Sainte_Radegonde%2C_Poitiers.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/%C3%89glise_Sainte_Radegonde%2C_Poitiers.jpg/440px-%C3%89glise_Sainte_Radegonde%2C_Poitiers.jpg 2x" data-file-width="853" data-file-height="1280" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:%C3%89glise_Sainte_Radegonde,_Poitiers.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Church_of_St._Radegonde_(Poitiers)" class="mw-redirect" title="Church of St. Radegonde (Poitiers)">Church of St. Radegonde (Poitiers)</a></div></div></div>
<p>Radegund was born about 520 to <a href="/wiki/Bertachar" title="Bertachar">Bertachar</a>, one of the three kings of the German land <a href="/wiki/Thuringia" title="Thuringia">Thuringia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-jcc_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jcc-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>  Radegund's uncle, <a href="/wiki/Hermanfrid" class="mw-redirect" title="Hermanfrid">Hermanfrid</a>, killed Bertachar in battle, and took Radegund into his household. After allying with the <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frankish</a> King <a href="/wiki/Theuderic_I_of_Austrasia" class="mw-redirect" title="Theuderic I of Austrasia">Theuderic</a>, Hermanfrid defeated his other brother <a href="/wiki/Baderic" title="Baderic">Baderic</a>. However, having crushed his brothers and seized control of Thuringia, Hermanfrid reneged on his agreement with Theuderic to share sovereignty.
</p><p>In 531, Theuderic returned to Thuringia with his brother <a href="/wiki/Clotaire_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Clotaire I">Clotaire I</a> (also known as Chlothar). Together they defeated Hermanfrid and conquered his kingdom.  Clotaire I also took charge of Radegund, taking her back to <a href="/wiki/Merovingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingian">Merovingian</a> <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a><sup id="cite_ref-jcc_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jcc-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> with him. He sent the child to his villa of Athies in Picardy for several years, before marrying her in 540.<sup id="cite_ref-columbia_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-columbia-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Radegund was one of Clotaire I's six wives or concubines (the other five being Guntheuca who was the widow of his brother Chlodomer, Chunsina, <a href="/wiki/Ingund,_wife_of_Clotaire_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Ingund, wife of Clotaire I">Ingund</a>, Ingund's sister <a href="/wiki/Ar%C3%A9gonde" class="mw-redirect" title="Arégonde">Aregund</a> and Wuldetrada the widow of Clotaire's grand-nephew Theudebald). She had no children with him. Radegund was noted for her almsgiving.<sup id="cite_ref-McNamara_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-McNamara-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>By 550 Radegund's brother was the last surviving male member of the Thuringian royal family. Clotaire had him murdered.  Radegund fled the court and sought the protection of the Church, persuading the bishop of Noyon to ordain her as a <a href="/wiki/Deaconess" title="Deaconess">deaconess</a>;<sup id="cite_ref-jcc_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jcc-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> she founded the monastery of Sainte-Croix in Poitiers circa 560, where she cared for the infirm. Radegund was widely believed to have the gift of healing.<sup id="cite_ref-McNamara_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-McNamara-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Living under the <i>Rule for Virgins</i> of <a href="/wiki/Caesarius_of_Arles" title="Caesarius of Arles">Caesarius of Arles</a>—which Radegund had received from his niece, <a href="/wiki/Caesaria_the_Younger" title="Caesaria the Younger">Caesaria</a>—the <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nuns</a> were required to be able to read and write, and to devote several hours of the day to reading the scriptures and copying manuscripts, as well as traditional tasks such as weaving and needlework.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> This Rule strictly enclosed women, to the point that nuns of Sainte-Croix were unable to attend Radegund's funeral.
</p><p>Her abbey was named for the relic of the <a href="/wiki/True_Cross" title="True Cross">True Cross</a> that Radegund obtained from the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine Emperor">Byzantine Emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Justin_II" title="Justin II">Justin II</a>.  Although the bishop <a href="/wiki/Maroveus_of_Poitiers" class="mw-redirect" title="Maroveus of Poitiers">Maroveus of Poitiers</a> refused to install it in the abbey, at Radegund's request king <a href="/wiki/Sigebert" title="Sigebert">Sigebert</a> sent <a href="/wiki/Eufronius_of_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Eufronius of Tours">Eufronius of Tours</a> to Poitiers to perform the ceremony.  To celebrate the relic and its installation into Sainte-Croix, <a href="/wiki/Venantius_Fortunatus" title="Venantius Fortunatus">Venantius Fortunatus</a> composed a series of hymns, including the famous <a href="/wiki/Vexilla_Regis" class="mw-redirect" title="Vexilla Regis">Vexilla Regis</a>, considered to be one of the most significant Christian hymns ever written, which is still sung for services on <a href="/wiki/Good_Friday" title="Good Friday">Good Friday</a>, <a href="/wiki/Palm_Sunday" title="Palm Sunday">Palm Sunday</a>, as well as the <a href="/wiki/Elevation_of_the_Holy_Cross" title="Elevation of the Holy Cross">Exaltation of the Holy Cross</a>
</p><p>Radegund was a close friend of <a href="/wiki/Junian_of_Maire" class="mw-redirect" title="Junian of Maire">Junian of Maire</a>; Junian and Radegund are said to have died on the same day, 13 August 587.<sup id="cite_ref-junien_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-junien-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Asceticism">Asceticism</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Radegund&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Asceticism">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Radegund was known for her <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetic</a> behaviour and has been described as an "extreme ascetic".<sup id="cite_ref-Effros_2002_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Effros_2002-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Muir_2019_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Muir_2019-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> She followed a vegetarian diet,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> refusing all animal products. She ate nothing but <a href="/wiki/Legume" title="Legume">legumes</a> and green <a href="/wiki/Vegetable" title="Vegetable">vegetables</a>: neither fruit nor fish nor eggs.<sup id="cite_ref-Effros_2002_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Effros_2002-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Radegund also abstained from wine, mead and beer. During <a href="/wiki/Lent" title="Lent">Lent</a> she abstained from bread, oil, and salt, and only drank a little water.<sup id="cite_ref-Effros_2002_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Effros_2002-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> She acted against the advice of others who warned her that her extreme ascetism might make her ill.<sup id="cite_ref-Effros_2002_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Effros_2002-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> She bound her neck and arms with three iron circlets; her flesh was badly cut because of this. On one occasion she heated a brass cross and pressed it on her body.<sup id="cite_ref-Muir_2019_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Muir_2019-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Literary_connections">Literary connections</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Radegund&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Literary connections">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The poet <a href="/wiki/Venantius_Fortunatus" title="Venantius Fortunatus">Venantius Fortunatus</a> and the bishop, <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographer</a>, and historian, <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Tours" title="Gregory of Tours">Gregory of Tours</a>, were close friends with Radegund and wrote extensively about her. She wrote Latin poems to Fortunatus on tablets that have been lost. The three of them seem to have been close and Fortunatus' relations with Radegund seem to have been based on friendship. There are two poems written in the voice of Radegund, <i>De Excidio Thoringiae</i> and <i>Ad Artachin</i>. While it has been proposed that Venantius wrote them, recent historians see her as the author.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Another biography was written by the nun <a href="/wiki/Baudovinia" title="Baudovinia">Baudovinia</a> following a rebellion at the abbey described by Gregory of Tours.
</p><p>Radegund's funeral, which Venantius Fortunatus and Gregory of Tours attended, was three days after her death. She was buried in what was to become the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Sainte-Radegonde_(Poitiers)" title="Church of Sainte-Radegonde (Poitiers)">Church of St. Radegonde</a> in Poitiers. Her tomb can still be found in the crypt of that church, which remains the center of devotion to her. In the 1260s a church decoration program included stained-glass windows depicting Radegund's life. These were later largely destroyed by <a href="/wiki/Huguenots" title="Huguenots">Huguenots</a>.
</p><p>In her book <i>Woman Under Monasticism: Chapters on Saint-Lore and Convent Life between A.D. 500 and A.D. 1500</i> (1896) <a href="/wiki/Lina_Eckenstein" title="Lina Eckenstein">Lina Eckenstein</a> drew the attention of modern readers to the rebellion of the nuns at Poitiers after the death of Radegund, during which, for a period of  two years, they refused to accept a new abbess who had been appointed by the male Catholic hierarchy.<sup id="cite_ref-nunsjstor_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nunsjstor-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Attributes">Attributes</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Radegund&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Attributes">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>She is typically depicted "with royal robes, crown, and sceptre" and nearby there are "wolves and wild beasts" which are tame in her presence. Also: "Crosier and book. Field of oats. White headdress, tunic with fleurs-de-lys, mantle with castles."<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Namesakes">Namesakes</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Radegund&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Namesakes">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<p>Five English <a href="/wiki/Parish_church" title="Parish church">parish churches</a> are dedicated to her, and she had a <a href="/wiki/Chapel" title="Chapel">chapel</a> in <a href="/wiki/Old_St_Paul%27s_Cathedral" title="Old St Paul&#39;s Cathedral">Old St Paul's Cathedral</a>, as well as in <a href="/wiki/Gloucester_Cathedral" title="Gloucester Cathedral">Gloucester</a>, <a href="/wiki/Lichfield_Cathedral" title="Lichfield Cathedral">Lichfield</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Exeter_Cathedral" title="Exeter Cathedral">Exeter Cathedrals</a>. <a href="/wiki/St._Radegund%27s_Abbey" title="St. Radegund&#39;s Abbey">St. Radegund's Abbey</a>, near Dover, was founded in her honour in 1191, and <a href="/wiki/Longleat_Priory" title="Longleat Priory">Longleat Priory</a> in <a href="/wiki/Wiltshire" title="Wiltshire">Wiltshire</a> was also dedicated to her. She is also a <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Jesus_College,_Cambridge" title="Jesus College, Cambridge">Jesus College, Cambridge</a>, which was founded on the site of the 12th century <a href="/wiki/St_Radegund%27s_Priory,_Cambridge" title="St Radegund&#39;s Priory, Cambridge">Priory of Saint Mary and Saint Radegund</a>.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/St_Radegund_public_house" class="mw-redirect" title="St Radegund public house">St Radegund public house</a> in <a href="/wiki/Cambridge" title="Cambridge">Cambridge</a> is named in her honour. St Rhadagund's Holiday and Conference Centre on the Isle of Wight is also named after her.
</p><p>There are many places named <a href="/wiki/Sainte-Radegonde_(disambiguation)" class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" title="Sainte-Radegonde (disambiguation)">Sainte-Radegonde</a> in <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Austria" title="Austria">Austria</a> has two locations named after Saint Radegund. <a href="/wiki/Sankt_Radegund" title="Sankt Radegund">Sankt Radegund</a> in <a href="/wiki/Upper_Austria" title="Upper Austria">Upper Austria</a> is a municipality in the district of <a href="/wiki/Braunau_am_Inn_(district)" class="mw-redirect" title="Braunau am Inn (district)">Braunau am Inn</a>, situated at the western rim of the <a href="/wiki/Innviertel" title="Innviertel">Innviertel</a> region, where the <a href="/wiki/Salzach" title="Salzach">Salzach</a> river forms the border to the <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">German</a> state of <a href="/wiki/Bavaria" title="Bavaria">Bavaria</a>. Saint Radegund is also the namesake of <a href="/wiki/Sankt_Radegund_bei_Graz" title="Sankt Radegund bei Graz">Sankt Radegund bei Graz</a>, a municipality in the district of <a href="/wiki/Graz-Umgebung" class="mw-redirect" title="Graz-Umgebung">Graz-Umgebung</a> in the Austrian state of <a href="/wiki/Styria" title="Styria">Styria</a>.
</p><p>Close to the ruins of the castle Mühlburg which can be dated back to 704 above the village of <a href="/wiki/M%C3%BChlberg,_Thuringia" title="Mühlberg, Thuringia">Mühlberg</a> in Thuringia in Germany, the foundations of a chapel dedicated to St Radegund can be visited.
</p>
