<p><b>Tatwine</b><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> (<a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/circa#English" class="extiw" title="wikt:circa">c.</a><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;670</span> – 30 July 734) was the tenth <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a> from 731 to 734. Prior to becoming archbishop, he was a monk and abbot of a Benedictine monastery. Besides his ecclesiastical career, Tatwine was a writer, and riddles he composed survive. Another work he composed was on the <a href="/wiki/Latin_grammar" title="Latin grammar">grammar</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin language</a>, which was aimed at advanced students of that language.  He was subsequently considered a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Tatwine&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Tatwine was a <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercian</a> by birth.<sup id="cite_ref-Brooks80_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks80-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His epigraph at Canterbury stated that when he died he was in old age, so perhaps he was born around 670.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He became a monk at the monastery at <a href="/wiki/Breedon-on-the-Hill" class="mw-redirect" title="Breedon-on-the-Hill">Breedon-on-the-Hill</a> in the present-day County of <a href="/wiki/Leicestershire" title="Leicestershire">Leicestershire</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Brooks80_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks80-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Yorke31_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke31-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> and then <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> of that house.<sup id="cite_ref-ASE183_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE183-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Through the influence of King <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelbald_of_Mercia" title="Æthelbald of Mercia">Æthelbald</a> he was appointed as Archbishop of Canterbury in 731 and was consecrated on 10 June 731.<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook213_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook213-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Kirby113_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby113-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He was one of a number of Mercians who were appointed to Canterbury during the 730s and 740s.<sup id="cite_ref-Kingship24_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kingship24-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Apart from his consecration of the Bishops of <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Lindsey" title="Bishop of Lindsey">Lindsey</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Selsey" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Selsey">Selsey</a> in 733, Tatwine's period as archbishop appears to have been uneventful.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He died in office on 30 July 734.<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook213_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook213-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Later considered a saint, his <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is 30 July.<sup id="cite_ref-DictSaints_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DictSaints-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Tatwine&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>'s commentary on Tatwine calls him a "<span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">vir religione et Prudentia insignis, sacris quoque literis nobiliter instructus</i></span>" (a man notable for his prudence, devotion and learning). These qualities were displayed in the two surviving manuscripts of his <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_riddles" title="Anglo-Saxon riddles">riddles</a> and four of his <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Ars Gramattica Tatuini</i></span>.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Law281_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Law281-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Ars_Gramattica_Tatuini"><i>Ars Gramattica Tatuini</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Tatwine&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Ars Gramattica Tatuini">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Ars</i></span> is one of only two surviving eighth-century Latin grammars from England.<sup id="cite_ref-Law281_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Law281-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The grammar is a reworking of <a href="/wiki/Aelius_Donatus" title="Aelius Donatus">Donatus</a>'s <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Ars Minor</i></span> with the addition of information drawn from other grammarians, such as <a href="/wiki/Priscian" title="Priscian">Priscian</a> and <a href="/wiki/Consentius" title="Consentius">Consentius</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> It was not designed for a newcomer to the Latin language, but rather for more advanced students.<sup id="cite_ref-Brooks98_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks98-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> It covers the eight <a href="/wiki/Parts_of_speech" class="mw-redirect" title="Parts of speech">parts of speech</a> through illustrations drawn from <a href="/wiki/Classical_Latin" title="Classical Latin">classical scholars</a>, although not directly but through other grammatical works. There are also some examples drawn from the <a href="/wiki/Psalms" title="Psalms">Psalms</a>. The work was completed before Tatwine became archbishop, and was used not only in England but also on the Continent.<sup id="cite_ref-Blair246_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Blair246-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Riddles">Riddles</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Tatwine&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Riddles">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>It is almost certain that Tatwine was inspired to develop the culture of <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_riddles" title="Anglo-Saxon riddles">riddle-writing in early medieval England</a> because he had read the <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la"><a href="/wiki/Epistola_ad_Acircium" title="Epistola ad Acircium">Epistola ad Acircium</a></i></span> by the West-Saxon scholar <a href="/wiki/Aldhelm" title="Aldhelm">Aldhelm</a> (d. 709), which combined studies of Latin grammar and metre with the presentation of one hundred hexametrical riddles.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Frederick Tupper believed that Aldhelm's influence was minimal,<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> but subsequent scholars have argued that Tatwine's riddles owed a substantial debt to those of Aldhelm.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Tatwine's riddles deal with such diverse topics as philosophy and charity, the five senses and the alphabet, and a book, and a pen,<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> yet, according to Mercedes Salvador-Bello, these riddles are placed in a carefully structured sequence: 1–3 and 21–26 on theology (e.g. 2, faith, hope, and charity), 4–14 on objects associated with ecclesiastical life (e.g. 7, a bell), 15–20 on wonders and monsters (e.g. 16, prepositions with two cases), 27–39 on tools and related natural phenomena (e.g. 28, an anvil, and 33, fire), with a final piece on the sun's rays.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-DNB_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Tatwine's riddles survive in two manuscripts: the early 11th-century London, British Library, Royal 12.Cxxiii (fols. 121v–7r) and the mid-11th-century Cambridge, University Library, Gg.5.35 (fols. 374v–77v).<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> In both manuscripts, they are written alongside the <a href="/wiki/Riddles_of_Eusebius" class="mw-redirect" title="Riddles of Eusebius">riddles of Eusebius</a>: it seems clear that Eusebius (whose identity is uncertain) added sixty riddles to Tatwine's forty to take the collection up to one hundred.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Tatwine gives a sign in one of the riddles of the growing acceptance among scholars in the Christian west of the legitimacy of philosophy: "<span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">De philosophia: est felix mea qui poterit cognoscere iura</i></span>" (Of Philosophy: happy is he who can know my laws).<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> The riddles are formed in <a href="/wiki/Acrostic" title="Acrostic">acrostics</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ASE_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<p>An example of Tatwine's work is enigma 11, on the needle:<sup id="cite_ref-:0_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 178">&#58;&#8202;178&#8202;</span></sup>
</p>
<table class="wikitable plainrowheaders">
<caption>Enigma 11
</caption>
<tbody><tr>
<th scope="col">Latin original
</th>
<th scope="col">English translation
</th></tr>
<tr>
<th scope="row"><div class="poem">
<p><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Torrens me genuit fornax de uiscere flammae,<br />
Condi&lt;t&gt;or inualido et finxit me corpore luscam;<br />
Sed constat nullum iam sine me uiuere posse.<br />
Est mirum dictu, cludam ni lumina uultus,<br />
Condere non artis penitus molimina possum.</i></span>
</p>
</div>
</th>
<td><div class="poem">
<p>Brought forth in the fiery womb of a blazing furnace,<br />
my maker formed me one-eyed and frail;<br />
yet surely none could ever live without me.<br />
Strange to say, unless my eye is blinded,<br />
my skill produces not the smallest piece of work.
</p>
</div>
</td></tr></tbody></table>
