<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Venantius_Fortunatus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Venantius Fortunatus was born between 530 and 540 AD at <a href="/wiki/Valdobbiadene" title="Valdobbiadene">Duplavis (or Duplavilis)</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Treviso" title="Treviso">Treviso</a> in <a href="/wiki/Veneto" title="Veneto">Veneto</a>, Italy.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He grew up during the <a href="/wiki/Gothic_War_(535%E2%80%93554)" title="Gothic War (535–554)">Roman reconquest of Italy</a>, but there is controversy concerning as to where Fortunatus spent his childhood. Some historians, such as <a href="/w/index.php?title=D._Tardi&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="D. Tardi (page does not exist)">D. Tardi</a>, suggest that Fortunatus' family moved to <a href="/wiki/Aquileia" title="Aquileia">Aquileia</a> because of the turbulent political situation in Treviso after the death of <a href="/wiki/Theoderic_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Theoderic the Great">King Theoderic</a>. This theory is suggested because there is evidence of Fortunatus speaking warmly about one of the bishops there, Bishop <a href="/w/index.php?title=Paul_of_Aquileia&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Paul of Aquileia (page does not exist)">Paul of Aquileia</a>. Other scholars, such as <a href="/w/index.php?title=Judith_George&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Judith George (page does not exist)">Judith George</a>, suggest that his family never moved to Aquileia, pointing out that the poet speaks more of Duplavis than any other place regarding his childhood.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
Sometime in the 550s or 60s, he travelled to <a href="/wiki/Ravenna" title="Ravenna">Ravenna</a> to study. While there, he was given a <a href="/wiki/Education_in_Ancient_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Education in Ancient Rome">classical education</a>, in the Roman style.  His later work shows familiarity with not only classical Latin poets such as <a href="/wiki/Virgil" title="Virgil">Virgil</a>, <a href="/wiki/Horace" title="Horace">Horace</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ovid" title="Ovid">Ovid</a>, <a href="/wiki/Statius" title="Statius">Statius</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Martial" title="Martial">Martial</a>, but also <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christian</a> poets, including <a href="/wiki/Arator" title="Arator">Arator</a>, <a href="/wiki/Claudian" title="Claudian">Claudian</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Coelius_Sedulius" title="Coelius Sedulius">Coelius Sedulius</a>, and bears their influence. In addition, Fortunatus likely had some knowledge of the Greek language and the classical Greek writers and philosophers, as he makes reference to them and Greek words at times throughout his poetry and prose.
</p><p>Fortunatus eventually moved to <a href="/wiki/Metz" title="Metz">Metz</a> in the spring of 566, probably with the specific intention of becoming a poet at the Merovingian Court. It was there his successful career really began. To reach Metz, he took a winding route, passing through four modern countries: Italy, Austria, Germany and France. Fortunatus himself explains two entirely different reasons for this route. Describing the first reason, he "portrays himself in the guise of a wandering minstrel, his journey just one in a series of adventures."<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The second reason is more religious, explaining in his <i>Vita S. Martini</i> that he took this route to worship at the shrine of St Martin in Tours, visiting other shrines as he went.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Fortunatus' arrival in Metz coincides with the marriage of <a href="/wiki/Sigebert_I" title="Sigebert I">King Sigibert</a> and <a href="/wiki/Brunhilda_of_Austrasia" title="Brunhilda of Austrasia">Queen Brunhild</a>, and at the ceremony he performed a celebration poem for the entire court. After this incident, Fortunatus had many noble patrons, as well as bishops, who wished him to write poetry for them. About a year after he arrived in Metz, Fortunatus travelled to the court of <a href="/wiki/Charibert_I" title="Charibert I">King Charibert</a>, Sigibert's brother, in Paris, and stayed there until Charibert's death in 567 or 568. Due to danger presented by <a href="/wiki/Chilperic_I" title="Chilperic I">King Chilperic</a>, brother of Sigibert and Charibert, Fortunatus had to move south to <a href="/wiki/Tours" title="Tours">Tours</a>, returning to Sigibert's lands. From there, he ventured to <a href="/wiki/Poitiers" title="Poitiers">Poitiers</a> where he met <a href="/wiki/Radegund" title="Radegund">Radegund</a>. They became close friends, and Fortunatus wrote many poems in her honour and in support of her political campaigns.
Fortunatus had made another great friendship in Tours and Poitiers: with <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Tours" title="Gregory of Tours">Gregory of Tours</a>, who was installed as Bishop of Tours in 573, from whom Fortunatus also received patronage. In 580, Fortunatus wrote a poem defending Gregory against treasonous charges placed upon him at Chilperic's court. 
After the death of Sigibert, and that of Chilperic, Fortunatus moved to <a href="/wiki/Childebert_II" title="Childebert II">Childebert</a>'s court in Poitiers. Childebert was Sigibert's son. Sometime around 576, he was ordained into the church.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He stayed there until around the year 599-600, when he was appointed Bishop of Poitiers, to replace Plato, Bishop of Poitiers. Fortunatus died in the early 7th century. He was called a saint after his death, but was never formally canonized.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Venantius_Fortunatus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Fortunatus is best known for two poems that have become part of the <a href="/wiki/Liturgy" title="Liturgy">liturgy</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, the <i><a href="/wiki/Pange_lingua_gloriosi_proelium_certaminis" title="Pange lingua gloriosi proelium certaminis">Pange lingua gloriosi proelium certaminis</a></i> ("Sing, O tongue, of the glorious struggle"), a hymn that later inspired St <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas" title="Thomas Aquinas">Thomas Aquinas</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Pange_Lingua_Gloriosi_Corporis_Mysterium" class="mw-redirect" title="Pange Lingua Gloriosi Corporis Mysterium">Pange Lingua Gloriosi Corporis Mysterium</a></i>.  He also wrote <i><a href="/wiki/Vexilla_Regis" class="mw-redirect" title="Vexilla Regis">Vexilla Regis prodeunt</a></i> ("The royal banners forward go"), which is a <a href="/wiki/Sequence_(poetry)" class="mw-redirect" title="Sequence (poetry)">sequence</a>  sung at <a href="/wiki/Vespers" title="Vespers">Vespers</a> during <a href="/wiki/Holy_Week" title="Holy Week">Holy Week</a>.  This poem was written in honour of a large piece of the <a href="/wiki/True_Cross" title="True Cross">True Cross</a>, which explains its association also with the feast of the <a href="/wiki/Exaltation_of_the_Holy_Cross#September_14" class="mw-redirect" title="Exaltation of the Holy Cross">Exaltation of the Holy Cross</a>.  The relic had been sent from the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_empire" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine empire">Byzantine Emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Justin_II" title="Justin II">Justin II</a> to Queen <a href="/wiki/Radegund" title="Radegund">Radegund</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Franks</a>, who after the death of her husband <a href="/wiki/Chlotar_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Chlotar I">Chlotar I</a> had founded a <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> in <a href="/wiki/Poitiers" title="Poitiers">Poitiers</a>.  The Municipal Library in Poitiers houses an 11th-century manuscript on the life of Radegunde, copied from a 6th-century account by Fortunatus.
</p><p>Venantius Fortunatus wrote eleven surviving books of poetry in Latin in a diverse group of genres including <a href="/wiki/Epitaph" title="Epitaph">epitaphs</a>, <a href="/wiki/Panegyric" title="Panegyric">panegyrics</a>, georgics, consolations, and religious poems. A major genre of Fortunatus' poetry is the panegyric. He wrote four major panegyrics to four Merovingian Kings: Sigibert and Brunhild,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Charibert,<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Chilperic <sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> and Childebert II and Brunhild.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The first was also his debut into the Merovingian Court in Gaul, at Metz, in honour of the marriage of Sigibert and Brunhild. It is a fanciful poem, telling the story of how the bride and groom were brought together by Cupid, recalling the style of the classical Latin poets. The second, for Charibert, celebrates his rule, and gives the impression that this Frankish king is descended from and succeeded the Roman kings in an unbroken line.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> This means that he has a legitimate rule. The third, addressed to King Chilperic, is full of controversy. Chilperic was known as a headstrong and hot-tempered ruler, however in this panegyric, Fortunatus depicts him as being gracious, compassionate and merciful, never making judgements too quickly, and even praises the king's poetry.  The poem was given on the occasion of the trial for treason of Gregory of Tours, Fortunatus' patron and friend. Some scholars have suggested that Fortunatus is simply trying to appease a new patron (Chilperic) because of Gregory's uncertain future. However, other scholars, such as Brennan and George, disagree, postulating that Fortunatus was evoking more of a correctional and moralistic poem towards Chilperic, reminding him how the ideal king ruled, and gently suggesting that he act in that way as well. Thus, the poem becomes a plea for his friend Gregory of Tours, while avoiding an open disagreement with the king.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Fortunatus wrote panegyrics and other types of poems, including praise, eulogies, personal poems to bishops and friends alike,<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> consolations and poems in support of political issues, particularly those presented by his friends Gregory of Tours and Radegunde. 
His eleven books of poetry contain his surviving poems, all ordered chronologically and by importance of subject. For instance, a poem about <a href="/wiki/God" title="God">God</a> will come before the panegyric to a king, which will come before a eulogy to a bishop. This collection of poems is the main primary source for writing about his life.
</p><p>His verse is important in the development of later <a href="/wiki/Latin_literature" title="Latin literature">Latin literature</a>, largely because he wrote at a time when Latin <a href="/wiki/Meter_(poetry)" class="mw-redirect" title="Meter (poetry)">prosody</a> was moving away from the quantitative verse of <a href="/wiki/Classical_Latin" title="Classical Latin">classical Latin</a> and towards the accentual meters of <a href="/wiki/Medieval_Latin" title="Medieval Latin">medieval Latin</a>. His style sometimes suggests the influence of <a href="/wiki/Hiberno-Latin" title="Hiberno-Latin">Hiberno-Latin</a>, in learned <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a> coinages that occasionally appear in his poems.
</p><p>Fortunatus' other major work was <i>Vita S. Martini</i> <sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> It is a long narrative poem, reminiscent of the classical epics of Greek and Roman cultures but replete with Christian references and allusions, depicting the life of Saint Martin.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
He also wrote a verse <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> of his patron Queen Radegund (continued by the nun <a href="/wiki/Baudovinia" title="Baudovinia">Baudovinia</a>).
</p><p>His hymns are used extensively in the <i><a href="/wiki/Hymnal_1982" class="mw-redirect" title="Hymnal 1982">Hymnal 1982</a></i> of the <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church</a>.  One of his hymns was set to music by the modern composer <a href="/wiki/Randall_Giles" title="Randall Giles">Randall Giles</a>. Another hymn as translated from the Latin (Welcome, happy morning! age to age shall say) celebrates Easter with music by Sir <a href="/wiki/Arthur_Sullivan" title="Arthur Sullivan">Arthur Sullivan</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Impact_and_contributions">Impact and contributions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Venantius_Fortunatus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Impact and contributions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In his time, Fortunatus filled a great social desire for <a href="/wiki/Latin_poetry" title="Latin poetry">Latin poetry</a>. He was one of the most prominent poets at this point, and had many contracts, commissions and correspondences with kings, bishops and noblemen and women from the time he arrived in Gaul until his death. He used his poetry to advance in society, to promote political ideas he supported, usually conceived of by Radegunde or by Gregory, and to pass on personal thoughts and communications. He was a master wordsmith and because of his promotion of the church, as well as the Roman tendencies of the Frankish royalty, he remained in favour with most of his acquaintances throughout his lifetime.
</p><p>From the point of view of the present day, Fortunatus provides another window into the world of the Merovingian court.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> For much of this period, the only reliable source on the subject is Gregory of Tours' history, but as it is well known that Gregory had his own political and personal agendas, the objectivity of his accounts can sometimes come into question.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> While Fortunatus tends to embellish or even mock the happenings and truth of the situations he writes about, there is an element of inferred truth, whether it is his classical embellishments on the marriage panegyric for Sigibert, or his recalling the traits of the ideal ruler to correct a bad king. With this, he supplies an alternate view of everything going on at court, a view which at times differs from Gregory's account.
</p><p>His works have been set to music in settings which themselves have become prominent artworks.  <a href="/wiki/Anton_Bruckner" title="Anton Bruckner">Anton Bruckner</a> composed <a href="/wiki/Vexilla_regis_(Bruckner)" title="Vexilla regis (Bruckner)">a motet</a> based on <i>Vexilla Regis</i>, and <a href="/wiki/Knut_Nystedt" title="Knut Nystedt">Knut Nystedt</a> a choral setting of <i>O Crux Splendidior</i>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Feast_Day">Feast Day</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Venantius_Fortunatus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Feast Day">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Fortunatus is a saint of the Roman Catholic Church, commemorated on 14 December.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
