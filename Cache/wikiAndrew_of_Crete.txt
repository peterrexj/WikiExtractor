<p><b>Andrew of Crete</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el"><span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Ἀνδρέας Κρήτης</span></span></span>, c. 650 – July 4, 712 or 726 or 740), also known as <b>Andrew of Jerusalem</b>, was an 8th-century <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a>, <a href="/wiki/Theologian" class="mw-redirect" title="Theologian">theologian</a>, <a href="/wiki/Homilist" class="mw-redirect" title="Homilist">homilist</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> and <a href="/wiki/Hymnographer" class="mw-redirect" title="Hymnographer">hymnographer</a>. He is venerated as a saint in <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy" title="Eastern Orthodoxy">Eastern Orthodoxy</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Andrew_of_Crete&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Born in <a href="/wiki/Damascus" title="Damascus">Damascus</a> c. 650, to Christian parents, Andrew was mute until the age of seven. According to his <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographers</a>, he was miraculously cured after receiving <a href="/wiki/Holy_Communion" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Communion">Holy Communion</a>. He began his <a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical" title="Ecclesiastical">ecclesiastical</a> career at fourteen in the <a href="/wiki/Lavra" title="Lavra">Lavra</a> of Saint <a href="/wiki/Sabbas_the_Sanctified" title="Sabbas the Sanctified">Sabbas the Sanctified</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>, where he quickly gained the notice of his superiors. Theodore, the <i><a href="/wiki/Locum_tenens" class="mw-redirect" title="Locum tenens">locum tenens</a></i> of the <a href="/wiki/Orthodox_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="Orthodox Patriarch of Jerusalem">Patriarchate of Jerusalem</a> (745–770) made him his <a href="/wiki/Archdeacon" title="Archdeacon">Archdeacon</a>, and sent him to the imperial capital of <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> as his official representative at the <a href="/wiki/Sixth_Ecumenical_Council" class="mw-redirect" title="Sixth Ecumenical Council">Sixth Ecumenical Council</a> (680–681), which had been called by Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantine_Pogonatus" class="mw-redirect" title="Constantine Pogonatus">Constantine Pogonatus</a> to counter the <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heresy</a> of <a href="/wiki/Monothelitism" title="Monothelitism">Monothelitism</a>.
</p><p>Shortly after the Council, he was summoned back to Constantinople from Jerusalem and appointed Archdeacon at the "Great Church" of <a href="/wiki/Hagia_Sophia" title="Hagia Sophia">Hagia Sophia</a>. Eventually, Andrew was appointed to the <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop" title="Metropolitan bishop">metropolitan</a> <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_see" title="Episcopal see">see</a> of <a href="/wiki/Gortyna" title="Gortyna">Gortyna</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Crete" title="Crete">Crete</a>. Although he had been an opponent of Monothelitism, he nevertheless attended the <a href="/wiki/Synod#See_also" title="Synod"><i>conciliabulum</i></a> of 712, in which the decrees of the <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical_Council" class="mw-redirect" title="Ecumenical Council">Ecumenical Council</a> were abolished. In the following year, he repented and returned to <a href="/wiki/Orthodoxy" title="Orthodoxy">orthodoxy</a> and thereafter occupied himself with preaching, composing hymns, etc. As a preacher, his discourses are known for their dignified and harmonious phraseology, for which he is considered to be one of the foremost ecclesiastical orators of the Byzantine Era.
</p><p>Church historians have no consensus as to the date of his death. What is known is that he died on the island of <a href="/wiki/Mytilene" title="Mytilene">Mytilene</a>, while returning to Crete from Constantinople, where he had been on church business. His <a href="/wiki/Relics" class="mw-redirect" title="Relics">relics</a> were later <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">translated</a> to Constantinople. In 1349, the pious Russian pilgrim <a href="/wiki/Stephen_of_Novgorod" title="Stephen of Novgorod">Stephen of Novgorod</a> saw his relics at the Monastery of Saint Andrew of Crete in Constantinople. At modern Skala Eresou on <a href="/wiki/Lesbos" title="Lesbos">Lesbos</a> (ancient Eresos) is a large, Early Christian basilical church in honour of Saint Andrew.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> of Saint Andrew of Crete is <a href="/wiki/July_4_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics)" title="July 4 (Eastern Orthodox liturgics)">July 4</a> on the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_liturgical_calendar" title="Eastern Orthodox liturgical calendar">Eastern Orthodox liturgical calendar</a> (for those churches which follow the <a href="/wiki/Julian_Calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Julian Calendar">Julian Calendar</a>, July 4 falls on July 17 of the <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_Calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Gregorian Calendar">Gregorian Calendar</a>).
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Hymnography">Hymnography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Andrew_of_Crete&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Hymnography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:252px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Andrew_of_Crete_(fresco).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b9/Andrew_of_Crete_%28fresco%29.jpg/250px-Andrew_of_Crete_%28fresco%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="321" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b9/Andrew_of_Crete_%28fresco%29.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="351" data-file-height="450" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Andrew_of_Crete_(fresco).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Fresco of Andrew of Crete (XXVII century)</div></div></div>
<p>Today, Saint Andrew is primarily known as a hymnographer. He is credited with the invention (or at least the introduction) of the <a href="/wiki/Canon_(hymnography)" title="Canon (hymnography)">canon</a>, a new form of <a href="/wiki/Hymnody" title="Hymnody">hymnody</a>, into the liturgy. Previously, the portion of the <a href="/wiki/Matins" title="Matins">Matins</a> service which is now the canon was composed of chanting the nine <a href="/wiki/Canticle#Eastern_Christian" title="Canticle">biblical canticles</a>, with short refrains inserted between scriptural verses. Saint Andrew expanded these refrains into fully developed poetic <a href="/wiki/Ode" title="Ode">Odes</a>, each of which begins with the theme (<i><a href="/wiki/Irmos" title="Irmos">Irmos</a></i>) of the scriptural <a href="/wiki/Canticle" title="Canticle">canticle</a>, but then goes on to expound the theme of the feast being celebrated that day (whether the <a href="/wiki/Jesus_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesus Christ">Lord</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Theotokos" title="Theotokos">Theotokos</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>, the departed, etc.).
</p><p>His masterpiece, the <i>Great Canon</i> (also known as the <i>Canon of Repentance</i> or the <i>Great Canon of Repentance</i>), is the longest canon ever composed with 250 <a href="/wiki/Strophes" class="mw-redirect" title="Strophes">strophes</a>. It is written primarily in the first person, and goes chronologically through the entire <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old</a> and <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testaments</a> drawing examples (both negative and positive) which it correlates to the need of the sinful soul for <a href="/wiki/Repentance" title="Repentance">repentance</a> and a <a href="/wiki/Humility" title="Humility">humble</a> return to God. It is divided into four parts (called <i>methymony</i>) which are chanted at <a href="/wiki/Compline#Great_Compline" title="Compline">Great Compline</a> on the first four nights of <a href="/wiki/Great_Lent" title="Great Lent">Great Lent</a> (one part per night); later, it is chanted in its entirety at <a href="/wiki/Matins" title="Matins">Matins</a> on Thursday of the fifth week of Great Lent.
</p><p>Twenty-four canons are reputed to have been written by Saint Andrew of Crete. Of these, we can be more or less certain that he wrote fourteen, including: the canons for the <a href="/wiki/Lazarus_Saturday" title="Lazarus Saturday">Resurrection of Lazarus</a> (chanted at <a href="/wiki/Compline#Small_Compline" title="Compline">Compline</a> on the Saturday&#8212;i.e., Friday night&#8212;before <a href="/wiki/Palm_Sunday" title="Palm Sunday">Palm Sunday</a>); the <a href="/wiki/Feast_of_the_Immaculate_Conception#Eastern_Orthodoxy" title="Feast of the Immaculate Conception">Conception of St. Anne</a> (9 December); the <a href="/wiki/Woman_with_seven_sons" title="Woman with seven sons">Maccabean Martyrs</a> (1 August); St. <a href="/wiki/Ignatius_of_Antioch" title="Ignatius of Antioch">Ignatius of Antioch</a> (20 December), as well as four <a href="/wiki/Triodion" title="Triodion">Triodia</a>, and no fewer than one hundred and seven <a href="/wiki/Irmos" title="Irmos">irmoi</a>.
</p>
