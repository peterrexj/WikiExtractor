<p><b>Verena of Zurzach</b>, mostly just called <i>Saint Verena</i> (c.  260 – c.  320) is an early Christian <a href="/wiki/Consecrated_virgin" title="Consecrated virgin">consecrated virgin</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a>. She is especially venerated in <a href="/wiki/Switzerland" title="Switzerland">Switzerland</a>, where her cult is attested in <a href="/wiki/Bad_Zurzach" title="Bad Zurzach">Bad Zurzach</a>, the reported place of her burial, from at least the 5th century. She is recognized as a saint in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a> as well as in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox_Churches" title="Oriental Orthodox Churches">Oriental Orthodox Churches</a>. Her feast is on 1 September.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legend">Legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Verena_of_Zurzach&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The oldest tradition of the life of Verena is found in the so-called <i>Vita prior</i> by <a href="/wiki/Hatto_I" title="Hatto I">Hatto</a>, the abbot of <a href="/wiki/Reichenau_Abbey" title="Reichenau Abbey">Reichenau</a> (and later bishop of Mainz), written in c. 888. The younger <i>Vita posterior</i> was most likely written by a monk in Zurzach in the 11th century, the oldest extant copy dating to the 12th century.
</p><p>According to Hatto's account, Verena was born in <a href="/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt" title="Thebes, Egypt">Thebes</a> as the daughter of a notable Christian family. She was educated by a bishop named Chaeremon (<i>Vita prior</i>, ch. 3).
A bishop Chaeremon of Nilopolis is mentioned by <a href="/wiki/Eusebius" title="Eusebius">Eusebius</a> as martyred in 250, which would place Verena's birth before that date.
</p><p>After the death of Chaeremon, Verena travelled to Lower Egypt with a group of Christians, where the <a href="/wiki/Theban_Legion" title="Theban Legion">Theban Legion</a> was being recruited. With the Theban Legion, she then travelled to <a href="/wiki/Milan" title="Milan">Milan</a> (<i>Vita prior</i> ch. 4). While still in Milan, she heard of the martyrdom of the Theban Legion (an event of uncertain historicity, traditionally dated to 286, during the reign of <a href="/wiki/Maximian" title="Maximian">Maximian</a>) and travelled to <a href="/wiki/Agaunum" title="Agaunum">Agaunum</a> (<a href="/wiki/Saint-Maurice,_Switzerland" title="Saint-Maurice, Switzerland">Saint-Maurice</a>). In later sources, she is said to have buried the martyred legionnaires.
</p><p>Verena then moved on to Salodurum (<a href="/wiki/Solothurn" title="Solothurn">Solothurn</a>) in a <a href="/wiki/Hermitage_(religious_retreat)" title="Hermitage (religious retreat)">hermitage</a>, and spent her days in fasting and prayer, and working miracles. Hatto presents her as a prototype of the <a href="/wiki/Consecrated_virgin" title="Consecrated virgin">consecrated virgin</a>, telling that she attracted a following of young virgins. She was at one point imprisoned by a local governor, and Saint Maurice appeared to her in jail to console and strengthen her. After she was released from, she continued her good works.
At the end of her natural days, she retired into a narrow cave.
</p><p>The year of her death was calculated as 344 by Johannes Laurentius Huber (1812&#8211;1879), provost at Verenamünster in Zurzach.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> If her birth before 250 is accepted (based on the identification of her mentor, bishop Chaeremon), this would imply that she was more than 95 years old at the time of her death.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Verena_of_Zurzach&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

Verena among other saints in celestial glory. </p><p>Her cult became widespread from the 12th century, and Verena was one of the most venerated saints in <a href="/wiki/Medieval_Switzerland" class="mw-redirect" title="Medieval Switzerland">medieval Switzerland</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Borrelli_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Borrelli-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> A Benedictine abbey existed in the 10th century at the site of her burial in <a href="/wiki/Bad_Zurzach" title="Bad Zurzach">Bad Zurzach</a>. The monastery was replaced by a <a href="/wiki/Collegiate_church" title="Collegiate church">collegiate church</a> at some time before 1265, with Saint Verena as its patroness. In southern Germany, a chapel dedicated to her was present at the site of the minster of <a href="/wiki/Salem_Abbey" title="Salem Abbey">Salem Abbey</a> in 1137. The convent of <a href="/wiki/Beguines_and_Beghards" title="Beguines and Beghards">beguines</a> in <a href="/wiki/Z%C3%BCrich" title="Zürich">Zürich</a>, established in the mid 13th century, had a chapel dedicated to St. Verena.
</p><p>Verena is often portrayed as a matron with either bread, or a jar of water in one hand, and a comb in the other, symbols of her care for the poor and lepers.<sup id="cite_ref-Borrelli_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Borrelli-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The given name <i>Verena</i> is not recorded outside of the context of this saint; 
it has been associated with the name <a href="/wiki/Berenice" title="Berenice">Berenice</a> (i.e. <a href="/wiki/Veronica_(name)" title="Veronica (name)">Veronica</a>).
In reference to the saint, Verena came to be a commonly given feminine name in Switzerland, in hypocoristic form [Vreni" becoming an almost archetypically Swiss girls' name (c.f. diminutive "<a href="/wiki/Vreneli#History" title="Vreneli">Vreneli</a>"). 
The name <i>Verena</i> or <i>Vreneli</i> was also transferred to numerous female figures in Swiss <a href="/wiki/Pre-Christian_Alpine_traditions" title="Pre-Christian Alpine traditions">folklore and mythology</a>; notable among these is the <i>Vrenelisgärtli</i> ("Verena's garden") glacier of the <a href="/wiki/Gl%C3%A4rnisch" title="Glärnisch">Glärnisch</a> massif.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Verena_Gorge_Hermitage" title="Verena Gorge Hermitage">Verena Gorge Hermitage</a> north of Solothurn, ostensibly the site of Verena's hermitage, is known to have been in existence since the 12th century (the older of the two chapels has foundations of the 12th century). The presence of a resident hermit is recorded for 1442. The site features stations of the cross dating from 1613 (restored around 1990). In the 18th century, the gorge was developed as a landscape garden in the style of <a href="/wiki/Romanticism" title="Romanticism">Romanticism</a>, notably due to the advocacy of French diplomat Louis Auguste Le Tonnelier de Breteuil. During 1810&#8211;1813, the footpath along the gorge was further developed as partly Roman Catholic pilgrimage site, partly national romantic shrine for the patriciate of Solothurn. In a century-old tradition, the resident hermit is provided for by the <a href="/wiki/B%C3%BCrgergemeinde" title="Bürgergemeinde">Bürgergemeinde</a> of the city of Solothurn.
</p><p>The municipality of <a href="/wiki/St%C3%A4fa" title="Stäfa">Stäfa</a> at <a href="/wiki/Lake_Z%C3%BCrich" class="mw-redirect" title="Lake Zürich">Lake Zürich</a> displays Verena in its coat of arms, from the coat of arms of Stäfa <a href="/wiki/Vogt_(Switzerland)" title="Vogt (Switzerland)">bailiwick</a> in use since 1526.
</p><p>In 1986, a delegation from Saint Verena's Church in <a href="/wiki/Switzerland" title="Switzerland">Switzerland</a>, brought a part of Saint Verena's <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> to <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a>. The first <a href="/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church_of_Alexandria" class="mw-redirect" title="Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria">Coptic</a> church consecrated in the name of Saint Verena is <a href="/wiki/Saint_Maurice" title="Saint Maurice">Saint Maurice</a> and Saint Verena's Church in <a href="/wiki/Cairo" title="Cairo">Cairo</a>, which was consecrated by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Shenouda_III" class="mw-redirect" title="Pope Shenouda III">Pope Shenouda III</a> on February 22, 1994.
</p><p>In October 2004, a delegation from the Diocese of Los Angeles in the <a href="/wiki/United_States_of_America" class="mw-redirect" title="United States of America">United States of America</a>, along with <a href="/w/index.php?title=Serapion_(Coptic_bishop_of_Southern_California_and_Hawaii)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Serapion (Coptic bishop of Southern California and Hawaii) (page does not exist)">Metropolitan Serapion</a> of Los Angeles, Fr. Joseph Boules and Fr. Gregory Bishay traveled to Switzerland to bring a part of Saint Verena's relics to her churches in Anaheim and Orange. The Anaheim church, now located in <a href="/wiki/Yorba_Linda" class="mw-redirect" title="Yorba Linda">Yorba Linda</a>, California,<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> now has a shrine dedicated to her relic, as well as the church in Orange.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
