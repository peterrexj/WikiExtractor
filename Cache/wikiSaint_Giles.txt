<p><b>Saint Giles</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/dʒ/: &#39;j&#39; in &#39;jam&#39;">dʒ</span><span title="/aɪ/: &#39;i&#39; in &#39;tide&#39;">aɪ</span><span title="&#39;l&#39; in &#39;lie&#39;">l</span><span title="&#39;z&#39; in &#39;zoom&#39;">z</span></span>/</a></span></span>, <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Aegidius</i>, <a href="/wiki/French_language" title="French language">French</a>: <i lang="fr">Gilles</i>, <a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Egidio</i>), also known as <b>Giles the Hermit</b>, was a hermit or monk active in the lower <a href="/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne" title="Rhône">Rhône</a> most likely in the 6th century. Revered as a saint, his cult became widely diffused but his <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> is mostly legendary. A <a href="/wiki/Saint-Gilles,_Gard" title="Saint-Gilles, Gard">town that bears his name</a> grew up around the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint-Gilles" title="Abbey of Saint-Gilles">monastery he purportedly founded</a>, which became a <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> centre and a stop on the <a href="/wiki/Way_of_Saint_James" class="mw-redirect" title="Way of Saint James">Way of Saint James</a>. He is traditionally one of the <a href="/wiki/Fourteen_Holy_Helpers" title="Fourteen Holy Helpers">Fourteen Holy Helpers</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Historicity">Historicity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Giles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Historicity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The legend of Giles connects him to <a href="/wiki/Caesarius_of_Arles" title="Caesarius of Arles">Caesarius of Arles</a>, who died in 543. In 514, Caesarius sent a messenger, Messianus, to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Symmachus" title="Pope Symmachus">Pope Symmachus</a> in the company of an abbot named Aegidius. It is possible that this abbot is the historical figure at the basis of the legend of Saint Giles.<sup id="cite_ref-DHGE_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DHGE-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There are two forged <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">Papal bulls</a> purporting to have been issued by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_VIII" title="Pope John VIII">Pope John VIII</a> in 878.<sup id="cite_ref-DHGE_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DHGE-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Sometimes taken as authentic, they record that the <a href="/wiki/Visigoth" class="mw-redirect" title="Visigoth">Visigothic</a> king <a href="/wiki/Wamba_(king)" title="Wamba (king)">Wamba</a> founded a monastery for Giles and that <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_II" title="Pope Benedict II">Pope Benedict II</a> granted a charter to this foundation in 684–685.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In actuality, the monastery was not dedicated to Saint Giles before about 910. The tomb of Giles dates to the correct historical period, but the inscription is from the 10th century.<sup id="cite_ref-DHGE_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DHGE-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legend">Legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Giles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Giles is the subject of an elaborate and largely unhistorical anonymous <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> legend first attested in the 10th century.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Giles first lived in retreats near the mouth of the <a href="/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne" title="Rhône">Rhône</a> and by the <a href="/wiki/Gardon" title="Gardon">River Gard</a> in <a href="/wiki/Septimania" title="Septimania">Septimania</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Visigothic_Kingdom" title="Visigothic Kingdom">Visigothic Kingdom</a>. The story that he was a <a href="/wiki/Greeks" title="Greeks">Greek</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> the son of King Theodore and Queen Pelagia of <a href="/wiki/Athens" title="Athens">Athens</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> is probably an embellishment of his early hagiographers; it was given wide currency in the <i><a href="/wiki/Golden_Legend" title="Golden Legend">Legenda Aurea</a></i>. The two main incidents in his legend were often depicted in art. 
</p><p>The <i>Legenda Aurea</i> links him with <a href="/wiki/Arles" title="Arles">Arles</a>, but finally he withdrew deep into the forest near <a href="/wiki/N%C3%AEmes" title="Nîmes">Nîmes</a>, where in the greatest solitude he spent many years, his sole companion being his beloved deer, or <a href="/wiki/Red_deer" title="Red deer">red deer</a>, who in some stories sustained him on her milk.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Giles ate a Christian vegetarian diet.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> This retreat was finally discovered by the king's hunters, who had pursued the hind to its place of refuge. An arrow shot at the deer wounded the saint instead, who afterwards became a patron of the physically disabled. The king, by legend, was Wamba, an anachronistic <a href="/wiki/Visigoths" title="Visigoths">Visigoth</a>, but must have been a <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frank</a> in the original story due to the historical setting.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> He held the hermit in high esteem for his humility in rejecting all honors save having some disciples. Wamba built him a monastery in his valley, Saint-Gilles-du-Gard, which Giles placed under the <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> rule. He died there in the early part of the 8th century, with the highest repute for sanctity and <a href="/wiki/Miracle" title="Miracle">miracles</a>.
</p><p>A 10th-century <i>Vita sancti Aegidii</i> recounts that, as Giles was celebrating Mass to pardon the Emperor Charlemagne's sins, an angel deposited upon the altar a letter outlining a sin so terrible Charlemagne had never dared confess it. Several Latin and French texts, including the <i>Legenda Aurea</i> refer to this hidden "sin of Charlemagne".  This legend, however, contradicts the well established later dates for the life of Charlemagne (approximately 742 – 28 January 814).
</p><p>A later text, the <i>Liber miraculorum sancti Aegidii</i> ("The Book of Miracles of Saint Giles") served to reinforce the flow of pilgrims to the abbey.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Giles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The town of <a href="/wiki/Saint-Gilles,_Gard" title="Saint-Gilles, Gard">St-Gilles-du-Gard</a> sprang up around the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_St._Gilles" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of St. Gilles">abbey allegedly founded by him in the 7th century</a>. That abbey (which was re-dedicated to him in the 10th century) remained the center of his cult, which was particularly strong in <a href="/wiki/Languedoc" title="Languedoc">Languedoc</a>, even after a rival body of Saint Giles appeared at <a href="/wiki/Toulouse" title="Toulouse">Toulouse</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>His cult spread rapidly far and wide throughout <a href="/wiki/Europe" title="Europe">Europe</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>, as is witnessed by the churches and monasteries dedicated to him in <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>, <a href="/wiki/Spain" title="Spain">Spain</a>, <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a>, <a href="/wiki/Poland" title="Poland">Poland</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hungary" title="Hungary">Hungary</a>, <a href="/wiki/Slovakia" title="Slovakia">Slovakia</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Great_Britain" title="Great Britain">Great Britain</a>; by the numerous <a href="/wiki/Manuscript" title="Manuscript">manuscripts</a> in prose and verse commemorating his virtues and miracles; and especially by the vast concourse of <a href="/wiki/Pilgrim" title="Pilgrim">pilgrims</a> who from all Europe flocked to his shrine.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>In 1562, the <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> of the saint were secretly transferred to <a href="/wiki/Toulouse" title="Toulouse">Toulouse</a> to protect them from  the <a href="/wiki/Huguenot" class="mw-redirect" title="Huguenot">Huguenots</a> and the level of <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimages</a> declined. The restoration of most of the relics to the abbey of Saint-Gilles-du-Gard in 1862 and the publicized rediscovery of his former tomb there in 1865 helped the pilgrimages recommence.<sup id="cite_ref-Murphy_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Murphy-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Saint Giles is the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Disability" title="Disability">people with disabilities</a> and is also invoked as a saint for childhood fears, convulsions, depression, particularly in <a href="/wiki/Normandy" title="Normandy">Normandy</a>, for example in <a href="/wiki/Iville" title="Iville">Eure Iville</a>, <a href="/wiki/Saint-Germain-Village" title="Saint-Germain-Village">Saint-Germain-Village</a> or <a href="/wiki/Bernay,_Eure" title="Bernay, Eure">Bernay</a> or in <a href="/wiki/Touques,_Calvados" title="Touques, Calvados">Calvados, Gilles Touques</a>. In medieval art, he is depicted with his symbol, the hind.<sup id="cite_ref-Murphy_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Murphy-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> His emblem is also an <a href="/wiki/Arrow" title="Arrow">arrow</a>. Giles is one of the <a href="/wiki/Fourteen_Holy_Helpers" title="Fourteen Holy Helpers">Fourteen Holy Helpers</a>, and the only non-<a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a>, initially invoked as protection against the <a href="/wiki/Black_Death" title="Black Death">Black Death</a>. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is 1 September.
</p><p>Besides Saint-Gilles-du-Gard, nineteen other cities bear his name. Cities that possess relics of St. Giles include Saint-Gilles, Toulouse and many other French cities; <a href="/wiki/Antwerp" title="Antwerp">Antwerp</a>, <a href="/wiki/Brugge" class="mw-redirect" title="Brugge">Brugge</a> and <a href="/wiki/Tournai" title="Tournai">Tournai</a> in Belgium; <a href="/wiki/Cologne" title="Cologne">Cologne</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bamberg" title="Bamberg">Bamberg</a> in Germany (known as <a href="/wiki/St._Egidien_(disambiguation)" class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" title="St. Egidien (disambiguation)">Egidien</a>); <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bologna" title="Bologna">Bologna</a> in Italy;<sup id="cite_ref-Murphy_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Murphy-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Prague" title="Prague">Prague</a> in the Czech Republic; and <a href="/wiki/Esztergom" title="Esztergom">Esztergom</a> in Hungary. Giles is also the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Edinburgh" title="Edinburgh">Edinburgh</a>, the capital of Scotland, where <a href="/wiki/St._Giles%27_Cathedral" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Giles&#39; Cathedral">St. Giles' High Kirk</a> is a prominent landmark. He is also the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Graz" title="Graz">Graz</a>, <a href="/wiki/Nuremberg" title="Nuremberg">Nuremberg</a>, <a href="/wiki/Osnabr%C3%BCck" title="Osnabrück">Osnabrück</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sankt_Gilgen" class="mw-redirect" title="Sankt Gilgen">Sankt Gilgen</a>, <a href="/wiki/Braunschweig" title="Braunschweig">Brunswick</a>, <a href="/wiki/Wollaberg" title="Wollaberg">Wollaberg</a>, <a href="/wiki/Saint-Gilles,_Belgium" title="Saint-Gilles, Belgium">Saint-Gilles</a> (Brussels Capital Region) and <a href="/wiki/Sint-Gillis-Waas" title="Sint-Gillis-Waas">Sint-Gillis-Waas</a>. In 1630, the church of <a href="/wiki/Sant%27Egidio,_Rome" title="Sant&#39;Egidio, Rome">Sant'Egidio</a> in <a href="/wiki/Trastevere" title="Trastevere">Trastevere</a> in Rome was dedicated to him, and which since 1968 has housed the lay <a href="/wiki/Community_of_Sant%27Egidio" title="Community of Sant&#39;Egidio">Community of Sant'Egidio</a>.
</p><p>The centuries-long presence of <a href="/wiki/Crusades" title="Crusades">Crusaders</a>, many of them of French origin, left the name of Saint Giles in some locations in the <a href="/wiki/Middle_East" title="Middle East">Middle East</a>. <a href="/wiki/Raymond_IV_of_Toulouse" class="mw-redirect" title="Raymond IV of Toulouse">Raymond of St Gilles</a> lent his name to <a href="/wiki/Citadel_of_Raymond_de_Saint-Gilles" title="Citadel of Raymond de Saint-Gilles">St. Gilles Castle</a> (<span title="Arabic-language romanization"><i lang="ar-Latn">Qala'at Sanjil</i></span>) in <a href="/wiki/Tripoli,_Lebanon" title="Tripoli, Lebanon">Tripoli</a>, Lebanon.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Giles_of_Provence" class="mw-redirect" title="Giles of Provence">Giles of Provence</a> is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(observance)" class="mw-redirect" title="Commemoration (observance)">commemoration</a> on <a href="/wiki/September_1" title="September 1">1 September</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
