<p><b>Saint Winifred</b> (or <b>Winefride</b>; <a href="/wiki/Welsh_language" title="Welsh language">Welsh</a>: <i lang="cy">Gwenffrewi</i>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Wenefreda, Winifreda</i>) was a <a href="/wiki/Wales_in_the_Early_Middle_Ages" title="Wales in the Early Middle Ages">Welsh</a> <a href="/wiki/Virgin_martyr" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin martyr">virgin martyr</a> of the 7th century. Her story was celebrated as early as the 8th century, but became popular in England in the 12th, when her <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> was first written down.
</p><p>A healing <a href="/wiki/Spring_(hydrosphere)" class="mw-redirect" title="Spring (hydrosphere)">spring</a> at the traditional site of her decapitation and restoration is now a shrine and pilgrimage site called <a href="/wiki/St_Winefride%27s_Well" title="St Winefride&#39;s Well">St Winefride's Well</a> in <a href="/wiki/Holywell,_Flintshire" title="Holywell, Flintshire">Holywell</a>, <a href="/wiki/Flintshire" title="Flintshire">Flintshire</a>, <a href="/wiki/Wales" title="Wales">Wales</a> and known as "the <a href="/wiki/Lourdes" title="Lourdes">Lourdes</a> of Wales".
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life_and_legend">Life and legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Winifred&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life and legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Shrewsbury_Cathedral_(37121758344).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Shrewsbury_Cathedral_%2837121758344%29.jpg/220px-Shrewsbury_Cathedral_%2837121758344%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="204" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Shrewsbury_Cathedral_%2837121758344%29.jpg/330px-Shrewsbury_Cathedral_%2837121758344%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Shrewsbury_Cathedral_%2837121758344%29.jpg/440px-Shrewsbury_Cathedral_%2837121758344%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="943" data-file-height="876" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Shrewsbury_Cathedral_(37121758344).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Stained_glass" title="Stained glass">Stained glass</a> window depicting the martyrdom of Winefride (by <a href="/wiki/Margaret_Agnes_Rope" title="Margaret Agnes Rope">Margaret Agnes Rope</a>, west window, <a href="/wiki/Shrewsbury_Cathedral" title="Shrewsbury Cathedral">Shrewsbury Cathedral</a>, 1910)</div></div></div>
<p>The oldest accounts of Winifred's life date to the 12th century.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> According to legend, Winifred was the daughter of a chieftain of <a href="/wiki/Tegeingl" title="Tegeingl">Tegeingl</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-cistercian_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cistercian-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Welsh nobleman Tyfid ap Eiludd. Her mother was Wenlo, a sister of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Beuno" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Beuno">Saint Beuno</a>, and a member of a family closely connected with the kings of south Wales.<sup id="cite_ref-chandlery_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chandlery-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to legend, her suitor, Caradog, was enraged when she decided to become a nun and <a href="/wiki/Decapitation" title="Decapitation">decapitated</a> her. A healing spring appeared where her head fell.<sup id="cite_ref-rice_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rice-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Winifred's head was subsequently rejoined to her body due to the efforts of Beuno, and she was restored to life. Seeing the murderer leaning on his sword with an insolent and defiant air, Beuno invoked the chastisement of heaven, and Caradog fell dead on the spot, the popular belief being that the ground opened and swallowed him. Beuno left Holywell, and returned to <a href="/wiki/Caernarfon" title="Caernarfon">Caernarfon</a>; before he left, the tradition is that he seated himself upon a stone, which now stands in the outer well pool, and there promised in the name of God "that whosoever on that spot should thrice ask for a benefit from God in the name of St. Winefride would obtain the grace he asked if it was for the good of his soul."<sup id="cite_ref-chandlery_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chandlery-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After eight years spent at Holywell, Winifred received an inspiration to leave the convent and retire inland. Accordingly, Winifred went upon her pilgrimage to seek for a place of rest. Ultimately she arrived at Gwytherin near the source of the River Elwy.<sup id="cite_ref-chandlery_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chandlery-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> She later became a nun and <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> at <a href="/wiki/Gwytherin" title="Gwytherin">Gwytherin</a> in <a href="/wiki/Denbighshire" title="Denbighshire">Denbighshire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-rice_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rice-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> More elaborate versions of this tale relate many details of her life, including Winefride's pilgrimage to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>.
</p><p>Given the late date of the earliest surviving written accounts of Winifred's life, her existence has been doubted since the 19th century. She is not recorded in any Welsh pedigree of saints nor in the 13th-century calendar of Welsh saints.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> There is, however, evidence of her cult from centuries before the appearance of her first hagiography. Two small pieces of an oak reliquary from the 8th century were discovered in 1991 and identified based on earlier drawings as belonging to the <i>Arch Gwenfrewi</i>, the reliquary of Winifred.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The reliquary probably contained an article of clothing or another object associated with the saint, but not her bones. According to historian Lynne Heidi Stumpe, the reliquary provides "good evidence for her having been recognized as a saint very soon after her death",<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and thus of her historicity.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The reliquary may even be "the earliest surviving testimony to the formal cultus of any Welsh saint".<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Winifred&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Veneration of Winifred as a martyr saint is attested from the 12th century. She is mostly venerated in England, not in Wales, which led <a href="/wiki/Caesar_Baronius" title="Caesar Baronius">Caesar Baronius</a> to list her as an "English saint" in his <i>Roman Martyrology</i> of 1584.
</p><p>In 1138, <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> of Winifred were carried to <a href="/wiki/Shrewsbury" title="Shrewsbury">Shrewsbury</a> to form the basis of an elaborate <a href="/wiki/Shrine" title="Shrine">shrine</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-isbn1-57003-630-6_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-isbn1-57003-630-6-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/w/index.php?title=Church_of_St._Winifred,_Stainton&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Church of St. Winifred, Stainton (page does not exist)">Church of St. Winifred, Stainton</a> is a 12th century church located in the village of <a href="/wiki/Stainton,_South_Yorkshire" title="Stainton, South Yorkshire">Stainton, South Yorkshire</a>, <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Cult">Cult</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Winifred&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Cult">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Prologue_life_of_St_Winifred.png" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Prologue_life_of_St_Winifred.png/220px-Prologue_life_of_St_Winifred.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="48" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Prologue_life_of_St_Winifred.png/330px-Prologue_life_of_St_Winifred.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Prologue_life_of_St_Winifred.png/440px-Prologue_life_of_St_Winifred.png 2x" data-file-width="1736" data-file-height="380" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Prologue_life_of_St_Winifred.png" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Part of the prologue of a life of St Winifred by <a href="/wiki/Robert_of_Shrewsbury_(died_1168)" title="Robert of Shrewsbury (died 1168)">Robert of Shrewsbury</a> (Bodleian Mss. Laud c.94.)</div></div></div> 
<p>The details of Winifred's life are gathered from a manuscript in the British Museum, said to have been the work of the British monk, Elerius, a contemporary of the saint, and also from a manuscript life in the Bodleian Library, generally believed to have been compiled in 1130 by <a href="/wiki/Robert_of_Shrewsbury_(died_1168)" title="Robert of Shrewsbury (died 1168)">Robert, prior of Shrewsbury</a> (<abbr title="died">d.</abbr> 1168).<sup id="cite_ref-chandlery_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chandlery-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Prior Robert is generally credited with greatly promoting the cult of St. Winifred by translating her relics from <a href="/wiki/Gwytherin" title="Gwytherin">Gwytherin</a> to Shrewsbury Abbey and writing the most influential life of the saint.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The chronicler <a href="/wiki/John_of_Tynemouth_(chronicler)" title="John of Tynemouth (chronicler)">John of Tynemouth</a> also wrote of Winifred.
</p><p>To further enhance the prestige of the Abbey, <a href="/wiki/Abbots_of_Shrewsbury#Nicholas_Stevens_(1361_-_1399)" title="Abbots of Shrewsbury">Abbot Nicholas Stevens</a> built a new shrine for St. Winifred in the 14th century, before then having some monks steal the relics of St. Beuno from <a href="/wiki/Rhewl_(River_Dee,_Denbighshire)" class="mw-redirect" title="Rhewl (River Dee, Denbighshire)">Rhewl</a> and installed in the abbey church. Although the abbey was fined, it was allowed to keep the relics.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/William_Caxton" title="William Caxton">William Caxton</a>'s 1483 edition of the <a href="/wiki/Golden_Legend" title="Golden Legend">Golden Legend</a> includes the story of St. Winifred. The following year, he printed a separate "Life" of the saint.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Treffynnon.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/Treffynnon.JPG/220px-Treffynnon.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/Treffynnon.JPG/330px-Treffynnon.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/Treffynnon.JPG/440px-Treffynnon.JPG 2x" data-file-width="2736" data-file-height="3648" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Treffynnon.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/St_Winefride%27s_Well" title="St Winefride&#39;s Well">St Winefride's Well</a> in Holywell, one of the oldest continually visited pilgrimage sites in Britain</div></div></div>
<p>The shrine and well at Shrewsbury became major <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> goals in the <a href="/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages" title="Late Middle Ages">Late Middle Ages</a>, but the shrine was destroyed by <a href="/wiki/Henry_VIII_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Henry VIII of England">Henry VIII</a> in 1540.
</p><p>The well at Holywell, originally formed from a mountain spring, is housed below the town on the side of a steep hill. The shrine of St. Winifride (known as <span title="Welsh-language text"><i lang="cy">Gwenffrwd</i></span> or <span title="Welsh-language text"><i lang="cy">Gwenfrewi</i></span> in <a href="/wiki/Welsh_language" title="Welsh language">Welsh</a>) is regarded as one of the finest surviving examples of a medieval holy well in Britain.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2019)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> The well precinct also houses an 'Interpretive Exhibition', setting forth the story of the saint and her shrine in detail; the Victorian former custodians' house has also been converted to house a museum of the pilgrimage.<sup id="cite_ref-holywell_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-holywell-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Winifreds_Well_Woolston.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2a/St_Winifreds_Well_Woolston.JPG/220px-St_Winifreds_Well_Woolston.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2a/St_Winifreds_Well_Woolston.JPG/330px-St_Winifreds_Well_Woolston.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2a/St_Winifreds_Well_Woolston.JPG/440px-St_Winifreds_Well_Woolston.JPG 2x" data-file-width="2048" data-file-height="1536" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Winifreds_Well_Woolston.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>St Winifred's Well, a 14th-century former well chapel, <a href="/wiki/Woolston,_north_Shropshire" title="Woolston, north Shropshire">Woolston</a>, Shropshire</div></div></div>
<p>Another well named after St. Winifred is found in the hamlet of <a href="/wiki/Woolston,_north_Shropshire" title="Woolston, north Shropshire">Woolston</a> near <a href="/wiki/Oswestry" title="Oswestry">Oswestry</a> in <a href="/wiki/Shropshire" title="Shropshire">Shropshire</a>. According to legend, it is thought that on her way to <a href="/wiki/Shrewsbury_Abbey" title="Shrewsbury Abbey">Shrewsbury Abbey</a>, Winifred's body was laid there overnight and a spring sprang up out of the ground. The water is supposed to have healing powers and be good at healing bruises, wounds and broken bones.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2009)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> The well is covered by a 15th-century half-timbered cottage. The water flows through a series of stone troughs and into a large pond, which then flows into a stream. The cottage is maintained by the <a href="/wiki/Landmark_Trust" title="Landmark Trust">Landmark Trust</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-landmark_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-landmark-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Another spring supposedly arising from the laying down of Winifred's body is at Holywell Farm, midway between <a href="/wiki/Tattenhall" title="Tattenhall">Tattenhall</a> and <a href="/wiki/Clutton,_Cheshire" title="Clutton, Cheshire">Clutton</a>, <a href="/wiki/Cheshire" title="Cheshire">Cheshire</a>. There is a spring in the garden of this non-working farm which supplies two houses with their drinking water.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (July 2008)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>A spring on <a href="/wiki/Lansdown,_Bath" title="Lansdown, Bath">Lansdown Hill, Bath</a> was known as St. Winifred's Spring, and gave its name to nearby Winifreds Lane. There appears to be no known connection to the life of the saint, but its waters were once supposed to help women conceive.<sup id="cite_ref-lansdown1_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lansdown1-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-lansdown2_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lansdown2-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A Norman <a href="/wiki/Saint_Winifred%27s_Church,_Branscombe,_Devon" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Winifred&#39;s Church, Branscombe, Devon">church</a> dedicated to St. Winifred can be found in the village of <a href="/wiki/Branscombe,_Devon" class="mw-redirect" title="Branscombe, Devon">Branscombe, Devon</a>. There is some archaeological evidence to suggest an earlier <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Saxon</a> church may have occupied the site.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2009)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Roman_Martyrology"><i>Roman Martyrology</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Winifred&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Roman Martyrology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In the 2004 edition of the <i><a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a></i>, Winifred is listed under 2 November with the Latin name <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Winefrídae</i></span>. She is listed as follows: "At the spring located at Holywell in Wales, St Winefride the Virgin, who is outstanding in her witness as a nun".<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Winifred is officially recognised by the <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Vatican</a> as a person with a historical basis, who lived an exemplary religious life, but with no discussion of miracles which she may have performed or been healed by. As a 1st-millennium saint, she is recognised as a saint by popular acclaim, rather than ever being formally <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a>.
</p><p>In the current <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Liturgical_calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Liturgical calendar">liturgical calendar</a> for Wales,<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Winifred is commemorated on 3 November, since 2 November is designated as <a href="/wiki/All_Souls%27_Day" title="All Souls&#39; Day">All Souls' Day</a>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconography">Iconography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Winifred&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Iconography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Winifred's representation in stained glass at Llandyrnog and Llanasa focuses on her learning and her status as an honorary martyr, but the third aspect of her life, her religious leadership, is also commemorated visually. On the seal of the cathedral chapter of St. Asaph (now in the National Museums and Galleries of Wales, Cathays Park, Cardiff), she appears wimpled as an abbess, bearing a crozier, symbol of leadership and authority and a reliquary.<sup id="cite_ref-cistercian_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cistercian-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="References_in_fiction">References in fiction</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Winifred&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: References in fiction">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>St. Winifred's Well, termed <span title="Middle English (1100-1500)-language text"><i lang="enm">"þe Holy Hede"</i></span>, is mentioned in the medieval poem <i><a href="/wiki/Sir_Gawain_and_the_Green_Knight" title="Sir Gawain and the Green Knight">Sir Gawain and the Green Knight</a></i> (in Passus II). She also appears as a character in the <a href="/wiki/The_Green_Knight_(film)" title="The Green Knight (film)">2021 film adaptation</a> of the poem, portrayed by actress <a href="/wiki/Erin_Kellyman" title="Erin Kellyman">Erin Kellyman</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/William_Rowley" title="William Rowley">William Rowley</a>'s 17th-century comedy <i><a href="/wiki/A_Shoemaker_a_Gentleman" title="A Shoemaker a Gentleman">A Shoemaker a Gentleman</a></i> dramatises St. Winifred's story, based on the version in <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Deloney" title="Thomas Deloney">Thomas Deloney</a>'s story <i>The Gentle Craft</i> (1584).
</p><p>English poet <a href="/wiki/Gerard_Manley_Hopkins" title="Gerard Manley Hopkins">Gerard Manley Hopkins</a> memorialised St. Winifred in his unfinished drama, <i>St Winifred's Well</i>.
</p><p>The moving of Winifred's bones to Shrewsbury is fictionalised in <i><a href="/wiki/A_Morbid_Taste_for_Bones" title="A Morbid Taste for Bones">A Morbid Taste for Bones</a></i>, the first of <a href="/wiki/Ellis_Peters" class="mw-redirect" title="Ellis Peters">Ellis Peters</a>' <a href="/wiki/Brother_Cadfael" class="mw-redirect" title="Brother Cadfael">Brother Cadfael</a> novels, with the plot twist that her bones are secretly left in Wales, and someone else is put into the shrine; St. Winifred is portrayed as an important character in all the books in the Brother Cadfael series. The celebration of her <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_Saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Calendar of Saints">Feast Day</a> provides the setting for two of the novels, <i><a href="/wiki/The_Rose_Rent" title="The Rose Rent">The Rose Rent</a></i> and <i><a href="/wiki/The_Pilgrim_of_Hate" title="The Pilgrim of Hate">The Pilgrim of Hate</a></i>. The casket is stolen from its shrine in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Holy_Thief" title="The Holy Thief">The Holy Thief</a></i>, and the campaign to find and restore it propels the action. Throughout the series, the protagonist, Brother Cadfael - a Welsh monk at the English monastery at Shrewsbury - develops a "special understanding" with the saint, whom he affectionately calls "The Girl".
</p><p>Australian novelist <a href="/wiki/Gerald_Murnane" title="Gerald Murnane">Gerald Murnane</a> makes reference to St. Winifred in his novel <i><a href="/wiki/Inland_(Murnane_novel)" title="Inland (Murnane novel)">Inland</a>.</i>
</p><p>St. Winifred appears as a spirit to Sir Gawain in the 2021 movie <i><a href="/wiki/The_Green_Knight_(film)" title="The Green Knight (film)">The Green Knight</a>.</i> Winifred asks Sir Gawain to retrieve her severed head from a spring, which he does. He places the head in her bed with the rest of her skeletal remains, and she provides him with information regarding the identity of the Green Knight.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Winifred&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>A statue of St. Winifred stands overlooking the <a href="/wiki/Hudson_River" title="Hudson River">Hudson River</a> in <a href="/wiki/Hudson,_New_York" title="Hudson, New York">Hudson, New York</a>.
</p>
