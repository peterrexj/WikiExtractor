					<div id="contentSub"><div id="mw-content-subtitle"><span class="mw-redirectedfrom">(Redirected from <a href="/w/index.php?title=Victor_of_Vita&amp;redirect=no" class="mw-redirect" title="Victor of Vita">Victor of Vita</a>)</span></div></div>
					
					
					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Roman African bishop (b. ca. 430)</div>
<p><b>Victor Vitensis</b> (or Victor of Vita; born circa 430) was an African bishop of the Province of <a href="/wiki/Byzacena" title="Byzacena">Byzacena</a> (called Vitensis from his See of <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Vita" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Vita">Vita</a>). His importance rests on his <i>Historia persecutionis Africanae Provinciae, temporibus Genserici et Hunirici regum Wandalorum</i> (<i>A History of the African Province Persecution, in the Times of Genseric and Huneric, the Kings of the Vandals</i>).
</p><p>Divided into three books, the work is a predominantly contemporary narrative of the cruelties practiced against the orthodox Nicene Christians of Northern Africa by the <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arian</a> <a href="/wiki/Vandals" title="Vandals">Vandals</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The first book provides an account of the reign of <a href="/wiki/Gaiseric" title="Gaiseric">Gaiseric</a>, from the Vandal invasion of Africa in 429 until the king's death in 477; whilst, the second and third record events of <a href="/wiki/Huneric" title="Huneric">Huneric</a>'s reign (477–484) – of which Victor was an eyewitness. Andy Merrills and Richard Miles have argued that with the work Victor 'created a coherent narrative of a Vandal persecution where previously there had been none'.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Little is known of the author or his circumstances and so historians have put forward deductions based on the internal evidence of his work. It has been argued by John Moorhead that Victor wrote the <i>Historia persecutionis</i> whilst he was a priest in <a href="/wiki/Carthage" title="Carthage">Carthage</a> and that he 'had access to the archives of the See of Carthage'.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The 'text originated in the Church of Carthage' and was published circa 488; however, scholars contend that Victor wrote much of his work in 484 but subsequently added perspectives from after Huneric's death.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>   Danuta Shanzer has argued that the use of medical terminology in Victor's descriptions of torture indicate that he may have had a medical background.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> It is contested in academic discourse whether or not Victor was actually the bishop of Vita or simply born there.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Victor throws much light on social and religious conditions in Carthage and on the <a href="/wiki/African_Rite" title="African Rite">African liturgy</a> of <a href="/wiki/Late_Roman_Empire" class="mw-redirect" title="Late Roman Empire">the period</a>, portraying 'the Vandals as being implacably and violently opposed to the true Catholic Faith'.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> His history contains many documents not otherwise accessible, e.g. the Confession of Faith drawn up for the orthodox bishops by <a href="/wiki/Eugenius_of_Carthage" title="Eugenius of Carthage">Eugenius of Carthage</a> and presented to <a href="/wiki/Huneric" title="Huneric">Huneric</a> at the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Carthage_(484)" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Carthage (484)">conference in 484</a> of <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Catholic</a> and <a href="/wiki/Arian" class="mw-redirect" title="Arian">Arian</a> bishops. Two documents: a <i>Passio beatissimorum martyrum qui apud Carthaginem passi sunt sub impio rege Hunerico (die VI. Non. Julias 484)</i> and a <i><a href="/wiki/Notitia_Provinciarum_et_Civitatum_Africae" class="mw-redirect" title="Notitia Provinciarum et Civitatum Africae">Notitia Provinciarum et Civitatum Africae</a></i> (List of the Provinces and Cities of Africa), formerly appended to all the manuscripts and now incorporated in the printed editions, are probably not Victor's. The former may be the work of one of his contemporaries.  The latter is a list of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishops</a> who were summoned to the 484 conference and their <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_see" title="Episcopal see">Episcopal sees</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_province" title="Roman province">Latin provinces</a> of North Africa, arranged according to provinces in this order: <a href="/wiki/Africa_Proconsularis" class="mw-redirect" title="Africa Proconsularis">Africa Proconsularis</a>, <a href="/wiki/Numidia" title="Numidia">Numidia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Byzacena" title="Byzacena">Byzacena</a>, <a href="/wiki/Mauretania_Caesariensis" title="Mauretania Caesariensis">Mauretania Caesariensis</a>, <a href="/wiki/Mauretania_Sitifensis" title="Mauretania Sitifensis">Mauretania Sitifensis</a>, <a href="/wiki/Tripolitana" class="mw-redirect" title="Tripolitana">Tripolitana</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sardinia" title="Sardinia">Sardinia</a>.
</p>
