<p><b>Saint Ursula</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> for 'little female bear', German: <i lang="de">Heilige Ursula</i>) is a legendary <a href="/wiki/Romano-British_culture" title="Romano-British culture">Romano-British</a> <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> who died on 21 October 383 or 385. Her <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> in the pre-1970 <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> is 21 October. There is little information about her and the anonymous group of holy virgins who accompanied and, on an uncertain date, were killed along with her at <a href="/wiki/Cologne" title="Cologne">Cologne</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> They remain in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> although their commemoration does not appear in the simplified Calendarium Romanum Generale (<a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a>) of the 1970 Missale Romanum.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The earliest evidence of a cult of martyred virgins at Cologne is an inscription from c. 400 in the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_St._Ursula,_Cologne" title="Basilica of St. Ursula, Cologne">Church of St. Ursula</a>, located on Ursulaplatz in Cologne which states that the ancient basilica had been restored on the site where some holy virgins were killed. The earliest source to name one of these virgins Ursula is from the 10th century.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Her legendary status comes from a medieval story<sup id="cite_ref-Poncelet_1912_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Poncelet_1912-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> in which she was a princess who, at the request of her father King <a href="/wiki/Dionotus" title="Dionotus">Dionotus</a> of <a href="/wiki/Dumnonia" title="Dumnonia">Dumnonia</a> in south-west Britain, set sail along with 11,000 virginal handmaidens to join her future husband, the pagan governor <a href="/wiki/Conan_Meriadoc" title="Conan Meriadoc">Conan Meriadoc</a> of <a href="/wiki/Armorica" title="Armorica">Armorica</a>. After a miraculous storm brought them over the sea in a single day to a Gaulish port, Ursula declared that before her marriage she would undertake a pan-European <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a>. She headed for Rome with her followers and persuaded the Pope, Cyriacus (unknown in the pontifical records, though from late 384 AD there was a <a href="/wiki/Pope_Siricius" title="Pope Siricius">Pope Siricius</a>), and Sulpicius, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Ravenna" title="Bishop of Ravenna">bishop of Ravenna</a>, to join them. After setting out for Cologne, which was being besieged by <a href="/wiki/Huns" title="Huns">Huns</a>, all the virgins were beheaded in a massacre. The Huns' leader fatally shot Ursula with an arrow in about 383 AD (the date varies).
</p><p>There is only one church dedicated to Saint Ursula in the United Kingdom. It is located in Wales at <a href="/wiki/Llangwyryfon" title="Llangwyryfon">Llangwyryfon</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ceredigion" title="Ceredigion">Ceredigion</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (July 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Islands" title="Virgin Islands">Virgin Islands</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Ursulines" title="Ursulines">Ursulines</a> are named in her honor.
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Lack_of_historical_credibility">Lack of historical credibility</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Lack of historical credibility">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <i><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia" title="Catholic Encyclopedia">Catholic Encyclopedia</a></i> (1912) article on Saint Ursula states that "this legend, with its countless variants and increasingly fabulous developments, would fill more than a hundred pages. Various characteristics of it were already regarded with suspicion by certain medieval writers, and since <a href="/wiki/Caesar_Baronius" title="Caesar Baronius">[Caesar] Baronius</a> have been universally rejected".<sup id="cite_ref-Poncelet_1912_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Poncelet_1912-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Neither <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> nor <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Tours" title="Gregory of Tours">Gregory of Tours</a> refers to Ursula in his writings.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Gregory of Tours mentions the legend of the <a href="/wiki/Theban_Legion" title="Theban Legion">Theban Legion</a>, to whom a church that once stood in Cologne was dedicated.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911803_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911803-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The most important hagiographers (<a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ado_(archbishop)" class="mw-redirect" title="Ado (archbishop)">Ado</a>, <a href="/wiki/Usuard" title="Usuard">Usuard</a>, <a href="/wiki/Notker_the_Stammerer" title="Notker the Stammerer">Notker the Stammerer</a>, <a href="/wiki/Rabanus_Maurus" title="Rabanus Maurus">Hrabanus Maurus</a>) of the early <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a> also do not enter Ursula under 21 October, her feast day.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911803_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911803-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Tenth-century_legend">Tenth-century legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Tenth-century legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>A legend resembling Ursula's appeared in the first half of the tenth century; it does not mention the name <i>Ursula</i>, but rather gives the leader of the martyred group as <i>Pinnosa</i> or <i>Vinnosa</i>. Pinnosa's relics were transferred about 947 from Cologne to Essen,<sup id="cite_ref-Montgomery_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Montgomery-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> and from this point forward Ursula's role was emphasised.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911803_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911803-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> In 970, for example, the first <i>Passio Ursulae</i> was written, naming Ursula rather than Pinnosa as the group's leader (although Pinnosa is mentioned as one of the group's members). This change might also be due in part to the discovery at this time of an epitaph speaking of Ursula, the "innocent virgin".<sup id="cite_ref-Montgomery_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Montgomery-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Misreading_of_Latin">Misreading of Latin</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Misreading of Latin">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>

<p>According to <a href="/wiki/Geoffrey_of_Monmouth" title="Geoffrey of Monmouth">Geoffrey of Monmouth</a>, a 12th-century British cleric and writer, Ursula was the daughter of <a href="/wiki/Dionotus" title="Dionotus">Dionotus</a>, ruler of Cornwall. However, this may have been based on his misreading of the words <i>Deo notus</i> in the second <i>Passio Ursulae</i>, written about 1105. The plot may have been influenced by a story told by the 6th-century writer <a href="/wiki/Procopius" title="Procopius">Procopius</a> about a British queen sailing with 100,000 soldiers to the mouth of the Rhine in order to compel her unwilling groom Radigis, king of the <a href="/wiki/Warini" title="Warini">Varni</a>, to marry her.
</p><p>While there was a tradition of virgin martyrs in Cologne by the fifth century, their number may have been limited to between two and eleven, according to different sources. Yet the cleric <a href="/wiki/Wandelbert" title="Wandelbert">Wandelbert</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Pr%C3%BCm" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of Prüm">Abbey of Prüm</a> stated in his <a href="/wiki/Martyrology" title="Martyrology">martyrology</a> in 848 that the number of martyrs counted "thousands of saints" who were slaughtered on the boards of the River Rhine.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The figure of 11,000 first appears in the late-9th century; suggestions as to where this number came from have included the reading of a name <i>Undecimillia</i> or <i>Ximillia</i> as a number, or reading the letters <i>XI. M. V.</i> as 'eleven thousand [in Roman numerals] virgins' rather than as 'eleven martyred virgins'. One scholar has suggested that in the eighth or ninth century, when the relics of virgin martyrs were found, they included those of a girl named Ursula, who was eleven years old—in <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a>, <i>undecimilia</i>. This was subsequently misread or misinterpreted as <i>undicimila</i> ('eleven thousand'), thus producing the legend of the 11,000 virgins.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> In fact, the stone bearing the virgin Ursula's name states that she lived eight years and two months. Another theory suggests that there was only one virgin martyr, named Undecimilla, "which by some blundering monk was changed into eleven thousand".<sup id="cite_ref-rochester1_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rochester1-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
It has also been suggested that <i>cum [...] militibus</i>, "with [...] soldiers", was misread as <i>cum [...] millibus</i>, "with [...] thousands".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911804_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911804-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Most contemporary sources, however, cling to the number 11,000. The <i>Passio</i> from the 970s tries to bridge conflicting traditions by stating that the eleven maidens each commanded a ship containing one thousand virgins. Implicitly, the legend also refers to the twelve heavenly legions, mentioned in Matthew 26:53.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Skeletal_remains">Skeletal remains</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Skeletal remains">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Ursula_bones.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/St_Ursula_bones.jpg/220px-St_Ursula_bones.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="138" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/St_Ursula_bones.jpg/330px-St_Ursula_bones.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/St_Ursula_bones.jpg/440px-St_Ursula_bones.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4985" data-file-height="3132" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Ursula_bones.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>One of the walls of bones in the Golden Chamber</div></div></div>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_St._Ursula,_Cologne" title="Basilica of St. Ursula, Cologne">Basilica of St. Ursula in Cologne</a> holds the alleged <a href="/wiki/Relics" class="mw-redirect" title="Relics">relics</a> of Ursula and her 11,000 companions.<sup id="cite_ref-rochester1_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rochester1-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> It contains what has been described as a "veritable tsunami of ribs, shoulder blades, and femurs ... arranged in zigzags and swirls and even in the shapes of Latin words".<sup id="cite_ref-autogenerated169_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated169-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The Goldene Kammer (Golden Chamber), a 17th-century chapel attached to the Basilica of St. Ursula, contains sculptures of their heads and torsos, "some of the heads encased in silver, others covered with stuff of gold and caps of cloth of gold and velvet; loose bones thickly texture the upper walls".<sup id="cite_ref-rochester1_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rochester1-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-autogenerated169_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated169-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The peculiarities of the relics themselves have thrown doubt upon the historicity of Ursula and her 11,000 maidens. When skeletons of little children ranging in age from two months to seven years were found buried with one of the sacred virgins in 1183, <a href="/wiki/Hermann_Joseph" class="mw-redirect" title="Hermann Joseph">Saint Hermann Joseph</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Praemonstratensian" class="mw-redirect" title="Praemonstratensian">Praemonstratensian</a> <a href="/wiki/Canon_(priest)" class="mw-redirect" title="Canon (priest)">canon</a> at Steinfeld, explained that they were distant relatives of the eleven thousand.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911804_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArcherGrieve1911804-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> A surgeon of eminence was once banished from Cologne for suggesting that, among the collection of bones which are said to pertain to the heads, there were several belonging to full-grown <a href="/wiki/English_Mastiff" title="English Mastiff">mastiffs</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-rochester1_13-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rochester1-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The relics may have come from a forgotten burial ground.<sup id="cite_ref-ecole_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ecole-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Catholic_official_stance">Catholic official stance</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Catholic official stance">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Nothing reliable is known about the girls said to have been martyred at the spot. A <a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(liturgy)" title="Commemoration (liturgy)">commemoration</a> of Saint Ursula and her companions in the <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">Mass</a> of Saint <a href="/wiki/Hilarion" title="Hilarion">Hilarion</a>, formerly in the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> on 21 October, was removed in 1969, because "their <i>Passio</i> is entirely fabulous: nothing, not even their names, is known about the virgin saints who were killed at Cologne at some uncertain time".<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> However, they are still mentioned in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a>, the official but professedly incomplete list of saints recognised by the Catholic Church, which speaks of them as follows: "At Cologne in Germany, commemoration of virgin saints who ended their life in martyrdom for Christ in the place where afterwards the city's basilica was built, dedicated in honour of the innocent young girl Ursula who is looked on as their leader".<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Catholic_order">Catholic order</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Catholic order">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<ul><li>The <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Ursulines" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of Ursulines">Order of Ursulines</a>, founded in 1535 by <a href="/wiki/Angela_Merici" title="Angela Merici">Angela Merici</a>, and devoted to the <a href="/wiki/Education_of_young_girls" class="mw-redirect" title="Education of young girls">education of young girls</a>, has also helped to spread Ursula's name throughout the world.<sup id="cite_ref-Poncelet_1912_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Poncelet_1912-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> St. Ursula was named the patron saint of school girls.</li></ul>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Celebrations">Celebrations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Celebrations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<ul><li>The town of <a href="/wiki/Binangonan" title="Binangonan">Binangonan</a> in the province of Rizal in the Philippines also has a church dedicated to St. Ursula, where her feast is celebrated on 21 October. A fluvial procession at Laguna Lake is carried out to commemorate Ursula's journey. It is made up of a group of men and women in colourful, traditional Filipino costumes dancing in the streets with the image of Ursula and chanting joyfully. Prior to her feast day, a nine-day <a href="/wiki/Novena" title="Novena">novena</a> is held at the 224-year-old Santa Ursula Church. During this novena, a woman is assigned as cantor to sing a chant in honour of Ursula.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup></li>
<li>The town of <a href="/wiki/Valls" title="Valls">Valls</a>, Catalonia, Spain, celebrates the "<span title="Catalan-language text"><i lang="ca">Fira de Santa Úrsula</i></span>" in the days around 21 October.<sup id="cite_ref-festes.208.2022.10.20_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-festes.208.2022.10.20-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> The fair concludes on the Sunday after 21 October with "one of the most important" <a href="/wiki/Castell" title="Castell">castell</a> (human tower) competitions.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Church_music_and_art">Church music and art</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Church music and art">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<ul><li><a href="/wiki/Hildegard_of_Bingen" title="Hildegard of Bingen">Hildegard of Bingen</a> composed many chants in honour of Ursula.</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Michael_Haydn" title="Michael Haydn">Michael Haydn</a> wrote the <i><a href="/wiki/Missa_in_honorem_Sanctae_Ursulae" title="Missa in honorem Sanctae Ursulae">Missa in honorem Sanctae Ursulae</a></i> to commemorate the day Ursula Oswald joined a Benedictine Abbey.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2012)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Hans_Memling" title="Hans Memling">Hans Memling</a> fashioned during the 1480s a <a href="/wiki/Shrine_of_St_Ursula" class="mw-redirect" title="Shrine of St Ursula">wooden shrine that contained the relics of Ursula</a>, which is now at the <a href="/wiki/Hans_Memling_Museum" class="mw-redirect" title="Hans Memling Museum">Hans Memling Museum</a> in <a href="/wiki/Bruges" title="Bruges">Bruges</a>. It tells the story of Ursula in six bow-arched panels, with the two front panels showing Ursula accompanied by 10 virgins, each representing 1,000 virgins.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>There is also a painting by <a href="/wiki/Caravaggio" title="Caravaggio">Caravaggio</a>, <i><a href="/wiki/The_Martyrdom_of_Saint_Ursula_(Caravaggio)" title="The Martyrdom of Saint Ursula (Caravaggio)">The Martyrdom of Saint Ursula</a></i> in the Gallaria d'italia in Naples. His last known work.</li></ul>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Places_named_after_her_companions">Places named after her companions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Places named after her companions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<ul><li><a href="/wiki/Christopher_Columbus" title="Christopher Columbus">Christopher Columbus</a> named the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Islands" title="Virgin Islands">Virgin Islands</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Caribbean" title="Caribbean">Caribbean</a> in her honour when sailing past them in 1493.</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Ferdinand_Magellan" title="Ferdinand Magellan">Ferdinand Magellan</a> rounded <a href="/wiki/Cape_Virgenes" title="Cape Virgenes">Cape Virgenes</a> on 21 October 1520, and entered the <a href="/wiki/Straits_of_Magellan" class="mw-redirect" title="Straits of Magellan">Straits of Magellan</a>, naming the cape after Ursula's virgins.</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Jo%C3%A3o_%C3%81lvares_Fagundes" title="João Álvares Fagundes">João Álvares Fagundes</a>, a Portuguese explorer, gave in 1521 the name <i>Eleven Thousand Virgins</i> to what is now known as <a href="/wiki/Saint-Pierre_and_Miquelon" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint-Pierre and Miquelon">Saint-Pierre and Miquelon</a>.</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Basel" title="Basel">Basel</a>, a Swiss city about 400&#160;km south of Cologne, has tradition of Ursula and her companions passing through Basel intending to go to Rome. The legend gave name to the Eleven Thousand Virgins Alley (<i>Elftausendjungfern-Gässlein</i>), which climbs one side of the Münsterberg, a hill in the center of the city.</li></ul>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="UK_and_Anglican_Church">UK and Anglican Church</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: UK and Anglican Church">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<ul><li>The small village of <a href="/wiki/Llangwyryfon" title="Llangwyryfon">Llangwyryfon</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Aberystwyth" title="Aberystwyth">Aberystwyth</a> in west Wales, has a church dedicated to Ursula. The village name translates as 'Church of the Virgins'. She is believed to have come from this area.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (July 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup></li>
<li>There are Anglican churches dedicated to St. Ursula in the United Kingdom, Switzerland, the United States and the Caribbean.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>The street in London called <a href="/wiki/St_Mary_Axe" title="St Mary Axe">St Mary Axe</a> is named after the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_St_Mary_Axe" title="Church of St Mary Axe">Church of St Mary Axe</a>, originally dedicated to St. Mary the Virgin, St. Ursula and the 11,000 virgins. It was demolished in the late 16th century; the site is located close to where the skyscraper informally known as <i><a href="/wiki/30_St_Mary_Axe" title="30 St Mary Axe">The Gherkin</a></i> now stands. A manuscript dated 1514 claims that the church contained a <a href="/wiki/Holy_relic" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy relic">holy relic</a>: an <a href="/wiki/Axe" title="Axe">axe</a> used by the Huns to execute the virgins.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Whitelands_College" title="Whitelands College">Whitelands College</a> in <a href="/wiki/Roehampton" title="Roehampton">Roehampton</a>, London, the oldest educational institution of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a>, has been under the patronage of St. Ursula since its formation. She is the patron saint of the college's chapel.</li></ul>

<h2><span id="Cordula.2C_Ursula.27s_companion"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Cordula,_Ursula's_companion">Cordula, Ursula's companion</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: Cordula, Ursula&#039;s companion">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Cordula was, according to a legend in an edition of the <i><a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a></i> presented in an English translation on a <a href="/wiki/Traditionalist_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Traditionalist Catholic">traditionalist Catholic</a> website,<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> one of Ursula's companions: "Being terrified by the punishments and slaughter of the others, Cordula hid herself, but repenting her deed, on the next day she declared herself to the Huns of her own accord, and thus was the last of them all to receive the crown of martyrdom". In his <i>Albert the Great</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> Joachim Sighart recounts that, on 14 February 1277, while work was being done at the church of St John the Baptist (<i>Johanniterkirche</i>) in Cologne, Cordula's body was discovered; it was fragrant and on her forehead was written: <i>Cordula, Queen and Virgin</i>. When <a href="/wiki/Albert_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Albert the Great">Albert the Great</a> heard of the finding, he sang mass and <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relics)" class="mw-redirect" title="Translation (relics)">transferred</a> the relics. Later, Cordula's supposed remains were moved to <a href="/wiki/K%C3%B6nigswinter" title="Königswinter">Königswinter</a> and <a href="/wiki/Rimini" title="Rimini">Rimini</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Cordula's head was claimed by the Cathedral of <a href="/wiki/Palencia" title="Palencia">Palencia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> She is listed in the <i>Roman Martyrology</i> on 22 October.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Similarities_with_Sunniva">Similarities with Sunniva</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Ursula&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: Similarities with Sunniva">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There are striking parallels between the 11th-century legend of Ursula and the story of <a href="/wiki/Sunniva" title="Sunniva">Sunniva of Selje</a>. Their names were sometimes confused by contemporaries. Both saints were considered to be Christian princesses who fled their homeland by ship in order to postpone or avert an undesired marriage with a pagan king. Both were accompanied by a large group of associates, both became victims of hostile foes. The development of their legends may have been interdependent. The martyrdom of Sunniva, however, took place after the first draft of the <i>Passio Ursulae</i>.
</p>
