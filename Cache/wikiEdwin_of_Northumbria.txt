<p><b>Edwin</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a>&#58; <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">Ēadwine</i></span>; c. 586&#160;– 12 October 632/633), also known as <b>Eadwine</b> or <b>Æduinus</b>, was the <a href="/wiki/List_of_monarchs_of_Northumbria" title="List of monarchs of Northumbria">King</a> of <a href="/wiki/Deira" title="Deira">Deira</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bernicia" title="Bernicia">Bernicia</a>&#160;– which later became known as <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbria</a>&#160;– from about 616 until his death. He converted to Christianity and was <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptised</a> in 627. After he fell at the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Hatfield_Chase" title="Battle of Hatfield Chase">Battle of Hatfield Chase</a>, he was venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>.
</p><p>Edwin was the son of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lla_of_Deira" title="Ælla of Deira">Ælle</a>, the first known king of <a href="/wiki/Deira" title="Deira">Deira</a>, and seems to have had at least two siblings. His sister <a href="/wiki/Acha_of_Deira" title="Acha of Deira">Acha</a> was married to <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelfrith_of_Bernicia" class="mw-redirect" title="Æthelfrith of Bernicia">Æthelfrith</a>, king of neighbouring <a href="/wiki/Bernicia" title="Bernicia">Bernicia</a>. An otherwise unknown sibling fathered Hereric, who in turn fathered Abbess <a href="/wiki/Hilda_of_Whitby" title="Hilda of Whitby">Hilda of Whitby</a> and Hereswith, wife to Æthelric, the brother of king <a href="/wiki/Anna_of_East_Anglia" title="Anna of East Anglia">Anna of East Anglia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_exile">Early life and exile</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edwin_of_Northumbria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life and exile">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> reported that on Ælle's death a certain "<a href="/wiki/Aethelric_of_Deira" class="mw-redirect" title="Aethelric of Deira">Æthelric</a>" assumed power. The exact identity of Æthelric is uncertain. He may have been a brother of Ælle, an elder brother of Edwin, an otherwise unknown Deiran noble, or the father of Æthelfrith. Æthelfrith himself appears to have been king of "<a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbria</a>"—both Deira and Bernicia—by no later than 604.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> During the reign of Æthelfrith, Edwin was an exile. The location of his early exile as a child is not known, but late traditions, reported by <a href="/wiki/Reginald_of_Durham" title="Reginald of Durham">Reginald of Durham</a> and <a href="/wiki/Geoffrey_of_Monmouth" title="Geoffrey of Monmouth">Geoffrey of Monmouth</a>, place Edwin in the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Gwynedd" title="Kingdom of Gwynedd">kingdom of Gwynedd</a>, fostered by king <a href="/wiki/Cadfan_ap_Iago" title="Cadfan ap Iago">Cadfan ap Iago</a>, so allowing biblical parallels to be drawn from the struggle between Edwin and his supposed foster-brother <a href="/wiki/Cadwallon_ap_Cadfan" title="Cadwallon ap Cadfan">Cadwallon</a>. By the 610s he was certainly in Mercia under the protection of king <a href="/wiki/Cearl_of_Mercia" title="Cearl of Mercia">Cearl</a>, whose daughter Cwenburg he married.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>By around 616, Edwin was in East Anglia under the protection of king <a href="/wiki/R%C3%A6dwald_of_East_Anglia" title="Rædwald of East Anglia">Rædwald</a>. <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> reports that Æthelfrith tried to have Rædwald murder his unwanted rival, and that Rædwald intended to do so until his wife persuaded him otherwise with Divine prompting.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>  Æthelfrith faced Rædwald in <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_the_River_Idle" title="Battle of the River Idle">battle by the River Idle</a> in 616, and Æthelfrith was defeated; Rædwald installed Edwin as king of Northumbria. Rædwald's son Rægenhere may have been killed at this battle, but the exact date or manner of Rædwald's death are not known. He likely died between the years 616–627, and the efficacy of Edwin's kingship ostensibly depended greatly on his fealty to Rædwald.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edwin was installed as king of Northumbria, effectively confirming Rædwald as <i><a href="/wiki/Bretwalda" title="Bretwalda">bretwalda</a></i>: Æthelfrith's sons went into exile in <a href="/wiki/Gaels" title="Gaels">Gaelic</a> kingdom of <a href="/wiki/D%C3%A1l_Riata" title="Dál Riata">Dál Riata</a> and <a href="/wiki/Picts" title="Picts">Pictland</a>. That Edwin was able to take power not only in his native Deira but also in Bernicia may have been due to his support from Rædwald, to whom he may have remained subject during the early part of his reign. Edwin's reign marks an interruption of the otherwise consistent domination of Northumbria by the Bernicians and has been seen as "contrary to the prevailing tendency".<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="As_king">As king</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edwin_of_Northumbria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: As king">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg/200px-Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg.png" decoding="async" width="200" height="248" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg/300px-Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg/400px-Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="994" data-file-height="1233" /></a><figcaption>The main Anglo-Saxon Kingdoms in Edwin's time.</figcaption></figure>
<p>With the death of Æthelfrith, and of the powerful <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelberht_of_Kent" title="Æthelberht of Kent">Æthelberht of Kent</a> the same year, Rædwald and his client Edwin were well placed to dominate England, and indeed Rædwald did so until his death a decade later. Edwin expelled <a href="/wiki/Ceretic_of_Elmet" title="Ceretic of Elmet">Ceretic</a> from the minor <a href="/wiki/Brython" class="mw-redirect" title="Brython">British</a> kingdom of <a href="/wiki/Elmet" title="Elmet">Elmet</a> in either 616 or 626. Elmet had probably been subject to Mercia and then to Edwin.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The  larger <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Lindsey" title="Kingdom of Lindsey">kingdom of Lindsey</a> appears to have been taken over c. 625, after the death of king Rædwald.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Edwin and <a href="/wiki/Eadbald_of_Kent" title="Eadbald of Kent">Eadbald of Kent</a> were allies at this time, and Edwin arranged to marry Eadbald's sister <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelburg_of_Kent" class="mw-redirect" title="Æthelburg of Kent">Æthelburg</a>. Bede notes that Eadbald would agree to marry his sister to Edwin only if he converted to Christianity. The marriage of Eadbald's <a href="/wiki/Merovingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingian">Merovingian</a> mother <a href="/wiki/Berthe" class="mw-redirect" title="Berthe">Bertha</a> had resulted in the conversion of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent" title="Kingdom of Kent">Kent</a> and Æthelburg's would do the same in Northumbria.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edwin's expansion to the west may have begun early in his reign. There is firm evidence of a war waged in the early 620s between Edwin and <a href="/wiki/Fiachnae_mac_B%C3%A1et%C3%A1in" class="mw-redirect" title="Fiachnae mac Báetáin">Fiachnae mac Báetáin</a> of the <a href="/wiki/D%C3%A1l_nAraidi" title="Dál nAraidi">Dál nAraidi</a>, king of the <a href="/wiki/Ulaid" title="Ulaid">Ulaid</a> in Ireland. A lost poem is known to have existed recounting Fiachnae's campaigns against the Saxons, and the <a href="/wiki/Irish_annals" title="Irish annals">Irish annals</a> report the siege, or the storming, of <a href="/wiki/Bamburgh" title="Bamburgh">Bamburgh</a> in Bernicia in 623–624. This should presumably be placed in the context of Edwin's designs on the <a href="/wiki/Isle_of_Man" title="Isle of Man">Isle of Man</a>, a target of Ulaid ambitions. Fiachnae's death in 626, at the hands of his namesake, Fiachnae mac Demmáin of the <a href="/wiki/D%C3%A1l_Fiatach" title="Dál Fiatach">Dál Fiatach</a>, and the second Fiachnae's death a year later in battle against the Dál Riata probably eased the way for Edwin's conquests in the Irish sea province.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The routine of kingship in Edwin's time involved regular, probably annual, wars with neighbours to obtain tribute, submission, and slaves. By Edwin's death, it is likely that these annual wars, unreported in the main, had extended the Northumbrian kingdoms from the <a href="/wiki/Humber" title="Humber">Humber</a> and the <a href="/wiki/River_Mersey" title="River Mersey">Mersey</a> north to the <a href="/wiki/Southern_Uplands" title="Southern Uplands">Southern Uplands</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Cheviots" class="mw-redirect" title="Cheviots">Cheviots</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The royal household moved regularly from one <a href="/wiki/Royal_vill" title="Royal vill">royal vill</a> to the next, consuming the <a href="/wiki/Food_render" title="Food render">food renders</a> given in tribute and the produce of the royal estates, dispensing justice, and ensuring that royal authority remained visible throughout the land. The royal sites in Edwin's time included <a href="/wiki/Yeavering" title="Yeavering">Yeavering</a> in Bernicia, where traces of a timber <a href="/wiki/Amphitheatre" title="Amphitheatre">amphitheatre</a> have been found. This "Roman" feature makes Bede's claim that Edwin was preceded by a standard-bearer carrying a "tufa" (<a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language"><i>OE</i></a> <i>thuuf</i>, this may have been a winged globe) appear to be more than antiquarian curiosity, although whether the model for this practice was Roman or Frankish is unknown. Other royal sites included <i>Campodunum</i> in Elmet (perhaps <a href="/wiki/Barwick-in-Elmet" title="Barwick-in-Elmet">Barwick</a>), <a href="/wiki/Sancton" title="Sancton">Sancton</a> in Deira, and <a href="/wiki/Goodmanham" title="Goodmanham">Goodmanham</a>, the site where the pagan high priest <a href="/wiki/Coifi" title="Coifi">Coifi</a> destroyed the idols according to Bede.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Edwin's realm included the former Roman cities of <a href="/wiki/York" title="York">York</a> and <a href="/wiki/Carlisle,_Cumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Carlisle, Cumbria">Carlisle</a>, and both appear to have been of some importance in the 7th century, although it is not clear whether urban life continued in this period.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Conversion_to_Christianity">Conversion to Christianity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edwin_of_Northumbria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Conversion to Christianity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Edwin_-_John_Speed.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Edwin_-_John_Speed.JPG/160px-Edwin_-_John_Speed.JPG" decoding="async" width="160" height="153" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Edwin_-_John_Speed.JPG/240px-Edwin_-_John_Speed.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Edwin_-_John_Speed.JPG/320px-Edwin_-_John_Speed.JPG 2x" data-file-width="605" data-file-height="577" /></a><figcaption>Depiction of Edwin from <a href="/wiki/John_Speed" title="John Speed">John Speed</a>'s 1611 "<a href="/wiki/History_of_Anglo-Saxon_England#Anglo-Saxon_England_heptarchy" title="History of Anglo-Saxon England">Saxon Heptarchy</a>".</figcaption></figure>
<p>The account of Edwin's conversion offered by Bede turns on two events. The first, during Edwin's exile, tells how Edwin's life was saved by <a href="/wiki/Paulinus_of_York" title="Paulinus of York">Paulinus of York</a>. The second, following his marriage to Æthelburg, was the attempted assassination at <a href="/wiki/York" title="York">York</a>, at Easter 626, by an agent of <a href="/wiki/Cwichelm_of_Wessex" title="Cwichelm of Wessex">Cwichelm of Wessex</a>. Apart from these events, the general character of Bede's account is one of an indecisive king, unwilling to take risks, unable to decide whether to convert or not.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Along with these events, the influence of Edwin's half-Merovingian Queen cannot be ignored, and the letters which Bede reproduces, sent by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_V" title="Pope Boniface V">Pope Boniface V</a> to Edwin and Æthelburg, are unlikely to have been unique. Given that Kent was under Frankish influence, while Bede sees the mission as being "Roman" in origin, the Franks were equally interested in converting their fellow Germans and in extending their power and influence.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Bede recounts Edwin's baptism, and that of his chief men, on 12 April 627.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Edwin's zeal, so Bede says, led to Rædwald's son <a href="/wiki/Eorpwald_of_East_Anglia" title="Eorpwald of East Anglia">Eorpwald</a> also converting.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bede's account of the conversion is oft-cited. After Paulinus explains the tenets of Christianity, the king asks his counselors what they think of the new doctrine. Edwin's priest Coifi responds that they may be worthwhile; after all, he says, no one has been more respectful of and devoted to their gods than he, and he has seen no benefits from his dedication to them. Then, an unnamed counselor stands up and addresses the king, also seeing the benefit of the new faith. Coifi speaks again and announces that they should destroy the idols and temples they had hitherto worshiped. King Edwin agrees and embraces Christianity; Coifi himself will set fire to the idols,<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> declaring "I will do this myself, for now that the true God has granted me knowledge, who more suitably than I can set a public example, and destroy the idols that I worshipped in ignorance?” Bede goes on to describe the scene as Coifi "formally renounces his superstitions, and asked the king to give him arms and a stallion." Armed with both a sword and spear, Coifi rides Edwin's horse towards the idols, all within view of the crowd gathered to witness Edwin's conversion. Upon reaching the temple, Coifi "cast a spear into it and profaned it."<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>In an article titled "How Coifi Pierced Christ’s Side", <a href="/wiki/Julia_Barrow" title="Julia Barrow">Julia Barrow</a> examines Bede's Latinate text and pays particular attention to the passage concerning Coifi's attack upon the temple. Barrow notes that Bede's use of <i>lancea</i> was "not the word medieval writers normally used for spear", while “<i>hasta</i> was the usual choice." Barrow goes on to claim that <i><a href="/wiki/Holy_Lance" title="Holy Lance">lancea</a></i> was likely used by Bede as a reference to the details of the <a href="/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus" title="Crucifixion of Jesus">crucifixion</a> of Christ provided in the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_John" title="Gospel of John">vulgate book of John</a>, thus Coifi's desecration of the shrine is to be understood "as an inversion of the piercing of the temple of Christ’s body." All of these details support an understanding that Bede had great "warmth and admiration" for Edwin.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The brief speech by the unnamed counselor, a nobleman, has attracted much attention; suggesting the "wisdom and hopefulness of the Christian message",<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> it has inspired poets such as <a href="/wiki/William_Wordsworth" title="William Wordsworth">William Wordsworth</a><sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> and was called "the most poetic simile in Bede":<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<blockquote><p>The present life man, O king, seems to me, in comparison with that time which is unknown to us, like to the swift flight of a sparrow through the room wherein you sit at supper in winter amid your officers and ministers, with a good fire in the midst whilst the storms of rain and snow prevail abroad; the sparrow, I say, flying in at one door and immediately out another, whilst he is within is safe from the wintry but after a short space of fair weather he immediately vanishes out of your sight into the dark winter from which he has emerged. So this life of man appears for a short space but of what went before or what is to follow we are ignorant. If, therefore, this new doctrine contains something more certain, it seems justly to deserve to be followed.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> </p></blockquote>
<p>Noteworthy is Bede's summation of the nature of Edwin's reign as King of Northumbria:
</p>
<blockquote><p>“It is told that at the time there was so much peace in Britannia, that whenever King Edwin’s power extended, as is said proverbially right up to today, even if a woman with a recently born child wanted to walk across the whole island, from sea to sea, she could do so without anyone harming her.”</p></blockquote>
<p>Kershaw indicates that “Bede’s decision to couch Edwin’s peace in proverbial terms offers ...a chilling insight into the levels of lawlessness accepted in eighth-century England”. Furthermore, a definition of “peace” is to be understood as “freedom from robbery, rape, or violence; security to travel at will and to literally ‘go in peace.’”<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Edwin's conversion and Eorpwald's were reversed by their successors, and in the case of Northumbria the Roman Paulinus appears to have had very little impact. Indeed, by expelling British clergy from Elmet and elsewhere in Edwin's realm, Paulinus may have weakened the Church rather than strengthening it. Very few Roman clergy were present in Paulinus's time, only <a href="/wiki/James_the_Deacon" title="James the Deacon">James the Deacon</a> being known, so that the "conversion" can have been only superficial, extending little beyond the royal court. Paulinus's decision to flee Northumbria at Edwin's death, unlike his <a href="/wiki/Acolyte" title="Acolyte">acolyte</a> James who remained in Northumbria for many years afterwards until his death, suggests that the conversion was not popular, and the senior Italian cleric unloved.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="As_overlord">As overlord</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edwin_of_Northumbria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: As overlord">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The first challenge to Edwin came soon after his marriage-alliance with Kent, concluded at <a href="/wiki/Canterbury" title="Canterbury">Canterbury</a> in the summer of 625. By offering his protection to lesser kings, such as the king of <a href="/wiki/Isle_of_Wight" title="Isle of Wight">Wight</a>, Edwin thwarted the ambitions of Cwichelm of Wessex. Cwichelm's response was to send an assassin, as noted already. Edwin did not immediately respond to this insult, suggesting either that he felt unable to do so, or that Bede's portrayal of him as a rather indecisive ruler is accurate. Following the failed assassination, as noted, Edwin committed himself to Christianity provided only that he was victorious against Cwichelm.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>From about 627 onwards, Edwin was the most powerful king among the Anglo-Saxons, ruling Bernicia, Deira and much of eastern Mercia, the <a href="/wiki/Isle_of_Man" title="Isle of Man">Isle of Man</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Anglesey" title="Anglesey">Anglesey</a>. His alliance with Kent, the subjection of Wessex, and his recent successes added to his power and authority. The <i>imperium</i>, as Bede calls it, that Edwin possessed was later equated with the idea of a <a href="/wiki/Bretwalda" title="Bretwalda">Bretwalda</a>, a later concept invented by <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_the_West_Saxons" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of the West Saxons">West Saxon</a> kings in the 9th century. Put simply, success confirmed Edwin's overlordship, and failure would diminish it.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edwin's supposed foster-brother <a href="/wiki/Cadwallon_ap_Cadfan" title="Cadwallon ap Cadfan">Cadwallon ap Cadfan</a> enters the record circa 629, but Cadwallon was defeated and either submitted to Edwin's authority or went into exile.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> With the defeat of Cadwallon, Edwin's authority appears to have been unchallenged for a number of years, until <a href="/wiki/Penda_of_Mercia" title="Penda of Mercia">Penda of Mercia</a> and Cadwallon rose against him in 632–633.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Edwin faced Penda and Cadwallon at the Battle of Hatfield Chase in the autumn of 632 or 633 and was defeated and killed. For a time his body was (allegedly) hidden in <a href="/wiki/Sherwood_Forest" title="Sherwood Forest">Sherwood Forest</a> at a location that became the village of <a href="/wiki/Edwinstowe" title="Edwinstowe">Edwinstowe</a> (trans. Edwin's resting place), his head being eventually buried at York and the rest of his body at Whitby. Of his two grown sons by Cwenburh of Mercia, Osfrith died at Hatfield, and Eadfrith was captured by Penda and killed some time afterwards.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After his death, Edwin's Queen Æthelburg, along with Paulinus, returned to Kent, taking her son Uscfrea, daughter Eanfled, and Osfrith's son Yffi into exile with her. Uscfrea and Yffi were sent to the court of Æthelburg's kinsman <a href="/wiki/Dagobert_I" title="Dagobert I">Dagobert I</a>, king of the Franks, but died soon afterwards. Eanfled, however, lived to marry her first cousin, King <a href="/wiki/Oswiu_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Oswiu of Northumbria">Oswiu</a>, son of Acha and Æthelfrith.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_legacy">Death and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edwin_of_Northumbria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Death and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1066479718"><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div style="display:inline;" class="fn">Edwin of Northumbria</div></th></tr><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-header" style="background-color:gold;">Convert, King, Martyr<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup></th></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Venerated&#160;in</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Feast</a></th><td class="infobox-data">12 October</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">Patronage</a></th><td class="infobox-data">converts; <a href="/wiki/Hobo" title="Hobo">hoboes</a>; homeless people; kings; parents of large families</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Edwin's realm was divided at his death. He was succeeded by <a href="/wiki/Osric_of_Deira" title="Osric of Deira">Osric</a>, son of Edwin's paternal uncle Ælfric, in Deira, and by <a href="/wiki/Eanfrith_of_Bernicia" title="Eanfrith of Bernicia">Eanfrith</a>, son of Æthelfrith and Edwin's sister Acha, in Bernicia.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> Both reverted to <a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">paganism</a>, and both were killed by Cadwallon; eventually Eanfrith's brother <a href="/wiki/Oswald_of_Northumbria" title="Oswald of Northumbria">Oswald</a> defeated and killed Cadwallon and united Northumbria once more.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> 
</p><p>After his death, Edwin came to be venerated as a saint by some, although his cult was eventually overshadowed by the ultimately more successful cult of Oswald, who was killed in 642. They met their deaths in battle against similar foes, the pagan Mercians and the British, thus allowing both of them to be perceived as martyrs; however, Bede's treatment of Oswald clearly demonstrates that he regarded him as an unambiguously saintly figure, a status that he did not accord to Edwin.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edwin's renown comes largely from his treatment at some length by Bede, writing from an uncompromisingly English and Christian perspective, and rests on his belated conversion to Christianity. His united kingdom in the north did not outlast him, and his conversion to Christianity was renounced by his successors. When his kingship is compared with his pagan brother-in-law Æthelfrith, or to Æthelfrith's sons Oswald and Oswiu, or to the resolutely pagan Penda of Mercia, Edwin appears to be something less than a key figure in Britain during the first half of the 7th century. Perhaps the most significant legacies of Edwin's reign lay in his failures: the rise of Penda and of Mercia, and the return from Irish exile of the sons of Æthelfrith, which tied the kingdom of Northumbria into the Irish sea world for generations.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
