<p><b>John William Johnston McCulley</b> (February 2, 1883&#160;– November 23, 1958) was an American writer of hundreds of stories, fifty novels, numerous screenplays for film and television, and the creator of the character <a href="/wiki/Zorro" title="Zorro">Zorro</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Johnston_McCulley&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Born in <a href="/wiki/Ottawa,_Illinois" title="Ottawa, Illinois">Ottawa</a>, Illinois, and raised in <a href="/wiki/Chillicothe,_Illinois" title="Chillicothe, Illinois">Chillicothe</a>, Illinois, McCulley graduated from <a href="/wiki/Illinois_Valley_Central_High_School" title="Illinois Valley Central High School">Chillicothe Township High School</a> in 1901.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He started as a police reporter for <i><a href="/wiki/The_Police_Gazette" class="mw-redirect" title="The Police Gazette">The Police Gazette</a></i> and served as an Army public affairs officer during <a href="/wiki/World_War_I" title="World War I">World War I</a>. An amateur history buff, he went on to a career in <a href="/wiki/Pulp_magazine" title="Pulp magazine">pulp magazines</a> and screenplays, often using a Southern California backdrop for his stories.
</p><p>Many of his novels and stories were written under the <a href="/wiki/Pseudonym" title="Pseudonym">pseudonyms</a> <b>Harrington Strong</b>, <b>Raley Brien</b>, <b>George Drayne</b>, <b>Monica Morton</b>, <b>Rowena Raley</b>, <b>Frederic Phelps</b>, <b>Walter Pierson</b>, and <b>John Mack Stone</b>, among others.
</p><p>Aside from Zorro, McCulley created many other pulp characters, including Black Star, The Spider, The Mongoose, and Thubway Tham. Many of McCulley's characters—The Green Ghost, The Thunderbolt, and The Crimson Clown—were inspirations for the masked heroes that have appeared in popular culture from McCulley's time to the present day.
</p>

<h3><span id="McCulley.27s_characters"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="McCulley's_characters">McCulley's characters</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Johnston_McCulley&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: McCulley&#039;s characters">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Zorro">Zorro</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Johnston_McCulley&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Zorro">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Capistrano2.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Capistrano2.jpg/220px-Capistrano2.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="313" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Capistrano2.jpg/330px-Capistrano2.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Capistrano2.jpg/440px-Capistrano2.jpg 2x" data-file-width="453" data-file-height="645" /></a><figcaption>The cover of "The Curse of Capistrano"</figcaption></figure>
<p>McCulley's <a href="/wiki/Zorro" title="Zorro">Zorro</a> character, reminiscent of <a href="/wiki/Emma_Orczy" class="mw-redirect" title="Emma Orczy">Baroness Orczy</a>'s <a href="/wiki/The_Scarlet_Pimpernel" title="The Scarlet Pimpernel">Scarlet Pimpernel</a>, was first serialized in the story <i><a href="/wiki/The_Curse_of_Capistrano" title="The Curse of Capistrano">The Curse of Capistrano</a></i> in 1919 in the <a href="/wiki/Pulp_magazine" title="Pulp magazine">pulp magazine</a> <i>All-Story Weekly</i>.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Zorro" title="Zorro">Zorro</a> became his most enduring character. The appearance of the 1920 <a href="/wiki/Douglas_Fairbanks" title="Douglas Fairbanks">Douglas Fairbanks</a> <a href="/wiki/Silent_movie" class="mw-redirect" title="Silent movie">silent movie</a> <i><a href="/wiki/The_Mark_of_Zorro_(1920_film)" title="The Mark of Zorro (1920 film)">The Mark of Zorro</a></i>, based on the first novel, was the direct cause for McCulley's reviving what had originally been a one-time hero plot.
</p><p>The popularity of the character led to three novellas appearing in <i><a href="/wiki/Argosy_(magazine)" title="Argosy (magazine)">Argosy</a></i>: <i>The Further Adventures of Zorro</i> (1922), <i><a href="/wiki/Zorro_Rides_Again" title="Zorro Rides Again">Zorro Rides Again</a></i> (1931), and <i><a href="/wiki/The_Sign_of_Zorro" class="mw-redirect" title="The Sign of Zorro">The Sign of Zorro</a></i> (1941).<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  In between, he wrote many other novels and stories set in early Spanish California which did not have Zorro as the lead character. Republic optioned the character for a serial, <i>Zorro's Fighting Legion</i>, which was released in 1939 and was well received. Over the coming decade Republic released three other serials connected in some way with the Zorro character. In 1940, <a href="/wiki/The_Mark_of_Zorro_(1940_film)" title="The Mark of Zorro (1940 film)"><i>The Mark of Zorro</i> remake</a> starring <a href="/wiki/Tyrone_Power" title="Tyrone Power">Tyrone Power</a> and <a href="/wiki/Linda_Darnell" title="Linda Darnell">Linda Darnell</a> made the character much more widely known to the public at large, and McCulley decided to bring Zorro back with new stories.
</p><p>McCulley made an arrangement with the pulp <i>West Magazine</i> to produce a brand new Zorro short story for every issue. The first of these stories appeared in July 1944 and the last one appeared in July 1951, the final issue of the publication. Fifty-three adventures in all were published in <i>West</i>. An additional story (possibly a story originally written for <i>West</i> which went unpublished when <i>West</i> folded) appeared in <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Max_Brand%27s_Western_Magazine&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Max Brand&#39;s Western Magazine (page does not exist)">Max Brand's Western Magazine</a></i> in the May 1954 issue. The final Zorro story appeared in <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Short_Story_Magazine&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Short Story Magazine (page does not exist)">Short Story Magazine</a></i> in April 1959, after McCulley's death and after <a href="/wiki/Walt_Disney" title="Walt Disney">Walt Disney</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Zorro_(1957_TV_series)" title="Zorro (1957 TV series)">Zorro</a> television program starring <a href="/wiki/Guy_Williams_(actor)" title="Guy Williams (actor)">Guy Williams</a> had become nationally popular.
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Black_Star">Black Star</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Johnston_McCulley&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Black Star">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Probably his second most popular character from the pulps was "The Black Star", a criminal mastermind who is pursued by Roger Verbeck-Flagellum and Muggs, a millionaire bachelor and his ex-thug partner.  Black Star first appeared in the <a href="/wiki/Street_%26_Smith" title="Street &amp; Smith">Street &amp; Smith</a> pulp <i><a href="/wiki/Detective_Story_Magazine" title="Detective Story Magazine">Detective Story Magazine</a></i> on 5 March 1916.
</p><p>Black Star was what was once termed a "gentleman criminal", in that he does not commit murder, nor does he permit any of his gang to kill anyone, not even the police or his arch enemy Roger Verbeck. He does not threaten women, always keeps his word, and is invariably courteous, nor does he deal with narcotics in any of his stories.  He is always seen in a black cloak and a black hood on which is embossed a jet black star.  The Black Star and his gang used "vapor bombs" and "vapor guns" which rendered their victims instantly unconscious, a technique which pre-dated the <a href="/wiki/Green_Hornet" title="Green Hornet">Green Hornet</a>'s gas gun by several decades.
</p><p>These stories were very popular with the readership of <i><a href="/wiki/Detective_Story_Magazine" title="Detective Story Magazine">Detective Story Magazine</a></i> and some of them were reprinted by Chelsea House, a division of Street &amp; Smith, in a series of inexpensive hardback books.  The character lasted through the end of 1930.
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Spider">The Spider</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Johnston_McCulley&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: The Spider">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The Spider was another long-running villain character, considered by some a significant pulp supervillain.  The Spider appeared in 11 short stories and three short-story collections between 1918 and 1930.  He was injured as a young man and used a wheelchair, but he used his mental abilities to run an international crime ring from his office, "The Spider's Den".
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Crimson_Clown">The Crimson Clown</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Johnston_McCulley&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: The Crimson Clown">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The Crimson Clown appeared in <i><a href="/wiki/Detective_Story_Magazine" title="Detective Story Magazine">Detective Story Magazine</a></i> beginning in 1926 and immediately attracted reader interest, so much so that Street &amp; Smith published two hardback collections of his adventures. <i>The Crimson Clown</i> (1927) was rushed to press just as soon as there was enough material available to fill a hardback volume. This was followed by <i>The Crimson Clown Again</i> (1928).
</p><p>The Crimson Clown is Delton Prouse, a wealthy young bachelor, able veteran of The Great War, explorer, and all-around adventurer who functions as a modern <a href="/wiki/Robin_Hood" title="Robin Hood">Robin Hood</a>, stealing from the unjustly rich and returning money to helpless victims or worthy organizations.  He dresses in a mostly red clown suit and uses a syringe of knockout drug (later this is replaced by a "gas gun").  Like McCulley's earlier "Man in Purple", who also stole from the unjustly rich, he frequently had to destroy his outfits to evade capture.
</p><p>McCulley retired Delton Prouse at the end of 1931, but "The Crimson Clown’s Return" (<i>Popular Detective</i>, Oct 1944) brought him back for one final adventure. Though an original story, it lifted the title from another Clown story in the October 18, 1930 issue of <i>Detective Story Magazine</i>.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Johnston_McCulley&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Johnston McCulley died on November 23, 1958, in <a href="/wiki/Los_Angeles,_California" class="mw-redirect" title="Los Angeles, California">Los Angeles</a>, California at age 75. The <i><a href="/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times" title="Los Angeles Times">Los Angeles Times</a></i> obituary gives his address in Los Angeles as 6533 Hollywood Blvd. at the time of his death, an address which is confirmed in the Marquis volume and places McCulley in the Hillview Hollywood Apartments. There is no record of when he moved there, although the Marquis article may have been originally prepared in the late 1940s/early 1950s, with additional material appended in the late 1950s. <i><a href="/wiki/The_New_York_Times" title="The New York Times">The New York Times</a></i> obituary mentions that he died "after a series of operations," a phrase echoed in other newspaper obituaries from other parts of the country, most likely taken from a <i>New York Times</i> feed.
</p><p>McCulley is entombed in <a href="/wiki/Forest_Lawn_Memorial_Park,_Glendale" class="mw-redirect" title="Forest Lawn Memorial Park, Glendale">Forest Lawn Memorial Park, Glendale</a>, Los Angeles, California.
</p>
