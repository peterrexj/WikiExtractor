<p><b>Notker the Stammerer</b> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;840</span> – 6 April 912), <b>Notker Balbulus</b>, or simply <b>Notker</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;n 2&#93;</a></sup> was a <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">Benedictine monk</a> at the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Gall" title="Abbey of Saint Gall">Abbey of Saint Gall</a> active as a composer, poet and scholar. Described as "a significant figure in the Western Church",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Notker made substantial contributions to both the music and literature of his time. He is usually credited with two major works of the <a href="/wiki/Carolingian_Empire" title="Carolingian Empire">Carolingian period</a>: the <i><a href="#Liber_Hymnorum">Liber Hymnorum</a></i>, which includes an important collection of early musical <a href="/wiki/Sequence_(musical_form)" title="Sequence (musical form)">sequences</a>, and an early biography of <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a>, the <i><a href="#Gesta_Karoli_Magni">Gesta Karoli Magni</a></i>. His other works include a biography of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Gall" title="Saint Gall">Saint Gall</a> known as the <i>Vita Sancti Galli</i> and a <a href="/wiki/Martyrology" title="Martyrology">martyrology</a>, among others.
</p><p>Born near the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Gall" title="Abbey of Saint Gall">Abbey of Saint Gall</a>, Notker was educated alongside the monks <a href="/wiki/Tuotilo" title="Tuotilo">Tuotilo</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ratpert_of_Saint_Gall" title="Ratpert of Saint Gall">Ratpert</a>; all three were composers, making the Abbey an important center of early <a href="/wiki/Medieval_music" title="Medieval music">medieval music</a>. Notker quickly became a central figure of the Abbey and among the leading literary scholars of the <a href="/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages" title="Early Middle Ages">Early Middle Ages</a>. A renowned teacher, he taught <a href="/wiki/Solomon_III_(bishop_of_Constance)" title="Solomon III (bishop of Constance)">Solomon III, the bishop of Constance</a> and on occasion advised <a href="/wiki/Charles_the_Fat" title="Charles the Fat">Charles the Fat</a>. Although venerated by the Abbey of Saint Gall and the namesake of later scholars there such as <a href="/wiki/Notker_Physicus" title="Notker Physicus">Notker Physicus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Notker_Labeo" title="Notker Labeo">Notker Labeo</a>, Notker was never formally canonized. He was given "the Stammerer" as an <a href="/wiki/Epithet" title="Epithet">epithet</a>, due to his lifelong <a href="/wiki/Stutter" class="mw-redirect" title="Stutter">stutter</a>.
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r886046785">.mw-parser-output .toclimit-2 .toclevel-1 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-3 .toclevel-2 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-4 .toclevel-3 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-5 .toclevel-4 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-6 .toclevel-5 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-7 .toclevel-6 ul{display:none}</style><div class="toclimit-2"><meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" /></div>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life_and_career">Life and career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life and career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Notker was born around 840, near the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Gall" title="Abbey of Saint Gall">Abbey of Saint Gall</a> in modern-day Switzerland.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> His wealthy family was of either <a href="/wiki/Alamannia" title="Alamannia">Alemannic</a> or Swiss descent and they owned land in <a href="/wiki/Jonschwil" title="Jonschwil">Jonschwil</a> of <a href="/wiki/Thurgau" title="Thurgau">Thurgau</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Notker's later biographer <a href="/wiki/Ekkehard_V" title="Ekkehard V">Ekkehard V</a> claims he was born in Heiligau—now <a href="/wiki/Elgg" title="Elgg">Elgg</a>—in the <a href="/wiki/Canton_of_Z%C3%BCrich" title="Canton of Zürich">Canton of Zürich</a>, but this has been rejected by the historian <a href="/w/index.php?title=Gerold_Meyer_von_Knonau_(historian)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Gerold Meyer von Knonau (historian) (page does not exist)">Gerold Meyer von Knonau</a><span class="noprint" style="font-size:85%; font-style: normal;">&#160;&#91;<a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerold_Meyer_von_Knonau_(historian)" class="extiw" title="de:Gerold Meyer von Knonau (historian)">de</a>&#93;</span>, who suggests a birthplace near Jonschwil.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Since childhood Notker had a <a href="/wiki/Stutter" class="mw-redirect" title="Stutter">stutter</a>, resulting in the Latin <a href="/wiki/Epithet" title="Epithet">epithet</a> <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">balbulus</i></span> (<abbr style="font-size:85%" title="literal translation">lit.</abbr><span style="white-space: nowrap;">&#8201;</span>'<a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/babbler#Noun" class="extiw" title="wikt:babbler">babbler</a>&#39;) or "the Stammerer" in English.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He began schooling at Saint Gall early in age and spent the rest of his life in the Abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> His teachers included the Swiss monk <a href="/w/index.php?title=Iso_von_St._Gallen&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Iso von St. Gallen (page does not exist)">Iso</a><span class="noprint" style="font-size:85%; font-style: normal;">&#160;&#91;<a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iso_von_St._Gallen" class="extiw" title="de:Iso von St. Gallen">de</a>&#93;</span> and the Irish monk Moengal, called "Marcellus" by Notker.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The later <i>Casus monasterii Sancti Galli</i> of <a href="/wiki/Ekkehard_IV" title="Ekkehard IV">Ekkehard IV</a> "paints a lively picture of the monastery school", and notes that Notker was taught alongside <a href="/wiki/Tuotilo" title="Tuotilo">Tuotilo</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ratpert_of_Saint_Gall" title="Ratpert of Saint Gall">Ratpert</a>; all three would become teachers and composers at the Abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Although first and foremost a scholar, Notker held numerous positions at the Abby including librarian in 890 and master of guests (<span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">hospitarius</i></span>) in 892 and 894.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He became established as a well-known teacher and was eventually appointed "master of the monastic school".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Among his students was <a href="/wiki/Solomon_III_(bishop_of_Constance)" title="Solomon III (bishop of Constance)">Solomon</a>, who was later <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Constance" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Constance">Bishop of Constance</a> from  890 until his death in 912.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Notker was often called upon for council from outside the Abbey; on occasion he advised <a href="/wiki/Charles_the_Fat" title="Charles the Fat">Charles the Fat</a> who visited the Abbey from 4–6 December 883.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Charles was the dedicatee of Notker's <i>De Carolo Magno</i>, an early biography of <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThorpe19692_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorpe19692-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Ekkehard IV lauded him as "delicate of body but not of mind, <a href="/wiki/Stuttering" title="Stuttering">stuttering</a> of tongue but not of intellect, pushing boldly forward in things Divine, a vessel of the Holy Spirit without equal in his time".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKampersLöffler1911§_&quot;Blessed_Notker_Balbulus_(Stammerer)&quot;_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKampersLöffler1911§_&quot;Blessed_Notker_Balbulus_(Stammerer)&quot;-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Despite his renown in the Abbey, Notker never became an <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> of Saint Gall, and repeatedly declined abbacy offers elsewhere.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812_8-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;n 3&#93;</a></sup> Notker died in Saint Gall on 6 April 912.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Musical_works">Musical works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Musical works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: <a href="/wiki/Jubilus" title="Jubilus">Jubilus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Media_vita_in_morte_sumus" title="Media vita in morte sumus">Media vita in morte sumus</a></div>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Liber_Hymnorum"><i>Liber Hymnorum</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Liber Hymnorum">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Notker created the <i>Liber Hymnorum</i> during the late 9th century, an important early collection of <i><a href="/wiki/Sequence_(poetry)" class="mw-redirect" title="Sequence (poetry)">Sequences</a></i> dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Liutward" title="Liutward">Liutward</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Vercelli" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Vercelli">bishop of Vercelli</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812_8-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Completed in 884, it is essentially a set of melodies and texts organized by the Church calendar.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The oldest surviving sources of the <i>Liber Hymnorum</i> date from either Notker's last years or directly after his death.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._6_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._6-14">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the preface to his <i>Liber Hymnorum</i>, Notker claimed his musical work was inspired by an antiphoner that was brought to Gall from the <a href="/wiki/Jumi%C3%A8ges_Abbey" title="Jumièges Abbey">Jumièges Abbey</a>, soon after its destruction in 851.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlanchart2001§_para._1_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlanchart2001§_para._1-15">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;n 4&#93;</a></sup> Notker was particularly inspired by the Jumièges chant book setting verses to the melodies, making them easier to remember; he goes on to discuss his childhood difficulties in recalling the <i>melodiae longissimae</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._5_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._5-18">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Others">Others</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Others">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Numerous other musical works have been ascribed to Notker, with varying certainty. The sequence melody "Ave beati germinis" is attributed to him in one mid-10th century source.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._3_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._3-19">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Ekkehard IV's attribution of the melodies "Frigdola" and "Occidentan" is problematic since these appear to have existed before Notker's time. <sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._3_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._3-19">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The hymn <i><a href="/wiki/Media_Vita" class="mw-redirect" title="Media Vita">Media Vita</a></i> was erroneously attributed to him by <a href="/w/index.php?title=Jos_Metzler&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Jos Metzler (page does not exist)">Jodocus Metzler</a><span class="noprint" style="font-size:85%; font-style: normal;">&#160;&#91;<a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos_Metzler" class="extiw" title="de:Jos Metzler">de</a>&#93;</span> in 1613.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKampersLöffler1911§_&quot;Blessed_Notker_Balbulus_(Stammerer)&quot;_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKampersLöffler1911§_&quot;Blessed_Notker_Balbulus_(Stammerer)&quot;-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._3_19-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._3-19">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Literary_works">Literary works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Literary works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Gesta_Karoli_Magni"><i>Gesta Karoli Magni</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Gesta Karoli Magni">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <i>Gesta Karoli Magni</i> ("The Deeds of Charles the Great")—also known as <i>De Carolo Magno</i> ("Concerning Charles the Great")—is among the earliest biographies of Charlemagne, after the <i><a href="/wiki/Vita_Karoli_Magni" title="Vita Karoli Magni">Vita Karoli Magni</a></i> of <a href="/wiki/Einhard" title="Einhard">Einhard</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEInnes199813_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInnes199813-20">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._8_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._8-21">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Notker the Stammerer is usually identified as the <a href="/wiki/Pseudonymous" class="mw-redirect" title="Pseudonymous">pseudonymous</a> "Monk of Saint Gall" (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Monachus Sangallensis</i>) to whom the work is attributed.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThorpe196922–23_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorpe196922–23-22">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Numerous life details are shared between Notker and the unknown Monk, including their origin, education and long-term stay at Saint Gall.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThorpe196922_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorpe196922-23">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;n 5&#93;</a></sup> The translator <a href="/wiki/Lewis_Thorpe" title="Lewis Thorpe">Lewis Thorpe</a> concludes that "the two are "probably the same person, although this cannot be proved".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThorpe1969inside_front_cover_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorpe1969inside_front_cover-26">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The work is not a linear biography, but instead two books of <i><a href="/wiki/Exempla" class="mw-redirect" title="Exempla">exempla</a></i>—anecdotal "moral tales"—relating chiefly to Charlemagne and his family.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> When discussed by scholars, Notker's work is often compared unfavorably to that of Einhard; the historian <a href="/wiki/Philipp_Jaff%C3%A9" title="Philipp Jaffé">Philipp Jaffé</a> derided Notker as one who "took pleasure in amusing anecdotes and witty tales, but who was ill-informed about the true march of historical events", and described the work itself as a "mass of legend, saga, invention and reckless blundering".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThorpe196927_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorpe196927-27">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> Similarly, the classical scholar <a href="/wiki/H._W._Garrod" title="H. W. Garrod">H. W. Garrod</a> dismissed it as a "a largely mythical record".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThorpe196927_27-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorpe196927-27">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> More sympathetically, the historian <a href="/wiki/Matthew_Innes" title="Matthew Innes">Matthew Innes</a> has cited Notker's use of "humour and anecdotal style" as encouraging "a negative judgement [of] his abilities", noting that "Recent scholarship [on the <i>Gesta Karoli Magni</i> ] has stressed the underlying clarity of its intellectual vision and found coherent ideas about the correct ordering of society, church and politics."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEInnes199813_20-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInnes199813-20">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Martyrology">Martyrology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Martyrology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Among Notker's literary works was the arrangement of a <a href="/wiki/Martyrology" title="Martyrology">martyrology</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKampersLöffler1911§_&quot;Blessed_Notker_Balbulus_(Stammerer)&quot;_12-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKampersLöffler1911§_&quot;Blessed_Notker_Balbulus_(Stammerer)&quot;-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> which is a catalogue of <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyrs</a> or other Christian <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saints</a> with short biographies.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTE&#39;&#39;OED&#39;&#39;:_martyrology1a_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTE&#39;&#39;OED&#39;&#39;:_martyrology1a-28">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Written around 900, only a single incomplete copy survives, not including some saints born on June 13–17, July 3–6, August 19–26, October 27 and December 31.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEe-codices§_&quot;Manuscript_Summary:&quot;_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEe-codices§_&quot;Manuscript_Summary:&quot;-29">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> That the work survives incomplete may suggest Notker simply never finished his "ambitious project".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEe-codices§_&quot;Manuscript_Summary:&quot;_29-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEe-codices§_&quot;Manuscript_Summary:&quot;-29">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In his martyrology, Notker appeared to corroborate one of <a href="/wiki/St_Columba" class="mw-redirect" title="St Columba">St Columba</a>'s miracles. St Columba, being an important father of Irish monasticism, was also important to <a href="/wiki/St_Gall" class="mw-redirect" title="St Gall">St Gall</a>, which had strong Irish connections. The abbot <a href="/wiki/Adomn%C3%A1n" title="Adomnán">Adomnán</a> wrote that at one point Columba had—through clairvoyance—seen a city in Italy near Rome being destroyed by fiery sulphur as a divine punishment and that three thousand people had perished. And shortly after Columba saw this, sailors from Gaul arrived to tell the news of it. Notker claimed in his martyrology that this event happened and that an earthquake had destroyed a city which was called 'new'. It is unclear what this city was that Notker was claiming, although some thought it may have been <a href="/wiki/Naples" title="Naples">Naples</a>, previously called Neapolis (<abbr style="font-size:85%" title="literal translation">lit.</abbr><span style="white-space: nowrap;">&#8201;</span>'new city&#39;). However Naples was destroyed by a volcano in 512 before Columba was born, and not during Columba's lifetime and the historian <a href="/wiki/Richard_Sharpe_(historian)" title="Richard Sharpe (historian)">Richard Sharpe</a> notes that "Notker was no better placed than anyone since to know what Adomnán intended".<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Others_2">Others</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Others">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Notker completed the <i>Breviarium Regum Francorum</i> ("Breviary of the Frankish Kings") begun by <a href="/wiki/Erchanbert" class="mw-redirect" title="Erchanbert">Erchanbert</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> A Latin key explaining significative letters (performance instructions in some <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_chant" title="Gregorian chant">Gregorian chant</a>) is attributed to Notker, though it is sometimes erronously ascribed to Notker Labeo.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGushee2001§_para._1_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGushee2001§_para._1-31">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._2_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._2-32">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;n 6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Scholars vary on evaluating Notker's main legacy; the priest <a href="/wiki/Alban_Butler" title="Alban Butler">Alban Butler</a> asserted that his sequences were his most important achievement,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> while the historian <a href="/wiki/Rosamond_McKitterick" title="Rosamond McKitterick">Rosamond McKitterick</a> states that he is best remembered for the <i>Gesta Karoli Magni</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcKitterick2018297_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcKitterick2018297-34">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> Notker and <a href="/wiki/Solomon_II_(bishop_of_Constance)" title="Solomon II (bishop of Constance)">Solomon II</a> were the most important writers educated at Saint Gall,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcKitterick2018297_34-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcKitterick2018297-34">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> and Notker was among the both leading literary scribes and scholars of his time.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_paras._2,_8_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_paras._2,_8-35">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> He was the namesake of the later scholars <a href="/wiki/Notker_Physicus" title="Notker Physicus">Notker Physicus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Notker_Labeo" title="Notker Labeo">Notker Labeo</a>, who are referred to as "Notker II" and "Notker III" respectively.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJaffe1985165_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJaffe1985165-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On Notker's canonization status, the English cleric <a href="/wiki/John_Donne" title="John Donne">John Donne</a> noted that "he is a private Saint, for a few Parishes".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaker-Smith1975171_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaker-Smith1975171-36">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> According to the 16th century historian <a href="/wiki/Henricus_Canisius" title="Henricus Canisius">Henricus Canisius</a>, Notker' Sainthood was granted by <a href="/wiki/Leo_X" class="mw-redirect" title="Leo X">Leo X</a> in 1512 for Saint Gall and nearby churches, and in 1513 for the <a href="/wiki/Prince-Bishopric_of_Constance" title="Prince-Bishopric of Constance">Diocese of Constance</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaker-Smith1975171_36-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaker-Smith1975171-36">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The <i><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia" title="Catholic Encyclopedia">Catholic Encyclopedia</a></i> interprets Leo's action as <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatification</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKampersLöffler1911§_&quot;Blessed_Notker_Balbulus_(Stammerer)&quot;_12-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKampersLöffler1911§_&quot;Blessed_Notker_Balbulus_(Stammerer)&quot;-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> In Saint Gall and other churches he is commemorated annually on 6 April.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1_10-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Notker was never formally canonized.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812_8-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892812-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the mid-19th century the Swiss music scholar Anselm Schubiger was the first to transcribe almost all of Notker's extant melodies into modern notation. Many of his transcriptions are still in use, though older manuscript sources are available now that Schubiger did not have access to, meaning that "a more comprehensive approach to the sources will produce readings that are closer to Notker's own use, and better musically".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrocker2001_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrocker2001-37">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<li id="cite_note-25"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-25">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">Due to textual details in the <i>Gesta Karoli Magni</i>, the "Monk of Saint Gall" is known to have been a native German-speaker, deriving from the <a href="/wiki/Thurgau" title="Thurgau">Thurgau</a>, only a few miles from the Abbey of Saint Gall; the region is also close to where Notker is believed to have derived from. The monk himself relates that he was raised by Adalbert, a former soldier who had fought against the <a href="/wiki/Saxons" title="Saxons">Saxons</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Pannonian_Avars" title="Pannonian Avars">Avars</a> and the Slavs under the command of Kerold, brother of <a href="/wiki/Hildegard,_wife_of_Charlemagne" class="mw-redirect" title="Hildegard, wife of Charlemagne">Hildegard</a>, Charlemagne's second wife. He was also a friend of Adalbert's son, Werinbert, another monk at Saint Gall, who died as the book was in progress. His teacher was <a href="/wiki/Grimald_von_Wei%C3%9Fenburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Grimald von Weißenburg">Grimald von Weißenburg</a>, the Abbot of Saint Gall from 841 to 872, who was, the monk claims, himself a pupil of <a href="/wiki/Alcuin" title="Alcuin">Alcuin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThorpe196921–25_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorpe196921–25-24">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup></span>
