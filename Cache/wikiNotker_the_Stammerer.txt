<p><b>Notker the Stammerer</b> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;840</span> – 6 April 912), <b>Notker Balbulus</b>, or simply <b>Notker</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;n 2&#93;</a></sup> was a <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">Benedictine monk</a> at the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Gall" title="Abbey of Saint Gall">Abbey of Saint Gall</a> active as a poet, scholar and (probably) composer. Described as "a significant figure in the Western Church",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Notker made substantial contributions to both the music and literature of his time. He is usually credited with two major works of the <a href="/wiki/Carolingian_Empire" title="Carolingian Empire">Carolingian period</a>: the <i><a href="/wiki/Liber_Hymnorum" class="mw-redirect" title="Liber Hymnorum">Liber Hymnorum</a></i>, which includes an important collection of early <a href="/wiki/Sequence_(musical_form)" title="Sequence (musical form)">sequences</a>, and the earliest biography of <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a>, <i>Gesta Caroli Magni</i>. His other works include a biography of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Gall" title="Saint Gall">Saint Gall</a>, the <i>Vita Sancti Galli</i>, and a <a href="/wiki/Martyrology" title="Martyrology">martyrology</a>.
</p><p>Born near the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Gall" title="Abbey of Saint Gall">Abbey of Saint Gall</a>, Notker was educated alongside the monks <a href="/wiki/Tuotilo" title="Tuotilo">Tuotilo</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ratpert_of_Saint_Gall" title="Ratpert of Saint Gall">Ratpert</a>; all three were composers, making the Abbey an important center of early <a href="/wiki/Medieval_music" title="Medieval music">medieval music</a>. Notker quickly became a central figure of the Abbey and among the leading literary scholars of the <a href="/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages" title="Early Middle Ages">Early Middle Ages</a>. A renowned teacher, he taught <a href="/wiki/Solomon_III_(bishop_of_Constance)" title="Solomon III (bishop of Constance)">Solomon III, the bishop of Constance</a> and on occasion advised <a href="/wiki/Charles_the_Fat" title="Charles the Fat">Charles the Fat</a>. Although venerated by the Abbey of Saint Gall and the namesake of later scholars there such as <a href="/wiki/Notker_Physicus" title="Notker Physicus">Notker Physicus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Notker_Labeo" title="Notker Labeo">Notker Labeo</a>, Notker was never formally canonized. He was given "the Stammerer" as an <a href="/wiki/Epithet" title="Epithet">epithet</a>, due to his lifelong stutter.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life_and_career">Life and career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life and career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Notker was born around 840, near the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Gall" title="Abbey of Saint Gall">Abbey of Saint Gall</a> in modern-day Switzerland.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> His family was wealthy and of either <a href="/wiki/Alamannia" title="Alamannia">Alemannic</a> or Swiss descent and they owned land in <a href="/wiki/Jonschwil" title="Jonschwil">Jonschwil</a> of <a href="/wiki/Thurgau" title="Thurgau">Thurgau</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Notker's later biographer <a href="/wiki/Ekkehard_V" title="Ekkehard V">Ekkehard V</a> claims he was born in Heiligau—now <a href="/wiki/Elgg" title="Elgg">Elgg</a>—in the <a href="/wiki/Canton_of_Z%C3%BCrich" title="Canton of Zürich">Canton of Zürich</a>, but this has been rejected by the historian <a href="/w/index.php?title=Gerold_Meyer_von_Knonau_(historian)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Gerold Meyer von Knonau (historian) (page does not exist)">Gerold Meyer von Knonau</a><span class="noprint" style="font-size:85%; font-style: normal;">&#160;&#91;<a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerold_Meyer_von_Knonau_(historian)" class="extiw" title="de:Gerold Meyer von Knonau (historian)">de</a>&#93;</span>, who suggests a birthplace near Jonschwil.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Since childhood Notker had a <a href="/wiki/Stutter" class="mw-redirect" title="Stutter">stutter</a>, resulting in the Latin <a href="/wiki/Epithet" title="Epithet">epithet</a> <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">balbulus</i></span> (<abbr style="font-size:85%" title="literal translation">lit.</abbr><span style="white-space: nowrap;">&#8201;</span>'<a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/babbler#Noun" class="extiw" title="wikt:babbler">babbler</a>&#39;) or "the Stammerer" in English.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He began schooling at Saint Gall early in age and spent the rest of his life in the Abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> His teachers included the Swiss monk <a href="/w/index.php?title=Iso_von_St._Gallen&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Iso von St. Gallen (page does not exist)">Iso</a><span class="noprint" style="font-size:85%; font-style: normal;">&#160;&#91;<a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iso_von_St._Gallen" class="extiw" title="de:Iso von St. Gallen">de</a>&#93;</span> and the Irish monk Moengal, called "Marcellus" by Notker.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The later <i>Casus monasterii Sancti Galli</i> of <a href="/wiki/Ekkehard_IV" title="Ekkehard IV">Ekkehard IV</a> "paints a lively picture of the monastery school", and notes that Notker was taught alongside <a href="/wiki/Tuotilo" title="Tuotilo">Tuotilo</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ratpert_of_Saint_Gall" title="Ratpert of Saint Gall">Ratpert</a>; all three would become teachers at the Abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGowan2002314-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Although first and foremost a scholar, Notker held numerous positions at the Abby including librarian in 890 and master of guests (<span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">hospitarius</i></span>) in 892 and 894.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He became established as a well-known teacher and was eventually appointed "master of the monastic school".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Among his students was <a href="/wiki/Solomon_III_(bishop_of_Constance)" title="Solomon III (bishop of Constance)">Solomon</a>, was later <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Constance" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Constance">Bishop of Constance</a> from  890 until his death in 912.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Notker was often called upon for council from outside the Abbey; on occasion he advised <a href="/wiki/Charles_the_Fat" title="Charles the Fat">Charles the Fat</a> who visited the Abbey from 4–6 December 883.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Charles was the dedicatee of Notker's <i>Gesta Karoli</i>, the earliest biography on <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Ekkehard IV lauded him as "delicate of body but not of mind, <a href="/wiki/Stuttering" title="Stuttering">stuttering</a> of tongue but not of intellect, pushing boldly forward in things Divine, a vessel of the Holy Spirit without equal in his time".<sup id="cite_ref-Kampers_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kampers-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Despite his renown in the Abbey, Notker never became an <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> of Saint Gall, and repeatedly declined abbacy offers elsewhere.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512_8-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;n 3&#93;</a></sup> Notker died in Saint Gall on 6 April 912.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_para._1-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Musical_works">Musical works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Musical works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: <a href="/wiki/Jubilus" title="Jubilus">Jubilus</a></div>
<p>His <i><a href="/wiki/Liber_Hymnorum" class="mw-redirect" title="Liber Hymnorum">Liber Hymnorum</a></i>, created between 881 and 887, is an early collection of <i><a href="/wiki/Sequence_(poetry)" class="mw-redirect" title="Sequence (poetry)">Sequences</a></i>, which he called "<a href="/wiki/Hymn" title="Hymn">hymns</a>", mnemonic poems for remembering the series of pitches sung during a <a href="/wiki/Melisma" title="Melisma">melisma</a> in <a href="/wiki/Plainchant" class="mw-redirect" title="Plainchant">plainchant</a>, especially in the <a href="/wiki/Alleluia" title="Alleluia">Alleluia</a>. It is unknown how many or which of the works contained in the collection are his. The hymn <i><a href="/wiki/Media_Vita" class="mw-redirect" title="Media Vita">Media Vita</a></i> was erroneously attributed to him late in the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Kampers_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kampers-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> In the preface to his <i>Liber Hymnorum</i>, Notker claimed his musical work was inspired by an antiphoner that was brought to Gall from the <a href="/wiki/Jumi%C3%A8ges_Abbey" title="Jumièges Abbey">Jumièges Abbey</a>, soon after its destruction in 851.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlanchart2001§_para._1_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlanchart2001§_para._1-13">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;n 4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Ekkehard_IV" title="Ekkehard IV">Ekkehard IV</a> wrote of fifty sequences composed by Notker. He was formerly considered to have been the inventor of the <a href="/wiki/Sequence_(poetry)" class="mw-redirect" title="Sequence (poetry)">sequence</a>, a new species of religious lyric, but this is now considered doubtful, though he did introduce the genre into Germany. It had been the custom to prolong the <a href="/wiki/Alleluia" title="Alleluia">Alleluia</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">Mass</a> before the <a href="/wiki/Gospel" title="Gospel">Gospel</a>, modulating through a skillfully harmonized series of tones. Notker learned how to fit the separate syllables of a <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> text to the tones of this jubilation; this poem was called the sequence (q.v.), formerly called the "jubilation". (The reason for this name is uncertain.) From 881–7 Notker dedicated a collection of such verses to Bishop <a href="/wiki/Liutward_of_Vercelli" class="mw-redirect" title="Liutward of Vercelli">Liutward of Vercelli</a>, but it is not known which or how many are his.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Literary_works">Literary works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Literary works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He completed Erchanbert's chronicle, arranged a <a href="/wiki/Martyrology" title="Martyrology">martyrology</a>, composed a metrical biography of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Gall" title="Saint Gall">Saint Gall</a>, and authored other works.<sup id="cite_ref-Kampers_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kampers-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Gesta_Caroli_Magni"><i>Gesta Caroli Magni</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Gesta Caroli Magni">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The "Monk of Saint Gall" (Latin: <i>Monachus Sangallensis</i>; the name is not contemporary, being given by modern scholars), the ninth-century writer of a volume of didactic eulogistic anecdotes regarding the Emperor Charlemagne, is now commonly believed to be Notker the Stammerer.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> This monk is known from his work to have been a native German-speaker, deriving from the <a href="/wiki/Thurgau" title="Thurgau">Thurgau</a>, only a few miles from the Abbey of Saint Gall; the region is also close to where Notker is believed to have derived from. The monk himself relates that he was raised by Adalbert, a former soldier who had fought against the <a href="/wiki/Saxons" title="Saxons">Saxons</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Pannonian_Avars" title="Pannonian Avars">Avars</a> ("Huns" in his text) and the Slavs under the command of Kerold, brother of <a href="/wiki/Hildegard,_wife_of_Charlemagne" class="mw-redirect" title="Hildegard, wife of Charlemagne">Hildegard</a>, Charlemagne's second wife; he was also a friend of Adalbert's son, Werinbert, another monk at Saint Gall, who died as the book was in progress.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> His teacher was <a href="/wiki/Grimald_von_Wei%C3%9Fenburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Grimald von Weißenburg">Grimald von Weißenburg</a>, the Abbot of Saint Gall from 841 to 872, who was, the monk claims, himself a pupil of <a href="/wiki/Alcuin" title="Alcuin">Alcuin</a>.
</p><p>The monk's untitled work, referred to by modern scholars as <i>De Carolo Magno</i> ("Concerning Charles the Great") or <i>Gesta Caroli Magni</i> ("The Deeds of Charles the Great"), is not a biography but consists instead of two books of anecdotes relating chiefly to the Emperor <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a> and his family, whose virtues are insistently invoked. It was written for <a href="/wiki/Charles_the_Fat" title="Charles the Fat">Charles the Fat</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> great-grandson of Charlemagne, who visited Saint Gall in 883.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> It has been scorned by traditional historians, who refer to the Monk as one who "took pleasure in amusing anecdotes and witty tales, but who was ill-informed about the true march of historical events", and describe the work itself as a "mass of legend, saga, invention and reckless blundering": historical figures are claimed as living when in fact dead; claims are attributed to false sources (in one instance,<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> the Monk claims that "to this King <a href="/wiki/Pepin_the_Short" title="Pepin the Short">Pepin</a> [the Short] the learned <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> has devoted almost an entire book of his <i>Ecclesiastical History</i>"; no such account exists in Bede's history – unsurprisingly, given that Bede died in 735 during the reign of Charlemagne's grandfather <a href="/wiki/Charles_Martel" title="Charles Martel">Charles Martel</a>); and Saint Gall is frequently referenced as a location in anecdotes,<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> regardless of historical verisimilitude (<a href="/wiki/Pepin_the_Hunchback" title="Pepin the Hunchback">Pepin the Hunchback</a>, for example, is supposed to have been sent to Saint Gall as punishment for his rebellion, and – in a <a href="/wiki/Trope_(literature)" title="Trope (literature)">trope</a> owed to <a href="/wiki/Livy" title="Livy">Livy</a>'s tale of <a href="/wiki/Tall_poppy_syndrome" title="Tall poppy syndrome">Tarquin and the poppies</a> – earns a promotion to rich <a href="/wiki/Pr%C3%BCm_Abbey" title="Prüm Abbey">Prüm Abbey</a> after advising Charlemagne through an implicit parable of hoeing thistles to execute another group of rebels). The Monk also mocks and criticizes bishops and the prideful, high-born incompetent, showy in dress and fastidious and lazy in habits, whilst lauding the wise and skillful government of the Emperor with nods to the deserving poor. Several of the Monk's tales, such as that of the nine rings of the Avar stronghold, have been used in modern biographies of Charlemagne.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2014)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Martyrology">Martyrology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Martyrology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In his martyrology, Notker appeared to corroborate one of <a href="/wiki/St_Columba" class="mw-redirect" title="St Columba">St Columba</a>'s miracles. <a href="/wiki/St_Columba" class="mw-redirect" title="St Columba">St Columba</a>, being an important father of Irish monasticism, was also important to <a href="/wiki/St_Gall" class="mw-redirect" title="St Gall">St Gall</a> and thus to Notker's own monastery. <a href="/wiki/Adomnan_of_Iona" class="mw-redirect" title="Adomnan of Iona">Adomnan of Iona</a> had written that at one point Columba had through clairvoyance seen a city in Italy near Rome being destroyed by fiery sulphur as a divine punishment and that three thousand people had perished. And shortly after Columba saw this, sailors from Gaul arrived to tell the news of it. Notker claimed in his martyrology that this event happened and that an earthquake had destroyed a city which was called 'new'. It is unclear what this city was that Notker was claiming, although some thought it may have been Naples (previously called 'Neapolis' – new city). However Naples was destroyed by a volcano in 512 before Columba was born, and not during Columba's lifetime.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Others">Others</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Others">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Notker completed the <i>Breviarium Regum Francorum</i> ("Breviary of the Frankish Kings") begun by <a href="/wiki/Erchanbert" class="mw-redirect" title="Erchanbert">Erchanbert</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Notker_the_Stammerer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Scholars vary on evaluating Notker's main legacy; the priest <a href="/wiki/Alban_Butler" title="Alban Butler">Alban Butler</a> asserted that his sequences were his most important achievement,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButlerDoyle199937-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> while the historian <a href="/wiki/Rosamond_McKitterick" title="Rosamond McKitterick">Rosamond McKitterick</a> states that he is best remembered for the <i>Gesta Caroli Magni</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcKitterick2018297_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcKitterick2018297-23">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Notker and <a href="/wiki/Solomon_II_(bishop_of_Constance)" title="Solomon II (bishop of Constance)">Solomon II</a> were the most important writers educated at Saint Gall,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcKitterick2018297_23-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcKitterick2018297-23">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> and Notker was among the both leading literary scribes and scholars of his time.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_paras._2,_8_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHiley2001§_paras._2,_8-24">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> He was the namesake of the later scholars <a href="/wiki/Notker_Physicus" title="Notker Physicus">Notker Physicus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Notker_Labeo" title="Notker Labeo">Notker Labeo</a>, who are referred to as "Notker II" and "Notker III" respectively.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJaffe1985165_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJaffe1985165-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On Notker's canonization status, the English cleric <a href="/wiki/John_Donne" title="John Donne">John Donne</a> noted that "he is a private Saint, for a few Parishes".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaker-Smith1975171_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaker-Smith1975171-25">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> According to the 16th century historian <a href="/wiki/Henricus_Canisius" title="Henricus Canisius">Henricus Canisius</a>, Notker' Sainthood was granted by <a href="/wiki/Leo_X" class="mw-redirect" title="Leo X">Leo X</a> in 1512 for Saint Gall and nearby churches, and in 1513 for the <a href="/wiki/Prince-Bishopric_of_Constance" title="Prince-Bishopric of Constance">Diocese of Constance</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaker-Smith1975171_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaker-Smith1975171-25">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In Saint Gall and other churches he is commemorated annually on 6 April.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer2022§_para._1-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Notker was never formally canonized.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512_8-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJulian1892512-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the mid-19th century the Swiss music scholar Anselm Schubiger was the first to transcribe almost all of Notker's extant melodies into modern notation. Many of his transcriptions are still in use, though older manuscript sources are available now that Schubiger did not have access to, meaning that "a more comprehensive approach to the sources will produce readings that are closer to Notker's own use, and better musically".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrocker2001_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrocker2001-26">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
