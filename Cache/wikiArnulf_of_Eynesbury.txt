					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><p>
Saint <b>Arnulf of Eynesbury</b> is an obscure English saint, apparently a <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a> who lived in the 8th or 9th century (Alford 1663 quotes a date of death of AD 740). He was venerated in <a href="/wiki/Eynesbury,_Cambridgeshire" title="Eynesbury, Cambridgeshire">Eynesbury</a>, about half a mile from <a href="/wiki/St_Neots" title="St Neots">St Neots</a>, <a href="/wiki/Cambridgeshire" title="Cambridgeshire">Cambridgeshire</a>, in the 9th century (feast day 22 August).<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background">Background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Arnulf_of_Eynesbury&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The name <i>Eynesbury</i> is probably a corruption of <i>Ernulfs-bury</i> and refers to the site where he had his cell, which was destroyed in the <a href="/wiki/Treaty_of_Wedmore" title="Treaty of Wedmore">Danish invasions</a> in the late 9th century. 
The saint seems to have been forgotten by the 11th century, as <i><a href="/wiki/On_the_resting-places_of_the_saints" class="mw-redirect" title="On the resting-places of the saints">On the resting-places of the saints</a></i> mentions <a href="/wiki/Saint_Neot" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Neot">Saint Neot</a> (feast day 31 July) but not Arnulf.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His historicity is doubtful. He may also have been a folkloristic duplicate of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Arnulf_of_Metz" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Arnulf of Metz">Saint Arnulf of Metz</a> (this supposition is also due to Alford 1663, who notes that the saints' feast days are identical, and who cites a French tradition according to which the remains of Arnulf of Metz were translated to England).<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
