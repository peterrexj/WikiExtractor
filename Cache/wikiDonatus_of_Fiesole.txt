<p><b>Donatus of Fiesole</b> (died 876) was an Irish teacher and poet, and <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Fiesole" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Fiesole">Bishop of Fiesole</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Donatus_of_Fiesole&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Donatus was born in Ireland to noble parents towards the end of the eighth century.<sup id="cite_ref-dnb_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dnb-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Despite there being little biographical detail in the tenth/eleventh-century <i>Vita sancti Donati episcopi</i>, Donatus is one of the better documented of the Irish <i>peregrini</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-breen_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-breen-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There is reason to believe that he was educated in the monastic school of <a href="/wiki/Inis_Cealtra" title="Inis Cealtra">Inishcaltra</a>, a little island in Lough Derg, near the Galway shore, now better known as Holy Island: so he was probably a native of that part of the country. He became a priest and in course of time a bishop: he was greatly distinguished as a professor.<sup id="cite_ref-joyce_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-joyce-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/William_Turner_(bishop_of_Buffalo)" title="William Turner (bishop of Buffalo)">William Turner</a>, writing in the <i><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia" title="Catholic Encyclopedia">Catholic Encyclopedia</a></i>, in an ancient collection of the <i>Vitae Patrum</i>, of which an eleventh-century copy exists in the Laurentian Library of <a href="/wiki/Florence" title="Florence">Florence</a>, there is an account of the life of Donatus, which states that about 816 Donatus visited the tombs of the <a href="/wiki/Apostles_in_the_New_Testament" title="Apostles in the New Testament">Apostles</a> in Rome with his friend, <a href="/wiki/Andrew_the_Scot" title="Andrew the Scot">Andrew Scotus</a>. They remained in Rome for a considerable time and then set out once more, directing their steps now towards Tuscany, till at length they reached Fiesole,<sup id="cite_ref-Turner_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Turner-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> where they entered the hospice of the monastery, intending to rest there for a week or two, and then to resume their journey.<sup id="cite_ref-joyce_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-joyce-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop_of_Fiesole">Bishop of Fiesole</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Donatus_of_Fiesole&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Bishop of Fiesole">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 829 Donatus was elected bishop of Fiesole. The traditional account relates that the people were praying to be sent a bishop to replace one drowned by the feudal lords.<sup id="cite_ref-staley_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-staley-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> When Donatus entered the <a href="/wiki/Fiesole_Cathedral" title="Fiesole Cathedral">Cathedral of Saint Romulus</a>,
the bells spontaneously began ringing and the candles lit. The people took that for a sign their prayers had been heard.<sup id="cite_ref-dnb_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dnb-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> It is also possible that no locals wanted the position given the fate of its previous incumbent.<sup id="cite_ref-staley_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-staley-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Raised by popular acclaim to the See of Fiesole, Donatus made Andrew his archdeacon. He encouraged Andrew to restore the church of San Martino di Mensola and to found a monastery there.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Donatus founded a school in Florence under the patronage of <a href="/wiki/Lothair_I" title="Lothair I">Lothair</a>, where he taught grammar and metrical composition for many years.<sup id="cite_ref-Turner_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Turner-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In 840, he led a contingent of troops against the Saracens. He was in Rome in 844 when Louis II was crowned King of Italy, and for his subsequent coronation as emperor in 850.<sup id="cite_ref-breen_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-breen-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Also in 850, he gave the Church St. Brigid at Piacenza, to Columbanus' abbey at Bobbio,<sup id="cite_ref-denver_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-denver-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> provided that they establish there a hospice for Irish pilgrims. Piacenza was an important stop on the <a href="/wiki/Via_Francigena" title="Via Francigena">Via Francigena</a>. He joined the king and the pope in judging a dispute between the bishops of Arezzo and Siena. He also attended the Roman synod of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Nicholas_I" title="Pope Nicholas I">Pope Nicholas I</a> on 18 November 861.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The church of Fiesole had suffered much in its property and prerogatives from the emperors, and the Normans had destroyed its charters. Donatus applied for redress to the emperor Louis, who in 866 granted his request. A confirmation of this grant was obtained subsequently by Donatus from Charles the Bald at Placentia, with the condition annexed that anyone who infringed it should pay the church thirty pounds of gold.<sup id="cite_ref-dnb_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dnb-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Donatus served as a bishop, teacher, and public administrator for forty-seven years.
</p><p>He was buried in the cathedral, where his epitaph, dictated by himself, may still be seen.<sup id="cite_ref-benigni_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-benigni-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> In it he alludes to his birth in Ireland, his years in the service of the princes of Italy (<a href="/wiki/Lothair_I" title="Lothair I">Lothair</a> and <a href="/wiki/Louis_II_of_Italy" title="Louis II of Italy">Louis</a>), his episcopate at Fiesole, and his activity as a teacher of <a href="/wiki/Grammar" title="Grammar">grammar</a> and poetry.<sup id="cite_ref-Turner_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Turner-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Donatus_of_Fiesole&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Donatus did much to promote the cult of <a href="/wiki/Brigid_of_Kildare" title="Brigid of Kildare">Brigid of Kildare</a> and composed a metrical "Life of the St. Brigid". When it was printed by Colgan in 1647, the text was attributed to Coelan, an Irish monk of the eighth century, and only its foreword, which refers to previous Lives by Ultan and Aileran, was ascribed to the pen of Donatus.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His <i>Life of Saint Brigid</i> is interspersed with short lyrical poems. The best known of these is the twelve-line poem in which he describes the beauty and fertility of his native land, and the prowess and piety of its inhabitants.
</p><p>Like Columkille, Donatus always cherished a tender regretful love for Ireland; and like him also he wrote a short poem in praise of it which is still preserved. It is in Latin, and the following is a translation, made by a Dublin poet many years ago:
</p>
<blockquote><div class="poem">
<p>Far westward lies an isle of ancient fame, By nature bless'd; and Scotia is her name, <br />
Enroll'd in books: exhaustless is her store, Of veiny silver, and of golden ore. <br />
Her fruitful soil, for ever teems with wealth, With gems her waters, and her air with health; <br />
Her verdant fields with milk and honey flow; Her woolly fleeces vie with virgin snow; <br />
Her waving furrows float with bearded corn; And arms and arts her envied sons adorn!<br />
No savage bear, with lawless fury roves, Nor fiercer lion, through her peaceful groves; <br />
No poison there infects, no scaly snake Creeps through the grass, nor frog annoys the lake; <br />
An island worthy of its pious race, In war triumphant, and unmatch'd in peace!<sup id="cite_ref-joyce_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-joyce-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
</div></blockquote>
<p>According to Donatus, St. Brigid visited his deathbed to give him spiritual strength and comfort. (A similar story is told of his archdeacon Andrew, whose sister Bridget, miraculously arrives from Ireland shortly before his death.) Donatus' story, preserved in manuscript in the Laurentian Library in Florence, recounts that Brigid flew to his deathbed, and before she touched him, she hung her cloak on a sunbeam to dry.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Donatus_of_Fiesole&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>His feast day is 22 October.<sup id="cite_ref-denver_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-denver-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
The numerous locations and churches incorporating his name, San Donato, provide evidence of his influence and popularity throughout Tuscany.<sup id="cite_ref-benigni_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-benigni-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
