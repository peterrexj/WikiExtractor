<p><b>Atto of Pistoia</b>: <i>Santo Atão</i>; c. 1070 – 22 May 1153) was a <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic</a> bishop and a professed member from the <a href="/wiki/Vallumbrosan_Order" class="mw-redirect" title="Vallumbrosan Order">Vallumbrosan Order</a> as well as the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Pistoia" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Pistoia">Bishop of Pistoia</a> and a noted historiographer.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Atto_of_Pistoia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Atto was born around 1070. Spanish historian <a href="/wiki/Enrique_Fl%C3%B3rez" title="Enrique Flórez">Enrique Flórez</a> thought Atto was from  <a href="/wiki/Badajoz" title="Badajoz">Badajoz</a> in Extremadura, Spain close to the Portuguese border.<sup id="cite_ref-Mateucci_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mateucci-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He went as a pilgrim to Italy, and stopped at <a href="/wiki/Vallombrosa_Abbey" title="Vallombrosa Abbey">Vallombrosa Abbey</a> where he was welcomed by the abbot, Bernardo degli Uberti.<sup id="cite_ref-dop_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dop-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> By 1100 he was a <a href="/wiki/Vallombrosians" title="Vallombrosians">Vallombrosian</a> monk at <a href="/wiki/Vallombrosa" title="Vallombrosa">Vallombrosa</a> (in <a href="/wiki/Tuscany" title="Tuscany">Tuscany</a>), and became Abbot in 1105.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He became abbott-general around 1120. He wrote lives of <a href="/wiki/John_Gualbert" title="John Gualbert">John Gualbert</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bernard_degli_Uberti" title="Bernard degli Uberti">Bernard degli Uberti</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Parma" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Parma">bishop of Parma</a>. 
</p><p>In 1135 Atto was made <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Pistoia" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Pistoia">Bishop of Pistoia</a>, also in Tuscany. He continued to follow the rule of his order and served as visitor to the monasteries.<sup id="cite_ref-Mateucci_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mateucci-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Together with his Canons he recited the Hours of the Divine Office, as was already customary in the Cloister. As bishop, he managed the delicate relationship with the municipal authorities of Pistoia, often marked by breakdowns and clashes.<sup id="cite_ref-dop_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dop-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1145 he transferred to <a href="/wiki/Pistoia" title="Pistoia">Pistoia</a> from <a href="/wiki/Santiago_de_Compostela" title="Santiago de Compostela">Santiago de Compostela</a> certain relics of the Apostle <a href="/wiki/James_the_Great" title="James the Great">James the Great</a> and dedicated an altar to Saint James in the <a href="/wiki/Pistoia_Cathedral" title="Pistoia Cathedral">Cathedral of Saint Zeno</a> to house them.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> This later gave rise to a chapel dedicated to St. James. This had both economic and political ramifications, as pilgrims soon began to come to Pistoia to honor the saint. He also built a hospital in honor of the apostle.
</p><p>In 1337 the body of the saint was found inside the Church of San Giovanni in Corte and from there moved to the <a href="/wiki/Pistoia_Cathedral" title="Pistoia Cathedral">Cathedral of San Zeno</a>. From the middle of the seventeenth century, up to the beginning of the nineteenth century, on the day of June 21, in his honor, the so-called "fires of Sant'Atto" were held in Piazza Duomo. People from neighboring districts would flock to the square, which was illuminated with countless torches displayed from ornate window sills.<sup id="cite_ref-dop_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dop-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
