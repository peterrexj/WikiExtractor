<p><b>Anthimus of Nicomedia</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Ἄνθιμος Νικομηδείας</span>; martyred 303 or 311&#8211;12), was the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Nicomedia" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Nicomedia">bishop of Nicomedia</a> in <a href="/wiki/Bithynia" title="Bithynia">Bithynia</a>, where he was beheaded during a <a href="/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians" title="Persecution of Christians">persecution of Christians</a>, traditionally placed under <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a> (following <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea" class="mw-redirect" title="Eusebius of Caesarea">Eusebius</a>), in which "rivers of blood" flowed.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthimus_of_Nicomedia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nicomedia was Diocletian's chief place of residence<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and was half-Christian, the palace itself being filled with Christians. Christian sources memorialized the "<a href="/wiki/20,000_Martyrs_of_Nicomedia" title="20,000 Martyrs of Nicomedia">20,000 Martyrs of Nicomedia</a>".  The main Christian church of Nicomedia was destroyed on 23 February 303; the First Edict was published on the following day. Shortly after the promulgation of the edict, a fire broke out in the imperial palace; the Christians were blamed.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The massacres transpired in the Christian communities of Bithynia after altars were set up in the marketplaces, in which no transactions were permitted until a token sacrifice to the gods and the <i><a href="/wiki/Daemon_(classical_mythology)" class="mw-redirect" title="Daemon (classical mythology)">daemon</a></i> of the Augustus had been performed.
</p><p>At the request of members of his congregation, Anthimus took refuge in the small village of Omana, where he provided aid to survivors<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and sent letters exhorting the Christians to stand firm. When the soldiers of Maximinus were sent to find him, he welcomed them and fed them before revealing who he was.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> (The detail referring to Maximinus suggests that two persecutions have been <a href="/wiki/Conflated" class="mw-redirect" title="Conflated">conflated</a>.) Amazed at his kindness, the soldiers promised him to tell Maximinus that they had not found him, but Anthimus returned with them, and converted and baptized them along the way.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Philip_Schaff" title="Philip Schaff">Philip Schaff</a> and <a href="/wiki/Henry_Wace_(Anglican_priest)" class="mw-redirect" title="Henry Wace (Anglican priest)">Henry Wace</a> note that a fragmentary letter preserved in the <i><a href="/wiki/Chronicon_Paschale" title="Chronicon Paschale">Chronicon Paschale</a></i>, as written in prison by the presbyter <a href="/wiki/Lucian_of_Antioch" title="Lucian of Antioch">Lucian of Antioch</a> awaiting death, mentions Anthimus, bishop of Nicomedia, as having just suffered martyrdom. Schaff and Wace note that Lucian was imprisoned and put to death during the persecution of <a href="/wiki/Maximinus_Daia" class="mw-redirect" title="Maximinus Daia">Maximinus Daia</a> (in 311 or 312) and therefore conclude that, if the fragment is genuine, Anthimus suffered martyrdom not under Diocletian but under Maximinus.
</p>
