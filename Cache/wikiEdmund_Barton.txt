<p><b>Sir Edmund</b> "<b>Toby</b>" <b>Barton</b> <span style="font-size:85%;"><span class="noexcerpt nowraplinks" style="font-size:100%; font-weight:normal;"><a href="/wiki/Knight_Grand_Cross_of_the_Order_of_St_Michael_and_St_George" class="mw-redirect" title="Knight Grand Cross of the Order of St Michael and St George">GCMG</a>&#32;<a href="/wiki/King%27s_Counsel" title="King&#39;s Counsel">KC</a></span></span> (18 January 1849&#160;&#8211;&#32;7 January 1920) was an Australian statesman, barrister and jurist who served as the first <a href="/wiki/Prime_minister_of_Australia" class="mw-redirect" title="Prime minister of Australia">prime minister of Australia</a> from 1901 to 1903, holding office as the leader of the <a href="/wiki/Protectionist_Party" title="Protectionist Party">Protectionist Party</a>. He resigned to become a founding member of the <a href="/wiki/High_Court_of_Australia" title="High Court of Australia">High Court of Australia</a>, on which he served until his death.
</p><p>Barton was an early supporter of the <a href="/wiki/Federation_of_Australia" title="Federation of Australia">federation of the Australian colonies</a>, the goal of which he summarised as "a nation for a continent, and a continent for a nation". After the retirement of <a href="/wiki/Henry_Parkes" title="Henry Parkes">Henry Parkes</a> he came to be seen as the leader of the federation movement in New South Wales. He was a delegate to the <a href="/wiki/Constitutional_Convention_(Australia)" title="Constitutional Convention (Australia)">constitutional conventions</a>, playing a key role in the drafting of <a href="/wiki/Constitution_of_Australia" title="Constitution of Australia">a national constitution</a>, and was one of the lead campaigners for federation in <a href="/wiki/1898%E2%80%931900_Australian_constitutional_referendums" title="1898–1900 Australian constitutional referendums">the subsequent referendums</a>. In late 1900, despite the initial "<a href="/wiki/Hopetoun_Blunder" title="Hopetoun Blunder">Hopetoun Blunder</a>", Barton was commissioned to form a <a href="/wiki/Caretaker_government" title="Caretaker government">caretaker government</a> as Australia's first prime minister. His term began on 1 January 1901, the date on which federation occurred.
</p><p>At the <a href="/wiki/1901_Australian_federal_election" title="1901 Australian federal election">first federal election</a> in March 1901, Barton and the Protectionists won the most seats, but were well short of a majority. He was able to remain as prime minister by forming an alliance with the fledgling <a href="/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party" title="Australian Labor Party">Australian Labor Party</a> (ALP), which held the balance of power. The <a href="/wiki/Barton_government" title="Barton government">Barton government</a> established a number of new national institutions, including the <a href="/wiki/Australian_Defence_Force" title="Australian Defence Force">Australian Defence Force</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Australian_Public_Service" title="Australian Public Service">Commonwealth Public Service</a>. It introduced <a href="/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_Australia" title="Women&#39;s suffrage in Australia">nation-wide women's suffrage</a>, and laid the foundations of the <a href="/wiki/White_Australia_policy" title="White Australia policy">White Australia policy</a> with the <i><a href="/wiki/Immigration_Restriction_Act_1901" title="Immigration Restriction Act 1901">Immigration Restriction Act 1901</a></i>.
</p><p>Barton left politics in 1903 to become one of the three founding members of the High Court, which his government had created. He was succeeded as prime minister by <a href="/wiki/Alfred_Deakin" title="Alfred Deakin">Alfred Deakin</a>. On the court, Barton was able to shape the judicial interpretation of the constitution he had helped write.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span></h2>
<p>Barton was born on 18 January 1849 in <a href="/wiki/Glebe,_New_South_Wales" title="Glebe, New South Wales">Glebe, Sydney, New South Wales</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton20005_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton20005-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He was the eleventh of twelve children born to Mary Louisa (née Whydah) and William Barton. He had seven sisters and four brothers, including the writer <a href="/wiki/George_Burnett_Barton" title="George Burnett Barton">George Burnett Barton</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton20006–7_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton20006–7-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Three of his siblings died during his childhood.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> His given name had not previously been used in the family, and may have been in honour of the recently deceased explorer <a href="/wiki/Edmund_Kennedy" title="Edmund Kennedy">Edmund Kennedy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton20005_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton20005-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Barton's parents were both born in London, England, although his father's family was originally from <a href="/wiki/Devon" title="Devon">Devon</a>. They arrived in the Colony of New South Wales in 1827, and all but two of their children were born in Australia. William Barton worked variously as an accountant, bazaar proprietor, stockbroker, and real estate agent. His business ventures were not always successful, and he went bankrupt on one occasion.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton20002–5_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton20002–5-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Childhood_and_education">Childhood and education</span></h3>
<p>Barton spent his early years in <a href="/wiki/Glebe" title="Glebe">Glebe</a>, but in 1851 the family moved into the inner city, living on Cumberland Street in <a href="/wiki/The_Rocks,_New_South_Wales" title="The Rocks, New South Wales">The Rocks</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton20005_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton20005-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He had a relatively comfortable upbringing, although his father faced financial difficulties on a number of occasions. To support the family during those periods, his mother ran a boarding school for girls.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton20004_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton20004-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> His parents were both highly literate, and his mother in particular "provided much of the direction and encouragement for Edmund's impressive academic achievement".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton200015_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton200015-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Barton began his formal education at what is now the <a href="/wiki/Fort_Street_Public_School" title="Fort Street Public School">Fort Street Public School</a>. He later attended <a href="/wiki/Sydney_Grammar_School" title="Sydney Grammar School">Sydney Grammar School</a>, possibly as one of the first students after the school's opening in 1857.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton20006_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton20006-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> One of his close friends in his youth was <a href="/wiki/Richard_Edward_O%27Connor" title="Richard Edward O&#39;Connor">Richard O'Connor</a>, who would eventually join him on the High Court. Barton was the <a href="/wiki/Dux#Education" title="Dux">dux</a> and school captain at Sydney Grammar in 1863 and 1864. He <a href="/wiki/Matriculated" class="mw-redirect" title="Matriculated">matriculated</a> at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Sydney" title="University of Sydney">University of Sydney</a> in 1865, aged 16, and was awarded a special prize by the university senate.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton20009_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton20009-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Barton failed to win one of the three entrance scholarships to the University, owing to "an insufficiency of mathematics".<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:EBarton1866.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ed/EBarton1866.jpg/170px-EBarton1866.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="240" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ed/EBarton1866.jpg/255px-EBarton1866.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ed/EBarton1866.jpg/340px-EBarton1866.jpg 2x" data-file-width="463" data-file-height="655" /></a><figcaption>Edmund Barton aged 17</figcaption></figure>
<p>At university, Barton specialised in <a href="/wiki/Classics" title="Classics">classics</a> but also studied English literature, mathematics, physics, and French.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton20009_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton20009-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> He became fluent in Ancient Greek and Latin, and retained a command of both later in life.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> A new professor, <a href="/wiki/Charles_Badham" title="Charles Badham">Charles Badham</a>, arrived in 1867 and was a "profound influence". Barton won scholarships in his second and third years. He graduated Bachelor of Arts in 1868 with first-class honours, and was awarded the equivalent of the <a href="/wiki/University_Medal" title="University Medal">University Medal</a> as well as a prize of £20. He proceeded to Master of Arts by examination in 1870.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton200010_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton200010-15">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Sporting_activities">Sporting activities</span></h3>
<p>Barton was a member of the <a href="/wiki/Sydney_University_Cricket_Club" title="Sydney University Cricket Club">Sydney University Cricket Club</a> and a founding member of the <a href="/wiki/Sydney_Rowing_Club" title="Sydney Rowing Club">Sydney Rowing Club</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> On a cricketing trip to <a href="/wiki/Newcastle,_New_South_Wales" title="Newcastle, New South Wales">Newcastle</a> in 1870 he met <a href="/wiki/Jane_Barton" title="Jane Barton">Jane Mason Ross</a>, whom he married in 1877.<sup id="cite_ref-austfirstpm_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-austfirstpm-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1879, Barton <a href="/wiki/Umpire_(cricket)" title="Umpire (cricket)">umpired</a> a cricket match at <a href="/wiki/Sydney_Cricket_Ground" title="Sydney Cricket Ground">Sydney Cricket Ground</a> between <a href="/wiki/New_South_Wales" title="New South Wales">New South Wales</a> and an English touring side captained by <a href="/wiki/George_Harris,_4th_Baron_Harris" title="George Harris, 4th Baron Harris">Lord Harris</a>. After a controversial decision by Barton's colleague <a href="/wiki/George_Coulthard" title="George Coulthard">George Coulthard</a> against the home side, the crowd spilled onto the pitch and assaulted some of the English players, leading to <a href="/wiki/Sydney_Riot_of_1879" title="Sydney Riot of 1879">international cricket's first riot</a>. In the subsequent public controversy, George Reid censured Coulthard and absolved the crowd, while Barton defended Coulthard and did not spare the crowd.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> The publicity that attended the young Barton's presence of mind in defusing that situation reputedly helped him take his first step towards becoming Australia's first prime minister, winning a state lower house seat later that year.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="New_South_Wales_politics">New South Wales politics</span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Portrait_of_Edmund_Barton_as_Speaker_of_the_New_South_Wales_Legislative_Assembly.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/Portrait_of_Edmund_Barton_as_Speaker_of_the_New_South_Wales_Legislative_Assembly.jpg/170px-Portrait_of_Edmund_Barton_as_Speaker_of_the_New_South_Wales_Legislative_Assembly.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="220" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/Portrait_of_Edmund_Barton_as_Speaker_of_the_New_South_Wales_Legislative_Assembly.jpg/255px-Portrait_of_Edmund_Barton_as_Speaker_of_the_New_South_Wales_Legislative_Assembly.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/Portrait_of_Edmund_Barton_as_Speaker_of_the_New_South_Wales_Legislative_Assembly.jpg/340px-Portrait_of_Edmund_Barton_as_Speaker_of_the_New_South_Wales_Legislative_Assembly.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2837" data-file-height="3666" /></a><figcaption>Barton as Speaker of the New South Wales Legislative Assembly</figcaption></figure>
<p>In <a href="/wiki/1876_University_of_Sydney_colonial_by-election" title="1876 University of Sydney colonial by-election">1876</a> Barton stood for the <a href="/wiki/New_South_Wales_Legislative_Assembly" title="New South Wales Legislative Assembly">New South Wales Legislative Assembly</a> in the poll of the graduates of the <a href="/wiki/Electoral_district_of_University_of_Sydney" title="Electoral district of University of Sydney">University of Sydney</a> (who were required to wear gowns for the occasion), but was beaten by <a href="/wiki/William_Charles_Windeyer" title="William Charles Windeyer">William Charles Windeyer</a> 49 votes to 43.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> When Windeyer resigned, Barton won the <a href="/wiki/1879_University_of_Sydney_colonial_by-election" title="1879 University of Sydney colonial by-election">1879 by-election</a>. When it was abolished in 1880, he became the member for <a href="/wiki/Electoral_district_of_Wellington_(New_South_Wales)" title="Electoral district of Wellington (New South Wales)">Wellington</a>, from November 1880 to 1882, and <a href="/wiki/Electoral_district_of_East_Sydney" title="Electoral district of East Sydney">East Sydney</a>, from November 1882 to January 1887. At this stage he considered it "almost unnecessary" to point out his support for free trade.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Edmund_Barton,_Q.C.,_M.L.C.,_ca._1889_SLNSW.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a6/Edmund_Barton%2C_Q.C.%2C_M.L.C.%2C_ca._1889_SLNSW.jpg/220px-Edmund_Barton%2C_Q.C.%2C_M.L.C.%2C_ca._1889_SLNSW.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="286" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a6/Edmund_Barton%2C_Q.C.%2C_M.L.C.%2C_ca._1889_SLNSW.jpg/330px-Edmund_Barton%2C_Q.C.%2C_M.L.C.%2C_ca._1889_SLNSW.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a6/Edmund_Barton%2C_Q.C.%2C_M.L.C.%2C_ca._1889_SLNSW.jpg/440px-Edmund_Barton%2C_Q.C.%2C_M.L.C.%2C_ca._1889_SLNSW.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1819" data-file-height="2364" /></a><figcaption>Barton in 1889</figcaption></figure>
<p>In 1882, he became <a href="/wiki/Speaker_of_the_New_South_Wales_Legislative_Assembly" title="Speaker of the New South Wales Legislative Assembly">Speaker of the New South Wales Legislative Assembly</a>. He was aged only 33, and was the youngest person to have been chosen presiding officer of any Australian legislative chamber. In 1884 he was elected President of the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Sydney_Union" title="University of Sydney Union">University of Sydney Union</a>. In 1887, he was appointed to the <a href="/wiki/New_South_Wales_Legislative_Council" title="New South Wales Legislative Council">New South Wales Legislative Council</a> at the instigation of Sir <a href="/wiki/Henry_Parkes" title="Henry Parkes">Henry Parkes</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-apmb_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-apmb-22">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> In January 1889, he agreed to being appointed <a href="/wiki/Attorney_General_of_New_South_Wales" title="Attorney General of New South Wales">Attorney General of New South Wales</a> in <a href="/wiki/George_Dibbs" title="George Dibbs">George Dibbs</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Protectionist_Party" title="Protectionist Party">Protectionist</a> government, despite his previous support for free trade. This government lasted only until March, when Parkes formed a government again.<sup id="cite_ref-nsw_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nsw-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Campaign_for_federation">Campaign for federation</span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="1891_National_Australasian_Convention">1891 National Australasian Convention</span></h3>
<p>Barton was an early supporter of federation, which became a serious political agenda after Henry Parkes' <a href="/wiki/Tenterfield_Oration" title="Tenterfield Oration">Tenterfield Oration</a>, and was a delegate to the March 1891 <a href="/wiki/Constitutional_Convention_(Australia)#1891_convention" title="Constitutional Convention (Australia)">National Australasian Convention</a>. At the convention he made clear his support for the principle that "trade and intercourse&#160;... shall be absolutely free" in a federal Australia. He also advocated that not just the lower house but also the upper house should be representative and that appeals to the <a href="/wiki/Judicial_Committee_of_the_Privy_Council" title="Judicial Committee of the Privy Council">Privy Council</a> should be abolished. He also took part in producing a draft constitution, which was substantially similar to the <a href="/wiki/Constitution_of_Australia" title="Constitution of Australia">Constitution of Australia</a> enacted in 1900.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Nevertheless, the protectionists were lukewarm supporters of federation and in June 1891, Barton resigned from the Council and stood for election to East Sydney and announced that "so long as Protection meant a Ministry of enemies to Federation, they would get no vote from him". He topped the poll and subsequently voted with Parkes, but refused to take a position in his minority government. After the <a href="/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party" title="Australian Labor Party">Labor Party</a> withdrew support and the government fell in October 1891, Parkes persuaded him to take over the leadership of the Federal movement in New South Wales.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Second_Attorney-Generalship">Second Attorney-Generalship</span></h3>
<p>Dibbs formed a Protectionist government in New South Wales, and Barton agreed to return to the office of Attorney General, with the right of carrying out private practice as a lawyer. His agreement was based on Dibbs agreeing to support federal resolutions in the coming parliamentary session. His attempt to draft the federal resolutions was delayed by a period as acting <a href="/wiki/Premier_of_New_South_Wales" title="Premier of New South Wales">Premier of New South Wales</a>, during which he had to deal with the <a href="/wiki/1892_Broken_Hill_miners%27_strike" title="1892 Broken Hill miners&#39; strike">1892 Broken Hill miners' strike</a> and the carriage of a complex electoral reform bill. He introduced the federal resolutions into the House on 22 November 1892, but was unable to get them considered in committee.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Meanwhile, he began a campaign to spread support for federation to the people with meetings in <a href="/wiki/Corowa" title="Corowa">Corowa</a> and <a href="/wiki/Albury" title="Albury">Albury</a> in December 1892. Although he finally managed to get the federal resolutions considered in committee in October 1893, he then could not get them listed for debate by the House. In December, he and <a href="/wiki/Richard_Edward_O%27Connor" title="Richard Edward O&#39;Connor">Richard O'Connor</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Justice_(New_South_Wales)" class="mw-redirect" title="Minister for Justice (New South Wales)">Minister for Justice</a>, were questioned about their agreement to act as private lawyers against the government in <i><a href="/wiki/David_Proudfoot_(engineer)" title="David Proudfoot (engineer)">Proudfoot</a> v. the Railway Commissioners</i>. While Barton resigned the brief, he lost a motion on the right of ministers to act in their professional capacity as lawyers in actions against the government, and immediately resigned as Attorney-General.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/1894_New_South_Wales_colonial_election" title="1894 New South Wales colonial election">July 1894</a>, Barton stood for re-election for <a href="/wiki/Electoral_district_of_Randwick" title="Electoral district of Randwick">Randwick</a>, since the electorate of East Sydney had been abolished, and lost. He did not stand for election in the <a href="/wiki/1895_New_South_Wales_colonial_election" title="1895 New South Wales colonial election">1895 election</a>, very possibly because of financial difficulties. However, he continued to campaign for federation and during the period between January 1893 to February 1897, Barton addressed nearly 300 meetings in New South Wales,<sup id="cite_ref-dab_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dab-24">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> including in the Sydney suburb of <a href="/wiki/Ashfield,_New_South_Wales" title="Ashfield, New South Wales">Ashfield</a> where he declared that "For the first time in history, we have a nation for a continent and a continent for a nation". By March 1897, he was considered "the acknowledged leader of the federal movement in all Australia".<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Australian_Federal_Convention_and_referendum">Australian Federal Convention and referendum</span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:EBarton2.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/ba/EBarton2.jpg/220px-EBarton2.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="159" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/ba/EBarton2.jpg/330px-EBarton2.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/ba/EBarton2.jpg/440px-EBarton2.jpg 2x" data-file-width="915" data-file-height="661" /></a><figcaption>Photo in 1898 of the future 1st Prime Minister of Australia Edmund Barton aged 49 and 2nd Prime Minister of Australia <a href="/wiki/Alfred_Deakin" title="Alfred Deakin">Alfred Deakin</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>In 1897, Barton topped the poll of the delegates elected from New South Wales to the <a href="/wiki/Constitutional_Convention_(Australia)" title="Constitutional Convention (Australia)">Constitutional Convention</a>, which developed a constitution for the proposed federation. Although <a href="/wiki/Samuel_Griffith" title="Samuel Griffith">Sir Samuel Griffith</a> wrote most of the text of the Constitution, Barton was the political leader who carried it through the Convention.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-apmb_22-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-apmb-22">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In May 1897, Barton was appointed for the second time to the Legislative Council on Reid's recommendations to take charge of the federation bill in the Upper House. This gave Reid's Attorney-General, <a href="/wiki/Jack_Want" title="Jack Want">Jack Want</a>, a free hand to oppose the bill. In September 1897, the convention met in <a href="/wiki/Sydney" title="Sydney">Sydney</a> to consider 286 proposed amendments from the colonies. It finalised its draft constitution in March 1898 and Barton went back to New South Wales to lead the campaign for a yes vote in the <a href="/wiki/1898%E2%80%931900_Australian_constitutional_referendums" title="1898–1900 Australian constitutional referendums">June referendum</a>. Although it gained majority support, it achieved only 71,595; 80,000 was the minimum number for it to pass.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In July 1898 Barton resigned from the Upper House to challenge Reid for the seat of <a href="/wiki/Electoral_district_of_Sydney-King" title="Electoral district of Sydney-King">Sydney-King</a> in the <a href="/wiki/1898_New_South_Wales_colonial_election" title="1898 New South Wales colonial election">1898 general election</a>, but narrowly lost. In September, he won a <a href="/wiki/1898_Hastings_and_Macleay_colonial_by-election" title="1898 Hastings and Macleay colonial by-election">by-election</a> for <a href="/wiki/Electoral_district_of_Hastings_and_Macleay" title="Electoral district of Hastings and Macleay">Hastings and Macleay</a> and was immediately elected <a href="/wiki/Leader_of_the_Opposition_(New_South_Wales)" title="Leader of the Opposition (New South Wales)">leader of the opposition</a>, which consisted of a mixture of pro-federation and anti-federation protectionists. In January 1899 Reid gained significant concessions from the other states and he joined Barton in campaigning for the <a href="/wiki/1898%E2%80%931900_Australian_constitutional_referendums" title="1898–1900 Australian constitutional referendums">second referendum</a> in June 1899, with Barton campaigning all over the state. It passed 107,420 votes to 82,741.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-nsw_23-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nsw-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In August 1899 when it became clear that the <a href="/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party" title="Australian Labor Party">Labor Party</a> could be manoeuvred into bringing down the Reid government, Barton resigned as leader of the opposition, as he was unacceptable to Labour, and <a href="/wiki/William_Lyne" title="William Lyne">William Lyne</a> took his place. He allegedly refused an offer to become Attorney-General again. Barton's Convention colleague, J.T. Walker, wrote at the time, "Barton is not well off financially, and some of his friends (he has many) are trying to help him. They would not be so ready to do so were he to join Lyne".<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> He resigned from Parliament in February 1900 so that he could travel to London with <a href="/wiki/Alfred_Deakin" title="Alfred Deakin">Alfred Deakin</a> and <a href="/wiki/Charles_Kingston" title="Charles Kingston">Charles Kingston</a> to explain the federation bill to the <a href="/wiki/Government_of_the_United_Kingdom" title="Government of the United Kingdom">Government of the United Kingdom</a>. Around the same time on 15 January 1900 Barton published a 28-page <i>United Australia</i> magazine to disseminate an informative narrative for the propositions of a future Federal Government that he hoped to lead.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> The British Government was adamant in its opposition to the abolition of appeals to the Privy Council as incorporated in the draft constitution; eventually, Barton agreed that constitutional (<i><a href="/wiki/Inter_se" title="Inter se">inter se</a></i>) matters would be finalised in the <a href="/wiki/High_Court_of_Australia" title="High Court of Australia">High Court of Australia</a>, but other matters could be appealed to the Privy Council. Edmund was a Justice of the High Court for 16 years.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Prime_Minister">Prime Minister</span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Barton_government" title="Barton government">Barton government</a></div>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Appointment">Appointment</span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Hopetoun_Blunder" title="Hopetoun Blunder">Hopetoun Blunder</a></div>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Federation_Pavilion,_Centennial_Park_01_(cropped).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c2/Federation_Pavilion%2C_Centennial_Park_01_%28cropped%29.jpg/220px-Federation_Pavilion%2C_Centennial_Park_01_%28cropped%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="282" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c2/Federation_Pavilion%2C_Centennial_Park_01_%28cropped%29.jpg/330px-Federation_Pavilion%2C_Centennial_Park_01_%28cropped%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c2/Federation_Pavilion%2C_Centennial_Park_01_%28cropped%29.jpg/440px-Federation_Pavilion%2C_Centennial_Park_01_%28cropped%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4052" data-file-height="5201" /></a><figcaption>The <a href="/wiki/Federation_Pavilion" title="Federation Pavilion">Federation Pavilion</a> at <a href="/wiki/Centennial_Parklands" title="Centennial Parklands">Centennial Park, Sydney</a>, during the swearing-in ceremony on 1 January 1901.<br /> <hr /> <div class="center" style="width:auto; margin-left:auto; margin-right:auto;"><b>External video</b></div> <div class="center" style="width:auto; margin-left:auto; margin-right:auto;"><span typeof="mw:File"><a href="/wiki/File:Nuvola_apps_kaboodle.svg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/Nuvola_apps_kaboodle.svg/16px-Nuvola_apps_kaboodle.svg.png" decoding="async" width="16" height="16" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/Nuvola_apps_kaboodle.svg/24px-Nuvola_apps_kaboodle.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/Nuvola_apps_kaboodle.svg/32px-Nuvola_apps_kaboodle.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="128" data-file-height="128" /></a></span> <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://aso.gov.au/titles/documentaries/inauguration-commonwealth/">Footage from <i>Inauguration of the Commonwealth</i></a></div></figcaption></figure>
<p>Barton was appointed <a href="/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Australia" title="Prime Minister of Australia">prime minister</a> on 1 January 1901, the day on which the new federal constitution came into effect. He and various other dignitaries were driven through the streets of Sydney in a procession watched by thousands of onlookers, beginning in <a href="/wiki/The_Domain,_Sydney" title="The Domain, Sydney">The Domain</a> and ending at <a href="/wiki/Centennial_Parklands" title="Centennial Parklands">Centennial Park</a>. A swearing-in ceremony for the new government was held at the purpose-built <a href="/wiki/Federation_Pavilion" title="Federation Pavilion">Federation Pavilion</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> The main focus was on <a href="/wiki/John_Hope,_7th_Earl_of_Hopetoun" title="John Hope, 7th Earl of Hopetoun">Lord Hopetoun</a>, Australia's first <a href="/wiki/Governor-General_of_Australia" title="Governor-General of Australia">governor-general</a>, who swore three separate oaths and read a message from <a href="/wiki/Queen_Victoria" title="Queen Victoria">Queen Victoria</a>. Barton and his ministry only took oaths of allegiance, and were not formally sworn in to the <a href="/wiki/Federal_Executive_Council_(Australia)" title="Federal Executive Council (Australia)">Federal Executive Council</a> until later in the afternoon.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELa_Nauze1962215_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELa_Nauze1962215-28">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> The events of the day were captured on film and distributed under the title <i><a href="/wiki/Inauguration_of_the_Commonwealth" title="Inauguration of the Commonwealth">Inauguration of the Commonwealth</a></i>, although the visibility of the leading participants is poor.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000228_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000228-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> The film has been described as Australia's first feature-length documentary.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Barton had only been commissioned to form a government one week before he became prime minister, due to what became known as the "<a href="/wiki/Hopetoun_Blunder" title="Hopetoun Blunder">Hopetoun Blunder</a>". The new governor-general Lord Hopetoun arrived in Australia on 15 December 1900.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000218_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000218-31">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> He was tasked by the <a href="/wiki/Colonial_Office" title="Colonial Office">Colonial Office</a> with selecting someone to form a caretaker government prior to the first federal election; this individual would be Australia's first prime minister.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000219_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000219-32">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> It was widely assumed that Barton would be offered the position, and he had begun making preparations for the role, including a draft platform for the first federal elections.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000217_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000217-33">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> On 19 December, however, Hopetoun commissioned <a href="/wiki/William_Lyne" title="William Lyne">William Lyne</a> to form a government. His reasons for doing so have been debated, but his decision was widely regarded as a mistake.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000220_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000220-34">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELa_Nauze1962208_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELa_Nauze1962208-35">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Lyne, the premier of New South Wales, had actively campaigned against federation, and the leading politicians in each of the other colonies refused to serve under him.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELa_Nauze1962209_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELa_Nauze1962209-36">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> On 24 December, he informed Hopetoun that he was unable to form a government and advised him to send for Barton.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000222_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000222-37">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> As a result, the composition of Australia's first cabinet was not formally announced until 30 December, two days before it was sworn in.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELa_Nauze1962210_38-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELa_Nauze1962210-38">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Caretaker_ministry_and_first_election">Caretaker ministry and first election</span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Barton_ministry" title="Barton ministry">Barton ministry</a></div>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Barton_Ministry_(cropped).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Barton_Ministry_%28cropped%29.jpg/220px-Barton_Ministry_%28cropped%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="192" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Barton_Ministry_%28cropped%29.jpg/330px-Barton_Ministry_%28cropped%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Barton_Ministry_%28cropped%29.jpg/440px-Barton_Ministry_%28cropped%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1216" data-file-height="1059" /></a><figcaption>Members of the first <a href="/wiki/Barton_ministry" title="Barton ministry">Barton ministry</a>, 1 January 1901</figcaption></figure>
<p>Barton assembled <a href="/wiki/Barton_ministry" title="Barton ministry">a ministry</a> that was described variously as a "cabinet of kings", "orchestra of conductors", and "army of generals".<sup id="cite_ref-abjorensen_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-abjorensen-39">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> It comprised four incumbent colonial premiers (including Lyne), two former premiers, and Barton's key allies <a href="/wiki/Alfred_Deakin" title="Alfred Deakin">Alfred Deakin</a> and <a href="/wiki/Richard_Edward_O%27Connor" title="Richard Edward O&#39;Connor">Richard O'Connor</a>. Deakin was ranked second in cabinet after Barton, and O'Connor would become the inaugural <a href="/wiki/Leader_of_the_Government_in_the_Senate_(Australia)" title="Leader of the Government in the Senate (Australia)">Leader of the Government in the Senate</a>. Only one change was made to the ministry prior to the election, necessitated by the death of <a href="/wiki/James_Dickson_(Queensland_politician)" title="James Dickson (Queensland politician)">James Dickson</a> on 10 January.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000226–227_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000226–227-40">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> Barton took on the <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Foreign_Affairs_(Australia)" title="Minister for Foreign Affairs (Australia)">external affairs portfolio</a> himself and conducted official business through the <a href="/wiki/Department_of_External_Affairs_(1901%E2%80%931916)" title="Department of External Affairs (1901–1916)">Department of External Affairs</a>, as there was no <a href="/wiki/Prime_Minister%27s_Department_(Australia)" title="Prime Minister&#39;s Department (Australia)">prime minister's department</a> until 1911.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000226_41-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000226-41">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The first goal for Barton and his ministry was to retain office at the <a href="/wiki/1901_Australian_federal_election" title="1901 Australian federal election">first federal elections</a>, which were held in late March 1901. His old rival <a href="/wiki/George_Reid" title="George Reid">George Reid</a> became the chief opponent of the government during the campaign and the <i>de facto</i> <a href="/wiki/Leader_of_the_Opposition_(Australia)" title="Leader of the Opposition (Australia)">opposition leader</a>. There were no national political parties in 1901, but most candidates self-declared as either protectionists or free-traders, following the primary divide in colonial politics. Reid, a free trader, made the tariff issue his primary focus, but its significance was diminished by the fact that the new federal government would have to depend on tariffs for its revenue.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000234_42-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000234-42">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> Barton instead delivered "an electoral masterstroke"<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> by making White Australia the centrepiece of the government's campaign, thereby attracting many working-class voters concerned about "coloured labour". Supporters of the government won 32 out of the 75 seats in the <a href="/wiki/Australian_House_of_Representatives" title="Australian House of Representatives">Australian House of Representatives</a>, with their grouping calling itself the <a href="/wiki/Protectionist_Party" title="Protectionist Party">Protectionist Party</a> (or Liberal Protectionists).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000235_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000235-43">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> Barton himself was elected unopposed in the <a href="/wiki/Division_of_Hunter" title="Division of Hunter">Division of Hunter</a>, having previously represented the area in the New South Wales parliament.<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> The government's continuation was secured by the support of the newly formed <a href="/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party" title="Australian Labor Party">Australian Labor Party</a> (ALP), which had 17 MPs. Reid and his <a href="/wiki/Free_Trade_Party" title="Free Trade Party">Free Trade Party</a> formed the <a href="/wiki/Opposition_(Australia)" title="Opposition (Australia)">official opposition</a> with 26 MPs.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000235_43-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000235-43">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> The situation in the <a href="/wiki/Australian_Senate" title="Australian Senate">Senate</a> was similarly complicated, with the government's position even less secure. However, <a href="/wiki/Party_discipline" title="Party discipline">party discipline</a> was virtually non-existent and Barton was widely seen as having won a <a href="/wiki/Mandate_(politics)" title="Mandate (politics)">mandate</a> to govern.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000236_45-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000236-45">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Domestic_policy">Domestic policy</span></h3>
<p>Barton outlined his platform for the first federal election at a speech on 17 January 1901, delivered at the <a href="/wiki/Maitland_Town_Hall" title="Maitland Town Hall">West Maitland Town Hall</a> within his prospective electorate. His plans included the establishment of the federal capital, the High Court and the <a href="/wiki/Inter-State_Commission" title="Inter-State Commission">Inter-State Commission</a>, and a "moderately protectionist tariff" in order to raise the revenue to introduce old-age pensions and a uniform postal system. He also promised to introduce <a href="/wiki/Universal_suffrage" title="Universal suffrage">universal suffrage</a> for federal elections, build the <a href="/wiki/Trans-Australian_Railway" title="Trans-Australian Railway">Trans-Australian Railway</a>, legislate to allow federal intervention in industrial disputes, and ban immigration from Asia and the Pacific Islands.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000230–232_46-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000230–232-46">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In April 1901, Barton announced that the government would sponsor a <a href="/wiki/1901_Federal_Flag_Design_Competition" title="1901 Federal Flag Design Competition">competition</a> to design a new national flag for Australia. The winning design was the basis for the current Australian national flag, although Barton personally favoured the existing <a href="/wiki/Australian_Federation_Flag" title="Australian Federation Flag">Australian Federation Flag</a> that had been popular in New South Wales for many years. As it was primarily intended to be used in shipping, the design had to be submitted to the <a href="/wiki/Admiralty_(United_Kingdom)" title="Admiralty (United Kingdom)">British Admiralty</a> for approval and was not officially adopted until 1903.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>An early piece of legislation of the Barton government was the <a href="/wiki/Immigration_Restriction_Act_1901" title="Immigration Restriction Act 1901"><i>Immigration Restriction Act</i> 1901</a>, which put the <a href="/wiki/White_Australia_policy" title="White Australia policy">White Australia policy</a> into law. The Labour Party required legislation to limit immigration from Asia as part of its agreement to support the government, but Barton had promised the introduction of the White Australia Policy in his election campaign. Barton stated, "The doctrine of the equality of man was never intended to apply to the equality of the Englishman and the Chinaman". One notable reform was the introduction of women's suffrage for federal elections in 1902.<sup id="cite_ref-apmio_48-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-apmio-48">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Barton was a moderate conservative, and advanced liberals in his party disliked his relaxed attitude to political life. A large, handsome, jovial man, he was fond of long dinners and good wine, and was given the nickname "Toby Tosspot" by <i><a href="/wiki/The_Bulletin_(Australian_periodical)" title="The Bulletin (Australian periodical)">The Bulletin</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-apmff_49-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-apmff-49">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="External_affairs">External affairs</span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Edmund_Barton_Vanity_Fair_16_October_1902.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fc/Edmund_Barton_Vanity_Fair_16_October_1902.jpg/170px-Edmund_Barton_Vanity_Fair_16_October_1902.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="286" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fc/Edmund_Barton_Vanity_Fair_16_October_1902.jpg/255px-Edmund_Barton_Vanity_Fair_16_October_1902.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fc/Edmund_Barton_Vanity_Fair_16_October_1902.jpg/340px-Edmund_Barton_Vanity_Fair_16_October_1902.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1901" data-file-height="3200" /></a><figcaption>Caricature in <i><a href="/wiki/Vanity_Fair_(British_magazine)" title="Vanity Fair (British magazine)">Vanity Fair</a></i>, October 1902</figcaption></figure>
<p>For much of 1902, Barton was in England for the <a href="/wiki/Coronation_of_Edward_VII_and_Alexandra" title="Coronation of Edward VII and Alexandra">coronation</a> of King <a href="/wiki/Edward_VII" title="Edward VII">Edward VII</a>, which was postponed from June to August, delaying Barton's return. Accompanying him on this trip was Sir <a href="/wiki/John_Forrest" title="John Forrest">John Forrest</a>, <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Defence_(Australia)" title="Minister for Defence (Australia)">Minister for Defence</a>, and the trip was also used to negotiate the replacement of the naval agreements between the Australian colonies and the United Kingdom (under which Australia funded <a href="/wiki/Royal_Navy" title="Royal Navy">Royal Navy</a> protection from foreign naval threats) by an agreement between the Commonwealth and the United Kingdom at the <a href="/wiki/1902_Colonial_Conference" title="1902 Colonial Conference">1902 Colonial Conference</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Deakin disliked this arrangement and discontinued it, moving for a substantial expansion of Australia's <a href="/wiki/Royal_Australian_Navy" title="Royal Australian Navy">own navy</a> in 1908.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup> On their way home from the United Kingdom, Barton and Forrest visited Canada, including Toronto, Ottawa (where they were guests of the Governor-General), and the Pacific Coast.<sup id="cite_ref-51" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-51">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-52" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-52">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Retirement">Retirement</span></h3>
<p>On 24 September 1903, Barton resigned as prime minister to become one of the founding justices of the High Court of Australia. Speculation about his political future had been rife for several months, particularly after the <i><a href="/wiki/Judiciary_Act_1903" title="Judiciary Act 1903">Judiciary Act 1903</a></i> was passed in August. There were several factors at play in his decision to retire from politics. Some within his own party thought that he lacked the energy and commitment to continue as the leader of the government.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAbjorensen2015_53-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbjorensen2015-53">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> Barton had also suffered from bouts of ill health, including a fainting spell in his office earlier in the year. He was advised by his doctor to quit active politics. Finally, he had a history of financial difficulties and appreciated the security that a lifetime appointment would bring.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000294_54-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000294-54">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup> The appointment of the new judges was made by the governor-general on the advice of cabinet, many of whom had ambitions of their own and encouraged Barton to accept the post.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAbjorensen2015_53-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbjorensen2015-53">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> His appointment was largely uncontroversial, given his role in drafting the constitution, experience as a barrister, and acceptance of a <a href="/wiki/Puisne_judge" title="Puisne judge">puisne judgeship</a> rather than the position of <a href="/wiki/Chief_Justice_of_Australia" title="Chief Justice of Australia">chief justice</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000299_55-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000299-55">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="High_Court_Justice">High Court Justice</span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Justice_Barton_(b%26w).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/86/Justice_Barton_%28b%26w%29.jpg/170px-Justice_Barton_%28b%26w%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="266" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/86/Justice_Barton_%28b%26w%29.jpg/255px-Justice_Barton_%28b%26w%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/86/Justice_Barton_%28b%26w%29.jpg/340px-Justice_Barton_%28b%26w%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1210" data-file-height="1895" /></a><figcaption>Barton in judicial regalia</figcaption></figure>
<p>Barton was sworn in to the High Court on 7 October 1903, along with his close friends <a href="/wiki/Samuel_Griffith" title="Samuel Griffith">Samuel Griffith</a> and <a href="/wiki/Richard_Edward_O%27Connor" title="Richard Edward O&#39;Connor">Richard Edward O'Connor</a>; Griffith became the inaugural <a href="/wiki/Chief_Justice_of_Australia" title="Chief Justice of Australia">Chief Justice of Australia</a>. The new justices had worked together in drafting the constitution and habitually lunched together. As a result of their closeness and the small number of cases heard, there were few dissents in the early years of the court.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000301_56-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000301-56">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Deakin succeeded Barton as prime minister, but the government performed poorly at the <a href="/wiki/1903_Australian_federal_election" title="1903 Australian federal election">December 1903 election</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Hung_parliament" title="Hung parliament">hung parliament</a> allowed <a href="/wiki/Chris_Watson" title="Chris Watson">Chris Watson</a> and the ALP a chance to form government in April 1904, but it was short-lived and <a href="/wiki/George_Reid" title="George Reid">George Reid</a> succeeded Watson as prime minister in August. Later in the year, the court came into conflict with <a href="/wiki/Josiah_Symon" title="Josiah Symon">Josiah Symon</a>, Reid's appointee as attorney-general. Symon objected to the judges hearing cases in each of the state capitals, believing the court should sit only in <a href="/wiki/Melbourne" title="Melbourne">Melbourne</a> and <a href="/wiki/Sydney" title="Sydney">Sydney</a>, and also thought their travelling expenses were excessive. Relations between the government and the court became so strained that the judges contemplated refusing to hear cases, and Barton seriously considered resigning.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000302_57-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000302-57">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> The situation was only resolved in June 1905 when Deakin returned as prime minister.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000303_58-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000303-58">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Court_dynamics">Court dynamics</span></h3>
<p>The Deakin government added two extra judges to the High Court in 1906, appointing former attorneys-general <a href="/wiki/H._B._Higgins" title="H. B. Higgins">H. B. Higgins</a> and <a href="/wiki/Isaac_Isaacs" title="Isaac Isaacs">Isaac Isaacs</a>. Their elevation brought an adversarial aspect to the court for the first time, as the new judges were political radicals and had not been involved in drafting the constitution.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000304–306_59-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000304–306-59">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup> Barton and Griffith formed a conservative bloc, almost always joined by O'Connor.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000306_60-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000306-60">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup> Barton did not dissent from Griffith in the first eight years of the court's existence. In the 44 cases heard by the court from 1904 to 1905 he delivered only 12 separate judgments, choosing to join Griffith's opinion in every other case. Some writers have taken this as indicative of laziness or reluctance to lead the court.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000304–305_61-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000304–305-61">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup> However, others have suggested that his lack of separate judgments was not representative of his work ethic and reflected more a general dislike of <a href="/wiki/Concurring_opinion" title="Concurring opinion">concurring opinions</a> as liable to complicate the interpretation of rulings.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000305_62-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000305-62">&#91;60&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Barton was acting chief justice for nine months in 1913, while Griffith was overseas. The same year saw a major shift in the composition of the court, with two new seats created and a third judge appointed to fill the vacancy caused by O'Connor's death. One of the new judges was Barton's friend <a href="/wiki/Albert_Piddington" title="Albert Piddington">Albert Piddington</a>, whose appointment proved controversial due to his political views. He resigned from the court after a month, despite Barton's attempts to convince him to stay.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000313_63-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000313-63">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup> The three vacancies were eventually filled by <a href="/wiki/Frank_Gavan_Duffy" title="Frank Gavan Duffy">Frank Gavan Duffy</a>, <a href="/wiki/Charles_Powers" title="Charles Powers">Charles Powers</a> and <a href="/wiki/George_Rich" title="George Rich">George Rich</a>, none of whom had been involved in the drafting of the constitution. As a result, Barton and Griffith increasingly found themselves in a minority on constitutional matters, seeking to preserve the intentions of the framers rather than allow the constitution to evolve. The new judges were also less familiar with the North American federal precedents that the original judges had often relied upon.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000314_64-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000314-64">&#91;62&#93;</a></sup> The influx of new voices contributed to Barton's movement away from Griffith, as he showed "a return of energy and initiative [...] greater independence and at times disagreement" than in his earliest years on the court.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000313_63-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000313-63">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Notable_cases">Notable cases</span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Edmund_Barton_-_Sarony_(b%26w).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Edmund_Barton_-_Sarony_%28b%26w%29.jpg/170px-Edmund_Barton_-_Sarony_%28b%26w%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="237" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Edmund_Barton_-_Sarony_%28b%26w%29.jpg/255px-Edmund_Barton_-_Sarony_%28b%26w%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Edmund_Barton_-_Sarony_%28b%26w%29.jpg/340px-Edmund_Barton_-_Sarony_%28b%26w%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="985" data-file-height="1376" /></a><figcaption>Barton later in life (Sarony Studios)</figcaption></figure>
<p>In <i><a href="/wiki/D%27Emden_v_Pedder" title="D&#39;Emden v Pedder">D'Emden v Pedder</a></i> (1904), the High Court formulated a doctrine of <a href="/wiki/Intergovernmental_immunity_(Australia)" title="Intergovernmental immunity (Australia)">implied immunity of instrumentalities</a>, modelled closely on the American concept of <a href="/wiki/Intergovernmental_immunity_(United_States)" title="Intergovernmental immunity (United States)">intergovernmental immunity</a> which Barton and the other authors of the constitution had closely studied. The court followed this precedent in <i><a href="/wiki/Deakin_v_Webb" title="Deakin v Webb">Deakin v Webb</a></i> (1904), which was subsequently overturned by the <a href="/wiki/Judicial_Committee_of_the_Privy_Council" title="Judicial Committee of the Privy Council">Judicial Committee of the Privy Council</a> in <i><a href="/wiki/Webb_v_Outtrim" class="mw-redirect" title="Webb v Outtrim">Webb v Outtrim</a></i> (1906). Barton was angered by the Privy Council's intervention, having always believed the High Court should be the final court of appeal. In personal correspondence he described the 83-year-old presiding judge <a href="/wiki/Hardinge_Giffard,_1st_Earl_of_Halsbury" title="Hardinge Giffard, 1st Earl of Halsbury">Lord Halsbury</a> as an "old pig" who did not understand the needs of a federation.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000306–307_65-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000306–307-65">&#91;63&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Sitting as the <a href="/wiki/Court_of_Disputed_Returns_(Australia)" title="Court of Disputed Returns (Australia)">Court of Disputed Returns</a>, Barton was the sole judge in <i><a href="/wiki/Blundell_v_Vardon" title="Blundell v Vardon">Blundell v Vardon</a></i> (1907), where he ruled that <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Vardon" title="Joseph Vardon">Joseph Vardon</a>'s election to the Senate was void due to electoral irregularities.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000305_62-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000305-62">&#91;60&#93;</a></sup> He joined with Griffith and O'Connor in the majority opinion in <i><a href="/wiki/R_v_Barger" title="R v Barger">R v Barger</a></i> (1908), "the High Court's most decisive intervention to date in thwarting social reforms passed by the Commonwealth parliament".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000308_66-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000308-66">&#91;64&#93;</a></sup> In a series of cases around the same time, the trio also restricted the powers of the <a href="/wiki/Commonwealth_Court_of_Conciliation_and_Arbitration" title="Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration">Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000309_67-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000309-67">&#91;65&#93;</a></sup> Barton and Griffith split notably in <i><a href="/wiki/New_South_Wales_v_Commonwealth_(1915)" title="New South Wales v Commonwealth (1915)">New South Wales v Commonwealth</a></i> (1915), with the court voting 4–2 to strip the <a href="/wiki/Inter-State_Commission" title="Inter-State Commission">Inter-State Commission</a> of any judicial power, much to Barton's dismay. Only Gavan Duffy sided with his argument that parliament should determine the commission's powers rather than the court. The decision was "a rebuff to Barton's authority as an interpreter of the Constitution and his special authority as one of its draftsman".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000318–319_68-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000318–319-68">&#91;66&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During World War I, Barton joined the majority in <i><a href="/wiki/Farey_v_Burvett" title="Farey v Burvett">Farey v Burvett</a></i> (1915), which saw the court adopt a broad view of the federal government's <a href="/wiki/Section_51(vi)_of_the_Constitution_of_Australia" title="Section 51(vi) of the Constitution of Australia">defence power</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000322_69-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000322-69">&#91;67&#93;</a></sup> In <i>Duncan v Queensland</i> (1916), he and Isaacs were the only dissenters from the court's interpretation of <a href="/wiki/Section_92_of_the_Constitution_of_Australia" title="Section 92 of the Constitution of Australia">section 92</a> of the constitution. With Griffith authoring the majority opinion, the court held that state governments could circumvent the constitution's free-trade provisions simply by banning the movement of goods across state boundaries. Barton expressed a "heavy sorrow" at the court's decision and Isaacs was highly critical. In 1920, after Barton's death, Isaacs led the court in reversing <i>Duncan</i>, in what became known as the <i><a href="/wiki/Amalgamated_Society_of_Engineers_v_Adelaide_Steamship_Co_Ltd" title="Amalgamated Society of Engineers v Adelaide Steamship Co Ltd">Engineers' case</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBolton2000322–323_70-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBolton2000322–323-70">&#91;68&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_activities_and_final_years">Other activities and final years</span></h3>
<p>Along with Griffith, Barton was several times consulted by Governors-General of Australia on the exercise of the <a href="/wiki/Reserve_power" title="Reserve power">reserve powers</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-71" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-71">&#91;69&#93;</a></sup> In 1919, although ill, he was extremely disappointed to be passed over for the position of Chief Justice on the retirement of Griffith.<sup id="cite_ref-72" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-72">&#91;70&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_family">Death and family</span></h2>
<p>Barton died on 7 January 1920 from heart failure at the <a href="/wiki/Hydro_Majestic_Hotel" title="Hydro Majestic Hotel">Hydro Majestic Hotel</a>, <a href="/wiki/Medlow_Bath,_New_South_Wales" title="Medlow Bath, New South Wales">Medlow Bath, New South Wales</a>. He was interred in South Head General Cemetery in the Sydney suburb of <a href="/wiki/Vaucluse,_New_South_Wales" title="Vaucluse, New South Wales">Vaucluse</a> (see <a href="/wiki/Waverley_Cemetery" title="Waverley Cemetery">Waverley Cemetery</a>). He was survived by his <a href="/wiki/Jane_Barton" title="Jane Barton">wife</a> and six children:<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<ul><li>Edmund Alfred (29 May 1879&#160;– 13 November 1949), a New South Wales judge</li>
<li>Wilfrid Alexander (1880–1953), first NSW <a href="/wiki/Rhodes_Scholarship" title="Rhodes Scholarship">Rhodes Scholar</a> (1904)</li>
<li>Jean Alice (1882–1957), married Sir <a href="/wiki/David_Maughan" title="David Maughan">David Maughan</a> (1873–1955) in 1909</li>
<li>Arnold Hubert (3 January 1884 – 1948), married Jane Hungerford in Sydney 1907; he later emigrated to Canada</li>
<li>Oswald (8 January 1888&#160;– 6 February 1956), medical doctor</li>
<li>Leila Stephanie (1892–1976), married Robert Christopher Churchill Scot Skirving, son of Sydney doctor <a href="/wiki/Robert_Scot_Skirving" title="Robert Scot Skirving">Robert Scot Skirving</a>, in London 1915<sup id="cite_ref-73" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-73">&#91;71&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<p>Barton's nephews via his older sister Harriet included Victorian federal MP <a href="/wiki/Arthur_Robinson_(Australian_politician)" title="Arthur Robinson (Australian politician)">Arthur Robinson</a> and businessmen <a href="/wiki/Lionel_Robinson" title="Lionel Robinson">Lionel</a> and <a href="/wiki/William_Sydney_Robinson" title="William Sydney Robinson">William Sydney Robinson</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-74" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-74">&#91;72&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Honours">Honours</span></h2>
<p>Barton refused knighthoods in 1887, 1891 and 1899, but agreed to be appointed a Knight Grand Cross of the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_St_Michael_and_St_George" title="Order of St Michael and St George">Order of St Michael and St George</a> (GCMG) in the <a href="/wiki/1902_Coronation_Honours" title="1902 Coronation Honours">1902 Coronation Honours</a> list published on 26 June 1902,<sup id="cite_ref-75" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-75">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-76" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-76">&#91;74&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-nsw_23-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nsw-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> and was invested by King <a href="/wiki/Edward_VII" title="Edward VII">Edward VII</a> at <a href="/wiki/Buckingham_Palace" title="Buckingham Palace">Buckingham Palace</a> on 8 August 1902.<sup id="cite_ref-77" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-77">&#91;75&#93;</a></sup> (He was the only prime minister to be knighted during his term of office until <a href="/wiki/Robert_Menzies" title="Robert Menzies">Robert Menzies</a> in 1963; various others were knighted after leaving the office; Sir <a href="/wiki/Earle_Page" title="Earle Page">Earle Page</a> was already a knight when he briefly became prime minister in 1939.) He received an honorary <a href="/wiki/Doctor_of_Law" title="Doctor of Law">Doctor of Laws</a> (LL.D.) from the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Cambridge" title="University of Cambridge">University of Cambridge</a> in 1900,<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> and honorary <a href="/wiki/Doctor_of_Civil_Law" title="Doctor of Civil Law">Doctor of Civil Law</a> from the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Oxford" title="University of Oxford">University of Oxford</a> and LL.D. from the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh" title="University of Edinburgh">University of Edinburgh</a> while visiting the United Kingdom in the summer of 1902.<sup id="cite_ref-78" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-78">&#91;76&#93;</a></sup> He also received the <a href="/wiki/Freedom_of_the_City" title="Freedom of the City">Freedom of the City</a> of <a href="/wiki/Edinburgh" title="Edinburgh">Edinburgh</a> during a visit to that city on 26 July 1902.<sup id="cite_ref-79" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-79">&#91;77&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1905, the Japanese government conferred the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_the_Rising_Sun" title="Order of the Rising Sun">Grand Cordon, Order of the Rising Sun</a>, and Barton was granted permission to retain and wear the insignia. The honour was presented in acknowledgement of his personal role in resolving a conflict concerning the Commonwealth's <a href="/wiki/Pacific_Island_Labourers_Act_1901" title="Pacific Island Labourers Act 1901">Pacific Island Labourers Act</a> and the Queensland protocol to the <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Japanese_Alliance" title="Anglo-Japanese Alliance">Anglo-Japanese Treaty</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_18-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1951 and again in 1969, Barton was honoured on postage stamps bearing his portrait issued by <a href="/wiki/Australia_Post" title="Australia Post">Australia Post</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-80" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-80">&#91;78&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-81" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-81">&#91;79&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Barton_Highway" title="Barton Highway">Barton Highway</a> near <a href="/wiki/Canberra" title="Canberra">Canberra</a>, Australia was named after Barton. The <a href="/wiki/Division_of_Barton" title="Division of Barton">Division of Barton</a> in New South Wales is named after him, as is <a href="/wiki/Barton,_Australian_Capital_Territory" title="Barton, Australian Capital Territory">Barton, Australian Capital Territory</a>, a suburb of <a href="/wiki/Canberra" title="Canberra">Canberra</a> close to Capital Hill which is the location of many government departments and national institutions, and the headquarters of Australia's main political parties.<sup id="cite_ref-82" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-82">&#91;80&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Edmund_Barton_Building" title="Edmund Barton Building">Edmund Barton Building</a> is a government office building in that suburb. The <a href="/wiki/Barton_College_(Deakin_University)" title="Barton College (Deakin University)">Barton College</a> of <a href="/wiki/Deakin_University" title="Deakin University">Deakin University</a> is also named after Barton.
</p>
