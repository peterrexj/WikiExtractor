<p><b>Sir William McMahon</b> <span class="noexcerpt nowraplinks" style="font-size:85%; font-weight:normal;"><a href="/wiki/Knight_Grand_Cross_of_the_Order_of_St_Michael_and_St_George" class="mw-redirect" title="Knight Grand Cross of the Order of St Michael and St George">GCMG</a>&#32;<a href="/wiki/Companion_of_the_Order_of_the_Companions_of_Honour" class="mw-redirect" title="Companion of the Order of the Companions of Honour">CH</a>&#32;<a href="/wiki/Her_Majesty%27s_Most_Honourable_Privy_Council" class="mw-redirect" title="Her Majesty&#39;s Most Honourable Privy Council">PC</a></span> (23 February 1908&#160;&#8211;&#32;31 March 1988) was an Australian politician who served as the 20th <a href="/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Australia" title="Prime Minister of Australia">Prime Minister of Australia</a>, from 1971 to 1972, holding office as the leader of the <a href="/wiki/Liberal_Party_of_Australia" title="Liberal Party of Australia">Liberal Party of Australia</a>. He was a government minister for over 21 years, the longest continuous service in Australian history.
</p><p>McMahon was born and raised in <a href="/wiki/Sydney" title="Sydney">Sydney</a>, and worked as a commercial lawyer before entering politics. He served in the <a href="/wiki/Australian_Army" title="Australian Army">Australian Army</a> during World War II, reaching the rank of major. After the war's end he returned to university to complete an economics degree. McMahon was elected to the <a href="/wiki/Australian_House_of_Representatives" title="Australian House of Representatives">House of Representatives</a> at the <a href="/wiki/1949_Australian_federal_election" title="1949 Australian federal election">1949 federal election</a>. <a href="/wiki/Robert_Menzies" title="Robert Menzies">Robert Menzies</a> promoted him to the ministry in 1951 and added him to cabinet in 1956. He held several different portfolios in the <a href="/wiki/Menzies_government_(1949%E2%80%9366)" class="mw-redirect" title="Menzies government (1949–66)">Menzies government</a>, most notably as <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Labour_and_National_Service" class="mw-redirect" title="Minister for Labour and National Service">Minister for Labour and National Service</a> from 1958 to 1966. In that capacity, he oversaw the <a href="/wiki/National_Service_Act_1964" title="National Service Act 1964">reintroduction of conscription</a> in 1964.
</p><p>In 1966, Menzies retired and was replaced as prime minister by <a href="/wiki/Harold_Holt" title="Harold Holt">Harold Holt</a>. McMahon then succeeded Holt as deputy leader of the Liberal Party. He was appointed <a href="/wiki/Treasurer_of_Australia" title="Treasurer of Australia">Treasurer</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Holt_government" title="Holt government">Holt government</a>, and over the following three years oversaw a large reduction in the national deficit. After <a href="/wiki/Disappearance_of_Harold_Holt" title="Disappearance of Harold Holt">Holt's death</a> in 1967, McMahon wished to contest the Liberal leadership but had his candidacy vetoed by <a href="/wiki/John_McEwen" title="John McEwen">John McEwen</a>, the leader of the <a href="/wiki/National_Party_of_Australia" title="National Party of Australia">Country Party</a>. The new prime minister was <a href="/wiki/John_Gorton" title="John Gorton">John Gorton</a>. McMahon initially continued on as Treasurer in the <a href="/wiki/Gorton_government" title="Gorton government">Gorton government</a>, but in 1969 was demoted to <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Foreign_Affairs_(Australia)" title="Minister for Foreign Affairs (Australia)">Minister for External Affairs</a> after <a href="/wiki/Liberal_Party_of_Australia_leadership_spill,_1969" class="mw-redirect" title="Liberal Party of Australia leadership spill, 1969">an unsuccessful challenge</a> for the leadership. He eventually forced Gorton's resignation in early 1971, <a href="/wiki/Liberal_Party_of_Australia_leadership_spill,_1971" class="mw-redirect" title="Liberal Party of Australia leadership spill, 1971">winning a vote</a> against <a href="/wiki/Billy_Snedden" title="Billy Snedden">Billy Snedden</a>.
</p><p>McMahon became prime minister at the age of 63, and remains the oldest non-interim prime minister to take office. <a href="/wiki/McMahon_government" title="McMahon government">His government</a> has been described by the <i><a href="/wiki/Australian_Dictionary_of_Biography" title="Australian Dictionary of Biography">Australian Dictionary of Biography</a></i> as "a blend of cautious innovation and fundamental orthodoxy". It continued many of the policies of its immediate predecessors, such as the phased <a href="/wiki/Military_history_of_Australia_during_the_Vietnam_War" title="Military history of Australia during the Vietnam War">withdrawal of Australian troops from Vietnam</a>. In its final year it faced high inflation and unemployment. <a href="/wiki/Gough_Whitlam" title="Gough Whitlam">Gough Whitlam</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party" title="Australian Labor Party">Labor Party</a> defeated McMahon at the <a href="/wiki/1972_Australian_federal_election" title="1972 Australian federal election">1972 federal election</a>, ending 23 consecutive years of Coalition rule. No other Australian prime minister has served for longer without winning a general election. He resigned the Liberal leadership, but remained in parliament until 1982 as a <a href="/wiki/Backbencher" title="Backbencher">backbencher</a>.
</p><p>McMahon has been described as one of Australia's worst prime ministers by Australian political scientists and historians,<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-afr_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-afr-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-age1_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-age1-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-strangio_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-strangio-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and after leaving office several of his former colleagues openly criticised his leadership style and personal character. Whitlam, his successor, acknowledged him as "an extraordinarily skilful, resourceful and tenacious politician", and credited him with having prevented a larger margin of defeat in 1972.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Birth_and_family_background">Birth and family background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Birth and family background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>McMahon was born in <a href="/wiki/Redfern,_New_South_Wales" title="Redfern, New South Wales">Redfern, Sydney, New South Wales</a>, on 23 February 1908. He was the third of five children born to solicitor William Daniel McMahon and Mary (née Walder), daughter of a sailmaker; an older brother predeceased him.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> His father, a Catholic, had a reputation as a heavy drinker and habitual gambler; his mother, an Anglican, was of English and Irish descent.<sup id="cite_ref-p144_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-p144-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> McMahon's paternal grandfather, James "Butty" McMahon, was born in <a href="/wiki/County_Clare" title="County Clare">County Clare</a>, <a href="/wiki/Munster" title="Munster">Munster</a>, Ireland, and married Mary Coyle of <a href="/wiki/County_Fermanagh" title="County Fermanagh">County Fermanagh</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ulster" title="Ulster">Ulster</a>, Ireland. He arrived in Australia as a child, and eventually founded his own <a href="/wiki/Freight_company" title="Freight company">freight company</a>, which became one of the largest in Sydney. Upon his death in 1914 his estate was valued at almost £240,000, an immense sum at the time.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Childhood_and_education">Childhood and education</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Childhood and education">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>McMahon spent his early life in Redfern. His mother died in 1917, when he was nine years old, and he was subsequently raised by her relatives.<sup id="cite_ref-p144_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-p144-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He moved home frequently as he was shifted between family members, living for periods in <a href="/wiki/Kensington,_New_South_Wales" title="Kensington, New South Wales">Kensington</a>, <a href="/wiki/Beecroft,_New_South_Wales" title="Beecroft, New South Wales">Beecroft</a>, <a href="/wiki/Gordon,_New_South_Wales" title="Gordon, New South Wales">Gordon</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Centennial_Park,_New_South_Wales" title="Centennial Park, New South Wales">Centennial Park</a>. McMahon saw little of his father or his siblings, who were raised separately; his older brother James died of <a href="/wiki/Spanish_flu" title="Spanish flu">Spanish flu</a> in 1919. His uncle <a href="/wiki/Samuel_Walder" title="Samuel Walder">Samuel Walder</a> – a businessman who was <a href="/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_Sydney" class="mw-redirect" title="Lord Mayor of Sydney">Lord Mayor of Sydney</a> in 1932 – acted as a sort of surrogate father. McMahon began his education at <a href="/wiki/Abbotsholme_College" title="Abbotsholme College">Abbotsholme College</a>, a short-lived private school in <a href="/wiki/Killara,_New_South_Wales" class="mw-redirect" title="Killara, New South Wales">Killara</a>. One of his schoolmates there was <a href="/wiki/Harold_Holt" title="Harold Holt">Harold Holt</a>, another future prime minister. He was later sent to <a href="/wiki/Sydney_Grammar_School" title="Sydney Grammar School">Sydney Grammar School</a>, where he was an above-average student without excelling academically.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>McMahon's father died when he was 18, leaving him a substantial inheritance.<sup id="cite_ref-p144_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-p144-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He had failed the leaving certificate at Sydney Grammar, but by passing a <a href="/wiki/Matriculation" title="Matriculation">matriculation</a> exam was able to enter the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Sydney" title="University of Sydney">University of Sydney</a> in 1927. At the insistence of his uncle, he chose to study law, graduating with a <a href="/wiki/Bachelor_of_Laws" title="Bachelor of Laws">Bachelor of Laws</a> in 1930.<sup id="cite_ref-p146_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-p146-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> McMahon, who lived at <a href="/wiki/St_Paul%27s_College,_University_of_Sydney" title="St Paul&#39;s College, University of Sydney">St Paul's College</a>, was more interested in the social scene than his degree. He spent his inheritance freely, owning several racehorses, and was known for betting significant amounts on the races. According to <a href="/wiki/Alan_Reid_(journalist)" title="Alan Reid (journalist)">Alan Reid</a>, "his reputation was that he completed his university career on less actual work than anyone in the college".<sup id="cite_ref-reid_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-reid-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Despite his diminutive physique – he stood 5&#160;feet 7&#160;inches (170&#160;cm) as an adult – McMahon did achieve some success as an athlete. He won his university's lightweight boxing title, and in his final year at Sydney Grammar rowed in the <a href="/wiki/Head_of_the_River_(New_South_Wales)" title="Head of the River (New South Wales)">Head of the River</a> race.<sup id="cite_ref-p146_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-p146-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Legal_career_and_military_service">Legal career and military service</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Legal career and military service">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>According to <a href="/wiki/Don_Whitington" title="Don Whitington">Don Whitington</a>, McMahon's life before entering politics was "the aimless, indolent existence of a wealthy young man with a position in a big city's smart set, no positive ambition or even interests, except in enjoying himself, and no family ties to give him a feeling of responsibility or even consideration for others".<sup id="cite_ref-p147_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-p147-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> After graduating from university, he secured a position as a solicitor with <a href="/wiki/Allens_(law_firm)" title="Allens (law firm)">Allen, Allen &amp; Hemsley</a>, a major Sydney law firm; he was made a junior partner in 1939.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He was assigned to the <a href="/wiki/Commonwealth_Bank" title="Commonwealth Bank">Commonwealth Bank</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Bank_of_New_South_Wales" title="Bank of New South Wales">Bank of New South Wales</a> for periods, which helped spark his interest in economics.<sup id="cite_ref-reid_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-reid-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> McMahon had hoped to practise as a <a href="/wiki/Barrister" title="Barrister">barrister</a>, but his partial deafness made this impractical. His hearing remained an issue throughout his life, making parliamentary debates hard to follow, but did improve somewhat through surgery and the use of hearing aids.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In April 1940, McMahon was commissioned as a lieutenant in the <a href="/wiki/Citizens_Military_Force" class="mw-redirect" title="Citizens Military Force">Citizens Military Force</a>. He transferred to the <a href="/wiki/Second_Australian_Imperial_Force" title="Second Australian Imperial Force">Australian Imperial Force</a> (the regular army) in October 1940, and was promoted to captain in 1942 and to major in 1943.<sup id="cite_ref-army_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-army-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> McMahon was turned down for overseas service due to his hearing loss and a knee injury. In the early part of the war he was attached to coastal defence units in Sydney.<sup id="cite_ref-p147_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-p147-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> He later served on the headquarters staff of the <a href="/wiki/II_Corps_(Australia)" title="II Corps (Australia)">II Corps</a> (1942–1943) and the <a href="/wiki/Second_Army_(Australia)" title="Second Army (Australia)">Second Army</a> (1943–1945). He was formally discharged in October 1945.<sup id="cite_ref-army_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-army-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After leaving the military, McMahon travelled overseas for 18 months, visiting Europe and North America. His experience of post-war Europe was said to have been one of the primary influences on his subsequent decision to enter politics.<sup id="cite_ref-p148_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-p148-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> In 1947, McMahon returned to the University of Sydney to study economics and <a href="/wiki/Public_administration" title="Public administration">public administration</a>. He graduated with a <a href="/wiki/Bachelor_of_Economics" title="Bachelor of Economics">Bachelor of Economics</a> degree in 1948, completing the course two years early due to his previous studies. He topped his economics class and won two prizes for proficiency in his final year.<sup id="cite_ref-reid_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-reid-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Politics">Politics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Politics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:William_McMahon_1950.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/55/William_McMahon_1950.jpg/170px-William_McMahon_1950.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="236" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/55/William_McMahon_1950.jpg/255px-William_McMahon_1950.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/55/William_McMahon_1950.jpg/340px-William_McMahon_1950.jpg 2x" data-file-width="579" data-file-height="803" /></a><figcaption>McMahon in 1950, as a newly elected backbencher</figcaption></figure>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Under_Menzies">Under Menzies</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Under Menzies">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>McMahon was elected to the House of Representatives at the <a href="/wiki/1949_Australian_federal_election" title="1949 Australian federal election">1949 federal election</a>, winning the newly created <a href="/wiki/Division_of_Lowe" title="Division of Lowe">Division of Lowe</a> for the Liberal Party. His candidacy was endorsed by <a href="/wiki/Billy_Hughes" title="Billy Hughes">Billy Hughes</a>, who had known his grandfather.<sup id="cite_ref-reid_11-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-reid-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> McMahon soon developed a reputation as "a deadly earnest, dogged, enormously hardworking and dedicated member".<sup id="cite_ref-p148_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-p148-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> In 1950, he successfully proposed an amendment to the <a href="/wiki/Menzies_government_(1949%E2%80%9366)" class="mw-redirect" title="Menzies government (1949–66)">Menzies government</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Australian_Communist_Party_v_Commonwealth#Background" title="Australian Communist Party v Commonwealth">Communist Party Dissolution Bill</a>, reversing the effect of a clause so that the <a href="/wiki/Burden_of_proof_(law)" title="Burden of proof (law)">burden of proof</a> was on the government rather than an accused person. However, the bill was subsequently struck down by the <a href="/wiki/High_Court_of_Australia" title="High Court of Australia">High Court</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-reid_11-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-reid-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> In July 1951, McMahon replaced <a href="/wiki/Philip_McBride" title="Philip McBride">Philip McBride</a> as <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_the_Navy_(Australia)" class="mw-redirect" title="Minister for the Navy (Australia)">Minister for the Navy</a> and <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Air_(Australia)" class="mw-redirect" title="Minister for Air (Australia)">Minister for Air</a>. He subsequently approved and oversaw <a href="/wiki/Donald_Hardman" title="Donald Hardman">Donald Hardman</a>'s proposal to reorganise the <a href="/wiki/Royal_Australian_Air_Force" title="Royal Australian Air Force">Royal Australian Air Force</a> (RAAF) along functional command lines (rather than the previous <a href="/wiki/RAAF_area_commands" title="RAAF area commands">area command</a> system).<sup id="cite_ref-adb_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After the <a href="/wiki/1954_Australian_federal_election" title="1954 Australian federal election">1954 election</a>, McMahon was appointed <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Social_Services_(Australia)" class="mw-redirect" title="Minister for Social Services (Australia)">Minister for Social Services</a> in place of <a href="/wiki/Athol_Townley" title="Athol Townley">Athol Townley</a>. In January 1956, he was instead made <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Agriculture_(Australia)" class="mw-redirect" title="Minister for Agriculture (Australia)">Minister for Primary Industry</a>, an appointment that was seen as a surprise given his lack of experience in agriculture. He effectively became the junior minister to <a href="/wiki/John_McEwen" title="John McEwen">John McEwen</a>, the deputy leader of the <a href="/wiki/National_Party_of_Australia" title="National Party of Australia">Country Party</a> and <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Trade_(Australia)" class="mw-redirect" title="Minister for Trade (Australia)">Minister for Trade</a>. It was hoped by the Country Party (and tacitly accepted by Menzies) that McMahon would simply be a proxy for McEwen on policy matters. However, he managed to preserve the influence and independence of <a href="/wiki/Department_of_Primary_Industry_(1956%E2%80%9374)" class="mw-redirect" title="Department of Primary Industry (1956–74)">his department</a>, and in fact made a number of cabinet submissions that were contrary to McEwen's wishes. This impressed his colleagues in the Liberal Party, but laid the foundations for the poor relations with the Country Party that would prove challenging later in his career.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>McMahon was promoted to <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Labour_and_National_Service" class="mw-redirect" title="Minister for Labour and National Service">Minister for Labour and National Service</a> after the <a href="/wiki/1958_Australian_federal_election" title="1958 Australian federal election">1958 election</a>, in place of <a href="/wiki/Harold_Holt" title="Harold Holt">Harold Holt</a>. This brought him firmly into the inner ranks of the Liberal Party, and in terms of cabinet rank placed him among the party's most senior figures in New South Wales. McMahon oversaw the creation and administration of what became the <i><a href="/wiki/National_Service_Act_1964" title="National Service Act 1964">National Service Act 1964</a></i>, which re-introduced <a href="/wiki/National_Service_Act_1964" title="National Service Act 1964">compulsory conscription</a> for 20-year-old males in anticipation of further Australian involvement in South-East Asia. On the labour side of his portfolio, he frequently came into conflict with the leadership of the <a href="/wiki/Australian_Council_of_Trade_Unions" title="Australian Council of Trade Unions">Australian Council of Trade Unions</a> (ACTU), though there was no major industrial action during his tenure. He attempted to reduce the influence of trade unions known to be controlled by the <a href="/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Australia" title="Communist Party of Australia">Communist Party</a>, particularly the <a href="/wiki/Waterside_Workers%27_Federation_of_Australia" title="Waterside Workers&#39; Federation of Australia">Waterside Workers' Federation</a>. In 1964, McMahon was made <a href="/wiki/Vice-President_of_the_Executive_Council" title="Vice-President of the Executive Council">Vice-President of the Executive Council</a>, further confirming his status within the government.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Under_Holt">Under Holt</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Under Holt">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Holt_government" title="Holt government">Holt government</a></div>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:WilliamMcMahon1963.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8c/WilliamMcMahon1963.jpg/170px-WilliamMcMahon1963.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="215" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8c/WilliamMcMahon1963.jpg/255px-WilliamMcMahon1963.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8c/WilliamMcMahon1963.jpg 2x" data-file-width="336" data-file-height="425" /></a><figcaption>McMahon in 1963</figcaption></figure>
<p>In 1966, McMahon was elected the Liberal Party of Australia's deputy leader under <a href="/wiki/Harold_Holt" title="Harold Holt">Harold Holt</a>. During his tenure, he became the <a href="/wiki/Treasurer_of_Australia" title="Treasurer of Australia">Treasurer of Australia</a> and held executive roles with the <a href="/wiki/International_Monetary_Fund" title="International Monetary Fund">International Monetary Fund</a> and <a href="/wiki/Asian_Development_Bank" title="Asian Development Bank">Asian Development Bank</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-adb_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-adb-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Under_Gorton">Under Gorton</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Under Gorton">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Gorton_government" title="Gorton government">Gorton government</a></div>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Gorton_and_McMahon.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8b/Gorton_and_McMahon.jpg/170px-Gorton_and_McMahon.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="171" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8b/Gorton_and_McMahon.jpg/255px-Gorton_and_McMahon.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8b/Gorton_and_McMahon.jpg/340px-Gorton_and_McMahon.jpg 2x" data-file-width="422" data-file-height="425" /></a><figcaption>McMahon with Prime Minister <a href="/wiki/John_Gorton" title="John Gorton">John Gorton</a> shortly after McMahon's unsuccessful leadership challenge in 1969</figcaption></figure>
<p>When <a href="/wiki/Disappearance_of_Harold_Holt" title="Disappearance of Harold Holt">Holt disappeared</a> in December 1967, McMahon was assumed to be his probable successor. However, <a href="/wiki/John_McEwen" title="John McEwen">John McEwen</a>, interim Prime Minister and leader of the <a href="/wiki/National_Party_of_Australia" title="National Party of Australia">Country Party</a>, announced that he and his party would not serve in a government led by McMahon. McEwen did not state his reasons publicly, but privately he told McMahon he did not trust him. McEwen, an <a href="/wiki/Protectionism" title="Protectionism">arch-protectionist</a>, correctly suspected that McMahon favoured policies of <a href="/wiki/Free_trade" title="Free trade">free trade</a> and <a href="/wiki/Deregulation" title="Deregulation">deregulation</a>.
</p><p>McMahon therefore withdrew, and <a href="/wiki/Australian_Senate" title="Australian Senate">Senator</a> <a href="/wiki/John_Gorton" title="John Gorton">John Gorton</a> won the <a href="/wiki/1968_Liberal_Party_of_Australia_leadership_election" title="1968 Liberal Party of Australia leadership election">subsequent party room ballot</a> for party leader and therefore Prime Minister. McMahon remained Treasurer and waited for his chance at a comeback. The Coalition was nearly defeated at the <a href="/wiki/1969_Australian_federal_election" title="1969 Australian federal election">1969 federal election</a>. After the election, McMahon <a href="/wiki/Liberal_Party_of_Australia_leadership_spill,_1969" class="mw-redirect" title="Liberal Party of Australia leadership spill, 1969">unsuccessfully challenged for the leadership</a>, but was nonetheless re-elected as deputy leader. He was subsequently demoted from Treasurer to <a href="/wiki/Minister_for_Foreign_Affairs_(Australia)" title="Minister for Foreign Affairs (Australia)">Minister for External Affairs</a>. <a href="/wiki/John_McEwen" title="John McEwen">John McEwen</a> had announced in the lead-up to the spill that he would lift his party's veto on McMahon as prime minister.
</p><p>In March 1971, the Defence Minister, <a href="/wiki/Malcolm_Fraser" title="Malcolm Fraser">Malcolm Fraser</a>, resigned from Cabinet and denounced Gorton, who then announced a <a href="/wiki/Leadership_spill" title="Leadership spill">leadership spill</a>. The ensuing party room vote was tied, and under the party rules of the time this meant the motion was lost and Gorton could have theoretically remained as leader and Prime Minister. Nevertheless, Gorton declared that a tie vote meant he no longer had the confidence of the party, and voluntarily resigned the leadership. McMahon <a href="/wiki/1971_Liberal_Party_of_Australia_leadership_election" class="mw-redirect" title="1971 Liberal Party of Australia leadership election">was then elected leader</a> (and thus prime minister), and Gorton was elected deputy leader.
</p>
<h2><span id="Prime_Minister_.281971.E2.80.931972.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Prime_Minister_(1971–1972)">Prime Minister (1971–1972)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Prime Minister (1971–1972)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/McMahon_government" title="McMahon government">McMahon government</a></div>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Rt_Hon_William_McMahon.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b3/Rt_Hon_William_McMahon.jpg/220px-Rt_Hon_William_McMahon.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="209" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b3/Rt_Hon_William_McMahon.jpg/330px-Rt_Hon_William_McMahon.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b3/Rt_Hon_William_McMahon.jpg 2x" data-file-width="424" data-file-height="402" /></a><figcaption>McMahon in 1971</figcaption></figure>
<p>McMahon came into office at a bad time for the Coalition, which was increasingly seen as tired and unfocused after 22 years in power.  His first problem was Gorton. Since Gorton had been elected as Liberal deputy leader, McMahon was all but forced to name him Defence Minister. This farcical situation came to a head when Gorton published two articles detailing the problems he had with ministers leaking information from cabinet. McMahon forced Gorton's resignation.<sup id="cite_ref-1971archives_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1971archives-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Billy_Snedden" title="Billy Snedden">Billy Snedden</a> was chosen as the new deputy Liberal leader.
McMahon found himself dealing with a resurgent <a href="/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party" title="Australian Labor Party">Labor Party</a> under <a href="/wiki/Gough_Whitlam" title="Gough Whitlam">Gough Whitlam</a>. Labor had come within four seats of winning government in 1969, and since then had positioned itself as a credible government-in-waiting. Over the next year-and-a-half, McMahon was unable to get the better of Whitlam. McMahon was no match in parliamentary debates for Whitlam, a witty and powerful orator. He frequently found himself on the defensive as Whitlam attacked the increasingly unpopular Vietnam War and advocated radical new policies such as universal health insurance. In a typical instance, McMahon attacked Whitlam for his demands that Australia recognise the <a href="/wiki/People%27s_Republic_of_China" class="mw-redirect" title="People&#39;s Republic of China">People's Republic of China</a>, only to have to back down when U.S President <a href="/wiki/Richard_Nixon" title="Richard Nixon">Richard Nixon</a> announced his <a href="/wiki/1972_Nixon_visit_to_China" class="mw-redirect" title="1972 Nixon visit to China">visit to China</a>. He was not helped by rising inflation, which hurt his reputation as a sound economic manager. Additionally, the Liberal Party was showing severe schisms, which came at an especially bad time since McMahon had, at most, two years before the next election.<sup id="cite_ref-1971archives_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1971archives-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> His voice and appearance also came across badly on television.
</p><p>In June 1971, McMahon cancelled Gorton's planned nuclear power program, which had included a reactor capable of generating weapons-grade plutonium. He considered it inconsistent with the goals of the <a href="/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Non-Proliferation_of_Nuclear_Weapons" title="Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons">Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty</a>, signed under Gorton in 1970 and ratified under Whitlam in 1973.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Head_of_State_visit_by_Prime_Minister_of_Australia_William_McMahan_-_NARA_-_194388.tif" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/Head_of_State_visit_by_Prime_Minister_of_Australia_William_McMahan_-_NARA_-_194388.tif/lossy-page1-220px-Head_of_State_visit_by_Prime_Minister_of_Australia_William_McMahan_-_NARA_-_194388.tif.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="322" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/Head_of_State_visit_by_Prime_Minister_of_Australia_William_McMahan_-_NARA_-_194388.tif/lossy-page1-330px-Head_of_State_visit_by_Prime_Minister_of_Australia_William_McMahan_-_NARA_-_194388.tif.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/Head_of_State_visit_by_Prime_Minister_of_Australia_William_McMahan_-_NARA_-_194388.tif/lossy-page1-440px-Head_of_State_visit_by_Prime_Minister_of_Australia_William_McMahan_-_NARA_-_194388.tif.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2013" data-file-height="2945" /></a><figcaption>McMahon visiting US President <a href="/wiki/Richard_Nixon" title="Richard Nixon">Richard Nixon</a> at the <a href="/wiki/White_House" title="White House">White House</a> in 1971</figcaption></figure>
<p>McMahon went into 1972 facing a statutory general election. By then, Labor had established a clear lead in the polls and McMahon's approval ratings had dwindled to 28 percent. The press had turned on him so violently that the British psephologist <a href="/wiki/David_Butler_(psephologist)" title="David Butler (psephologist)">David Butler</a> recalled on a visit to Australia that he could not recall a prime minister in any country being "so comprehensively panned" as McMahon. By then, it was widely perceived that McMahon simply "did not look or sound like a Prime Minister". He waited for as long as he could, but finally called <a href="/wiki/1972_Australian_federal_election" title="1972 Australian federal election">a federal election for 2 December</a>. During the campaign, McMahon was abandoned by some of his own ministers, unheard of in a Westminster system.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> The Coalition was swept from power on an eight-seat swing. Late on election night, with the result beyond doubt, McMahon conceded defeat, ending the longest unbroken run in government in Australian history.
</p><p>McMahon had been a minister continuously for 21 years and 6 months, a record in the Australian Government that has never been threatened. Only Sir <a href="/wiki/George_Pearce" title="George Pearce">George Pearce</a> and Sir John McEwen had longer overall ministerial service, but their terms were not continuous.
</p><p>Political journalist <a href="/wiki/Laurie_Oakes" title="Laurie Oakes">Laurie Oakes</a> described McMahon as "devious, nasty, dishonest - he lied all the time and stole things" before describing an incident where McMahon attempted to steal a tape recorder from his radio station by claiming ownership of the device despite it having the radio station's name engraved on it. He concludes by saying that McMahon was a "totally unworthy individual and the fact that he was Prime Minister of this country was a disgrace".<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Later_parliamentary_career_.281972.E2.80.931982.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Later_parliamentary_career_(1972–1982)">Later parliamentary career (1972–1982)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Later parliamentary career (1972–1982)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>McMahon's term as prime minister ended on 5 December 1972. He did not immediately resign as Liberal leader, but it soon became clear that there was no support for him to continue.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>McMahon became the first Prime Minister to have lost an election and had retained his seat who did not then serve as Leader of the Opposition.
</p><p>On 20 December, the Liberal Party elected <a href="/wiki/Billy_Snedden" title="Billy Snedden">Billy Snedden</a> as his successor. As a mark of respect for his past service, McMahon was included in Snedden's new shadow cabinet (as was John Gorton). However, at his own request he was not allocated a specific portfolio.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> In an interview with <a href="/wiki/HSV7" class="mw-redirect" title="HSV7">HSV7</a> in June 1973, McMahon stated that "disloyalty within our own party" was the main reason the Liberals had lost the election. He also said that he had three regrets from his time as prime minister – that he failed to abolish national service, that he had mishandled the 1971 budget, and that he had been a poor communicator.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After the <a href="/wiki/1974_Australian_federal_election" title="1974 Australian federal election">1974 election</a>, McMahon returned to the backbench for the first time since 1951.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> In the lead-up to <a href="/wiki/1975_Australian_constitutional_crisis" title="1975 Australian constitutional crisis">the dismissal</a> of the Whitlam government in 1975, he strongly defended the power of the Senate to block <a href="/wiki/Confidence_and_supply" title="Confidence and supply">supply</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> However, he believed that Governor-General <a href="/wiki/John_Kerr_(governor-general)" title="John Kerr (governor-general)">John Kerr</a> had acted unconstitutionally in dismissing the prime minister, and said that he would have challenged the decision in the <a href="/wiki/High_Court_of_Australia" title="High Court of Australia">High Court</a> if he had been in Whitlam's position.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> McMahon believed that those responsible for the "<a href="/wiki/Loans_affair" title="Loans affair">loans affair</a>" – including Whitlam and several of his ministers – had acted illegally and should be prosecuted for their involvement. He assisted Danny Sankey (a private citizen) in bringing a <a href="/wiki/Private_prosecution" title="Private prosecution">private prosecution</a> against Whitlam, which eventually came before the High Court as <i><a href="/wiki/Sankey_v_Whitlam" title="Sankey v Whitlam">Sankey v Whitlam</a></i>. <a href="/wiki/Malcolm_Fraser" title="Malcolm Fraser">Malcolm Fraser</a> had promised Kerr that his government would bring no action against its predecessor, and was frustrated by McMahon's actions. In his memoirs, he said: "I knew McMahon was running around up to his tricks ... I couldn't control what he did, but I could make damn sure that the government, my government, did not get involved".<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Prior to the <a href="/wiki/1977_Australian_federal_election" title="1977 Australian federal election">1977 election</a>, McMahon was unsuccessfully challenged for Liberal preselection by <a href="/wiki/John_Abel_(politician)" title="John Abel (politician)">John Abel</a>, whose <a href="/wiki/Division_of_Evans" title="Division of Evans">Division of Evans</a> had been abolished in an electoral redistribution.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> After being re-elected, he became the joint <a href="/wiki/Father_of_the_Australian_House_of_Representatives" class="mw-redirect" title="Father of the Australian House of Representatives">Father of the House of Representatives</a> with <a href="/wiki/Clyde_Cameron" title="Clyde Cameron">Clyde Cameron</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> He was the sole Father of the House after the <a href="/wiki/1980_Australian_federal_election" title="1980 Australian federal election">1980 election</a>, winning election for a fourteenth and final time at the age of 72. In his final years in parliament he was often critical of the <a href="/wiki/Fraser_government" title="Fraser government">Fraser government</a>. McMahon left parliament in January 1982, citing dissatisfaction with the 1981 budget as a major factor in his decision to retire before a general election.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> He nominated future prime minister <a href="/wiki/Malcolm_Turnbull" title="Malcolm Turnbull">Malcolm Turnbull</a> as his preferred successor in Lowe,<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> but the Liberal Party chose another candidate Philip Taylor. <a href="/wiki/1982_Lowe_by-election" title="1982 Lowe by-election">The by-election</a> was won by the Labor Party on a 9.4-point swing.<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He was the last former Prime Minister to be reelected to Parliament until Kevin Rudd in 2010.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Final_years_and_death">Final years and death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Final years and death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In retirement, McMahon devoted much of his time to working on his memoirs, which he planned to title <i>A Liberal View</i>. They were rejected by six publishers, and reviewers (who included <a href="/wiki/Barry_Jones_(Australian_politician)" title="Barry Jones (Australian politician)">Barry Jones</a> and <a href="/wiki/Phillip_Adams_(writer)" title="Phillip Adams (writer)">Phillip Adams</a>) considered them to be poorly written and overly detailed.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> In 1984, McMahon endorsed <a href="/wiki/Bob_Hawke" title="Bob Hawke">Bob Hawke</a> and the Labor Party for re-election over the Coalition, which he said would not be ready for government for another four or five years.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Later that year, he described <a href="/wiki/Andrew_Peacock" title="Andrew Peacock">Andrew Peacock</a>'s hold on the Liberal leadership as "very, very fragile", and tacitly endorsed <a href="/wiki/John_Howard" title="John Howard">John Howard</a> as a future leader.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In his final years, McMahon underwent a series of operations related to skin cancer.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> He died in his sleep at St Luke's Private Hospital, <a href="/wiki/Potts_Point,_New_South_Wales" title="Potts Point, New South Wales">Potts Point</a>, on the morning of 31 March 1988. His remains were cremated at the <a href="/wiki/Northern_Suburbs_Crematorium,_Sydney" title="Northern Suburbs Crematorium, Sydney">Northern Suburbs Crematorium</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> A state memorial service was held at <a href="/wiki/St_Andrew%27s_Cathedral,_Sydney" title="St Andrew&#39;s Cathedral, Sydney">St Andrew's Cathedral, Sydney</a>, on 8 April, with the eulogy given by <a href="/wiki/David_Fairbairn_(politician)" title="David Fairbairn (politician)">David Fairbairn</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Personal_life">Personal life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Personal life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1965, aged 57, McMahon married <a href="/wiki/Sonia_McMahon" title="Sonia McMahon">Sonia Rachel Hopkins</a>, who was then aged 32. McMahon had proposed six months after the pair first met. The wedding was held three months later at <a href="/wiki/St_Mark%27s_Church,_Darling_Point" title="St Mark&#39;s Church, Darling Point">St Mark's Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Darling_Point,_New_South_Wales" class="mw-redirect" title="Darling Point, New South Wales">Darling Point</a>, followed by a reception for 400 people at the <a href="/wiki/Royal_Sydney_Golf_Club" title="Royal Sydney Golf Club">Royal Sydney Golf Club</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-theaustralian.com.au_42-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-theaustralian.com.au-42">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> She would survive him by over twenty years, dying aged 77 on 2 April 2010.<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>McMahon had three children; Melinda, <a href="/wiki/Julian_McMahon" title="Julian McMahon">Julian</a> and Deborah. Julian McMahon is an actor and model while Melinda and Deborah lead largely private lives.
</p><p>Throughout his life there were also frequent rumours that he was homosexual.<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> The suggestion was repeatedly denied by Lady McMahon;<sup id="cite_ref-theaustralian.com.au_42-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-theaustralian.com.au-42">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> one occasion in the 1970s resulted in an infamous tabloid headline "My Billy's No Poofter - Sonia Tells".<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Religion">Religion</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Religion">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>McMahon was an <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Church_of_Australia" title="Anglican Church of Australia">Anglican</a>. He did not have a strong religious upbringing – his father was a lapsed Catholic and self-described "rationalist", while his mother's family were Anglican.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> McMahon developed an interest in theology as a teenager, and read widely on the subject over the rest of his life. He cited the works of <a href="/wiki/William_Temple_(bishop)" title="William Temple (bishop)">William Temple</a> as a major influence.<sup id="cite_ref-51" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-51">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup> McMahon was one of the few contemporary politicians to speak publicly on the connection between their religious and political beliefs. In 1953, he gave an address to the <a href="/wiki/Australian_Institute_of_Political_Science" class="mw-redirect" title="Australian Institute of Political Science">Australian Institute of Political Science</a> in which he explained how he believed Christian doctrines necessitated parliamentary democracy and a market economy.<sup id="cite_ref-52" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-52">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Evaluation">Evaluation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: Evaluation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Historical_rankings_of_Prime_Ministers_of_Australia" class="mw-redirect" title="Historical rankings of Prime Ministers of Australia">Historical rankings of Prime Ministers of Australia</a></div>
<p>McMahon is often ranked among Australia's worst prime ministers. In 2001, five out of six historians surveyed by <i><a href="/wiki/Australian_Financial_Review" title="Australian Financial Review">Australian Financial Review</a></i> ranked him among their worst five prime ministers.<sup id="cite_ref-53" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-53">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup> Similarly, <i><a href="/wiki/The_Age" title="The Age">The Age</a></i> surveyed eight historians in 2004 and all but one ranked McMahon as Australia's worst prime minister since World War II.<sup id="cite_ref-ag_54-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ag-54">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> Some of McMahon's most prominent critics have been those who served with him in cabinet. <a href="/wiki/John_Gorton" title="John Gorton">John Gorton</a> called him "utterly untrustworthy",<sup id="cite_ref-h86_55-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-h86-55">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup> while <a href="/wiki/Doug_Anthony" title="Doug Anthony">Doug Anthony</a> said he was "just not big enough for the job".<sup id="cite_ref-wf_56-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-wf-56">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Malcolm_Fraser" title="Malcolm Fraser">Malcolm Fraser</a> said he "had an insatiable ambition [...] he wasn't immoral, he was totally amoral".<sup id="cite_ref-57" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-57">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Billy_Snedden" title="Billy Snedden">Billy Snedden</a> considered McMahon "conspiratorial, devious, untrustworthy",<sup id="cite_ref-h86_55-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-h86-55">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup> and <a href="/wiki/Paul_Hasluck" title="Paul Hasluck">Paul Hasluck</a> viewed him as "disloyal, devious, dishonest, untrustworthy, petty, cowardly", in his diaries referring to him as "that treacherous bastard".<sup id="cite_ref-oakes1_58-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oakes1-58">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>McMahon was nicknamed "Billy the Leak" for his willingness to divulge intimate and confidential information to the media. Despite this, he was disliked by many journalists and political commentators. <a href="/wiki/Donald_Horne" title="Donald Horne">Donald Horne</a> called him "perhaps the silliest prime minister we ever had",<sup id="cite_ref-wf_56-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-wf-56">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> and <a href="/wiki/Peter_Ryan_(columnist)" title="Peter Ryan (columnist)">Peter Ryan</a> said that "McMahon's way of politics was one of lying and leaking, conniving and conspiring, deceit and double-crossing".<sup id="cite_ref-59" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-59">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Malcolm_Mackerras" title="Malcolm Mackerras">Malcolm Mackerras</a> thought that he had "no achievements beyond actually getting the top job".<sup id="cite_ref-h84_60-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-h84-60">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Laurie_Oakes" title="Laurie Oakes">Laurie Oakes</a>, who spent over 50 years in the <a href="/wiki/Canberra_Press_Gallery" title="Canberra Press Gallery">Canberra Press Gallery</a>, viewed McMahon as "a liar and a sneak" and rated him as the worst prime minister he had worked with.<sup id="cite_ref-61" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-61">&#91;60&#93;</a></sup> Oakes recalled that he had continued leaking cabinet discussions even after becoming prime minister, and accused him of once having stolen a tape recorder.<sup id="cite_ref-oakes1_58-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oakes1-58">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Some writers have defended McMahon's reputation, arguing that he was a skilled politician who has been unfairly scapegoated for an almost inevitable election loss. According to John Hawkins, McMahon was "grudgingly admired for his energy and diligence",<sup id="cite_ref-62" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-62">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup> and generally acknowledged as having a mastery of economic policy.<sup id="cite_ref-h84_60-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-h84-60">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Mungo_Wentworth_MacCallum" title="Mungo Wentworth MacCallum">Mungo MacCallum</a>, while noting that he left no lasting achievements, called his prime ministership a "brief but cheerful interlude" and praised him for leaving office with good grace.<sup id="cite_ref-63" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-63">&#91;62&#93;</a></sup> Marian Simms compared McMahon to <a href="/wiki/Richard_Nixon" title="Richard Nixon">Richard Nixon</a>, suggesting that his character traits have been overemphasised,<sup id="cite_ref-ag_54-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ag-54">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> while Troy Bramston viewed him as "a prime minister who clearly understood the challenge of the times and was fighting to get his ship back on course" when he was forced out of office.<sup id="cite_ref-wf_56-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-wf-56">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Andrew_Peacock" title="Andrew Peacock">Andrew Peacock</a>, who served in McMahon's <a href="/wiki/McMahon_Ministry" class="mw-redirect" title="McMahon Ministry">ministry</a>, said that McMahon was "much better than he has been painted... He is somewhat ill-treated by history", and described him as "difficult, irascible, nervous yet capable".<sup id="cite_ref-ap_64-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ap-64">&#91;63&#93;</a></sup> In his memoirs, <a href="/wiki/Gough_Whitlam" title="Gough Whitlam">Gough Whitlam</a> wrote that McMahon was "an extraordinarily skilful, resourceful and tenacious politician ... had he been otherwise, the ALP victory in December 1972 would have been more convincing than it was".<sup id="cite_ref-65" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-65">&#91;64&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Honours">Honours</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_McMahon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: Honours">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:William_McMahon_bust.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/William_McMahon_bust.jpg/170px-William_McMahon_bust.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="226" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/William_McMahon_bust.jpg/255px-William_McMahon_bust.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/William_McMahon_bust.jpg/340px-William_McMahon_bust.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1098" data-file-height="1462" /></a><figcaption>Bust of McMahon by sculptor Victor Greenhalgh located in the <a href="/wiki/Prime_Minister%27s_Avenue" class="mw-redirect" title="Prime Minister&#39;s Avenue">Prime Minister's Avenue</a> in the Ballarat Botanical Gardens</figcaption></figure>
<p>McMahon was appointed a <a href="/wiki/Privy_Council_of_the_United_Kingdom" class="mw-redirect" title="Privy Council of the United Kingdom">Privy Counsellor</a> in 1966, a <a href="/wiki/Companion_of_Honour" class="mw-redirect" title="Companion of Honour">Companion of Honour</a> in the <a href="/wiki/1972_New_Year_Honours" title="1972 New Year Honours">1972 New Year Honours</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-66" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-66">&#91;65&#93;</a></sup> and a <a href="/wiki/Knight_Grand_Cross_of_the_Order_of_St_Michael_and_St_George" class="mw-redirect" title="Knight Grand Cross of the Order of St Michael and St George">Knight Grand Cross of the Order of St Michael and St George</a> in the <a href="/wiki/1977_Silver_Jubilee_and_Birthday_Honours" title="1977 Silver Jubilee and Birthday Honours">Queen's Birthday Honours of 1977</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-67" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-67">&#91;66&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Following the 2009 redistribution of New South Wales federal electorates, the <a href="/wiki/Division_of_Prospect" title="Division of Prospect">Division of Prospect</a> was renamed the <a href="/wiki/Division_of_McMahon" title="Division of McMahon">Division of McMahon</a> starting at the <a href="/wiki/2010_Australian_federal_election" title="2010 Australian federal election">2010 federal election</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-68" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-68">&#91;67&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
