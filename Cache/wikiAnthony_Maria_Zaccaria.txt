<p><b>Anthony Maria Zaccaria</b>, <a href="/wiki/Barnabites" title="Barnabites">CRSP</a> (Italian: <b>Antonio Maria Zaccaria</b>; 1502 – 5 July 1539) was an early leader of the <a href="/wiki/Counter_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Counter Reformation">Counter Reformation</a>, the founder of religious orders (<a href="/wiki/Barnabites" title="Barnabites">Barnabites</a>) and a promoter of the devotion to the <a href="/wiki/Passion_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Passion of Christ">Passion of Christ</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a> and the renewal of the religious life among the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_laity" title="Catholic laity">laity</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, which celebrates his <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> on 5 July.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Anthony Zaccaria was born in the city of <a href="/wiki/Cremona" title="Cremona">Cremona, Italy</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-loyola_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-loyola-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> in December 1502 to Lazzaro and Antonia Pescaroli Zaccaria. He was <a href="/wiki/Baptism_in_the_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Baptism in the Catholic Church">baptized</a> the same day in <a href="/wiki/Cremona_Cathedral" title="Cremona Cathedral">Cremona Cathedral</a>, probably by his uncle Don Tommaso Zaccaria, <a href="/wiki/Canon_regular" title="Canon regular">canon</a> of the cathedral. When he was two, his father died. His family was of the nobility, and in order to teach him compassion for the poor, his mother made him her <a href="/wiki/Almoner" title="Almoner">almoner</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Kelly_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kelly-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>After attending the Episcopal School annexed to the cathedral, he studied philosophy at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Pavia" title="University of Pavia">University of Pavia</a>, and, from 1520, medicine at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Padua" title="University of Padua">University of Padua</a>. After completing studies in 1524, he returned to Cremona and practised as a physician for three years.<sup id="cite_ref-barnabites_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-barnabites-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In 1527, he started studying for the <a href="/wiki/Priest" title="Priest">priesthood</a>, and continued his theological studies in <a href="/wiki/Bologna" title="Bologna">Bologna</a>.
</p><p>On February 20, 1529 Zaccaria was ordained a priest in the Chapel of Saint Joseph in Cremona Cathedral. Having explored his calling, mainly by working in hospitals and institutions for the poor, he became spiritual advisor to Countess <a href="/wiki/Ludovica_Torelli" title="Ludovica Torelli">Ludovica Torelli</a> of <a href="/wiki/Guastalla" title="Guastalla">Guastalla</a> (then the tiny <a href="/wiki/County" title="County">County</a> of Guastalla), and in 1530 followed her to <a href="/wiki/Milan" title="Milan">Milan</a>. There, he became a member of the Oratory of Eternal Wisdom,<sup id="cite_ref-angelic_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-angelic-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> where he met Bartolomeo Ferrari and <a href="/wiki/Giacomo_Antonio_Morigia" title="Giacomo Antonio Morigia">Giacomo Antonio Morigia</a>.
</p><p>Their devotions mainly focused on the teachings of <a href="/wiki/Paul_of_Tarsus" class="mw-redirect" title="Paul of Tarsus">Paul of Tarsus</a> with emphasis on love for the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a> and Christ crucified. They were soon joined by others. They instructed in the rudiments of their faith, gave missions in parishes in the city and elsewhere, and cared for the sick in the hospitals. Zaccaria was occupied with preaching in churches and on street corners. In August 1531 he  (Milan). Zaccaria revived the custom of ringing church bells at 3 p.m. on Fridays, in remembrance of the passion and death of Jesus.<sup id="cite_ref-barnabites_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-barnabites-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> There activities aroused some opposition, but the group persevered. 
</p><p>In 1533, having received encouragement from <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_VII" title="Pope Clement VII">Pope Clement VII</a>, Zaccaria took a small house near the church of St. Catherine at the Ponte dei Fabbri, and here they began their community life. The congregation was named after the companion of Paul.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> In 1534 at St. Catherine's, he popularized the <a href="/wiki/Forty-hour_devotion" class="mw-redirect" title="Forty-hour devotion">Forty-hour devotion</a> for the laity – the solemn exposition of the <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Sacrament" title="Blessed Sacrament">Blessed Sacrament</a> for <a href="/wiki/Eucharistic_adoration" title="Eucharistic adoration">adoration</a> by the faithful – accompanied by preaching. In July 1535 <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_III" title="Pope Paul III">Pope Paul III</a>, with a <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">Bull</a> of approbation, confirms the devotion to Saint Paul for Zaccaria and his group.
</p><p>While on mission to <a href="/wiki/Guastalla" title="Guastalla">Guastalla</a> in 1539, he caught a fever. Combined with the strict penances he performed, his health worsened and he died on 5 July 1539, at the age of 36.<sup id="cite_ref-loyola_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-loyola-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Suffragan_bishop" title="Suffragan bishop">suffragan bishop</a>, Luca di Seriate, who had ordained him a priest, presided over his funeral. In attendance were the aristocrats and people of Cremona and the surrounding towns.<sup id="cite_ref-barnabites_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-barnabites-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He was buried in the San Paolo Convent of the <a href="/wiki/Angelics_of_St_Paul" class="mw-redirect" title="Angelics of St Paul">Angelics of Saint Paul</a>, the female branch of the <a href="/wiki/Barnabites" title="Barnabites">Barnabites</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Milan" title="Milan">Milan</a>. In his last will and testament, Zaccaria stipulated the construction of a chapel in honor of the Conversion of Saint Paul in his parish, Saint Donato.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Foundations">Foundations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Foundations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>While in Milan, he laid the foundations of three religious institutes: one for men (the Clerics Regular of Saint Paul, commonly known as the <a href="/wiki/Barnabites" title="Barnabites">Barnabites</a>); a female branch of uncloistered nuns, the <a href="/wiki/Angelic_Sisters_of_St._Paul" class="mw-redirect" title="Angelic Sisters of St. Paul">Angelic Sisters of St. Paul</a>; and a lay congregation for married people, the Laity of Saint Paul, originally called the Married of Saint Paul (sometimes  called the Oblates of Saint Paul in <a href="/wiki/North_America" title="North America">North America</a>). The three foundations met regularly, and engaged together in various forms of apostolic action. Their aim was the reform of decadent society, beginning with the clergy and religious.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="The_Clerics_Regular_of_St._Paul_.28the_Barnabites.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Clerics_Regular_of_St._Paul_(the_Barnabites)">The Clerics Regular of St. Paul (the Barnabites)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: The Clerics Regular of St. Paul (the Barnabites)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>"The Congregation of the Regular Clerks of St. Paul" was canonically sanctioned by Pope Clement VII in 1533.<sup id="cite_ref-Kelly_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kelly-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Since the order criticized what they saw as abuses in the Church, Zaccaria soon gained a number of enemies, and as the order's founder, he was twice investigated for <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heresy</a>, in 1534 and 1537. He was acquitted both times. In 1536, he stepped down as General of the Order and went to <a href="/wiki/Vicenza" title="Vicenza">Vicenza</a>, where he reformed two convents and founded the Order's second house.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Angelic_Sisters_of_St._Paul">The Angelic Sisters of St. Paul</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: The Angelic Sisters of St. Paul">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>On January 15, 1535 Pope Paul III approved the Angelic Sisters with the Bull, <i>Debitum pastoralis officii</i>. On February 27, 1536 Zaccaria conferred the <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">habit</a> on six <a href="/wiki/Postulant" title="Postulant">postulants</a> of the Angelic Sisters. Zaccaria appointed <a href="/wiki/Paola_Antonia_Negri" title="Paola Antonia Negri">Paola Antonia Negri</a> as <a href="/wiki/Novice_master" title="Novice master">Mistress of Novices</a> on March 4, 1537.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After his death, a number of cures were attributed to the intercession of Anthony Mary Zaccaria. 27 years after his death, his body was found to be <a href="/wiki/Incorruptibility" title="Incorruptibility">incorrupt</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Kelly_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kelly-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His mortal remains are now enshrined at the Church of Saint Barnabas in Milan, Italy.<sup id="cite_ref-angelic_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-angelic-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He was canonized by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIII" title="Pope Leo XIII">Pope Leo XIII</a> on 27 May 1897. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is celebrated on 5 July,<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and he is a patron saint of physicians.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconography">Iconography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Iconography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In art, he is depicted wearing the black <a href="/wiki/Cassock" title="Cassock">cassock</a> of the Order and holding a <a href="/wiki/Lily" class="mw-redirect" title="Lily">lily</a>, <a href="/wiki/Christian_cross" title="Christian cross">cross</a>, <a href="/wiki/Chalice_(cup)" class="mw-redirect" title="Chalice (cup)">chalice</a> and/or a <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">host</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Chronology_of_the_Life_of_St._Anthony_Mary_Zaccaria">Chronology of the Life of  St. Anthony Mary Zaccaria</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Chronology of the Life of St. Anthony Mary Zaccaria">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1097763485"/><table class="box-Prose plainlinks metadata ambox ambox-style ambox-Prose" role="presentation"><tbody><tr><td class="mbox-image"><div class="mbox-image-div"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/f/f2/Edit-clear.svg/40px-Edit-clear.svg.png" decoding="async" width="40" height="40" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/f/f2/Edit-clear.svg/60px-Edit-clear.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/f/f2/Edit-clear.svg/80px-Edit-clear.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="48" data-file-height="48" /></div></td><td class="mbox-text"><div class="mbox-text-span">This section <b>is in <a href="/wiki/MOS:LIST" class="mw-redirect" title="MOS:LIST">list</a> format but may read better as <a href="/wiki/MOS:PROSE" class="mw-redirect" title="MOS:PROSE">prose</a></b>.<span class="hide-when-compact"> You can help by <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit">converting this section</a>, if appropriate. <a href="/wiki/Help:Editing" title="Help:Editing">Editing help</a> is available.</span>  <span class="date-container"><i>(<span class="date">February 2022</span>)</i></span></div></td></tr></tbody></table>
<ul><li><b>December 25, 1535 (Milan)</b>. On <a href="/wiki/Christmas_Day" class="mw-redirect" title="Christmas Day">Christmas Day</a>, Anthony Mary Zaccaria celebrates Mass for the first time at the Oratory of the Monastery of Saint Paul of the Angelic Sisters.</li>
<li><b>January 25, 1536 (Milan)</b>. Zaccaria officially inaugurates the new Monastery of Saint Paul.</li>
<li><b>April 15, 1536 (Milan</b>). Giacomo Antonio Morigia is elected Superior.</li>
<li><b>May 7, 1536 (Milan)</b>. Zaccaria promotes the exposition of the Holy Shroud from the balcony of Castello Sforzesco for the first time in history.</li>
<li><b>November 30, 1536 (Milan)</b>. Zaccaria proposes to Fr. Francis Castellino to establish permanently the School of Christian Doctrine for the youth.</li>
<li><b>April 19, 1537 (Guastalla)</b>. With a handwritten letter undersigned by Torelli, Zaccaria appoints Giuseppe Fellini of Cremona, <i>Podestà</i> (Mayor) of Guastalla.</li>
<li><b>July 2, 1537 (Milan)</b>. On Tuesday, Zaccaria accompanies the first Pauline missionaries (Barnabites, Angelic Sisters, and Laity of St. Paul) and some collaborators (Castellino da Castello and Fra Bono Lizzari) to <a href="/wiki/Vicenza" title="Vicenza">Vicenza</a>, and builds an altar in honor of Saint Paul in the Church of the Converted.</li>
<li><b>August 21, 1537 (Milan)</b>. The Senate President, Giacomo Filippo Sacchi, issues a full acquittal <i>ex capite innocentiae</i> on all the charges of heresy leveled against the Paulines.</li>
<li><b>Year 1537 (Milan)</b>. Zaccaria promotes the solemn <a href="/wiki/Forty_Hours%27_Devotion" title="Forty Hours&#39; Devotion">Forty Hours</a> <a href="/wiki/Eucharistic_adoration" title="Eucharistic adoration">Adoration</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Sacrament" title="Blessed Sacrament">Blessed Sacrament</a> in the Cathedral of Milan, and in shifts at the <i>Quattro Porte</i> ("Four Gates") of the city.</li>
<li><b>August 29, 1537 (Milan)</b>. At the request of the Milanese, Pope Paul III issues the Papal brief <i>Universis Christifidelibus</i>, addressed to the Vicar-General, Cardinal Marino Caracciolo, approving and supporting the Forty Hours devotion.</li>
<li><b>November 13, 1538 (Guastalla)</b>. Zaccaria asks and obtains justice, with the intervention of the <i>Podestà</i> (Mayor), for Giandomenico Mangalassi, a victim of injustice.</li>
<li><b>June 20, 1539 (Guastalla)</b>. Zaccaria writes to the couple Omodei in Milan and speaks of a great "weariness of the body." He feels that his end is imminent and wants to be brought back to Cremona through a boat of dealers who have two mandatory stops (in Cremona and <a href="/wiki/Casalmaggiore" title="Casalmaggiore">Casalmaggiore</a>) of their trade route along the <a href="/wiki/Po_(river)" title="Po (river)">Po River</a>.</li>
<li><b>July 5, 1539 (Cremona</b>). On Saturday, at 3 o’clock in the afternoon, the eve of the Octave Day of Saints Peter and Paul the Apostles, Anthony Mary Zaccaria dies in the house where he was born, in the arms of his mother and surrounded by his first companions.</li></ul>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He's left only a few writings: twelve letters, six <a href="/wiki/Sermon" title="Sermon">sermons</a>, and the constitution of the Barnabites.
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Letters">Letters</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Letters">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>There are eleven letters signed by Anthony Mary. Four are original manuscripts: <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-2">Letter II</a> (addressed to Bartolomeo Ferrari and Giacomo Antonio Morigia, January 4, 1531); Letter IV (to Giovan Giacomo Piccinini, January 16, 1534); <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-6">Letter VI</a> (to Ferrari, October 8, 1538); and <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-7">Letter VII</a> (to Battista Soresina, November 3, 1538). Of the other seven, we have only copies, though they are very early. Three letters are cosigned by Anthony Mary and Angelic Paola Antonia Negri. They are, <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-4">Letter VI</a>, <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-7">Letter VII&#93;</a>, and Letter VIII. In addition, there is a twelfth letter: though it bears only Negri's signature, it was without a doubt penned by Anthony Mary. In fact, the original manuscript of this letter is in Anthony Mary's own handwriting. 
</p><p>One letter is addressed to Fra Battista da Crema (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-1">Letter I</a>); two are addressed to the Angelics (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-5">Letter V</a> and <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-9">Letter IX</a>); three to laymen (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-3">Letter III</a>, Letter IV, and Letter XI); and four to the Barnabites (Letter II, Letter VII, Letter VIII, and <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-10">Letter X</a>). One (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-6">Letter VI</a>) is addressed to Bartolomeo Ferrari, but it is meant for both Barnabites and Angelics who were doing missionary work in Vicenza.
</p><p>The eleven letters cover a nine-year period, 1530 to 1539. However, there are gaps between 1531 and 1534, and between 1534 and 1537. <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-1">Letter IX</a> and <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.barnabites.com/letter-1">Letter XII</a> are undated. The last three letters, a remarkable total of 2,200 words penned in the brief space of ten busy days, were addressed to an Angelic, a Barnabite, and a Married Couple. Written respectively on June 10, 11, and 20, 1539, that is, within less than a month of his death, these letters unwittingly became, as it were, his final testament to the three families of his foundation. Anthony Mary's letters do not belong to any literary genre nor can they be styled “spiritual letters” <i>per se</i>. They were occasional writings dashed off without any concern for style, in plain, totally unadorned language. However, they do contain a wealth of extraordinary spirituality, a fact easily recognized by his earliest biographers.
</p><p>Anthony Mary himself, in his last letter, pointedly remarked: “I have not written one word without some special meaning in it. If you discover it, it will be, I think, most useful and gainful for you.”
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Sermons">Sermons</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Sermons">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The manuscript <a href="/wiki/Codex" title="Codex">codex</a> of the Sermons is kept in the General Archives of the Barnabites in Rome. It was entrusted by Anthony Mary's mother to the Angelics of Santa Marta Convent in Cremona. Early Barnabite historian, Father Giovanni Antonio Gabuzio, retrieved it during his stay in that city from 1584 to 1595. It is an index-notebook. When he was a student at the University of Padua, Anthony Mary recorded in it some lines of the philosopher, <a href="/wiki/Averroes" title="Averroes">Averroës</a>. Later on, as a priest in Cremona, he wrote in it the talks on the <a href="/wiki/Ten_Commandments_in_Catholic_theology" title="Ten Commandments in Catholic theology">Ten Commandments</a>, which he gave at the Amicizia Oratory in Saint Vitalis Church. Clearly, he planned to write out ten sermons, one on each commandment. However, the notebook contains only five sermons: four on the first four commandments. The fifth one is on the commandment, but is only half finished. Sermon I has an appendix on how nuns should practice the first commandment. It was likely intended for the <a href="/wiki/Augustinians" title="Augustinians">Augustinian</a> Community of Santa Maria Annunziata in Cremona.
</p><p>A sixth sermon was part of a projected trilogy on moral and spiritual lukewarmness. The Sermons are addressed to noble laymen, who were married and had children, and were active members of the Amicizia Oratory, in the years 1529-1530 when Anthony Mary was a priest; however, their content is applicable to everyone. The above-mentioned appendix to Sermon I proves it. All the Sermons have the same structure. They are divided in two parts. The first one treats of a specific theme. In Sermon I it is the “due order” of the spiritual life; in Sermon II, “true spiritual life”; in Sermon III, “acknowledgment”; in Sermon IV, love; in Sermon V, passions; in Sermon VI, the “way of God.” The second part of Sermons 1–V is an extensive exposition of each commandment and its practice. In the case of Sermon VI, the second part is a detailed explanation of lukewarmness. 
</p><p>The Sermons exhibit a more elaborate style than that of the Letters. The language, though direct, reveals greater care and elegance. The reasoning is cogently logical and is structured on solid theological preparation. The numerous Biblical quotations reveal a mastery of the Scriptures.
</p><p>Recently, a hypothesis was put forth, saying the Sermons are not liturgical homilies but opening talks given at the Amicizia Oratory meetings, where all present could then speak. It is noteworthy that Anthony Mary reserves the term “sermon” only for his talk on lukewarmness.
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Constitutions">Constitutions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anthony_Zaccaria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Constitutions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Constitutions_of_1579.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/24/The_Constitutions_of_1579.jpg/220px-The_Constitutions_of_1579.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="126" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/24/The_Constitutions_of_1579.jpg/330px-The_Constitutions_of_1579.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/24/The_Constitutions_of_1579.jpg/440px-The_Constitutions_of_1579.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1340" data-file-height="770" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Constitutions_of_1579.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Representation of the Constitutions of 1579</div></div></div><p>No original manuscript of the <i>Constitutions</i> survive, only a very early copy. The <i>Constitutions</i> is no more than an extended outline. It was never approved nor promulgated, hence, it was never binding. In all probability, it is a reworked translation of a previous Latin outline by Fra Battista, the so-called “Primitive Constitutions.” It was a basic text worked on by the first Fathers toward a definitive text.
</p><p>The available text consists of <b>19 chapters</b>, but a close scrutiny points to several layers of composition. There is a conclusion at the end of Chapter 16; another one at the end of <b>Chapter 18</b>; and a third one at the end of Chapter 19. This is evidence that the text went through several writings and underwent multiple reworking.
</p><p>A letter of Father Nicolò D’Aviano, dated October 10, 1570 (even as the definitive <i>Constitutions</i> of 1579 were being redacted), informs us that three chapters of the <i>Constitutions</i> were undoubtedly written by Anthony Mary himself. They are <b>Chapter 12</b>: “Formation of Novices”; Chapter 17: “Signs of Deteriorating 17 Religious Life”; and Chapter 19: “Qualities of a Reformer.” In addition, Anthony Mary's hand can be recognized, more or less, throughout the entire document.
</p><p>The <i>Constitutions</i> is a document of laws, hence its classification in the juridical literary genre. However, in Anthony Mary's additions, the peremptory style turns exhortatory. This change of style helps locate Anthony Mary's interpolations in the original text of Battista da Crema.
</p>
