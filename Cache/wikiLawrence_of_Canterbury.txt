<p><b>Laurence</b><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> (died 2 February 619) was the second <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, serving from about 604 to 619. He was a member of the <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_mission" title="Gregorian mission">Gregorian mission</a> sent from Italy to England to <a href="/wiki/Christianization" title="Christianization">Christianise</a> the <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxons</a> from their native <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_paganism" title="Anglo-Saxon paganism">Anglo-Saxon paganism</a>, although the date of his arrival is disputed. He was <a href="/wiki/Consecration" class="mw-redirect" title="Consecration">consecrated</a> archbishop by his predecessor, <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury" title="Augustine of Canterbury">Augustine of Canterbury</a>, during Augustine's lifetime, to ensure continuity in the office. While archbishop, he attempted unsuccessfully to resolve differences with the native British bishops by corresponding with them about points of dispute. Laurence faced a crisis following the death of King <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelberht_of_Kent" title="Æthelberht of Kent">Æthelberht of Kent</a>, when the king's successor abandoned Christianity; he eventually reconverted. Laurence was <a href="/wiki/Canonization#Historical_development_of_the_process" title="Canonization">revered</a> as a saint after his death in 619.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Laurence_of_Canterbury&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Laurence was part of the Gregorian mission originally dispatched from Rome in 595 to convert the Anglo-Saxons from their native paganism to Christianity; he landed at <a href="/wiki/Thanet_District" title="Thanet District">Thanet</a>, <a href="/wiki/Kent" title="Kent">Kent</a>, with Augustine in 597,<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-ASE106_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE106-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> or, as some sources state, first arrived in 601 and was not a part of the first group of missionaries.<sup id="cite_ref-Hindley36_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hindley36-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Brooks9_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks9-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He had been a monk in Rome before his travels to England,<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> but nothing else is known of his history or background.<sup id="cite_ref-World59_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-World59-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The medieval chronicler <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> says that Augustine sent Laurence back to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I" title="Pope Gregory I">Pope Gregory I</a> to report on the success of converting King Æthelberht of Kent and to carry a letter with questions for the pope. Accompanied by <a href="/wiki/Peter_of_Canterbury" title="Peter of Canterbury">Peter of Canterbury</a>, another missionary, he set off some time after July 598, and had returned by June 601.<sup id="cite_ref-Blair63_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Blair63-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He brought back with him Gregory's replies to Augustine's questions, a document commonly known as the <i><a href="/wiki/Libellus_responsionum" title="Libellus responsionum">Libellus responsionum</a></i>, that Bede incorporated in his <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_ecclesiastica_gentis_Anglorum" class="mw-redirect" title="Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum">Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-ASE_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Laurence is probably the Laurence referred to in the letter from Gregory to <a href="/wiki/Bertha_of_Kent" title="Bertha of Kent">Bertha</a>, queen of Kent. In that letter, Gregory praises Bertha for her part in the conversion of her husband, details of which Gregory says he received from Laurence the priest.<sup id="cite_ref-Blair60_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Blair60-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> It is known that Laurence returned to England with <a href="/wiki/Mellitus" title="Mellitus">Mellitus</a> and others of the second group of missionaries in the summer of 601, but there is no record of Peter being with them.<sup id="cite_ref-Blair66_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Blair66-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Archbishop">Archbishop</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Laurence_of_Canterbury&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Archbishop">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:England_green_top.svg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="Map showing the kingdoms of Dyfed, Powys, and Gwynedd in the west central part of the island of Great Britain. Dumnonia is below those kingdoms. Mercia, Middle Anglia and East Anglia run across the middle of the island from west to east. Below those kingdoms are Wessex, Sussex and Kent, also from west to east. The northern kingdoms are Elmet, Deira, and Bernicia." src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0b/England_green_top.svg/250px-England_green_top.svg.png" decoding="async" width="250" height="314" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0b/England_green_top.svg/375px-England_green_top.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0b/England_green_top.svg/500px-England_green_top.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="963" data-file-height="1210" /></a><figcaption>Map of some of the English kingdoms as of about AD 600</figcaption></figure>
<p>Laurence succeeded Augustine to the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">see of Canterbury</a> in about 604, and ruled until his death on 2&#160;February 619.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Handbook213_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook213-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> To secure the succession, Augustine had consecrated Laurence before he died, even though that was prohibited by <a href="/wiki/Canon_law" title="Canon law">canon law</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ASE_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Augustine was afraid though that if someone did not step into the office immediately, it would damage the missionary efforts in Britain.<sup id="cite_ref-Brooks9_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks9-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Hindley43_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hindley43-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> However, Laurence never received a <a href="/wiki/Pallium" title="Pallium">pallium</a> from Rome, so he may have been considered uncanonical by the papacy.<sup id="cite_ref-ASE112_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE112-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Bede makes a point of comparing Augustine's action in consecrating Laurence to Saint Peter's action of consecrating <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Pope Clement I">Clement</a> as <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Rome">Bishop of Rome</a> during Peter's lifetime, which the theologian <a href="/wiki/J._Robert_Wright" title="J. Robert Wright">J. Robert Wright</a> believes may be Bede's way of criticising the practices of the church in his day.<sup id="cite_ref-Wright47_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Wright47-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 610 Laurence received letters from <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_IV" title="Pope Boniface IV">Pope Boniface IV</a>, addressed to him as archbishop and Augustine's successor.<sup id="cite_ref-Blair80_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Blair80-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The correspondence was in response to Laurence having sent Mellitus to Rome earlier in 610, to solicit advice from the papacy on matters concerning the English Church. While in Rome Mellitus attended a <a href="/wiki/Synod" title="Synod">synod</a>, and brought the synodical decrees back with him to Laurence.<sup id="cite_ref-Blair86_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Blair86-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 613 Laurence consecrated the monastery church built by Augustine in Canterbury, and dedicated it to saints <a href="/wiki/Saint_Peter" title="Saint Peter">Peter</a> and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Paul" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Paul">Paul</a>; it was later re-consecrated as <a href="/wiki/St_Augustine%27s_Abbey" title="St Augustine&#39;s Abbey">St Augustine's Abbey</a>, Canterbury.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Laurence also wrote to the bishops in the lands held by the <a href="/wiki/Scoti" title="Scoti">Scots</a> and by the <a href="/wiki/Britons_(historical)" class="mw-redirect" title="Britons (historical)">Britons</a>, urging them to hold <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Easter</a> on the day that the Roman church celebrated it, instead of their traditional date, as part of the <a href="/wiki/Easter_controversy#Third_phase" title="Easter controversy">Easter controversy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The letter is also preserved in Bede's history.<sup id="cite_ref-Bede105_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bede105-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Laurence in 609 stated that <a href="/wiki/Dagan_(bishop)" class="mw-redirect" title="Dagan (bishop)">Dagan</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">native</a> bishop, would not eat with Laurence or share a roof with the archbishop, due to the differences between the two Churches.<sup id="cite_ref-MonksCiv261_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MonksCiv261-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Pagan_reaction">Pagan reaction</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Laurence_of_Canterbury&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Pagan reaction">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:AugsutineGospelsFolio129vStLuke.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="An illuminated manuscript illustration of a central seated figure holding an open book. He is flanked by two colonnades, which are filled with small scenes. Over the central figure is an arch with surmounts a winged bull." src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/82/AugsutineGospelsFolio129vStLuke.jpg/220px-AugsutineGospelsFolio129vStLuke.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="285" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/82/AugsutineGospelsFolio129vStLuke.jpg/330px-AugsutineGospelsFolio129vStLuke.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/82/AugsutineGospelsFolio129vStLuke.jpg/440px-AugsutineGospelsFolio129vStLuke.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1449" data-file-height="1878" /></a><figcaption>The <a href="/wiki/Evangelist_portrait" title="Evangelist portrait">evangelist portrait</a> of Luke, from the <a href="/wiki/St._Augustine_Gospels" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Augustine Gospels">St. Augustine Gospels</a> (c. 6th century), which were probably used by Laurence.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Æthelberht died in 616, during Laurence's tenure; his son <a href="/wiki/Eadbald_of_Kent" title="Eadbald of Kent">Eadbald</a> abandoned Christianity in favour of Anglo-Saxon paganism, forcing many of the Gregorian missionaries to flee the pagan backlash that followed Æthelberht's death.<sup id="cite_ref-Hindley43_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hindley43-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Among them in <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a> were Mellitus, who was <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_London" title="Bishop of London">Bishop of London</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Justus" title="Justus">Justus</a>, who was <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Rochester" title="Bishop of Rochester">Bishop of Rochester</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby30_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby30-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Remaining in Britain, Laurence succeeded in reconverting Eadbald to Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-Hindley43_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hindley43-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Bede relates the story that Laurence had been prepared to give up when he was visited by St Peter in a dream or vision. St Peter chastised Laurence and whipped him, and the marks of the whipping remained after the vision or dream ended. Laurence then displayed them to Eadbald, and the king was converted on the spot.<sup id="cite_ref-Brooks64_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks64-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Bede, however, hints that it was the death of some of the leaders of the pagan party in battle that really persuaded Laurence to stay.<sup id="cite_ref-Blair86_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Blair86-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/wiki/Benedicta_Ward" title="Benedicta Ward">Benedicta Ward</a>, a historian of Christianity, Bede uses the story of the whipping as an example of how suffering was a reminder of Christ's suffering for humans, and how that example could lead to conversion.<sup id="cite_ref-Ward120_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ward120-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Wright argues that another point Bede is making is that it is because of the intercession of <a href="/wiki/St_Peter" class="mw-redirect" title="St Peter">St Peter</a> himself that the mission continued.<sup id="cite_ref-Wright49_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Wright49-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> David Farmer, in the <i><a href="/wiki/Oxford_Dictionary_of_Saints" title="Oxford Dictionary of Saints">Oxford Dictionary of Saints</a></i>, suggests that the whipping story may have been a blending of the <i><a href="/wiki/Quo_vadis" class="mw-redirect" title="Quo vadis">Quo vadis</a></i> story with some information given by <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> in a letter.<sup id="cite_ref-Farmer313_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Farmer313-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Modern historians have seen political overtones in the pagan reaction. The historian D. P. Kirby sees Eadbald's actions as a repudiation of his father's pro-<a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frankish</a> policies.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby30_20-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby30-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Alcuin" title="Alcuin">Alcuin</a>, a later medieval writer, wrote that Laurence was "censured by apostolic authority".<sup id="cite_ref-QKirby31_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-QKirby31-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> This may have been a letter from Pope <a href="/wiki/Pope_Adeodatus_I" title="Pope Adeodatus I">Adeodatus&#160;I</a>, commanding Laurence to stay in Kent. Kirby goes on to argue that it was Justus, not Laurence, who converted Eadbald, and this while Justus was archbishop, sometime around 624.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby30_20-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby30-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Not all historians agree with this argument, however. <a href="/wiki/Nicholas_Brooks_(historian)" title="Nicholas Brooks (historian)">Nicholas Brooks</a> states that the king was converted during Laurence's archiepiscopate, within a year of him succeeding his father.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The historian <a href="/wiki/Barbara_Yorke" title="Barbara Yorke">Barbara Yorke</a> argues that there were two co-rulers of Kent after Æthelberht's death, Eadbald and a Æthelwald, and that Eadbald was converted by Laurence while Æthelwald was converted by Justus after his return to Rochester.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke32_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke32-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> Another factor in the pagan reaction was Laurence's objection to Eadbald's marriage to his father's widow, something that Christians considered to be unlawful.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke123_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke123-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>All efforts to extend the church beyond Kent encountered difficulties due to the attitude of King <a href="/wiki/R%C3%A6dwald_of_East_Anglia" title="Rædwald of East Anglia">Rædwald of East Anglia</a>, who had become the leading king in the south after Æthelberht's death.<sup id="cite_ref-ASE127_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE127-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Rædwald was converted before the death of Æthelberht, perhaps at the urging of Æthelberht, but his kingdom was not, and Rædwald seems to have converted only to the extent of placing a Christian altar in his pagan temple.<sup id="cite_ref-ASE112a_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE112a-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> It proved impossible for Mellitus to return to London as bishop, although Justus did resume his duties at Rochester.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby30_20-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby30-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_legacy">Death and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Laurence_of_Canterbury&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Death and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Laurence died on 2&#160;February 619, and was buried in the abbey of St Peter and Paul in Canterbury, later renamed St Augustine's; his <a href="/wiki/Relics" class="mw-redirect" title="Relics">relics</a>, or remains, were moved, or <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relics)" class="mw-redirect" title="Translation (relics)">translated</a>, to the new church of St Augustine's in 1091.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_4-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His <a href="/wiki/Shrine" title="Shrine">shrine</a> was in the axial chapel of the abbey church, flanking the shrine of Augustine, his predecessor.<sup id="cite_ref-Shrines67_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Shrines67-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Laurence came to be regarded as a saint, and was given the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a> of 3&#160;February.<sup id="cite_ref-Walsh357_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Walsh357-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> The ninth century <a href="/wiki/Stowe_Missal" title="Stowe Missal">Stowe Missal</a> commemorates his feast day, along with Mellitus and Justus.<sup id="cite_ref-ODS366_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODS366-32">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> A <i><a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">Vita</a></i> (or <i>Life</i>) was written about the time of his translation, by <a href="/wiki/Goscelin" title="Goscelin">Goscelin</a>, but it is mainly based on information in Bede.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_4-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His tomb was opened in 1915. Besides his feast day, the date of his translation, 13&#160;September, was also celebrated after his death.<sup id="cite_ref-Farmer313_24-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Farmer313-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Laurence's tenure as archbishop is mainly remembered for his failure to secure a settlement with the Celtic church, and for his reconversion of Eadbald following Æthelbert's death.<sup id="cite_ref-ASE112_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE112-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> He was succeeded as archbishop by Mellitus, the Bishop of London.<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook213_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook213-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
