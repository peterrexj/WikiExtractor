<p><b>Mary Magdalene de' Pazzi</b>, OCarm (<a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Maria Maddalena de' Pazzi</i>; April 2, 1566 – May 25, 1607), was an Italian <a href="/wiki/Carmelite" class="mw-redirect" title="Carmelite">Carmelite</a> <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nun</a> and <a href="/wiki/Christian_mysticism" title="Christian mysticism">mystic</a>. She has been declared a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene_de%27_Pazzi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>De' Pazzi was born at <a href="/wiki/Florence" title="Florence">Florence</a>, Italy, on April 2, 1566,<sup id="cite_ref-capes_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-capes-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> to Camillo di Geri de' <a href="/wiki/Pazzi" title="Pazzi">Pazzi</a>, a member of one of the wealthiest and most distinguished noble families of <a href="/wiki/Renaissance" title="Renaissance">Renaissance</a> Florence, and Maria Buondelmonti. She was christened Caterina, but in the family was called Lucrezia, out of respect for her paternal grandmother, Lucrezia Mannucci.<sup id="cite_ref-smet_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-smet-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At the age of nine de' Pazzi was taught how to <a href="/wiki/Meditation" title="Meditation">meditate</a> by the family <a href="/wiki/Chaplain" title="Chaplain">chaplain</a>, using a then-recently published work explaining how one should meditate on the <a href="/wiki/Passion_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Passion of Christ">Passion of Christ</a>. Years later, this book was one of the items she brought with her to the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-smet_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-smet-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Around the age of nine is also when de' Pazzi began practicing <a href="/wiki/Mortification_of_the_flesh" title="Mortification of the flesh">mortification of the flesh</a> through <a href="/wiki/Self-flagellation" title="Self-flagellation">self-flagellation</a>, wearing a barbed metal <a href="/wiki/Cilice" title="Cilice">cilice</a>, and wearing a home-made <a href="/wiki/Crown_of_thorns" title="Crown of thorns">crown of thorns</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Dingwall120_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dingwall120-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> She received her <a href="/wiki/First_Communion" title="First Communion">First Communion</a> at the then-early age of 10 and made a vow of virginity the same year.<sup id="cite_ref-Dingwall120_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dingwall120-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> She experienced her first <a href="/wiki/Religious_ecstasy" title="Religious ecstasy">ecstasy</a> when she was only twelve, in her mother's presence. From then on, she continued to exhibit what she considered to be many varied mystical experiences.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2019)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>In 1580, at age fourteen, de' Pazzi was sent by her father to be educated at a monastery of nuns of the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Malta" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of Malta">Order of Malta</a>, but she was soon recalled to wed a young nobleman.<sup id="cite_ref-capes_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-capes-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Caterina advised her father of her vow, and he eventually relented and allowed her to enter <a href="/wiki/Monastic_life" class="mw-redirect" title="Monastic life">monastic life</a>. She chose the Carmelite monastery of <a href="/wiki/St._Mary_of_the_Angels_Monastery_(Florence)" title="St. Mary of the Angels Monastery (Florence)">Santa Maria degli Angeli</a> in Florence because the rule there allowed her to receive <a href="/wiki/Holy_Communion" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Communion">Holy Communion</a> daily. On January 30, 1583, she was accepted as a <a href="/wiki/Novice" title="Novice">novice</a> by that community,<sup id="cite_ref-smet_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-smet-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and took the <a href="/wiki/Religious_name" title="Religious name">religious name</a> of Sister Mary Magdalene.<sup id="cite_ref-capes_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-capes-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Dingwall122_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dingwall122-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mystic">Mystic</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene_de%27_Pazzi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Mystic">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>De' Pazzi had been a novice for a year when she became critically ill. Upon receiving the religious habit, one of the sisters asked her how she could bear so much pain without a murmur. Mary pointed to the <a href="/wiki/Crucifix" title="Crucifix">crucifix</a> and said:<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>Those who call to mind the sufferings of Christ, and who offer up their own to God through His passion, find their pains sweet and pleasant.</p></blockquote>
<p>Death seemed near, so her superiors let her make her profession of <a href="/wiki/Religious_vows" title="Religious vows">religious vows</a> in a private ceremony, while lying on a cot in the chapel. Immediately after, she fell into an ecstasy that lasted about two hours.<sup id="cite_ref-fabrini_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fabrini-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> This was repeated on the following 40 mornings, each time after Communion.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As a safeguard against deception and to preserve the revelations, the de' Pazzi <a href="/wiki/Confessor" title="Confessor">confessor</a> asked her to dictate her experiences to her fellow nuns. Over the next six years, five large volumes were filled. The first three record ecstasies from May 1584 through <a href="/wiki/Pentecost" title="Pentecost">Pentecost</a> week of the following year. That particular week was a preparation for a severe five-year trial that she relates. The fourth book records that trial, and the fifth is a collection of letters concerning reform and renewal. Another book, <i>Admonitions</i>, is a collection of her sayings arising from her experiences in the formation of women in religious orders.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It was believed that de' Pazzi could read the thoughts of others and predict future events. For instance, during one ecstatic event she predicted the future elevation to the papacy of Cardinal Alessandro de' Medici (as Pope Leo XI).<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> During her lifetime, she allegedly appeared to several persons in distant places and cured a number of sick people.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>De' Pazzi died on May 25, 1607, at the age of 41. She was buried in the <a href="/wiki/Choir_(architecture)" title="Choir (architecture)">choir</a> of the monastery chapel.<sup id="cite_ref-authors_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-authors-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> At her canonization in 1668 her body was declared miraculously incorrupt.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Her relic corpse is located in the Monastery of Maria Maddalena de' Pazzi in Careggi.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene_de%27_Pazzi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Medal_commemorating_the_canonization_of_Pedro_de_Alc%C3%A1ntara_and_Maria_Maddalena_de%27_Pazzi_Women_of_the_Book.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Medal_commemorating_the_canonization_of_Pedro_de_Alc%C3%A1ntara_and_Maria_Maddalena_de%27_Pazzi_Women_of_the_Book.jpg/220px-Medal_commemorating_the_canonization_of_Pedro_de_Alc%C3%A1ntara_and_Maria_Maddalena_de%27_Pazzi_Women_of_the_Book.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="156" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Medal_commemorating_the_canonization_of_Pedro_de_Alc%C3%A1ntara_and_Maria_Maddalena_de%27_Pazzi_Women_of_the_Book.jpg/330px-Medal_commemorating_the_canonization_of_Pedro_de_Alc%C3%A1ntara_and_Maria_Maddalena_de%27_Pazzi_Women_of_the_Book.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Medal_commemorating_the_canonization_of_Pedro_de_Alc%C3%A1ntara_and_Maria_Maddalena_de%27_Pazzi_Women_of_the_Book.jpg/440px-Medal_commemorating_the_canonization_of_Pedro_de_Alc%C3%A1ntara_and_Maria_Maddalena_de%27_Pazzi_Women_of_the_Book.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1200" data-file-height="850" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Medal_commemorating_the_canonization_of_Pedro_de_Alc%C3%A1ntara_and_Maria_Maddalena_de%27_Pazzi_Women_of_the_Book.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Medal commemorating the simultaneous canonization of Maria Maddalena de' Pazzi and her fellow mystic Pedro de Alcántara, 1669 (from the <a href="/wiki/Women_of_the_Book_Collection" title="Women of the Book Collection">Women of the Book Collection</a>, nr. 7595989, Sheridan Libraries, Johns Hopkins University.</div></div></div>
<p>Two years after de' Pazzi died, the Jesuit Vincenzo Puccini, her confessor, published the life of this Carmelite nun as an edifying example. The 1639 edition was augmented with material relating to the mystic's canonization, and was purposefully dedicated to two nieces of Pope Urban VIII.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Numerous miracles allegedly followed de' Pazzi's death, and the process for her <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatification</a> was begun in the year 1610 under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_V" title="Pope Paul V">Pope Paul V</a>, and completed under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_VIII" title="Pope Urban VIII">Pope Urban VIII</a> in the year 1626. She was not, however, <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> until 62 years after her death, when <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_IX" title="Pope Clement IX">Pope Clement IX</a> raised her to the altars on April 28, 1669. The church of the <a href="/wiki/Pa%C5%BEaislis_Monastery" title="Pažaislis Monastery">Monastery of Pažaislis</a>, commissioned in 1662 in Lithuania, was one of the first to be consecrated in her honor.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2018)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> Puccini's successful biography first was translated into French in honor of her canonization.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Nowadays, de' Pazzi herself is little known outside Italy, but her cult is very strong, especially in Florence. <a href="/wiki/Paulist_Press" class="mw-redirect" title="Paulist Press">Paulist Press</a> issued a selection of her writings in English translation in their series of <i>Classics of Western Spirituality</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-authors_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-authors-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Her importance in the Mission to the East especially in connection with India is recently explored.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The quote <i>Never utter in your neighbors' absence what you would not say in their presence.</i> is attributed to her on the web.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Feast_day">Feast day</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene_de%27_Pazzi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Feast day">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1670, the year after de' Pazzi's canonization, the feast day of the saint was inserted in the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> for celebration on May 25, the day of her death. In 1728, the date of May 25 was assigned instead to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_VII" title="Pope Gregory VII">Pope Gregory VII</a>, and her <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> was moved to May 29, where it remained until 1969, when it was restored to its original place in the calendar, as the true anniversary of her death.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mortification">Mortification</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mary_Magdalene_de%27_Pazzi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Mortification">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Pazzi was known to have worn little clothing and to have whipped herself with a <a href="/wiki/Crown_of_thorns" title="Crown of thorns">crown of thorns</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> According to researcher <a href="/wiki/Ian_Wilson_(author)" title="Ian Wilson (author)">Ian Wilson</a>, sometimes she would wear only a single garment but she would tear this off "in order to roll herself on thorns, or give herself another savage beating". Wilson described Pazzi as a "florid, sadomasochistic neurotic".<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Asti Hustvedt has written that "Pazzi wore a crown of thorns and a corset onto which she had attached piercing nails. She also walked barefoot through the snow, dripped hot wax onto her body, and licked the wounds of the diseased, including those afflicted with leprosy."<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The anthropologist <a href="/wiki/Eric_Dingwall" title="Eric Dingwall">Eric Dingwall</a> wrote a chapter on Pazzi's alleged <a href="/wiki/Sadomasochism" title="Sadomasochism">masochism</a> and flagellant behaviors in <i>Very Peculiar People</i> (1962).<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Psychiatrist <a href="/wiki/Armando_Favazza" title="Armando Favazza">Armando Favazza</a> in <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bodies_under_Siege&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Bodies under Siege (page does not exist)">Bodies under Siege</a></i> (3rd edition, 2011) wrote:
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"/><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>At about age 37, emaciated and racked with coughing and pain, she took to her bed until she died four years later. Her painful gums were so badly infected that her teeth fell out, one by one. Her body was covered with putrefying bed sores, but when the sisters offered to move her she warned them off for fear that by touching her body they might experience sexual desires. ... A large statue of her holding a flagellant whip can be seen in her church in Florence, where people around the world still come to pay her tribute.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Psychiatrist Kathryn J. Zerbe has written that Pazzi was a sufferer of <i><a href="/wiki/Anorexia_mirabilis" title="Anorexia mirabilis">anorexia mirabilis</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> She also displayed behavioral symptoms of <a href="/wiki/Bulimia_nervosa" title="Bulimia nervosa">bulimia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
