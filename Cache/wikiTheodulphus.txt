					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Writer, poet and the Bishop of Orléans</div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_02.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d2/Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_02.jpg/220px-Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_02.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="177" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d2/Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_02.jpg/330px-Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_02.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d2/Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_02.jpg/440px-Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_02.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3055" data-file-height="2457" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_02.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>The oratory at <a href="/wiki/Germigny-des-Pr%C3%A9s" title="Germigny-des-Prés">Germigny-des-Prés</a>, after a restoration in the 19th century.</div></div></div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_01.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_01.jpg/220px-Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_01.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="157" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_01.jpg/330px-Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_01.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_01.jpg/440px-Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_01.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2000" data-file-height="1431" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Germigny_Des_Pres_2007_01.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Mosaic of the <a href="/wiki/Ark_of_the_Covenant" title="Ark of the Covenant">Ark of the Covenant</a>, <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 806</div></div></div>
<p><b>Theodulf of Orléans</b> (<a href="/wiki/Saragossa" class="mw-redirect" title="Saragossa">Saragossa</a>, <a href="/wiki/Spain" title="Spain">Spain</a>, <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 750(/60) – 18 December 821<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Disputed_statement" class="mw-redirect" title="Wikipedia:Disputed statement"><span title="This claim has reliable sources with contradicting facts (December 2018)">disputed</span></a>&#32;<span class="metadata"> &#8211; <a href="/wiki/Talk:Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans#Death_date" title="Talk:Theodulf of Orléans">discuss</a></span></i>&#93;</sup>) was a writer, poet and the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Orl%C3%A9ans" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Orléans">Bishop of Orléans</a> (c. 798 to 818) during the reign of <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a> and <a href="/wiki/Louis_the_Pious" title="Louis the Pious">Louis the Pious</a>. He was a key member of the <a href="/wiki/Carolingian_Renaissance" title="Carolingian Renaissance">Carolingian Renaissance</a> and an important figure during the many reforms of the church under Charlemagne, as well as almost certainly the author of the <i><a href="/wiki/Libri_Carolini" title="Libri Carolini">Libri Carolini</a></i>, "much the fullest statement of the Western attitude to representational art that has been left to us by the Middle Ages".<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He is mainly remembered for this and the survival of the private oratory or chapel made for his villa at <a href="/wiki/Germigny-des-Pr%C3%A9s" title="Germigny-des-Prés">Germigny-des-Prés</a>, with a <a href="/wiki/Mosaic" title="Mosaic">mosaic</a> probably from about 806.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> In Bible manuscripts produced under his influence, the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Baruch" title="Book of Baruch">Book of Baruch</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Letter_of_Jeremiah" title="Letter of Jeremiah">Letter of Jeremiah</a> (as Chapter 6 of the Book of Baruch) became part of the Western (<a href="/wiki/Vulgate" title="Vulgate">Vulgate</a>) Bible canon.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Theodulf was born in <a href="/wiki/Spain" title="Spain">Spain</a>, probably <a href="/wiki/Saragossa" class="mw-redirect" title="Saragossa">Saragossa</a>, between 750 and 760, and was of <a href="/wiki/Visigothic" class="mw-redirect" title="Visigothic">Visigothic</a> descent.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGaripzanov2008298_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGaripzanov2008298-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He fled Spain because of the Moorish occupation of the region and traveled to the South-Western province of Gaul called <a href="/wiki/Aquitaine" title="Aquitaine">Aquitaine</a>, where he received an education.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He went on to join the monastery near <a href="/wiki/Maguelonne" class="mw-redirect" title="Maguelonne">Maguelonne</a> in Southern Gaul led by the abbot <a href="/wiki/Benedict_of_Aniane" title="Benedict of Aniane">Benedict of Aniane</a>. During his trip to Rome in 786, Theodulf was inspired by the centres of learning there, and sent letters to a large number of abbots and bishops of the Frankish empire, encouraging them to establish public schools.<sup id="cite_ref-Baunard_1860,_p.54_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Baunard_1860,_p.54-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Charlemagne recognized Theodulf's importance within his court and simultaneously named him Bishop of Orléans (c. 798) and abbot of many monasteries, most notably the <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> abbey of <a href="/wiki/Fleury-sur-Loire" title="Fleury-sur-Loire">Fleury-sur-Loire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He then went on to establish public schools outside the monastic areas which he oversaw, following through on this idea that had impressed him so much during his trip to Rome. Theodulf quickly became one of Charlemagne's favoured theologians alongside <a href="/wiki/Alcuin" title="Alcuin">Alcuin</a> of Northumbria and was deeply involved in many facets of Charlemagne’s desire to reform the church, for example by editing numerous translated texts that Charlemagne believed to be inaccurate and translating sacred texts directly from the <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Greek language">classical Greek</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hebrew" class="mw-redirect" title="Hebrew">Hebrew</a> languages.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> He was a witness to the <a href="/wiki/The_Testament_of_Charlemagne" class="mw-redirect" title="The Testament of Charlemagne">emperor's will</a> in 811.
</p><p>Charlemagne died in 814 and was succeeded by his son Louis the Pious.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Louis' nephew, King <a href="/wiki/Bernard_of_Italy" title="Bernard of Italy">Bernard of Italy</a>, sought independence from the Frankish empire and raised his army against the latter. Bernard was talked into surrendering, but was punished by Louis severely, sentencing him to have his sight removed. The procedure of blinding Bernard went wrong and he died as a result of the operation.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Louis believed that numerous people in his court were conspiring against him with Bernard, and Theodulf was one of many who were accused of treason. He was forced to abandon his position of Bishop of Orléans in 817 and was exiled to a monastery in <a href="/wiki/Angers" title="Angers">Angers</a> in 818 where he spent the next two years of his life.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> After he was released in 820, he tried to reclaim his bishopric in Orléans but was never able to reach the city because it is believed that he died during the trip or some time after that. Theodulf of Orléans died on January 18, 821,<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Disputed_statement" class="mw-redirect" title="Wikipedia:Disputed statement"><span title="This claim has reliable sources with contradicting facts (December 2018)">disputed</span></a>&#32;<span class="metadata"> &#8211; <a href="/wiki/Talk:Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans#Death_date" title="Talk:Theodulf of Orléans">discuss</a></span></i>&#93;</sup> and his body was brought back to <a href="/wiki/Angers" title="Angers">Angers</a> where it was buried.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to some sources Theodulf may have been married at the beginning of his career and had a daughter named Gisla.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Villa_at_Germigny-des-Pr.C3.A9s"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Villa_at_Germigny-des-Prés">Villa at Germigny-des-Prés</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Villa at Germigny-des-Prés">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The oratory at Germigny-des-Prés (Loiret, Orléanais), an example of <a href="/wiki/Carolingian_architecture" title="Carolingian architecture">Carolingian architecture</a>, was built by Bishop Theodulf in 806 as part of his Gallo-Roman villa in Germaniacus. Theodulf was also abbot of the neighboring monastery of <a href="/wiki/Saint-Beno%C3%AEt-sur-Loire" title="Saint-Benoît-sur-Loire">Saint-Benoît-sur-Loire</a>. His complex at Germigny-des-Prés was in a general sense modelled on Charlemagne's <a href="/wiki/Palace_of_Aachen" title="Palace of Aachen">Palace of Aachen</a>, the center of the <a href="/wiki/Carolingian_Empire" title="Carolingian Empire">Carolingian Empire</a> at that time.
</p><p>All except the oratory was destroyed by the <a href="/wiki/Normans" title="Normans">Normans</a> (then just emerging from the <a href="/wiki/Viking" class="mw-redirect" title="Viking">Vikings</a>) within a century of construction. The villa had <a href="/wiki/Fresco" title="Fresco">frescos</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Seven_liberal_arts" class="mw-redirect" title="Seven liberal arts">Seven liberal arts</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Season" title="Season">Four Seasons</a>, and the <a href="/wiki/Mappa_Mundi" class="mw-redirect" title="Mappa Mundi">Mappa Mundi</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> and the <a href="/wiki/Mosaic" title="Mosaic">mosaic</a> in the oratory is virtually the only <a href="/wiki/Carolingian_art" title="Carolingian art">Carolingian mosaic</a> to survive, though over-restored in the 1860s, when the oratory was given what is generally agreed to have been a disastrous over-restoration and reconstruction. The subject of the mosaic, the <a href="/wiki/Ark_of_the_Covenant" title="Ark of the Covenant">Ark of the Covenant</a> with attendant angels, is otherwise only found in early Jewish bibles (usually as the sole illustration), and probably relates to the <i>Libri Carolini</i>, where the Ark, with its golden <a href="/wiki/Cherubim" class="mw-redirect" title="Cherubim">cherubim</a>, is mentioned as a significant proof of divine approval of religious images.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Capitularies">Capitularies</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Capitularies">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>As Bishop of Orléans (798–818), Theodulf wrote two important <a href="/wiki/Capitulary" title="Capitulary">capitularies</a>.
</p><p><i><b>Capitula ad presbyteros parochiae</b></i>
</p><p>The first capitulary was a reminder to the priests of his diocese of the importance of manual labour, studying, prayer and chastity.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><i><b>Capitula altera Theodulpho episcopo Aurelianensi adscripta</b></i>
</p><p>The second capitulary focused on his code of penance where he lists the consequences of murder, adultery, fornication, incest, thievery, <a href="/wiki/Usury" title="Usury">usury</a> and other infractions.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
It is divided in ten main parts:
</p>
<ul><li><i>De ammonitione sacerdotum</i></li>
<li><i>De adulteriis et incestis et fornicationibus</i></li>
<li><i>De confessionibus laicorum</i></li>
<li><i>De homicidio</i></li>
<li><i>De mulierum delictis</i></li>
<li><i>De furto et falso testimonio</i></li>
<li><i>De inrationabili fornicatione</i></li>
<li><i>De adultero presbytero publice et occulte</i></li>
<li><i>De usurariis</i></li>
<li><i>De inquisition octo vitiorium ad confessionem</i></li></ul>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Hymns_and_poems">Hymns and poems</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Hymns and poems">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Theodulf composed hymns and poems as well, of which 80 are preserved. <i><a href="/wiki/All_Glory,_Laud_and_Honour" title="All Glory, Laud and Honour">Gloria, laus et honor</a></i> is the most famous one of them.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Libri_Carolini">Libri Carolini</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Libri Carolini">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Theodulf was also almost certainly responsible for composing the <i><a href="/wiki/Libri_Carolini" title="Libri Carolini">Libri Carolini</a></i> (c. 793) which served as a rebuttal to a faulty translation of the acts of the <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Nicaea" title="Second Council of Nicaea">Second Council of Nicaea</a> of 787 which was mistakenly interpreted as saying that the worship ("adoration") of images was acceptable in the church.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> According to it, the Council suggested that an end was to be put to the <a href="/wiki/Iconoclasm" title="Iconoclasm">iconoclastic period</a> which had led to the destruction of many sacred images in the church, especially in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>, which was only partially true. This translation made its way from Rome to the court of Charlemagne where it infuriated the Frankish emperor and his loyal theologians, including Theodulf, who was ordered to write the <i>Libri Carolini</i> in Charlemagne's name in a way that portrayed him as the sole representative of the Western world and defender of the church against a supposed <a href="/wiki/Idolatry" title="Idolatry">idolatry</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to the <i>Libri Carolini</i>, images may be used as ecclesiastical ornaments, for purposes of instruction, and in memory of past events. It would be foolish, however, to burn incense before them and to use lights, though it would be quite wrong to cast them out of the churches and destroy them. Strong opposition is voiced in it to "adoration" of images, wrongly believing that the Council in Nicaea used this word, taken to mean the absolute adoration reserved for God alone, while only an appropriate <a href="/wiki/Veneration" title="Veneration">veneration</a> is to be given to the <a href="/wiki/Saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Saints">saints</a>, and reverential honour to the Cross of Christ, Scripture, sacred vessels, and <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> of the saints. The Greek word προσκύνησις that the Council in fact used means no more than reverence in a prostrate attitude.<sup id="cite_ref-CE_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CE-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Key_values">Key values</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Key values">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Hospitality">Hospitality</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Hospitality">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Theodulf brought fresh ideas and an open mind to the period known as the Carolingian Renaissance. He believed in always keeping the door open and never refusing pilgrims, travelers or the poor if they needed a meal or a place to stay for the night. He believed that you had to offer the less fortunate a seat at your dinner table if you one day wished to have a seat at the banquet of God. These ideas were highly influenced by his readings of Augustine.<sup id="cite_ref-Baunard_1860,_p.196_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Baunard_1860,_p.196-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> He often referred to himself as the poor traveler or stranger, being born in Spain and of Visigothic descent, and being accepted with open arms by the royal court of Charlemagne.<sup id="cite_ref-Baunard_1860,_p.54_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Baunard_1860,_p.54-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Literature_and_the_liberal_arts">Literature and the liberal arts</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodulf_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Literature and the liberal arts">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Theodulf was an avid reader of Christian literature, and some of his favorite writers are listed in one of his letters to the <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_III" title="Pope Leo III">Pope Leo III</a> which include mentions of texts by <a href="/wiki/Gregory_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Gregory the Great">Gregory the Great</a>, <a href="/wiki/St._Augustine" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Augustine">St. Augustine</a>, <a href="/wiki/St._Jerome" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Jerome">St. Jerome</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Isidore_of_Seville" title="Isidore of Seville">St. Isidore</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> He also mentions in his letters that he enjoyed reading pagan literature including poems by Virgil and Ovid which he thought may seem filled with heresy at first, but underneath the surface had useful morals which could be applied to Christian morality.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> He was also very fond of the Seven Liberal Arts and they were depicted in fresco in his dining room so that his spirit and body could be fed simultaneously.<sup id="cite_ref-Baunard_1860,_p.196_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Baunard_1860,_p.196-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
