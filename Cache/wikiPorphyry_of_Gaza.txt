<p><b>Saint Porphyrius</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Porphyrius</i>; <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Πορφύριος</span></span>, <i>Porphyrios</i>; <a href="/wiki/Church_Slavonic" title="Church Slavonic">Slavonic</a>: Порфирий, <i>Porfiriy</i>; <span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;347</span>–420) was <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a> of <a href="/wiki/Gaza_City" title="Gaza City">Gaza</a> from 395 to 420, known, from the account in his <i>Life</i>, for <a href="/wiki/Christianization" title="Christianization">Christianizing</a> the recalcitrant <a href="/wiki/Pagan" class="mw-redirect" title="Pagan">pagan</a> city of Gaza, and <a href="/wiki/Iconoclasm" title="Iconoclasm">demolishing its temples</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Porphyrius of Gaza is known only from a vivid biography by <a href="/wiki/Mark_the_Deacon" title="Mark the Deacon">Mark the Deacon</a> and from a reference made by <a href="/wiki/John_II,_Bishop_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="John II, Bishop of Jerusalem">John II, Bishop of Jerusalem</a>. The <i>Vita Porphyrii</i> appears to be a contemporary account of Porphyrius that chronicles in some detail the end of paganism in Gaza in the early fifth century. However, the text has been viewed by some in the 20th century as <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> rather than history, and some elements of it are examples of the stereotyped fictional events characteristic of this literary form.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  On the other hand, the author was certainly intimately familiar with Gaza in late Antiquity,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and his statements are of interest for reflecting 5th century attitudes. 
</p><p>The saint's body is said to be buried in <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Porphyrius" title="Church of Saint Porphyrius">Saint Porphyrius' Church</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Gaza_City" title="Gaza City">Gaza City</a>, in the <a href="/wiki/State_of_Palestine" title="State of Palestine">State of Palestine</a>.
</p><p>A street in <a href="/wiki/%C5%BBejtun" title="Żejtun">Zejtun</a>, <a href="/wiki/Malta" title="Malta">Malta</a>, bears the saint's name.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="The_account_in_Vita_Porphyrii">The account in <i>Vita Porphyrii</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Porphyrius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: The account in Vita Porphyrii">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gaza had a history as a place hostile to the early <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christians</a>.  Several had suffered <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyr" title="Christian martyr">martyrdom</a> there in the <a href="/wiki/Diocletianic_Persecution" title="Diocletianic Persecution">persecution of Diocletian</a> (303-313), and the brief pagan revival under <a href="/wiki/Julian_the_Apostate" class="mw-redirect" title="Julian the Apostate">Julian</a> (362-363) had seen the burning of the Christian basilica and various Christians put to death.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The people of Gaza were so hostile to Christians that the Christian church had to be built outside the walls, at a safe distance, and the Christian bishops of the 4th century were specifically termed "bishops of the churches about Gaza". The Christian community in Gaza then scarcely numbered 280, according to the <i><a href="/wiki/Biography" title="Biography">vita</a></i> of St. Porphyrius, and the community-at-large resisted the closing of temples and destruction of pagan images which had started in more Christianized regions.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to the <i>vita</i>, St. Porphyrius was appointed bishop at the age of 45. He arrived in the city without incident, but a drought followed the same year, and the pagans "imputed [related] the thing to the coming of the blessed man, saying that 'It was revealed unto us by <a href="/wiki/Marnas" class="mw-redirect" title="Marnas">Marnas</a> that the feet of Porphyrius bring bad luck to the city'." (Vita 19–20)  Further harassment followed (Vita 21, 25) with the support of local officials.
</p><p>In response, St. Porphyrius sent Marcus, his deacon and chronicler, to <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> in 398 to obtain an order to close the pagan temples of Gaza. An official named Hilarius duly arrived with soldiers to close the temples, but the <a href="/wiki/Marneion" class="mw-redirect" title="Marneion">Marneion</a> remained open because Hilarius was bribed with a large sum of money (<i>Vita</i> 27). There was no great change, however, in the attitude of the people, who refused to allow Christians "to hold any civil office, but entreated them as naughty slaves" (<i>Vita</i> 32).
</p><p>St. Porphyrius then went to Constantinople during the winter of 401–402, accompanied by the bishop of <a href="/wiki/Caesarea_Palaestina" class="mw-redirect" title="Caesarea Palaestina">Caesarea Palaestina</a>, and together they convinced the Empress <a href="/wiki/Aelia_Eudoxia" title="Aelia Eudoxia">Eudoxia</a>, who was the dominant force at the court of <a href="/wiki/Arcadius" title="Arcadius">Arcadius</a>, to prevail upon the Emperor and obtain from him a decree for the destruction of the pagan temples at Gaza. <a href="/wiki/Cynegius" title="Cynegius">Cynegius</a>, a special imperial envoy, executed the decree in May, 402. Eight temples, those of <a href="/wiki/Aphrodite" title="Aphrodite">Aphrodite</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hecate" title="Hecate">Hecate</a>, the Sun, <a href="/wiki/Apollo" title="Apollo">Apollo</a>, <a href="/wiki/Persephone" title="Persephone">Kore (Persephone)</a>, <a href="/wiki/Tyche" title="Tyche">Tyche</a> (<i>Tychaion</i>), the shrine of a hero (<i>Heroeion</i>), and even the Marneion, were either pulled down or burnt. "And there were also other very many idols in the houses and in the villages," Marcus relates, but the upper class who had such things had fled from the city in advance. Simultaneously soldiers, who were billeted in the vacated houses visited every house, seizing and <a href="/wiki/Book_burning" title="Book burning">burning the idols and private libraries</a> as "books of magic".
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Marneion" class="mw-redirect" title="Marneion">Marneion</a>, a temple sacred to <a href="/wiki/Zeus" title="Zeus">Zeus Marnas</a>, who was the local <a href="/wiki/Hellenistic" class="mw-redirect" title="Hellenistic">Hellenistic</a> incarnation of <a href="/wiki/Dagon" title="Dagon">Dagon</a>, the patron of agriculture, a god who had been worshipped in the Levant since the <a href="/wiki/Third_millennium_BCE" class="mw-redirect" title="Third millennium BCE">third millennium BCE</a>, was set afire with pitch, sulfur and fat; it continued to burn for many days; stones of the Marneion were triumphantly reused for paving the streets. This temple had been rebuilt under the direction of <a href="/wiki/Hadrian" title="Hadrian">Hadrian</a> (ruled 117–138), who visited Gaza; it was first represented on the Gaza coins of Hadrian himself. To one of Hadrian's visits, also, we may conjecturally assign the foundation of the great temple of the god Marnas, which the <i>Vita</i> describes with a mixture of pride and abhorrence. it was believed that the 'Olympian' Emperor who founded the great temple of Zeus on the sacred mountain <a href="/wiki/Gerizim" class="mw-redirect" title="Gerizim">Gerizim</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Samaritans" title="Samaritans">Samaritans</a> would not be slow to recognize the claims of the <i>Cretan Zeus</i> of the Gazaeans. After the suppression of a revolt of the <a href="/wiki/Jewish_people" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish people">Jews</a> in 119 AD, Hadrian allegedly selected Gaza as the place at which to sell his Jewish captives.
</p><p>Directly upon the ruins of the Marneion, at the expense of the empress, a large church called the <i>Eudoxiana</i> was erected in her honor and dedicated on 14 April 407. Thus with approved violence, paganism officially ceased to exist in Gaza.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Modern_assessment_of_the_Vita_Porphyrii">Modern assessment of the <i>Vita Porphyrii</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Porphyrius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Modern assessment of the Vita Porphyrii">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The text exists in a Greek and a Georgian recension.
</p><p>Grégoire and Kugener (1930),<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> the editors of the <i>Vita Porphyrii</i>, reviewed the challenges to the integrity of the work and summarized the previous scholarship.  These included the lack of other attestation to major figures, including Porphyrius himself, in an otherwise well-documented period of history. But they concluded that the text had a historical basis and "that the solution of most problems is to be found in the fact that the text of the <i>Vita</i> transmitted to us represents a revision of the sixth century, which borrowed from the church history of <a href="/wiki/Theodoret" title="Theodoret">Theodoret of Cyrrhus</a> of 444, e.g. for the Proemium, and deleted in particular each mention of <a href="/wiki/John_II,_Bishop_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="John II, Bishop of Jerusalem">John II, Bishop of Jerusalem</a>, replacing it with the name of <a href="/wiki/Praylius" class="mw-redirect" title="Praylius">Praylius</a>, his successor as bishop of Jerusalem in the time of Porphyrius".<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Paul Peeters (1941)<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> published the <a href="/wiki/Georgian_(language)" class="mw-redirect" title="Georgian (language)">Georgian</a> texts and showed that they depended on a lost Syriac original that must have been written in the later fifth or the sixth century.
</p><p>Head wrote, "The textual problems can be resolved if we assume that the <i>Life of St Porphyrius</i> was composed in two successive stages: the original notes by a contemporary and eyewitness (whom we may call 'Mark') were later, perhaps in the 450's, given their final shape and put into circulation by another author who does not appear in the text." (Head 2001:55).  He adds that "the text abounds with such convincing historical detail and shows such an intimate knowledge of the region of Gaza in late antiquity, that at the very least the general storyline merits our confidence." (2001:56)  But he acknowledges that Porphyrius is otherwise undocumented in the historical record, and that the text contains the "usual stereotypes" of <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> documented by <a href="/wiki/Hippolyte_Delehaye" title="Hippolyte Delehaye">Hippolyte Delehaye</a>.
</p><p>Other scholars are more dismissive.  "Richly detailed glimpses of imperial circles and great names in Constantinople are all fake; specific important people—an archbishop, a governor, and others—are all fake; and Mark and Porphyrius themselves may never have existed at all," is MacMullen's conclusion (1984:87). "The <i>vita</i> "comes to be routinely cited as real history by all sorts of fine scholars" writes <a href="/wiki/Ramsay_MacMullen" title="Ramsay MacMullen">Ramsay MacMullen</a> in <i>Christianizing the Roman Empire</i>, 1984, p 86. "There is a strong temptation to use it because it is so full, specific and vivid." He concludes that "it should be possible, then, to learn about the general way things happened in well-known and recurring situations around the turn of the fourth century, even as they appear in a manifestly deceptive text" (MacMullen 1984:87).
</p>
