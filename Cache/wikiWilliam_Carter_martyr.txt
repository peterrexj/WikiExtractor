<p><b>William Carter</b> (c. 1548 – 11 January 1584) was a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> English <a href="/wiki/Printer_(publisher)" class="mw-redirect" title="Printer (publisher)">printer</a> and <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_Carter_(martyr)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>William Carter was born in London in 1548, the son of John Carter, a draper, and Agnes, his wife. He was apprenticed to John Cawood, queen's printer, on <a href="/wiki/Candlemas_Day" class="mw-redirect" title="Candlemas Day">Candlemas Day</a>, 1563, for ten years, and afterwards acted as secretary to <a href="/wiki/Nicholas_Harpsfield" title="Nicholas Harpsfield">Nicholas Harpsfield</a>, last Catholic <a href="/wiki/Archdeacon" title="Archdeacon">archdeacon</a> of <a href="/wiki/Canterbury" title="Canterbury">Canterbury</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Wainewright_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Wainewright-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> while Harpsfield was a prisoner in <a href="/wiki/Fleet_Prison" title="Fleet Prison">Fleet Prison</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Burton_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burton-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On the latter's death he married and set up a press on Tower Hill. In September 1578 he was confined for about a month in the <a href="/wiki/Poultry_Compter" title="Poultry Compter">Poultry Compter</a>, a small prison run by a <a href="/wiki/Sheriff" title="Sheriff">Sheriff</a> in the <a href="/wiki/City_of_London" title="City of London">City of London</a>, apparently for failure to attend divine service as established by act of Parliament.<sup id="cite_ref-Burton_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burton-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> In December 1579 he was committed to the <a href="/wiki/Gatehouse_Prison" title="Gatehouse Prison">Gatehouse</a> "for not conforming himself in matters of religion".<sup id="cite_ref-Burton_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burton-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> As the prisons were at that time unusually overcrowded he was released on bond in June 1581.
</p><p>By that time it had become necessary to provide false information in <a href="/wiki/Colophon_(publishing)" title="Colophon (publishing)">colophons</a> for safety's sake. "Carter called himself Johannem Bogardi, and claimed his edition of a <a href="/wiki/Peter_Canisius" title="Peter Canisius">Peter Canisius</a> book was actually printed in "Duaci" (Douay)."<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Among Catholic books he printed a new edition (1000 copies) of <a href="/wiki/Gregory_Martin_(scholar)" title="Gregory Martin (scholar)">Gregory Martin</a>'s "A Treatise of Schisme", in 1580, for which he was arrested and imprisoned in the <a href="/wiki/Tower_of_London" title="Tower of London">Tower of London</a>, 1582, and paid for his own meals there down to midsummer, 1583.<sup id="cite_ref-Wainewright_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Wainewright-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> His wife died while he was in prison.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Having been tortured on the rack, he was indicted at the <a href="/wiki/Old_Bailey" title="Old Bailey">Old Bailey</a> — the central criminal court in England – on 10 January 1584, for having printed Dr. Martin's book, in which was a paragraph where confidence was expressed that the Catholic Hope would triumph, and pious <a href="/wiki/Judith" class="mw-redirect" title="Judith">Judith</a> would slay <a href="/wiki/Holofernes" title="Holofernes">Holofernes</a>. This was interpreted as an incitement to assassinate the Queen. At this time, with increasing tensions between Queen <a href="/wiki/Elizabeth_I_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Elizabeth I of England">Elizabeth I of England</a> and King <a href="/wiki/Philip_II_of_Spain" title="Philip II of Spain">Philip II of Spain</a>, which would culminate with the sailing of the <a href="/wiki/Spanish_Armada" title="Spanish Armada">Spanish Armada</a> four years later, manifestations of Catholic faith in England were often interpreted as a treasonable taking the side of the Spanish enemy and punished accordingly. He was executed for <a href="/wiki/Treason" title="Treason">treason</a> at <a href="/wiki/Tyburn" title="Tyburn">Tyburn</a> the next day.
</p>
