<p>Saint <b>Joseph the Hymnographer</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Όσιος Ιωσήφ ο Υμνογράφος</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek" title="Romanization of Greek">romanized</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Greek-language romanization"><i lang="el-Latn">Ósios Iosif o Ymnográfos</i></span>) was a Greek monk of the ninth century. He is one of the greatest liturgical poets and <a href="/wiki/Hymnography" class="mw-redirect" title="Hymnography">hymnographers</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>. He is also known for his confession of the Orthodox Faith in opposition to <a href="/wiki/Iconoclasm" title="Iconoclasm">Iconoclasm</a>.
</p><p>As a poet he is often confused with <a href="/wiki/Joseph_the_Confessor" title="Joseph the Confessor">Joseph</a>, the Archbishop of Thessalonica and brother of <a href="/wiki/Theodore_the_Studite" title="Theodore the Studite">Theodore the Studite</a>, who were one generation older than he was, so that in many cases, attribution of specific hymns to him is uncertain.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Joseph_the_Hymnographer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was born around 816 AD in <a href="/wiki/Sicily" title="Sicily">Sicily</a> of devout parents, Plotinus and Agatha.<sup id="cite_ref-birthyear_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-birthyear-1">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> Joseph's family had to flee from Sicily due to the <a href="/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Sicily" title="Muslim conquest of Sicily">Arab invasion of the island</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-place_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-place-2">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> According to the hagiographer Theophanes they went to Peloponnese. At the age of fifteen he was <a href="/wiki/Tonsure" title="Tonsure">tonsured</a> a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> at the <a href="/wiki/Latomos_Monastery" class="mw-redirect" title="Latomos Monastery">Latomos Monastery</a> of <a href="/wiki/Thessalonica" class="mw-redirect" title="Thessalonica">Thessalonica</a>. About 840 the bishop of Thessalonica <a href="/wiki/Ordination" title="Ordination">ordained</a> him a <a href="/wiki/Hieromonk" title="Hieromonk">hieromonk</a> (priest-monk). While visiting Thessalonica the distinguished <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Dekapolis" title="Gregory of Dekapolis">Gregory of Dekapolis</a> was so impressed with Joseph, because of his rare character, that he invited him to join his <a href="/wiki/Stoudios_Monastery" class="mw-redirect" title="Stoudios Monastery">Stoudios Monastery</a> in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconodule_mission_to_Rome">Iconodule mission to Rome</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Joseph_the_Hymnographer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Iconodule mission to Rome">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>With the resurgence of <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm" title="Byzantine Iconoclasm">Byzantine Iconoclasm</a> under <a href="/wiki/Leo_the_Armenian" class="mw-redirect" title="Leo the Armenian">Leo V the Armenian</a> and <a href="/wiki/Theophilos_(emperor)" title="Theophilos (emperor)">Theophilos</a>, Joseph was sent by Gregory to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> following an invitation of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_III" title="Pope Leo III">Pope Leo III</a> in 841. While en route, Joseph was captured by slave-trading pirates and sold as a <a href="/wiki/Slave" class="mw-redirect" title="Slave">slave</a> in <a href="/wiki/Crete" title="Crete">Crete</a>. In slavery <a href="/wiki/St._Nicholas" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Nicholas">St. Nicholas</a> appeared to Joseph and asked him to sing in the name of God. Nicholas then said to him: "Arise and follow me!" Joseph found freedom soon after his vision. He could finally return to Constantinople after more than one year in slavery in Crete.<sup id="cite_ref-hagiographiclegends_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hagiographiclegends-3">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup> Theophanes is not clear, when Joseph returned to Constantinople, but he mentioned in one paragraph a triumphal return after the death of Theophilos and the restoration of the icons, but also after the recent death of Gregory of Decapolis.<sup id="cite_ref-vita_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vita-4">&#91;d&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastic_foundations">Monastic foundations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Joseph_the_Hymnographer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Monastic foundations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>According to the temporal reconstruction of the early <i>vitae</i> by Daniel Stiernon, Joseph founded a monastery dedicated to his deceased mentor, Gregory of Dekapolis, in 855. Joseph started with an inclosure together with his and Gregory's disciple John at St. Antipas. After the latter's death in 850, he spent some years in a kind of sanctuary dedicated to St. <a href="/wiki/John_Chrysostom" title="John Chrysostom">John Chrysostom</a>, where he continued his ascetic labors and attracted followers. Joseph transferred the <a href="/wiki/Relics" class="mw-redirect" title="Relics">relics</a> taken from Gregory's corpse, together with those from their disciple named John,<sup id="cite_ref-earliest_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-earliest-5">&#91;e&#93;</a></sup> and placed them in a sanctuary of his monastery's church dedicated to St. Bartholomew the Apostle.<sup id="cite_ref-datation_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-datation-6">&#91;f&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Exile_and_recognition_as_an_anachorete_and_saint">Exile and recognition as an anachorete and saint</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Joseph_the_Hymnographer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Exile and recognition as an anachorete and saint">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 858, he was exiled to the theme of <a href="/wiki/Cherson_(theme)" title="Cherson (theme)">Cherson</a> after denouncing <i><a href="/wiki/Caesar_(title)" title="Caesar (title)">Caesar</a></i> <a href="/wiki/Bardas" title="Bardas">Bardas</a>, brother of the <a href="/wiki/Theodora_(wife_of_Theophilos)" title="Theodora (wife of Theophilos)">Empress Theodora</a>, for illicit cohabitation. Joseph returned again to Constantinople in 867, after Bardas had been assassinated.<sup id="cite_ref-exile_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-exile-7">&#91;g&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Through the favour of the Patriarch <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_Ignatius_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Patriarch Ignatius of Constantinople">Ignatius I</a>, he was appointed <i><a href="/wiki/Skeuophylax" title="Skeuophylax">skeuophylax</a></i> (keeper of the sacred vessels—i.e., the official responsible for the building containing the treasure of the church) in the <a href="/wiki/Hagia_Sophia" title="Hagia Sophia">Great Church</a> of Constantinople. Joseph also stood high in the favor of Patriarch <a href="/wiki/Photius_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Photius the Great">Photius the Great</a>, the rival and successor of Ignatius, and accompanied Photius into banishment. He was among those who inspired the first missionaries to Russia.
</p><p>He reportedly possessed the "<a href="/wiki/Discernment_in_Christianity" class="mw-redirect" title="Discernment in Christianity">gift of discernment</a>" because of which Photius appointed him the <a href="/wiki/Starets" title="Starets">spiritual father</a> and <a href="/wiki/Confessor" title="Confessor">confessor</a> for priests, recommending him as, "A man of God, an angel in the flesh and father of fathers." He died 3 April 886 AD according to Theophanes.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Hymnography">Hymnography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Joseph_the_Hymnographer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Hymnography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Since Joseph's contribution to the Studites reform is often confused with the works of Joseph of Thessalonica, <a href="/wiki/Theodore_the_Studite" title="Theodore the Studite">Theodore</a>'s brother, the exact attribution of poems "by Joseph" is still a controversial issue.
Tomadakes (1971) has attributed 385 <a href="/wiki/Canon_(hymnography)" title="Canon (hymnography)">canons</a> and 9 <a href="/wiki/Kontakion" title="Kontakion">kontakia</a><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMioni1948_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMioni1948-8">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> of the <a href="/wiki/Menaion" title="Menaion">menaion</a>, 68 canons of the <a href="/wiki/Octoechos_(liturgy)" title="Octoechos (liturgy)">parakletike</a>, 6 complete canons of the <a href="/wiki/Triodion" title="Triodion">triodion</a> and 34 <a href="/wiki/Canon_(hymnography)#Abbreviated_Canons" title="Canon (hymnography)">triodes-tetraodes</a>, 2 canons and 24 triodes-tetraodes of the <a href="/wiki/Pentekostarion" class="mw-redirect" title="Pentekostarion">pentekostarion</a> to the Sicilian Joseph. He also created more than 6 canons and 13 <a href="/wiki/Sticheron" title="Sticheron">stichera</a>—so-called <span title="Greek-language romanization"><i lang="el-Latn">apokrypha</i></span> which were not included in the new chant books of the sticherarion created by the <a href="/wiki/Stoudion" class="mw-redirect" title="Stoudion">Studites</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETomadakes1971_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETomadakes1971-9">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStiernon1973244_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStiernon1973244-10">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> This attribution regards Joseph more or less as the author or even inventor of the Parakletike, but earlier sources which had been recently discovered, do not confirm this view, it rather reframes the question, how the repertoire was changed and re-ordered by Joseph's initiative.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Hagiography_and_veneration">Hagiography and veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Joseph_the_Hymnographer&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Hagiography and veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Joseph the Hymnographer appears as well in Latin as in Greek hagiography.<sup id="cite_ref-hagsource_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hagsource-11">&#91;h&#93;</a></sup> The earliest <i><a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">Vita</a></i> was written by Theophanes who followed Joseph in his monastery as <a href="/wiki/Hegoumenos" class="mw-redirect" title="Hegoumenos">hegoumenos</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPapadopulos-Kerameus1901_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPapadopulos-Kerameus1901-12">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> There is a later <a href="/wiki/Synaxarion" class="mw-redirect" title="Synaxarion">synaxarion</a>, probably of the 11th century, attributed to one John the Deacon whose exact identity is still a controversial matter.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStiernon1973248_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStiernon1973248-13">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Godfrey_Henschen" title="Godfrey Henschen">Godfrey Henschen</a>'s edition of the synaxarion was reprinted at <a href="/wiki/Patrologia_Graeca" title="Patrologia Graeca">Patrologia Graeca</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEIoannes_o_Diakonos1862939–976_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEIoannes_o_Diakonos1862939–976-14">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> A younger <i>Vita</i> was written by Theodore Pediasimos during the early Palaiologan period (early 14th century).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETreu1899_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETreu1899-15">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Menologium" title="Menologium">feast</a> of Joseph the Hymnographer is celebrated on 3 April in the Greek tradition, on 4 April in the Slavic rite, and on 14 June in the calendar of saints of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>.
</p>

<li id="cite_note-birthyear-1"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-birthyear_1-0">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">The exact year of birth is not known, but it can be roughly assumed between 812 and 818, most probably soon after 816.</span>
</li>
<li id="cite_note-place-2"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-place_2-0">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">The exact place of origin varied among different hagiographers. He was likely from the <a href="/wiki/Three_valli_of_Sicily" title="Three valli of Sicily">Val Demone</a> of Sicily, where most of the Orthodox communities were located. <a href="#CITEREFTomadakes1971">Tomadakes 1971</a> dates his family's departure from Sicily to 831, from the port of <a href="/wiki/Palermo" title="Palermo">Palermo</a> which he also regards as his birth town. Theodore Pediasimos on the other hand regarded <a href="/wiki/Syracuse,_Sicily" title="Syracuse, Sicily">Syracuse</a> as his port and birth town.</span>
</li>
<li id="cite_note-hagiographiclegends-3"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-hagiographiclegends_3-0">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">Later hagiographic legends, probably influenced by the inventions of Theodore Pediasimos, tent to prolong his stay as a prisoner to six years. The vision of Nicholas of Myra which is directly connected with Theophilos' death, ends with a direct exit from the prison's gates to Constantinople. They also regard the "Saracene" pirates as hired by the iconoclastic emperor. Berbers often acted as slave traders on their own behalf and they were particular interested in educated Greeks due to their high value on Mediterranean slave markets. According to hagiographic conventions, Joseph's adventurous journey to Rome was treated as a <i>via purgativa.</i></span>
</li>
<li id="cite_note-vita-4"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-vita_4-0">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">According to Tomadakes' interpretation of Theophanes' <i>Vita</i> Joseph was liberated after 28 January 842, and arrived at Constantinople by February 843, before the feast of Orthodoxy on March 11.(<a href="#CITEREFStiernon1973">Stiernon 1973</a>, p.&#160;252) </span>
</li>
<li id="cite_note-earliest-5"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-earliest_5-0">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">The earliest hagiographic sources are not clear to which saint the relics do really belong. Gregory, Bartholomew, and John (in Georgian versions) are mentioned, but they probably do not refer to Joseph's circle, but to Joseph's own hagiographic synaxaries dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Bartholomew_the_Apostle" title="Bartholomew the Apostle">Bartholomew the Apostle</a> and <a href="/wiki/John_of_Egypt" title="John of Egypt">John of Egypt</a>.(<a href="#CITEREFStiernon1973">Stiernon 1973</a>, p.&#160;260) </span>
</li>
<li id="cite_note-datation-6"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-datation_6-0">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text"><a href="#CITEREFStiernon1973">Stiernon 1973</a>, pp.&#160;252f proposed the following datation: the enclosure with his disciple John at the hermitage St. <a href="/wiki/Antipas_of_Pergamum" title="Antipas of Pergamum">Antipas</a> (843-850), John's death (850),  Joseph's stay at a sanctuary  (σῆκος) of St. John Chrysostom (850-855), and the foundation of his monastery (855).</span>
</li>

</li>
