<p><b>Elizabeth of Hungary</b> (<a href="/wiki/German_language" title="German language">German</a>: <i lang="de">Heilige Elisabeth von Thüringen</i>, <a href="/wiki/Hungarian_language" title="Hungarian language">Hungarian</a>: <i lang="hu">Árpád-házi Szent Erzsébet</i>, <a href="/wiki/Slovak_language" title="Slovak language">Slovak</a>: <i lang="sk">Svätá Alžbeta Uhorská</i>; 7 July 1207 – 17 November 1231),<sup id="cite_ref-bihl_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bihl-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> also known as <b>Elisabeth of Thuringia</b>, was a princess of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary" title="Kingdom of Hungary">Kingdom of Hungary</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Landgrave" title="Landgrave">landgravine</a> of <a href="/wiki/Duchy_of_Thuringia" title="Duchy of Thuringia">Thuringia</a> in <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a>.
</p><p>Elizabeth was married at the age of 14, and widowed at 20.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> After her husband's death, she regained her <a href="/wiki/Dowry" title="Dowry">dowry</a>, using the money to build a hospital where she herself served the sick. She became a symbol of Christian charity after her death at the age of 24 and was <a href="/wiki/Canonize" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonize">canonized</a> on 25 May 1235. She is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>. She was an early member of the <a href="/wiki/Third_Order_of_St._Francis" class="mw-redirect" title="Third Order of St. Francis">Third Order of St. Francis</a>, and is today honored as its <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patroness</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_marriage">Early life and marriage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life and marriage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Elizabeth was the daughter of King <a href="/wiki/Andrew_II_of_Hungary" title="Andrew II of Hungary">Andrew II of Hungary</a> and <a href="/wiki/Gertrude_of_Merania" title="Gertrude of Merania">Gertrude of Merania</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Her mother's sister was <a href="/wiki/Hedwig_of_Andechs" class="mw-redirect" title="Hedwig of Andechs">Hedwig of Andechs</a>, wife of Duke <a href="/wiki/Henry_the_Bearded" title="Henry the Bearded">Heinrich I of Silesia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-bihl_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bihl-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Her ancestry included many notable figures of European royalty, going back as far as <a href="/wiki/Vladimir_the_Great" title="Vladimir the Great">Vladimir the Great</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Kievan_Rus%27" title="Kievan Rus&#39;">Kievan Rus'</a>.
</p><p>According to tradition, she was born in <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary" title="Kingdom of Hungary">Hungary</a>, possibly in the castle of <a href="/wiki/S%C3%A1rospatak" title="Sárospatak">Sárospatak</a>, on 7 July 1207.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> However, a sermon printed in 1497 by the <a href="/wiki/Franciscan" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan">Franciscan</a> <a href="/wiki/Friar" title="Friar">friar</a> <a href="/wiki/Osv%C3%A1t_Laskai" title="Osvát Laskai">Osvaldus de Lasco</a>, a church official in Hungary, is the first source to specifically name <a href="/wiki/S%C3%A1rospatak" title="Sárospatak">Sárospatak</a> as Elizabeth's birthplace, potentially building on local tradition. Osvaldus also translates the <a href="/wiki/Miracle_of_the_roses" title="Miracle of the roses">miracle of the roses</a> to Elizabeth's childhood in Sárospatak and has her leave Hungary at the age of five.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to a different tradition she was born in <a href="/wiki/Pozsony" class="mw-redirect" title="Pozsony">Pozsony</a>, Hungary (present-day <a href="/wiki/Bratislava" title="Bratislava">Bratislava</a>, <a href="/wiki/Slovakia" title="Slovakia">Slovakia</a>), where she lived in the <a href="/wiki/Bratislava_Castle" title="Bratislava Castle">Castle of Posonium</a> until the age of four. Elizabeth was brought to the court of the <a href="/wiki/Rulers_of_Thuringia" class="mw-redirect" title="Rulers of Thuringia">rulers of Thuringia</a> in central Germany, to be betrothed to <a href="/wiki/Louis_IV,_Landgrave_of_Thuringia" title="Louis IV, Landgrave of Thuringia">Louis IV, Landgrave of Thuringia</a><sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> (also known as Ludwig IV), a future union which would reinforce political alliances between the two families.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> She was raised by the Thuringian court and would have been familiar with the local language and culture.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:StElisabethKo%C5%A1iceAltar.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/StElisabethKo%C5%A1iceAltar.JPG/170px-StElisabethKo%C5%A1iceAltar.JPG" decoding="async" width="170" height="228" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/StElisabethKo%C5%A1iceAltar.JPG/255px-StElisabethKo%C5%A1iceAltar.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/StElisabethKo%C5%A1iceAltar.JPG/340px-StElisabethKo%C5%A1iceAltar.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1936" data-file-height="2592" /></a><figcaption><i>St. Elizabeth washing a sick man</i>—a scene from the main altar of <a href="/wiki/St._Elisabeth_Cathedral" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Elisabeth Cathedral">St. Elisabeth Cathedral</a> in <a href="/wiki/Ko%C5%A1ice" title="Košice">Kassa</a>, 15th century</figcaption></figure>
<p>In 1221, at the age of fourteen, Elizabeth married Louis; the same year he was enthroned as <a href="/wiki/Landgrave" title="Landgrave">landgrave</a>, and the marriage appears to have been happy.
</p>
<h3><span id="Religious_inclinations.2C_influences"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Religious_inclinations,_influences">Religious inclinations, influences</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Religious inclinations, influences">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 1223, Franciscan friars arrived, and the teenage Elizabeth not only learned about the ideals of <a href="/wiki/Francis_of_Assisi" title="Francis of Assisi">Francis of Assisi</a>, but started to live them.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Louis was not upset by his wife's charitable efforts, believing that the distribution of his wealth to the poor would bring eternal reward; he is venerated in <a href="/wiki/Thuringia" title="Thuringia">Thuringia</a> as a saint, though he was never canonized by the church.<sup id="cite_ref-libri_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-libri-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It was also about this time that the <a href="/wiki/Priest_(Catholic_Church)" class="mw-redirect" title="Priest (Catholic Church)">priest</a> and later <a href="/wiki/Inquisition" title="Inquisition">inquisitor</a> <a href="/wiki/Konrad_von_Marburg" title="Konrad von Marburg">Konrad von Marburg</a> gained considerable influence over Elizabeth when he was appointed as her <a href="/wiki/Confessor" title="Confessor">confessor</a>. In the spring of 1226, when floods, famine and <a href="/wiki/Epidemic" title="Epidemic">plague</a> wrought havoc in Thuringia, Louis, a staunch supporter of the <a href="/wiki/Hohenstaufen" title="Hohenstaufen">Hohenstaufen</a> <a href="/wiki/Frederick_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor">Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor</a>, represented Frederick II at the <a href="/wiki/Imperial_Diet_(Holy_Roman_Empire)" title="Imperial Diet (Holy Roman Empire)">Imperial Diet</a> held in <a href="/wiki/Cremona" title="Cremona">Cremona</a>.
</p><p>Elizabeth assumed control of affairs at home and distributed alms in all parts of their territory, even giving away state robes and ornaments to the poor.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Widowhood">Widowhood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Widowhood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Marianne_Stokes_St_Elizabeth_of_Hungary_Spinning_for_the_Poor.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/be/Marianne_Stokes_St_Elizabeth_of_Hungary_Spinning_for_the_Poor.jpg/170px-Marianne_Stokes_St_Elizabeth_of_Hungary_Spinning_for_the_Poor.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="285" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/be/Marianne_Stokes_St_Elizabeth_of_Hungary_Spinning_for_the_Poor.jpg/255px-Marianne_Stokes_St_Elizabeth_of_Hungary_Spinning_for_the_Poor.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/be/Marianne_Stokes_St_Elizabeth_of_Hungary_Spinning_for_the_Poor.jpg/340px-Marianne_Stokes_St_Elizabeth_of_Hungary_Spinning_for_the_Poor.jpg 2x" data-file-width="600" data-file-height="1006" /></a><figcaption><i>St. Elizabeth spinning wool for the poor</i> by <a href="/wiki/Marianne_Stokes" title="Marianne Stokes">Marianne Stokes</a> (1895)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Elizabeth's life changed irrevocably on 11 September 1227 when Louis, en route to join the <a href="/wiki/Sixth_Crusade" title="Sixth Crusade">Sixth Crusade</a>, died of a fever in <a href="/wiki/Otranto" title="Otranto">Otranto</a>, <a href="/wiki/Italy" title="Italy">Italy</a>, just a few weeks before the birth of her daughter Gertrude. Upon hearing the news of her husband's death, Elizabeth reportedly said, "He is dead. He is dead. It is to me as if the whole world died today."<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> His remains were returned to Elizabeth in 1228 and entombed at the abbey of <a href="/wiki/Reinhardsbrunn" title="Reinhardsbrunn">Reinhardsbrunn</a>.
</p><p>After Louis' death, his brother, <a href="/wiki/Henry_Raspe" title="Henry Raspe">Henry Raspe</a>, assumed the regency during the minority of Elizabeth's eldest child, <a href="/wiki/Hermann_II,_Landgrave_of_Thuringia" title="Hermann II, Landgrave of Thuringia">Hermann</a> (1222–1241). After bitter arguments over the disposal of her dowry—a conflict in which Konrad was appointed as the official Defender of her case by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_IX" title="Pope Gregory IX">Pope Gregory IX</a>—Elizabeth left the court at <a href="/wiki/Wartburg" title="Wartburg">Wartburg</a> and moved to <a href="/wiki/Marburg" title="Marburg">Marburg</a> in <a href="/wiki/Hesse" title="Hesse">Hesse</a>.
</p><p>Up to 1888 it was believed, on account of the testimony of one of Elizabeth's servants during the canonization process, that Elizabeth was driven from Wartburg in the winter of 1227 by her brother-in-law, Heinrich Raspe, who acted as regent for her son, then only five years old. About 1888 various investigators (Börner, Mielke, Wenck, E. Michael, etc.) asserted that Elizabeth left Wartburg voluntarily. She was not able at the castle to follow Konrad's command to eat only food obtained in a way that was certainly right and proper.<sup id="cite_ref-bihl_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bihl-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Following her husband's death, Elizabeth made solemn vows to Konrad similar to those of a <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nun</a>. These vows included <a href="/wiki/Celibacy" title="Celibacy">celibacy</a>, as well as complete obedience to Konrad as her confessor and <a href="/wiki/Spiritual_director" class="mw-redirect" title="Spiritual director">spiritual director</a>. Konrad's treatment of Elizabeth was extremely harsh, and he held her to standards of behavior which were almost impossible to meet. Among the punishments he is alleged to have ordered were physical beatings; he also ordered her to send away her three children. Her pledge to celibacy proved a hindrance to her family's political ambitions. Elizabeth was more or less held hostage at <a href="/wiki/Pottenstein_Castle" title="Pottenstein Castle">Pottenstein</a>, the castle of her uncle, Bishop Ekbert of Bamberg, in an effort to force her to remarry. Elizabeth, however, held fast to her vow, even threatening to cut off her own nose so that no man would find her attractive enough to marry.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Elizabeth's second child <a href="/wiki/Sophie_of_Thuringia,_Duchess_of_Brabant" title="Sophie of Thuringia, Duchess of Brabant">Sophie of Thuringia</a> (1224–1275) married <a href="/wiki/Henry_II,_Duke_of_Brabant" title="Henry II, Duke of Brabant">Henry II, Duke of Brabant</a>, and was the ancestress of the Landgraves of Hesse, since in the <a href="/wiki/War_of_the_Thuringian_Succession" title="War of the Thuringian Succession">War of the Thuringian Succession</a> she won Hesse for her son <a href="/wiki/Henry_I,_Landgrave_of_Hesse" title="Henry I, Landgrave of Hesse">Heinrich I</a>, called <i>the Child</i>. Elizabeth's third child, <a href="/wiki/Gertrude_of_Altenberg" class="mw-redirect" title="Gertrude of Altenberg">Gertrude of Altenberg</a> (1227–1297), was born several weeks after the death of her father; she became <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> of Altenberg Abbey, Hesse near <a href="/wiki/Wetzlar" title="Wetzlar">Wetzlar</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Elizabeth built a hospital at <a href="/wiki/Marburg" title="Marburg">Marburg</a> for the poor and the sick with the money from her dowry, where she and her companions cared for them.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Miracles">The Miracles</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: The Miracles">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Miracle_of_the_roses">Miracle of the roses</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Miracle of the roses">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Szent_Erzsebet-templom_03.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Szent_Erzsebet-templom_03.jpg/170px-Szent_Erzsebet-templom_03.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="227" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Szent_Erzsebet-templom_03.jpg/255px-Szent_Erzsebet-templom_03.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Szent_Erzsebet-templom_03.jpg/340px-Szent_Erzsebet-templom_03.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1200" data-file-height="1600" /></a><figcaption>A statue showing the miracle of the roses in the rose garden in front of the <a href="/wiki/Gothic_Revival" class="mw-redirect" title="Gothic Revival">neo-Gothic</a> church dedicated to her at Roses' Square (<i>Rózsák tere</i>), <a href="/wiki/Budapest" title="Budapest">Budapest</a><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup></figcaption></figure>
<p>Elizabeth is perhaps best known for her <a href="/wiki/Miracle_of_the_roses" title="Miracle of the roses">miracle of the roses</a>. While taking bread to the poor in secret, she met her husband Ludwig on a hunting party. Ludwig, to quell suspicions of the gentry that she was stealing treasure from the castle, asked her to reveal what was hidden under her cloak. In that moment, her cloak fell open and a vision of white and red roses could be seen, which proved to Ludwig that God's protecting hand was at work.<sup id="cite_ref-Montalembert_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Montalembert-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Her husband, according to the <i>vitae</i>, was never troubled by her charity and always supported it. In some versions of this story, her brother-in-law, Heinrich Raspe, questions her. Hers is one of many miracles that associate Christian saints with roses.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Christ_in_the_bed">Christ in the bed</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Christ in the bed">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Another story told of Elizabeth, also found in Dietrich of Apolda's <i>Vita</i>, relates how she laid the <a href="/wiki/Leper" class="mw-redirect" title="Leper">leper</a> Helias of Eisenach in the bed she shared with her husband. Her mother-in-law, who was horrified, told this immediately to Ludwig on his return. When Ludwig removed the bedclothes in great indignation, at that instant "Almighty God opened the eyes of his soul, and instead of a leper he saw the figure of Christ crucified stretched upon the bed."<sup id="cite_ref-Montalembert_23-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Montalembert-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> This story also appears in <a href="/wiki/Franz_Liszt" title="Franz Liszt">Franz Liszt</a>'s oratorio about Elizabeth.<sup id="cite_ref-mcnichols_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnichols-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_legacy">Death and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Death and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Elisabethkirche2.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9e/Elisabethkirche2.jpg/170px-Elisabethkirche2.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="228" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9e/Elisabethkirche2.jpg/255px-Elisabethkirche2.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9e/Elisabethkirche2.jpg/340px-Elisabethkirche2.jpg 2x" data-file-width="560" data-file-height="752" /></a><figcaption>Elisabethkirche in Marburg</figcaption></figure>

<p>In 1231, Elizabeth died in Marburg at the age of twenty-four.<sup id="cite_ref-Mueller127_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mueller127-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Miracles_after_death_and_canonization">Miracles after death and canonization</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Miracles after death and canonization">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Very soon after the death of Elizabeth, miracles were reported that happened at her grave in the church of the hospital, especially those of healing. On the suggestion of Konrad, and by papal command, examinations were held of those who had been healed between August 1232 and January 1235. The results of those examinations was supplemented by a brief <i>vita</i> of the saint-to-be, and together with the testimony of Elizabeth's handmaidens and companions (bound in a booklet called the <i>Libellus de dictis quatuor ancillarum s. Elizabeth confectus</i>), proved sufficient reason for quick canonization. She was <a href="/wiki/Canonize" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonize">canonized</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_IX" title="Pope Gregory IX">Pope Gregory IX</a> on 24 May 1235.<sup id="cite_ref-Mueller127_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mueller127-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">papal bull</a> declaring her a saint is on display in the <i>Schatzkammer</i> of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Teutonic_Order,_Vienna" title="Church of the Teutonic Order, Vienna"><i>Deutschordenskirche</i> in Vienna</a>. Her body was laid in a magnificent golden shrine—still to be seen today—in the <a href="/wiki/St._Elizabeth%27s_Church,_Marburg" title="St. Elizabeth&#39;s Church, Marburg"><i>Elisabethkirche</i></a> in Marburg. Marburg became a center of the <a href="/wiki/Teutonic_Order" title="Teutonic Order">Teutonic Order</a>, which adopted Saint Elizabeth as its secondary patroness. The Order remained in Marburg until its official dissolution by <a href="/wiki/Napoleon" title="Napoleon">Napoleon</a> in 1803. The <i>Elisabethkirche</i> is now a Protestant church, but has spaces set aside for Catholic worship.
</p><p>Elizabeth's shrine became one of the main German centers of <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> of the 14th century and early 15th century. During the course of the 15th century, the popularity of the cult of Saint Elizabeth slowly faded, though to some extent this was mitigated by an aristocratic devotion to St Elizabeth, since through her daughter Sophia she was an ancestor of many leading aristocratic German families.
</p><p>Three hundred years after her death, one of Elizabeth's many descendants, <a href="/wiki/Philip_I,_Landgrave_of_Hesse" title="Philip I, Landgrave of Hesse">Philip I, Landgrave of Hesse</a>, a leader of the <a href="/wiki/Protestant_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant Reformation">Protestant Reformation</a>, raided the church in Marburg. He demanded that the Teutonic Order hand over Elizabeth's bones, in order to disperse her relics and thus put an end to the already declining pilgrimages to Marburg.<sup id="cite_ref-bihl_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bihl-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Philip took away the crowned agate chalice in which her head rested, but returned it after being imprisoned by <a href="/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor">Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor</a>.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Reliquary_of_St._Elizabeth" title="Reliquary of St. Elizabeth">reliquary chalice</a> was subsequently plundered by Swedish troops during the <a href="/wiki/Thirty_Years%27_War" title="Thirty Years&#39; War">Thirty Years' War</a>, and is now on display at the <a href="/wiki/Swedish_History_Museum" title="Swedish History Museum">Swedish History Museum</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-swhist_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-swhist-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> Her skull and some of her bones can be seen at the convent in Vienna bearing her name. A portion of her relics were kept in the church of the Carmelites in <a href="/wiki/Brussels" title="Brussels">Brussels</a>; another in the magnificent chapel of <a href="/wiki/La_Roche-Guyon" title="La Roche-Guyon">La Roche-Guyon</a>, and a considerable part in a precious shrine is in the electoral treasury of Hanover.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> Another part of her relics were taken to <a href="/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1" title="Bogotá">Bogotá</a>, then the capital of the Spanish <a href="/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Granada" title="New Kingdom of Granada">New Kingdom of Granada</a>, by friar <a href="/wiki/Luis_Zapata_de_C%C3%A1rdenas" title="Luis Zapata de Cárdenas">Luis Zapata de Cárdenas</a>. The relics are today inside a chapel dedicated to the saint in the <a href="/wiki/Primatial_Cathedral_of_Bogot%C3%A1" title="Primatial Cathedral of Bogotá">Primatial Cathedral of Bogotá</a>.
</p><p>Elizabeth of Hungary is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered in the Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Lesser_Festival_(Anglicanism)" title="Lesser Festival (Anglicanism)">Lesser Festival</a> on 18 November<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> and in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church</a> on <a href="/wiki/November_19" title="November 19">19 November</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Association_with_the_Franciscans">Association with the Franciscans</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Association with the Franciscans">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>After her death, Elizabeth was commonly associated with the <a href="/wiki/Third_Order_of_Saint_Francis" title="Third Order of Saint Francis">Third Order of Saint Francis</a>, the primarily <a href="/wiki/Laity" title="Laity">lay</a> branch of the Franciscan Order, which has helped propagate her cult. Whether she ever actually joined the order, only recently founded in 1221, the year when she married Louis at the age of fourteen, is not proven to everyone's satisfaction.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It must be kept in mind though that the Third Order was such a new development in the Franciscan movement, that no one official ritual had been established at that point. Elizabeth clearly had a ceremony of <a href="/wiki/Consecration" class="mw-redirect" title="Consecration">consecration</a> in which she adopted a Franciscan <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">religious habit</a> in her new way of life, as noted above.
</p><p>Because of her support of the friars sent to Thuringia, she was made known to the founder, St <a href="/wiki/Francis_of_Assisi" title="Francis of Assisi">Francis of Assisi</a>, who sent her a personal message of blessing shortly before his death in 1226. Upon her canonization, she was declared the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the Third Order of St Francis, an honor she shares with St <a href="/wiki/Louis_IX_of_France" title="Louis IX of France">Louis IX of France</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Depiction_in_art_and_music">Depiction in art and music</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Depiction in art and music">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Saint Elizabeth is often depicted holding a basket of bread, or some other sort of food or beverage, characteristic of her devotion to the poor and hungry.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> The "miracle of the roses" has also proved a popular theme for artists.

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="2007_octocentennial_celebrations">2007 octocentennial celebrations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: 2007 octocentennial celebrations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The year 2007 was proclaimed <i><b>Elizabeth Year</b></i> in Marburg. All year, events commemorating Elizabeth's life and works were held, culminating in a town-wide festival to celebrate the 800th anniversary of her birth on 7 July 2007. Pilgrims came from all over the world for the occasion, which ended with a special service in the Elisabeth Church that evening.
</p><p>A new musical based on Elizabeth's life, <i>Elisabeth--die Legende einer Heiligen</i> (<i>Elizabeth--Legend of a Saint</i>), starring Sabrina Weckerlin as Elizabeth, Armin Kahn as Ludwig, and Chris Murray as Konrad, premiered in Eisenach in 2007. It was performed in Eisenach and Marburg for two years, and closed in Eisenach in July 2009.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The entire Third Order of St. Francis, both the friars and sisters of the Third Order Regular and the <a href="/wiki/Secular_Franciscan_Order" title="Secular Franciscan Order">Secular Franciscan Order</a>, joined in this celebration through a two-year-long program of study of her life. This was conducted throughout the Order, across the globe. There were also religious ceremonies held worldwide during that period. The yearlong observance of the centennial which began on her <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> in 2007 was closed at the <a href="/wiki/General_Chapter" class="mw-redirect" title="General Chapter">General Chapter</a> of the Order, held in <a href="/wiki/Budapest" title="Budapest">Budapest</a> in 2008. The New York region of the Order produced a movie of her life, produced by a sister of the Order, Lori Pieper.<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Honors">Honors</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Elizabeth_of_Hungary&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Honors">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Saint Elizabeth of Hungary: On the 700-year anniversary of her death, Hungary issued a set of four stamps in her honor: on 21 April 1932;<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> on 1 August 1944 one postage stamp was issued;<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> on 16 July 1938 Czechoslovakia issued a stamp in her honor showing the <a href="/wiki/Cathedral_of_St._Elizabeth" title="Cathedral of St. Elizabeth">Cathedral of St. Elizabeth</a> in <a href="/wiki/Ko%C5%A1ice" title="Košice">Košice</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> She was declared the patron saint of the same city in 2019.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The hymn "<a href="/wiki/Wenn_das_Brot,_das_wir_teilen" title="Wenn das Brot, das wir teilen">Wenn das Brot, das wir teilen</a>", written for a pilgrimage to places in Thuringia connected to her life, refers to her Miracle of the roses, and mentions other works of charity.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1011085734">.mw-parser-output .reflist{font-size:90%;margin-bottom:0.5em;list-style-type:decimal}.mw-parser-output .reflist .references{font-size:100%;margin-bottom:0;list-style-type:inherit}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns-2{column-width:30em}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns-3{column-width:25em}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns{margin-top:0.3em}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns ol{margin-top:0}.mw-parser-output .reflist-columns li{page-break-inside:avoid;break-inside:avoid-column}.mw-parser-output .reflist-upper-alpha{list-style-type:upper-alpha}.mw-parser-output .reflist-upper-roman{list-style-type:upper-roman}.mw-parser-output .reflist-lower-alpha{list-style-type:lower-alpha}.mw-parser-output .reflist-lower-greek{list-style-type:lower-greek}.mw-parser-output .reflist-lower-roman{list-style-type:lower-roman}</style><div class="reflist reflist-lower-alpha">
<div class="mw-references-wrap"><ol class="references">
<li id="cite_note-15"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-15">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">Some, however (see the <i>Catholic Encyclopedia</i>), have suggested that Ludwig's brother Hermann was in fact the eldest, and that she was first betrothed to him until his death in 1232, but this is doubtful. An event of this magnitude would almost certainly be mentioned at least once in the many original sources at our disposal, and this is not the case. Rather, the 14th-century <i>Cronica Reinhardsbrunnensis</i> specifically names Hermann as the second son.

In addition, the only contemporary document (dated 29 May 1214) that might support Hermann's claim to be the eldest by putting his name before that of Louis relates to a monastery in Hesse. This, it has been suggested, actually supports the claim that Hermann was the younger of the two, as Hesse was traditionally the domain of the second son, and thus it would be normal that his name be mentioned first, as this document deals with his territory.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup></span>
</li>
</ol></div></div>
