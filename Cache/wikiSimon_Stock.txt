<p><b>Simon Stock</b>, <a href="/wiki/Carmelites" title="Carmelites">OCarm</a> was an <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_England_and_Wales" title="Catholic Church in England and Wales">English Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Priest" title="Priest">priest</a> and <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> who lived in the 13th century and was an early <a href="/wiki/Prior_(ecclesiastical)" title="Prior (ecclesiastical)">prior</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Carmelite" class="mw-redirect" title="Carmelite">Carmelite</a> order. The <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Blessed Virgin Mary">Blessed Virgin Mary</a> is traditionally said to have appeared to him and given him the Carmelite <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">habit</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Brown_Scapular" class="mw-redirect" title="Brown Scapular">Brown Scapular</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Thus, popular devotion to Stock is usually associated with devotion to <a href="/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Mount_Carmel" title="Our Lady of Mount Carmel">Our Lady of Mount Carmel</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Simon_Stock&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Brothers of the Blessed Virgin Mary of Mount Carmel had their origins as a Christian hermit community in <a href="/wiki/Palestine_(region)" title="Palestine (region)">Palestine</a>; with the <a href="/wiki/Fall_of_Acre" class="mw-redirect" title="Fall of Acre"> enfeebling and fall of the Crusader Kingdoms</a> and the resumption of Muslim rule, in the early 13th century the members moved to Europe where they became <a href="/wiki/Mendicant_order" class="mw-redirect" title="Mendicant order">mendicant</a> <a href="/wiki/Friars" class="mw-redirect" title="Friars">friars</a>. Simon was born in <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a><sup id="cite_ref-hilgers_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hilgers-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and became an early leader of the Order soon after it migrated to that country.
</p><p>Historical evidence about Simon's life comes primarily from medieval catalogues of saints and of Carmelite priors general, which are not consistent with one another in their details. The earliest of these describe Simon as someone known for holiness during his life, and miracles attested to this after his death. The surname "Stock" appears in some documents but not in others, and is related to a story that Simon lived for a time in a hollow tree ("stock" meant tree trunk) before the arrival of the Carmelites in England, in keeping with prophetic tradition.<sup id="cite_ref-carmel.net_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-carmel.net-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He is believed to have lived at <a href="/wiki/Aylesford" title="Aylesford">Aylesford</a> in <a href="/wiki/Kent" title="Kent">Kent</a>, a place that hosted in 1247 the first general chapter of the Carmelite Order held outside the Holy Land, and where there is still a monastery of Carmelite friars.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Simon was probably the fifth or sixth prior general of the Carmelites (historical evidence suggests perhaps from about 1256–1266). During his service, the order spread widely in southern and western Europe, especially in England. Stock is credited with founding houses in the university cities of that era, as in 1248 at Cambridge, in 1253 at Oxford, in 1260 at Paris and Bologna. This action was important for both the growth of the institution and for the training of its younger members.<sup id="cite_ref-hilgers_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hilgers-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Stock lived an ascetic life. He lived on a diet of <a href="/wiki/Herb" title="Herb">herbs</a>, <a href="/wiki/Root" title="Root">roots</a> and wild <a href="/wiki/Apple" title="Apple">apples</a> and drank only water.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He is said to have died in <a href="/wiki/Bordeaux" title="Bordeaux">Bordeaux</a>, France on May 16, though the year is not documented, and was buried there.<sup id="cite_ref-bedeedwards_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bedeedwards-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The earliest extant <a href="/wiki/Liturgy_of_the_hours" class="mw-redirect" title="Liturgy of the hours">liturgical office</a> in Simon Stock's honour was composed in Bordeaux in France, and dates from 1435.<sup id="cite_ref-hilgers_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hilgers-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Liturgies are first known to have been celebrated in Ireland and England in 1458, and throughout the Carmelite Order in 1564. His feast day, an optional memorial, is May 16. Simon's bones are still preserved in a cathedral in <a href="/wiki/Bordeaux" title="Bordeaux">Bordeaux</a>; a tibia was brought to England in the 1860s for the <a href="/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Mount_Carmel_and_St_Simon_Stock" title="Our Lady of Mount Carmel and St Simon Stock">Carmelite church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Kensington" title="Kensington">Kensington</a>, London, and a part of the skull was enshrined at <a href="/wiki/Aylesford" title="Aylesford">Aylesford</a> in 1950. Simon Stock is the patron saint of the English province of <a href="/wiki/Discalced_Carmelites" title="Discalced Carmelites">Discalced Carmelites</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-bedeedwards_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bedeedwards-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Brown_Scapular">Brown Scapular</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Simon_Stock&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Brown Scapular">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:IMG_3918_-_Milano_-_Chiesa_del_Carmine_-_Dettaglio_dl_portale_-_Foto_Giovanni_Dall%27Orto_19-jan_2007.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a3/IMG_3918_-_Milano_-_Chiesa_del_Carmine_-_Dettaglio_dl_portale_-_Foto_Giovanni_Dall%27Orto_19-jan_2007.jpg/250px-IMG_3918_-_Milano_-_Chiesa_del_Carmine_-_Dettaglio_dl_portale_-_Foto_Giovanni_Dall%27Orto_19-jan_2007.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="166" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a3/IMG_3918_-_Milano_-_Chiesa_del_Carmine_-_Dettaglio_dl_portale_-_Foto_Giovanni_Dall%27Orto_19-jan_2007.jpg/375px-IMG_3918_-_Milano_-_Chiesa_del_Carmine_-_Dettaglio_dl_portale_-_Foto_Giovanni_Dall%27Orto_19-jan_2007.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a3/IMG_3918_-_Milano_-_Chiesa_del_Carmine_-_Dettaglio_dl_portale_-_Foto_Giovanni_Dall%27Orto_19-jan_2007.jpg/500px-IMG_3918_-_Milano_-_Chiesa_del_Carmine_-_Dettaglio_dl_portale_-_Foto_Giovanni_Dall%27Orto_19-jan_2007.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2048" data-file-height="1360" /></a><figcaption>Chiesa del Carmine, Milan. Simon Stock and an angel.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The earliest accounts of Simon's life which are still extant do not mention him having a vision. The first such reference that has survived intact dates from the late 14th century, over 100 years after the July 16, 1251 date when tradition says the vision occurred. It states that "St. Simon was an Englishman, a man of great holiness and devotion, who always in his prayers asked the <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Blessed Virgin Mary">Virgin</a> to favour his Order with some singular privilege. The Virgin appeared to him holding the <a href="/wiki/Scapular_of_Our_Lady_of_Mount_Carmel" title="Scapular of Our Lady of Mount Carmel">Scapular</a> in her hand.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In its original context, the meaning of this promise was that <a href="/wiki/Carmelite" class="mw-redirect" title="Carmelite">Carmelite</a> religious who persevered in their vocation would be saved. Beginning in the 16th century, the Carmelites began giving the <a href="/wiki/Brown_Scapular" class="mw-redirect" title="Brown Scapular">Brown Scapular</a> to lay people who wanted to be affiliated with the Order, and it became increasingly popular as a religious article.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Carmelite tradition has held that in 1251 the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a> made the "Scapular Promise" to Simon Stock regarding the <a href="/wiki/Scapular_of_Our_Lady_of_Mount_Carmel" title="Scapular of Our Lady of Mount Carmel">Scapular of Our Lady of Mount Carmel</a>, namely: "whoever dies clothed in this habit shall not suffer the fires of Hell."<sup id="cite_ref-Thomas_Petrisko_2000,_page_105_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Thomas_Petrisko_2000,_page_105-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> This is understood to mean that anyone who remains faithful to the Carmelite vocation until death will be granted the grace of final perseverance.<sup id="cite_ref-catechesis_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-catechesis-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The long-standing tradition of the church has approved the vision of Simon Stock as an acceptable cult, but that is distinguishable from authenticating it as a historical experience. "The question then, from a historical perspective, is not whether Mary appeared to Simon Stock and gave him the scapular, but rather did Simon Stock perceive the Mother of God bestowing this sign of her protection on him and his brothers in Carmel."<sup id="cite_ref-catechesis_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-catechesis-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> However, wearing the scapular remains a valuable devotion as a sign of one's commitment to Mary, and a pledge of her protection.
</p><p>Scholarly investigation into historical source documents has raised questions about whether Simon Stock's vision actually happened, or if this tradition about him arose later, perhaps as a means of expressing in the form of a story, the strongly held Carmelite spiritual belief in the favor and protection of the Blessed Virgin Mary. Several other religious orders in the Middle Ages had similar stories of Mary giving their habit or promising protection.<sup id="cite_ref-carmel.net_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-carmel.net-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-bedeedwards_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bedeedwards-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> The great Carmelite authors of the 14th century do not mention the <a href="/wiki/Scapular_of_Our_Lady_of_Mount_Carmel" title="Scapular of Our Lady of Mount Carmel">scapular</a> at all. Challenges to the historicity of the scapular vision (and passionate defenses of it) are not a new phenomenon; a notable challenge came in 1653, from a scholar at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Paris" title="University of Paris">University of Paris</a>, <a href="/wiki/Jean_de_Launoy" title="Jean de Launoy">Jean de Launoy</a>. In response, a Carmelite named John Cheron published a fragment of a letter <sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> which he purported to be an account by Simon Stock's secretary Peter Swanington (or Swanyngton), giving details of Simon's life, and the scapular vision.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> This document was also the origin of the date that has become traditional for the vision, <a href="/wiki/July_16" title="July 16">July 16</a>, 1251 (July 16 was already in the 17th century the feast of <a href="/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Mount_Carmel" title="Our Lady of Mount Carmel">Our Lady of Mount Carmel</a>, though that liturgy made no reference to the scapular). Today, scholars affirm that this document was a <a href="/wiki/Pseudepigrapha" title="Pseudepigrapha">forgery</a> and Cheron himself the likely author.<sup id="cite_ref-souza_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-souza-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Benedict Zimmerman proposed that an apparition did take place in the 13th century, but was to another Carmelite brother, which was later attributed to Simon Stock, and that the vision was not of the Virgin Mary, but of a recently deceased Carmelite.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Over time, the scapular took an increasingly Marian tone, became identified with Carmelite piety toward the Virgin Mary, and the feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel began to be called the "scapular feast".<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Shortly after <a href="/wiki/Vatican_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Vatican II">Vatican II</a>, the historical uncertainties revealed by 20th-century scholars such as Ludovico Saggi, of the "Institutum Carmelitanum" in Rome,<sup id="cite_ref-carmel.net_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-carmel.net-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-bedeedwards_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bedeedwards-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> resulted in the Catholic Church briefly striking the feast day of Simon Stock from the <a href="/wiki/Carmelite" class="mw-redirect" title="Carmelite">Carmelite</a> <a href="/wiki/Liturgical_calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Liturgical calendar">liturgical calendar</a>. It was restored in 1979 as an optional memorial, on the condition that no mention be made of the scapular vision.<sup id="cite_ref-souza_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-souza-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>
Devotion to the <a href="/wiki/Brown_Scapular" class="mw-redirect" title="Brown Scapular">Brown Scapular</a> remains widespread and recommended by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>. The Carmelites continue to find meaning in the traditional story and iconography of Simon Stock receiving the scapular, particularly as reflecting their filial relationship with Mary. When <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> addressed the Carmelite family in 2001 on the occasion of the 750th anniversary of the bestowal of the Scapular, he said that </p><blockquote><p>"Over time this rich Marian heritage of Carmel has become, through the spread of the Holy Scapular devotion, a treasure for the whole <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Church</a>. By its simplicity, its anthropological value and its relationship to Mary's role in regard to the Church and humanity, this devotion was so deeply and widely accepted by the People of God that it came to be expressed in the memorial of 16 July on the <a href="/wiki/Liturgical_calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Liturgical calendar">liturgical calendar</a> of the universal Church," the Feast of <a href="/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Mount_Carmel" title="Our Lady of Mount Carmel">Our Lady of Mount Carmel</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p><br />
</p>
