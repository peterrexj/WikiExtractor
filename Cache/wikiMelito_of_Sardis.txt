<p><b>Melito of Sardis</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Μελίτων Σάρδεων</span> <i>Melítōn Sárdeōn</i>; died <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;180</span>) was the <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a> of <a href="/wiki/Sardis" title="Sardis">Sardis</a> near <a href="/wiki/Smyrna" title="Smyrna">Smyrna</a> in western <a href="/wiki/Early_centers_of_Christianity#Anatolia" class="mw-redirect" title="Early centers of Christianity">Anatolia</a>, and a great authority in early Christianity. Melito held a foremost place in terms of bishops in Asia due to his personal influence and his literary works, most of which have been <a href="/wiki/Lost_works" class="mw-redirect" title="Lost works">lost</a>. What has been recovered, however, has provided a great insight into Christianity during the second century. <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>, speaking of the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old Testament</a> <a href="/wiki/Biblical_canon" title="Biblical canon">canon</a> established by Melito, quotes <a href="/wiki/Tertullian" title="Tertullian">Tertullian</a> to the effect that he was esteemed as a prophet by many of the faithful. This work by Tertullian has been lost but Jerome quotes pieces regarding Melito for the high regard in which he was held at that time. Melito is remembered for his work on developing the first Old Testament Canon. Though it cannot be determined what date he was elevated to an <a href="/wiki/Episcopacy" class="mw-redirect" title="Episcopacy">episcopacy</a>, it is probable that he was bishop during the arising controversy at Laodicea in regard to the observance of Easter, which resulted in him writing his most famous work, an Apology for Christianity to <a href="/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius" title="Marcus Aurelius">Marcus Aurelius</a>. Little is known of his life outside what works are quoted or read by <a href="/wiki/Clement_of_Alexandria" title="Clement of Alexandria">Clement of Alexandria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Eusebius" title="Eusebius">Eusebius</a>. A letter of <a href="/wiki/Polycrates_of_Ephesus" title="Polycrates of Ephesus">Polycrates of Ephesus</a> to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Victor_I" title="Pope Victor I">Pope Victor</a> about 194 (Eusebius, Church History V.24) states that "Melito the eunuch [this is interpreted "the virgin" by <a href="/wiki/Tyrannius_Rufinus" title="Tyrannius Rufinus">Rufinus</a> in his translation of Eusebius], whose whole walk was in the Holy Spirit", was buried at Sardis.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> His feast day is celebrated on April 1.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Jewish_and_Hellenistic_roots">Jewish and Hellenistic roots</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Melito_of_Sardis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Jewish and Hellenistic roots">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Polycrates of Ephesus, a notable bishop of the time, was a contemporary of Melito, and in one of the letters preserved by Eusebius, Polycrates describes Melito as having fully lived in the Spirit. Jewish by birth, Melito lived in an atmosphere where the type of Christianity practiced was largely oriented toward the Jewish form of the Christian faith.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Though Melito's extant writings never quote directly from the <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a> corpus,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> it is thought that his orientation represents the Johannine tradition, and that his theological understanding of Christ often mirrored that of John.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> However, like most of his contemporaries, Melito was fully immersed in Greek culture. This Johannine tradition led Melito to consider the Gospel of John as the chronological timeline of Jesus's life and death. This in turn led to Melito's standpoint on the proper date of Easter discussed in <i><a href="/wiki/Peri_Pascha" title="Peri Pascha">Peri Pascha</a></i> which he held as the 14th of <a href="/wiki/Nisan" title="Nisan">Nisan</a>. He is often described, based on a passage in Eusebius, as a <a href="/wiki/Quartodecimanism" title="Quartodecimanism">Quartodecimanist</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-lynn_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lynn-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Formerly the capital of the <a href="/wiki/Lydia" title="Lydia">Kingdom of Lydia</a>, Sardis underwent a process of Hellenization due to the influence of Alexander the Great, thus making Sardis a thoroughly Greek city long before Melito was born.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Trained in the art of rhetorical argumentation, Melito is believed to have been greatly influenced by two Stoic philosophers in particular, namely, <a href="/wiki/Cleanthes" title="Cleanthes">Cleanthes</a> and <a href="/wiki/Poseidonius" class="mw-redirect" title="Poseidonius">Poseidonius</a>. Also proficient in the allegorical interpretation of Homer due to being schooled by sophists, it is highly likely that his background in <a href="/wiki/Stoicism" title="Stoicism">Stoicism</a> fed into how he wrote and how he interpreted past events and figures of religious significance such as Moses and the Exodus.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Both his Jewish background and background in Stoicism led to his beliefs that the <a href="/wiki/Christian_observance_of_Passover" title="Christian observance of Passover">Christian Passover</a>, celebrated during <a href="/wiki/Holy_week" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy week">holy week</a>, should be celebrated at the same time as the <a href="/wiki/Jewish_Passover" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish Passover">Jewish Passover</a>. His belief in the Old Covenant being fulfilled in Jesus Christ also led to his opinion of the date of Easter.
</p>

<p>Written during the second century AD, and only coming to light within the modern world due to the efforts of <a href="/wiki/Campbell_Bonner" title="Campbell Bonner">Campbell Bonner</a> in 1940,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> some have argued that <i>Peri Pascha</i> (<i>On the Passover</i>) is not a homily, but is based on the <a href="/wiki/Haggadah" title="Haggadah">haggadah</a>, which is a retelling of the works of God at Passover.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The Quartodeciman celebration mainly being a commemoration of Christ's passion and death, Melito stood by the belief that Christ died on the evening of the 14th, when the Passover meal was being prepared.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> F. L. Cross states that Melito's treatise on Pascha is “the most important addition to Patristic literature in the present century”. Aside from the liturgical function of the <i>Peri Pascha</i>, this early Christian document has traditionally been perceived as a somewhat reliable indicator concerning how early Christians felt toward Judaism in general.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Peri Pascha</i> provides an accurate description of Christian feelings towards Jews at the time and their opinion of Judaism. The Jewish people are explicitly blamed for killing Christ.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Melito does not blame Pontius Pilate for the crucifixion of Jesus Christ. The goal may not have been to incite anti-Semitic thoughts in Christians, and rather to bring light to what truly happened during the Passion of Jesus Christ, but it nonetheless helped to feed and establish the anti-Jewish tropes that persist to this day.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Melito is widely remembered for his supersessionism, a belief that the Jewish people fail to fulfill the Old Covenant due to their lack of belief in Jesus Christ. However, he was not satisfied with dismissing Judaism as misguided, and compared Jewish practice to a first draft that, in the wake of Christianity, ought to be "destroyed" or "dissolved." He closes with the fervid accusation "you smashed the Lord to the ground, you were razed to the ground. And you lie dead, while he rose from the dead.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Quartodeciman_controversy">Quartodeciman controversy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Melito_of_Sardis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Quartodeciman controversy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Attracting the attention of persons such as Epiphanius, Chrysostom, and Pseudo-Hippolytus, Quartodeciman practices have encouraged many to deeply ponder questions pertaining to the duration of the period of fasting, and when it should end within the celebration of any Christian Passover. Another question which bothered many individuals was whether everyone ought to uniformly observe Easter on the same day. Melito thought that the Christian Passover should be on the 14th of Nisan, but the Council of Nicaea determined that Jesus Christ's resurrection from the dead should always be celebrated on a Sunday.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Uniformity in church practice was thus the primary drive behind this initiative. Known for following a Johannine chronology, and for believing in a paschal lamb typology, Quartodeciman thought is constituted as such.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> One of the issues raised is that Quartodeciman thought is the idea that Christian Passover would be celebrated at the same time as Jewish Passover. Ultimately the Council of Nicea decided otherwise and agreed that it would always be on a Sunday.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Apology_to_Marcus_Aurelius">Apology to Marcus Aurelius</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Melito_of_Sardis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Apology to Marcus Aurelius">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>During the controversy in Laodicea over the observance of Easter, Melito presented an Apology for Christianity to Marcus Aurelius, according to Eusebius, in his Chronicon, during the years 169–170 AD. A Syriac translation of this apology was rediscovered and placed in a British museum where it was translated into English by Cureton. In this apology, Melito describes Christianity as a philosophy that had originated among the barbarians, but had attained to a flourishing status under the Roman Empire. Melito asks the emperor to rethink the accusations against the Christians and to renounce the edict against them. Melito argues that Christianity had in no way weakened the empire which continued to grow despite the presence of Christianity. Complaining about how the godly are being persecuted and harassed by new decrees, Christians are openly robbed and plundered by those who are taking advantage of the said ordinances. The suffering of Christians at the time in regard to these decrees was mostly of property and taxations while not as much physical suffering. Certainly Christians were persecuted physically as well but in terms of the decrees they were openly robbed and considered to be incestuous and take part in ritualistic acts such as eating children. Melito aimed to dispel the suffering of the Christian people and to change the Greek opinion of them. Demonstrating how Christian thought first flourished among the Gentiles, and how it has benefited the empire, Melito tried to convince the emperor to rethink his current policies since Christianity only brought greatness and success to Rome. Reminding the emperor of the virtuous conduct of Hadrian, Melito called for an end to all violence toward the growing Christian communities within the empire.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Melito.27s_high_Christology"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Melito's_high_Christology">Melito's high Christology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Melito_of_Sardis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Melito&#039;s high Christology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Emphasizing, like John, the unity of Christ and the Father,<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Melito declared that Christ is at once God and a perfect man. Having two essences while being one and the same, his godhead was demonstrated by way of all of the signs and miracles he performed after being baptized. Successfully managing to hide his divinity from the world before that central event occurred with John the Baptist, Jesus felt the pangs of hunger just like everyone else. Writing against Marcion, Melito focused on Christ's divinity and humanity in order to counter the claim that Jesus was simply and uniquely divine; having no material counterpart.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Melito does not anthropomorphize the divine nature of Christ and keeps the attributes of the divine nature and the human nature wholly separate. While he describes the attributes of each nature separately, he also speaks of the two natures of Christ combined. The form of speech used is that of two natures in one Christ. According to Melito, Jesus Christ was both entirely human and entirely divine.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Old_Testament_canon">Old Testament canon</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Melito_of_Sardis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Old Testament canon">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Melito%27s_canon" title="Melito&#39;s canon">Melito's canon</a></div>
<p>Melito gave the first Christian list of the canon in the Old Testament. In his canon he excludes the book of Esther and possibly all the <a href="/wiki/Apocrypha" title="Apocrypha">Apocrypha</a> though there is debate on whether he includes Wisdom. Around 170 after traveling to Palestine, and probably visiting the library at <a href="/wiki/Caesarea_Maritima" title="Caesarea Maritima">Caesarea Maritima</a>, Melito compiled the earliest known Christian canon of the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old Testament</a>, a term he coined. A passage cited by <a href="/wiki/Eusebius" title="Eusebius">Eusebius</a> contains Melito's famous canon of the Old Testament. Melito presented elaborate parallels between the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old Testament</a> or <a href="/wiki/Old_Covenant" class="mw-redirect" title="Old Covenant">Old Covenant</a>, which he likened to the form or mold, and the <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a> or <a href="/wiki/New_Covenant" title="New Covenant">New Covenant</a>, which he likened to the truth that broke the mold, in a series of Eklogai,<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="margin-left:0.1em; white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify" title="Wikipedia:Please clarify"><span title="Had been a link to Eclogues, possibly Eclogue intended target, but this doesn&#39;t seem to be poetry about shepherding. (January 2021)">clarification needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> six books of extracts from the <a href="/w/index.php?title=Law_and_the_Prophets&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Law and the Prophets (page does not exist)">Law and the Prophets</a> presaging Christ and the Christian faith. His opinion of the Old Covenant was that it was fulfilled by Christians, whereas the Jewish people failed to fulfill it. The New Covenant is the truth found through Jesus Christ.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Millennialism">Millennialism</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Melito_of_Sardis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Millennialism">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>It is thought by some that Melito, following the likes of Irenaeus, was a chiliast who expected a millennial reign of Christ on Earth.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> This impression is sometimes based upon information conveyed by Jerome and Gennadius. These ancient sources, however, are far from conclusive on Melito's position. Jerome nowhere mentions Melito in connection with millennialism, even if at <i>Comm. on Ezek.</i> 36 he does mention Tertullian, Lactantius, Victorinus of Petovium, Irenaeus, and Apollinaris of Laodicea as being chiliasts. Nor does Jerome suggest that Melito was a chiliast in his description of Melito's life and work in <i>On Virtuous Men</i> 24. The same is true for the source on which Jerome likely draws for his information on Melito: Eusebius of Caesarea's <i>Church History</i> 4.26.2-4. Gennadius (<i>De Dogm. Eccl.,</i> Ch. 52) likely does not refer to Melito of Sardis at all in his reference to the Meletians but rather to Meletius of Lycopolis, who together with his followers may have been chiliasts.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_legacy">Death and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Melito_of_Sardis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Death and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In regard to the death of Melito, there is not much information preserved or recorded. Polycrates of Ephesus, in a letter addressed to Pope Victor (AD. 196) preserved in Eusebius’ history, says, “What shall I say of Melito, whose actions' were all guided by the operations of the Holy Spirit? Who was interred at Sardis, where he waits the resurrection and the judgement?". From this it may be deduced that he had died some time previous to the date of this letter at Sardis, which is the place of his interment.
	Melito's reputation as a writer remained strong into the Middle Ages: numerous works were pseudepigraphically ascribed to him. Melito was especially skilled in the literature of the Old Testament, and was one of the most prolific authors of his time. Eusebius furnished a list of Melito's works. While many of these works are lost, the testimony of the fathers remains to inform us how highly they were viewed. Eusebius presents some fragments of Melito's works and some others are found in the works of different writers. Fragments' of his works found preserved in a Syriac translation are now stored in the library of the British Museum. Cureton has translated some and others have been published in Kitto's Journal of Sacred Literature, vol 15. Due to Melito's reputation, many works are falsely attributed to him due to the lack of recorded literature surrounding him. 
</p>
