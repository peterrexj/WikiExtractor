<p><b>John Melchior Bosco</b>, <a href="/wiki/Salesians_of_Don_Bosco" title="Salesians of Don Bosco">SDB</a> (<a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Giovanni Melchiorre Bosco</i>; <a href="/wiki/Piedmontese_language" title="Piedmontese language">Piedmontese</a>: <i lang="pms">Gioann Melchior Bòsch</i>; 16 August 1815<sup id="cite_ref-Lemoyne_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lemoyne-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>&#160;&#8211;&#32;31 January 1888),<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> popularly known as <b>Don Bosco</b> (<a href="/wiki/International_Phonetic_Alphabet" title="International Phonetic Alphabet">IPA</a>: <small></small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="it-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Italian" title="Help:IPA/Italian">[ˈdɔm ˈbɔsko, bo-]</a></span>),<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> was an <a href="/wiki/Italian_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Italian Catholic">Italian Catholic</a> priest, educator, writer, and <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> of the 19th century. While working in <a href="/wiki/Turin" title="Turin">Turin</a>, where the population suffered many of the ill effects of <a href="/wiki/Industrialization" class="mw-redirect" title="Industrialization">industrialization</a> and <a href="/wiki/Urbanization" title="Urbanization">urbanization</a>, he dedicated his life to the betterment and education of <a href="/wiki/Street_children" title="Street children">street children</a>, <a href="/wiki/Juvenile_delinquents" class="mw-redirect" title="Juvenile delinquents">juvenile delinquents</a>, and other disadvantaged youth. He developed teaching methods based on love rather than punishment, a method that became known as the <a href="/wiki/Salesian_Preventive_System" title="Salesian Preventive System">Salesian Preventive System</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A follower of the spirituality and philosophy of <a href="/wiki/Francis_de_Sales" title="Francis de Sales">Francis de Sales</a>, Bosco was an ardent devotee of the <a href="/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus" title="Mary, mother of Jesus">Virgin Mary</a> under the title <a href="/wiki/Mary_Help_of_Christians" title="Mary Help of Christians">Mary Help of Christians</a>. He later dedicated his works to de Sales when he founded the <a href="/wiki/Salesians_of_Don_Bosco" title="Salesians of Don Bosco">Salesians of Don Bosco</a>, based in Turin.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Together with <a href="/wiki/Maria_Domenica_Mazzarello" title="Maria Domenica Mazzarello">Maria Domenica Mazzarello</a>, he founded the <a href="/wiki/Institute_of_the_Daughters_of_Mary_Help_of_Christians" class="mw-redirect" title="Institute of the Daughters of Mary Help of Christians">Institute of the Daughters of Mary Help of Christians</a>, now commonly known as the Salesian Sisters of Don Bosco, a religious congregation of nuns dedicated to the care and education of poor girls. He taught <a href="/wiki/Dominic_Savio" title="Dominic Savio">Dominic Savio</a>, of whom he wrote a biography that helped the young boy be <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a>. He is one of the pioneers of <a href="/wiki/Mutual_aid_(organization_theory)" title="Mutual aid (organization theory)">mutual aid</a> societies that were initiated as collaborative financial support to young migrant Catholic workers in the city of Turin. In 1850, he drew up regulations to assist apprentices and their companions when any of them was involuntarily without work or fell ill.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 18 April 1869, one year after the construction of the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Mary_Help_of_Christians_in_Turin" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica of Mary Help of Christians in Turin">Basilica of Mary Help of Christians in Turin</a>, Bosco established the Association of Mary Help of Christians (ADMA) connecting it with commitments easily fulfilled by most common people, to the spirituality and the mission of the Salesian Congregation (CG 24 SDB, 1996, NR. 80). The ADMA was founded to promote the veneration of the Most Holy Sacrament and Mary Help of Christians (Don Bosco, Association of the Devotees of Mary Help of Christians, San Benigno Canavese, 1890, page 33).<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1875, Bosco began to publish the <i><a href="/wiki/Salesian_Bulletin" title="Salesian Bulletin">Salesian Bulletin</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Bulletin</i> has remained in continuous publication, and is currently published in 50 different editions and 30 languages.<sup id="cite_ref-The_Salesian_Bulletin_in_the_World_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-The_Salesian_Bulletin_in_the_World-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> In 1876, he founded a movement of laity, the <a href="/wiki/Association_of_Salesian_Cooperators" title="Association of Salesian Cooperators">Association of Salesian Cooperators</a>, with the same educational mission to the poor.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Bosco established a network of organizations and centres to carry on his work. Following his <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatification</a> in 1929, he was canonized as a <a href="/wiki/Catholic_saint" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic saint">Catholic saint</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XI" title="Pope Pius XI">Pope Pius XI</a> in 1934.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Bosco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>John Bosco was born on the evening of 16 August 1815 in the hillside hamlet of Becchi, Italy. Becchi is located in a region that was called Castelnuovo d'Asti, which was later renamed <a href="/wiki/Castelnuovo_Don_Bosco" title="Castelnuovo Don Bosco">Castelnuovo Don Bosco</a> in honour of the saint.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> He was the youngest son of Francesco Bosco (1784–1817) and <a href="/wiki/Margherita_Occhiena" title="Margherita Occhiena">Margherita Occhiena</a> (1788–1856). He had two older brothers, Antonio and Giuseppe (1813–1862).<sup id="cite_ref-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_3,_Pages_20-28_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_3,_Pages_20-28-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The Boscos of Becchi were farmhands of the Moglian Family. He was born in a time of great shortage and famine in the Piedmontese countryside, following the devastation wrought by the <a href="/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars" title="Napoleonic Wars">Napoleonic Wars</a> and drought in 1817.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Bosco was little more than two years old, his father died, which left the support of three boys to his mother.<sup id="cite_ref-saxton_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-saxton-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> She played a strong role in Bosco's formation and personality,<sup id="cite_ref-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_7,_Pages_44-49_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_7,_Pages_44-49-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> and was an early supporter of her son's ideals.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> In 1825, when he was nine, Bosco had the first of a series of dreams that would play an influential role in his outlook and work. This first dream "left a profound impression on him for the rest of his life" according to his own memoirs.<sup id="cite_ref-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_15,_Pages_95-96_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_15,_Pages_95-96-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Bosco apparently saw a multitude of very poor boys playing and blaspheming, and a man, who "appeared, nobly attired, with a manly and imposing bearing",<sup id="cite_ref-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_15,_Pages_95-96_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_15,_Pages_95-96-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> and said to him, "You will have to win these friends of yours not with blows, but with gentleness and kindness. So begin right now to show them that sin is ugly and virtue beautiful."<sup id="cite_ref-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_15,_Pages_95-96_19-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_15,_Pages_95-96-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bosco, when he was ten years old at the festive oratory, started watching his classmates' attitudes, and in every fight was the referee. The older boys were scared of him because he knew their strengths and their weaknesses.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> When travelling entertainers performed at a local feast in the nearby hills, he watched and studied the jugglers' tricks and the acrobats' secrets. He would then put on shows of his skills as a juggler, magician, and acrobat,<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> with prayers before and after the performance.<sup id="cite_ref-Magnificat_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Magnificat-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> The money that he needed to prepare all the shows was taken from selling the birds that he hunted and given to him by his mother because she trusted him.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Poverty prevented any serious attempt at schooling. His early years were spent as a shepherd,<sup id="cite_ref-saxton_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-saxton-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and he received his first instruction from <a href="/wiki/Don_Calosso" class="mw-redirect" title="Don Calosso">Don Calosso</a> who "was impressed by John’s memory and understanding of the sermons he had heard at a parish mission in a nearby Church."<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> His childhood experiences are thought to have inspired him to become a <a href="/wiki/Priest" title="Priest">priest</a>. Being a priest was then more commonly a profession for the privileged classes than for farmers.<sup id="cite_ref-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_22,_Pages_142-152_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_22,_Pages_142-152-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> Some biographers portray his older brother, Antonio, as the main obstacle for Bosco's ambition to study, as Antonio protested that John was just "a farmer like us!"<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On a cold morning in February 1827, John left his home and went to look for work as a farm servant. At 12, he found life at home unbearable because of the continuous quarrels with Antonio. Having to face life by himself at such a young age may have developed his later sympathies to help abandoned boys. After begging unsuccessfully for work, he ended up at the wine farm of Louis Moglia.<sup id="cite_ref-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_22,_Pages_142-152_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_22,_Pages_142-152-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> Although he could pursue some studies by himself, he was not able to attend school for two more years. In 1830, he met <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Cafasso" title="Joseph Cafasso">Joseph Cafasso</a>, a young priest who identified some natural talent and supported his first schooling.<sup id="cite_ref-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_24,_Pages_159-163_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lemoyne,_Vol_I,_Chapter_24,_Pages_159-163-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Bosco's mother, Margherita, managed to earn enough money to finance his education. In 1835, Bosco entered the seminary at <a href="/wiki/Chieri" title="Chieri">Chieri</a>, next to the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Immacolata_Concezione,_Chieri" class="mw-redirect" title="Church of the Immacolata Concezione, Chieri">Church of the Immacolata Concezione</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> In 1841, after six years of study, he was <a href="/wiki/Ordained" class="mw-redirect" title="Ordained">ordained</a> a priest on the eve of Trinity Sunday by Archbishop Franzoni of Turin. He was twenty-six years old.<sup id="cite_ref-saxton_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-saxton-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Priesthood_and_first_apostolates">Priesthood and first apostolates</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Bosco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Priesthood and first apostolates">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Donbosco_furbo.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/ce/Donbosco_furbo.jpg" decoding="async" width="212" height="236" class="mw-file-element" data-file-width="212" data-file-height="236" /></a><figcaption>Don Bosco in the 1880s</figcaption></figure>
<p>After ordination, Bosco went to Turin, where Cafasso headed the Institute of Saint Francis of Assisi, which provided higher education for the diocesan priests. Turin then had a population of 117,000 inhabitants. The city reflected the effects of industrialization and urbanization. Numerous poor families lived in the slums of the city and had come from the countryside in search of a better life. During his studies, Bosco accompanied Cafasso in visiting the prisons and became concerned regarding the recidivism of young offenders. He began to work with orphaned and abandoned boys, teaching them catechism and helping them find work. Upon completion of his studies, Cafasso secured for Bosco an appointment as almoner of the Rifugio (Refuge), a girls' boarding school founded in <a href="/wiki/Turin" title="Turin">Turin</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Marchioness" class="mw-redirect" title="Marchioness">Marchioness</a> Giulia di Barolo, so that he could remain in Turin.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> His other ministries included visiting prisoners, teaching <a href="/wiki/Catechism" title="Catechism">catechism</a>, and helping out at many country parishes.
</p><p>Because of population growth and migration to the city, Bosco found the traditional methods of parish ministry to be inefficient. He decided that it was necessary to try another form of apostolate, and he began to meet the boys where they worked and gathered in shops and marketplaces. They were pavers, stonecutters, masons, and plasterers who had come from far away, as he recalled in his brief <i>Memoires</i>.
</p><p>The Oratorio was not simply a charitable institution, and its activities were not limited to Sundays. For Don Bosco, it became his permanent occupation. He looked for jobs for the unemployed. Some of the boys did not have sleeping quarters and slept under bridges or in bleak public dormitories. Twice, he tried to provide lodgings in his house. The first time, they stole the blankets; the second, time, they emptied the hayloft. He did not give up, and in May 1847, he gave shelter to a young boy from Valencia in one of the three rooms he was renting in the slums of Valdocco, where he was living with his mother. He and his mother began taking in orphans. The boys sheltered by Don Bosco numbered 36 in 1852, 115 in 1854, 470 in 1860, and 600 in 1861, reaching a maximum of 800 sometime later.<sup id="cite_ref-ewtn_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ewtn-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bosco and his oratory moved around town for several years; he was turned out of several places in succession. After only two months based in the church of St. Martin, the entire neighbourhood expressed its annoyance with the noise coming from the boys at play. A formal complaint was lodged against them with the municipality. Rumours also circulated that the meetings conducted by the priest with his boys were dangerous; their recreation could be turned into a revolution against the government. The group was evicted.<sup id="cite_ref-ReferenceA_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ReferenceA-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Work_with_apprentices">Work with apprentices</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Bosco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Work with apprentices">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In the archives of the Salesian Congregation is a contract of apprenticeship, dated November 1851; another one on stamped paper costing 40 cents, dated 8 February 1852; and others have later dates. They are among the first contracts of apprenticeship to be found in Turin. All of them are signed by the employer, the apprentice, and Don Bosco. In those contracts, Don Bosco touched on many sensitive issues. Some employers customarily made servants and scullery boys of the apprentices. Don Bosco obliged them to agree to employ the boys only in their acknowledged trade. Employers used to beat the boys, and Don Bosco required them to agree that corrections be made only verbally. He cared for their health and demanded that they be given rest on feast days and an annual holiday. Despite all the efforts and contracts, the situation of the apprentices of the time remained difficult.<sup id="cite_ref-ewtn_30-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ewtn-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>One influential friend was the Piedmontese justice minister <a href="/wiki/Urbano_Rattazzi" title="Urbano Rattazzi">Urbano Rattazzi</a>. He was <a href="/wiki/Anticlerical" class="mw-redirect" title="Anticlerical">anticlerical</a> in his politics but saw some value in Bosco's work.<sup id="cite_ref-This_Saint&#39;s_for_You!_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-This_Saint&#39;s_for_You!-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> While Rattazzi was pushing a bill through the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Sardinia_(1720%E2%80%931861)" title="Kingdom of Sardinia (1720–1861)">Sardinian</a> legislature to suppress religious orders, he advised Bosco on how to get around the law. He found a religious order to keep the oratory going after its founder's death.<sup id="cite_ref-This_Saint&#39;s_for_You!_32-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-This_Saint&#39;s_for_You!-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> Bosco had been thinking about that problem too and had been slowly organizing his helpers into a loose Congregation of St. Francis de Sales. He was also training select older boys for the priesthood. Another supporter of the idea to establish a religious order to carry out Bosco's vision was the reigning pope, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_IX" title="Pope Pius IX">Pope Pius IX</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:San_Giovanni_Bosco.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/San_Giovanni_Bosco.jpg/220px-San_Giovanni_Bosco.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="289" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/San_Giovanni_Bosco.jpg/330px-San_Giovanni_Bosco.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/San_Giovanni_Bosco.jpg/440px-San_Giovanni_Bosco.jpg 2x" data-file-width="536" data-file-height="704" /></a><figcaption>Photograph of Bosco, <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1887</span></figcaption></figure>
<p>Bosco disliked the ideals that had been exported by Revolutionary France, as part of the process of <a href="/wiki/Dechristianization_of_France_during_the_French_Revolution" title="Dechristianization of France during the French Revolution">dechristianization of France during the French Revolution</a>, and called <a href="/wiki/Rousseau" class="mw-redirect" title="Rousseau">Rousseau</a> and <a href="/wiki/Voltaire" title="Voltaire">Voltaire</a> "two vicious leaders of incredulity".<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> He favoured an <a href="/wiki/Ultramontane" class="mw-redirect" title="Ultramontane">ultramontane</a> view of politics that acknowledged the supreme authority of the pope. In 1854, when the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Sardinia_(1720%E2%80%931861)" title="Kingdom of Sardinia (1720–1861)">Sardinia-Piedmont</a> was about to pass a law suppressing monastic orders and confiscating ecclesiastical properties, Bosco reported a series of dreams about "great funerals at court" that referred to politicians or members of the Savoy court.<sup id="cite_ref-The_Dreams_of_St._John_Bosco_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-The_Dreams_of_St._John_Bosco-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In November 1854, Bosco sent a letter to King <a href="/wiki/Victor_Emmanuel_II" title="Victor Emmanuel II">Victor Emmanuel II</a> and admonished him to oppose the confiscation of church property and suppression of the orders; the King failed to respond.<sup id="cite_ref-bosconet.aust.com_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bosconet.aust.com-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> His actions, which had been described by the Italian historian Roberto Petoia as having "manifest blackmailing intentions",<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> ended only after the intervention of the then prime minister, <a href="/wiki/Camillo_Benso,_Count_of_Cavour" title="Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour">Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour</a>. The king's family suffered several deaths in a short period. From January to May 1855, the king's mother <a href="/wiki/Maria_Theresa_of_Austria_(1801%E2%80%931855)" title="Maria Theresa of Austria (1801–1855)">Maria Theresa of Austria</a> (aged 54), his wife <a href="/wiki/Adelaide_of_Austria" title="Adelaide of Austria">Adelaide of Austria</a> (aged 32), their newborn son Vittorio Emanuele, Count of Genoa (nearly four months old), and his only brother, <a href="/wiki/Prince_Ferdinando,_Duke_of_Genoa_(1822%E2%80%931855)" title="Prince Ferdinando, Duke of Genoa (1822–1855)">Prince Ferdinando, Duke of Genoa</a> (aged 32), all died.<sup id="cite_ref-The_Dreams_of_St._John_Bosco_36-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-The_Dreams_of_St._John_Bosco-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-bosconet.aust.com_37-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bosconet.aust.com-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Opposition to Bosco and his work came from various quarters. Traditionalist clergy accused him of stealing the young and old people away from their own parishes. Nationalist politicians, including some clergy, saw his several hundred young men as a recruiting ground for revolution. The Marquis de Cavour, the chief of police in Turin, regarded the open-air catechisms as overtly political and a threat to the state and was highly suspicious of Bosco's support for the powers of the papacy. Bosco was interrogated on several occasions; no charges were made. Closure may have been prevented by orders from the king that Bosco was not to be disturbed.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> Several attempts were also made on Bosco's life, including a near-stabbing, bludgeoning, and a shooting. Early biographers put that down to the growing influence of the <a href="/wiki/Waldensians" title="Waldensians">Waldensians</a> in opposition to Catholic clergy.<sup id="cite_ref-reference_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-reference-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<p>Some of the boys helped by Don Bosco decided to do what he was doing: working in the service of abandoned boys. That was the origin of the Salesian Congregation. Among the first members were Michael Rua, John Cagliero (who later became a Cardinal), and John Baptist Francesca.<sup id="cite_ref-ewtn_30-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ewtn-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> In 1859, Bosco selected the experienced priest Vittorio Alasonatti, 15 seminarians, and one high school boy and formed them into the Society of St. Francis de Sales. That was the nucleus of the Salesians, the religious order that would carry on his work. When the group had its next meeting, it voted on the admission of Joseph Rossi as a lay member, the first Salesian brother. The Salesian Congregation was divided into priests, seminarians, and coadjutors (the lay brothers).
</p><p>Bosco then worked with <a href="/wiki/Mary_Mazzarello" class="mw-redirect" title="Mary Mazzarello">Mary Mazzarello</a> and a group of girls in the hill town of <a href="/wiki/Mornese" title="Mornese">Mornese</a>. In 1871, he founded a group of religious sisters to do for girls what the Salesians were doing for boys. They were called the Daughters of Mary Help of Christians. In 1874, he founded yet another group, the Salesian Cooperators, who were mostly lay people who would work for young people like the Daughters and the Salesians but would not join a religious order. The first Salesians departed for <a href="/wiki/Argentina" title="Argentina">Argentina</a> in 1875. After his ordination, Bosco himself would have become a missionary if his director, <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Cafasso" title="Joseph Cafasso">Joseph Cafasso</a>, had not opposed the idea. Bosco nevertheless eagerly read the Italian edition of the Annals of the Propagation of the Faith and used this magazine to illustrate his <i>Cattolico Provveduto</i> (1853) and his <i>Month of May</i> booklets (1858).
</p><p>When Bosco founded the Salesian Society, the thought of the missions still obsessed him but completely lacked the financial means. Bosco stated that in another dream,<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2012)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> he was on a vast plain inhabited by primitive peoples, who spent their time hunting or fighting among themselves or against soldiers in European uniforms. Along came a band of missionaries but they were all massacred. A second group appeared, which Bosco at once recognized as Salesians. Astonished, he witnessed an unexpected change when the fierce savages laid down their arms and listened to the missionaries. It seems the dream made a great impression on Bosco because he tried hard to identify the men and the country of the dream, and for three years he collected information about different countries. A request from Argentina turned him towards the Indians of <a href="/wiki/Patagonia" title="Patagonia">Patagonia</a>, and a study of its people convinced him that the country and its inhabitants were the ones that he had seen in his dream. In late 1874, Bosco received letters from the Argentine consul at Savona requesting that he accept an Italian parish in Buenos Aires and a school for boys at San Nicolas de Los Arroyos.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2012)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Bosco regarded it as a sign of Providence and started to prepare a mission. Adopting a way of <a href="/wiki/Evangelization" class="mw-redirect" title="Evangelization">evangelization</a> that would not expose his missionaries to wild, uncivilized tribes, he proposed setting up bases in safe locations at which missionary efforts were to be launched. Negotiations started after Archbishop Aneiros of <a href="/wiki/Buenos_Aires" title="Buenos Aires">Buenos Aires</a> had indicated that he would be glad to receive the Salesians. In a ceremony held on 29 January 1875, Bosco was able to convey the great news to the oratory. On 5 February, he announced the fact in a circular letter to all Salesians asking volunteers to apply in writing. He proposed for the first missionary departure to start in October. There were many volunteers.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2012)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Salesian_Preventive_System_and_other_works"><i>Salesian Preventive System</i> and other works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Bosco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Salesian Preventive System and other works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Salesian_Preventive_System" title="Salesian Preventive System">Salesian Preventive System</a></div>
<p>In the years that Bosco had spent running his oratory and giving spiritual and practical instruction to the boys he had housed there, he relied on a different approach to education and general instruction, which he believed to be superior to traditional educational methods, such as <a href="/wiki/School_discipline" title="School discipline">school discipline</a>, which he labelled as a repressive system of education. On 12 March 1877, Bosco gave an opening address on the systems of education during the day for the opening of the St. Peter's Youth Center in the new quarters of the Patronage de Saint Pierre in <a href="/wiki/Nice" title="Nice">Nice</a> in which he first mentioned the term "Preventive System".<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Upon his return to <a href="/wiki/Turin" title="Turin">Turin</a>, Bosco wrote down the address as a polished essay under the title <i>The Preventive System in the Education of the Youth</i>, which was published in 1877 and in which he included in the initial draft of the Rule for the Salesian Order.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> It espoused the values of reason, religion, and loving kindness with a goal of producing "good Christians and honest citizens".<sup id="cite_ref-The_Salesian_Bulletin_in_the_World_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-The_Salesian_Bulletin_in_the_World-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> That was the only attempt that Bosco made at a systematic exposition of his educational system. Though the idea itself was not innovative by any means, Bosco having drawn the inspiration for his system through the contemporary criticisms of the punitive and outdated educational systems prevalent in Europe during his time, and he was one of the first to combat it and to put his criticisms into practice.<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Though Bosco's written works were little known outside of his own order and the subscribers of his <i><a href="/wiki/Salesian_Bulletin" title="Salesian Bulletin">Salesian Bulletin</a></i>, which he founded in August 1877, he wrote frequently and voluminously. Though Don Bosco was described as more of a man of action than a scholar,<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> he was an exceptional historian. He penned the 1881 <i>A Compendium of Italian History from the Fall of the Roman Empire</i>, which was translated and continued to the present by <a href="/wiki/John_Daniel_Morell" title="John Daniel Morell">John Daniel Morell</a> and was noted by scholars for its cultural importance on the knowledge base of ancient to modern civilization.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> He was also a skilled biographer. His two most well-known biographies were on his mentor, <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Cafasso" title="Joseph Cafasso">Joseph Cafasso</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> and one of his students, <a href="/wiki/Dominic_Savio" title="Dominic Savio">Dominic Savio</a>, which would later be instrumental in his <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_canonization">Death and canonization</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Bosco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Death and canonization">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Saint Bosco died on 31 January 1888. His funeral was attended by thousands. The Archdiocese of Turin investigated, and witnesses were called to determine if Bosco was worthy to be declared a saint. The Salesians, Daughters, and Cooperators gave supportive testimonies. <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XI" title="Pope Pius XI">Pope Pius XI</a> had known Bosco and pushed the cause forward. Pius XI <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> Bosco on 2 June 1929 and canonised him on Easter Sunday (1 April) of 1934, when he was given the title of "Father and Teacher of Youth". <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII" title="Pope Pius XII">Pope Pius XII</a> proclaimed him <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of Catholic publishers in 1949. His repertoire of writings and publications consists of over 220 titles collected in 38 volumes.<sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> They were printed at his own peerless paper-to-print workshop, where boys learned the art of printing and publishing in view of future employment.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bosco had been popularly known as the patron saint of illusionists. On 30 January 2002, <a href="/wiki/Silvio_Mantelli" title="Silvio Mantelli">Silvio Mantelli</a> petitioned <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> to declare Bosco formally to the patron of stage <a href="/wiki/Magic_(illusion)" title="Magic (illusion)">magicians</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-magic_51-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-magic-51">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> Catholic stage magicians who practice <a href="/wiki/Gospel_magic" title="Gospel magic">gospel magic</a> venerate Bosco by offering free magic shows to underprivileged children on his feast day.<sup id="cite_ref-magic_51-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-magic-51">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> Bosco's work was carried on by an early pupil, collaborator, and companion, <a href="/wiki/Michael_Rua" class="mw-redirect" title="Michael Rua">Michael Rua</a>, who was appointed rector major of the Salesian Society by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIII" title="Pope Leo XIII">Pope Leo XIII</a> in 1888. He is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(observance)" class="mw-redirect" title="Commemoration (observance)">commemoration</a> on 31 January.<sup id="cite_ref-coecalendar_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-coecalendar-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_popular_culture">In popular culture</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Bosco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: In popular culture">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Bosco is the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Bras%C3%ADlia" title="Brasília">Brasília</a>, which he supposedly foresaw in a dream concerning an extraordinary new civilization that would flourish in central Brazil. <a href="/wiki/Don_Bosco_School_(disambiguation)" class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" title="Don Bosco School (disambiguation)">Many educational institutions</a> are named after him, in countries as diverse as Australia, India, the Philippines, Pakistan, and the United States. Several institutions in <a href="/wiki/Engadine,_New_South_Wales" title="Engadine, New South Wales">Engadine, New South Wales</a>, were also named for Bosco, including St John Bosco Parish, St John Bosco Primary School, and <a href="/wiki/St_John_Bosco_College,_Sydney" class="mw-redirect" title="St John Bosco College, Sydney">St John Bosco College</a>.
</p><p>Bosco was the subject of the 1935 biopic <i><a href="/wiki/Don_Bosco_(1935_film)" title="Don Bosco (1935 film)">Don Bosco</a></i>, directed by <a href="/wiki/Goffredo_Alessandrini" title="Goffredo Alessandrini">Goffredo Alessandrini</a>, and was played by the actor <a href="/wiki/Gian_Paolo_Rosmino" class="mw-redirect" title="Gian Paolo Rosmino">Gian Paolo Rosmino</a>. Bosco was also the subject of two Italian movies: <i><a href="/wiki/Don_Bosco_(1988_film)" title="Don Bosco (1988 film)">Don Bosco</a></i> (1988) and <i><a href="/wiki/Saint_John_Bosco:_Mission_to_Love" title="Saint John Bosco: Mission to Love">Saint John Bosco: Mission to Love</a></i> (2004). A <a href="/wiki/Don_Bosco,_Buenos_Aires" title="Don Bosco, Buenos Aires">borough</a> in <a href="/wiki/Quilmes" title="Quilmes">Quilmes</a>, Argentina, is named after him. An Italian church, San Giovanni Bosco, is named after him in <a href="/wiki/Montreal" title="Montreal">Montreal</a>, Canada, in the <a href="/wiki/Ville-%C3%89mard" title="Ville-Émard">Ville-Émard</a> area.
</p>
