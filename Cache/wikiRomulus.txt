<p><b>Romulus</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;r&#39; in &#39;rye&#39;">r</span><span title="/ɒ/: &#39;o&#39; in &#39;body&#39;">ɒ</span><span title="&#39;m&#39; in &#39;my&#39;">m</span><span title="/j/: &#39;y&#39; in &#39;yes&#39;">j</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;l&#39; in &#39;lie&#39;">l</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;s&#39; in &#39;sigh&#39;">s</span></span>/</a></span></span>) was the legendary <a href="/wiki/Foundation_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Foundation of Rome">founder</a> and <a href="/wiki/King_of_Rome" title="King of Rome">first king</a> of <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Rome" title="Ancient Rome">Rome</a>. Various traditions attribute the establishment of many of Rome's oldest legal, political, religious, and social institutions to Romulus and his contemporaries. Although many of these traditions incorporate elements of <a href="/wiki/Folklore" title="Folklore">folklore</a>, and it is not clear to what extent a historical figure underlies the God-like Romulus, the events and institutions ascribed to him were central to the myths surrounding Rome's origins and cultural traditions.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Traditional_account">Traditional account</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Traditional account">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The myths concerning Romulus involve several distinct episodes and figures, including the miraculous birth and youth of Romulus and his twin brother, <a href="/wiki/Remus" class="mw-redirect" title="Remus">Remus</a>; Remus' murder and the founding of Rome; <a href="/wiki/The_Rape_of_the_Sabine_Women" title="The Rape of the Sabine Women">the Rape of the Sabine Women</a>, and the subsequent war with the <a href="/wiki/Sabines" title="Sabines">Sabines</a>; a period of joint rule with <a href="/wiki/Titus_Tatius" title="Titus Tatius">Titus Tatius</a>; the establishment of various Roman institutions; the death or <a href="/wiki/Apotheosis" title="Apotheosis">apotheosis</a> of Romulus, and the succession of <a href="/wiki/Numa_Pompilius" title="Numa Pompilius">Numa Pompilius</a>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Romulus_and_Remus">Romulus and Remus</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Romulus and Remus">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Romulus_and_Remus" title="Romulus and Remus">Romulus and Remus</a></div>
<p>According to <a href="/wiki/Roman_polytheism" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman polytheism">Roman polytheism</a>, Romulus and Remus were the sons of <a href="/wiki/Rhea_Silvia" title="Rhea Silvia">Rhea Silvia</a> by the god <a href="/wiki/Mars_(mythology)" title="Mars (mythology)">Mars</a>.  Their maternal grandfather was <a href="/wiki/Numitor" title="Numitor">Numitor</a>, the rightful king of <a href="/wiki/Alba_Longa" title="Alba Longa">Alba Longa</a>, through whom the twins were descended from both the <a href="/wiki/Troy" title="Troy">Trojan</a> hero <a href="/wiki/Aeneas" title="Aeneas">Aeneas</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Latinus" title="Latinus">Latinus</a>, the king of <a href="/wiki/Latium" title="Latium">Latium</a>.
</p><p>Before the twins' birth, Numitor's throne had been usurped by his brother, <a href="/wiki/Amulius" title="Amulius">Amulius</a>, who murdered Numitor's son or sons, and condemned Rhea Silvia to perpetual virginity by consecrating her a <a href="/wiki/Vestal_virgins" class="mw-redirect" title="Vestal virgins">Vestal</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;i&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Livy_i_3_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Livy_i_3-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> When Rhea became pregnant, she asserted that she had been visited by the god Mars. Amulius imprisoned her, and upon the twins' birth, ordered that they be thrown into the <a href="/wiki/Tiber" title="Tiber">Tiber</a>. But as the river had been swollen by rain, the servants tasked with disposing of the infants could not reach its banks, and so <a href="/wiki/Infant_exposure" title="Infant exposure">exposed</a> the twins beneath a fig tree at the foot of the <a href="/wiki/Palatine_Hill" title="Palatine Hill">Palatine Hill</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Livy_i_3_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Livy_i_3-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the traditional account, a <a href="/w/index.php?title=She-wolf_(Roman_polytheism)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="She-wolf (Roman polytheism) (page does not exist)">she-wolf</a> happened upon the twins, and suckled them until they were found by the king's herdsman, <a href="/wiki/Faustulus" title="Faustulus">Faustulus</a>, and his wife, <a href="/wiki/Acca_Larentia" title="Acca Larentia">Acca Larentia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;ii&#93;</a></sup> The brothers grew to manhood among the shepherds and hill-folk.  After becoming involved in a conflict between the followers of Amulius and those of their grandfather Numitor, Faustulus told them of their origin. With the help of their friends, they lured Amulius into an ambush and killed him, restoring their grandfather to the throne.<sup id="cite_ref-Livy_i_3-6_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Livy_i_3-6-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The princes then set out to establish a city of their own.
</p><p>They returned to the <a href="/wiki/Seven_hills_of_Rome" title="Seven hills of Rome">hills overlooking the Tiber</a>, the site where they had been exposed as infants. They could not agree on which hill should house the new city. When an omen to resolve the controversy failed to provide a clear indication, the conflict escalated and Romulus or one of his followers killed Remus.<sup id="cite_ref-Livy_i_3-6_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Livy_i_3-6-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> In a variant of the legend, the augurs favoured Romulus, who proceeded to plough a square furrow around the Palatine Hill to demarcate the walls of the future city. When Remus derisively leapt over the "walls" to show how inadequate they were against invaders, Romulus struck him down in anger. In another variant, Remus was killed during a melée, along with Faustulus.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Establishment_of_the_city">Establishment of the city</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Establishment of the city">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The founding of Rome was commemorated annually on April 21, with the festival of the <a href="/wiki/Parilia" title="Parilia">Parilia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;iii&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;iv&#93;</a></sup> Romulus' first act was to fortify the Palatine, in the course of which he made a sacrifice to the gods. He laid out the city's boundaries with a furrow that he ploughed, performed another sacrifice, and with his followers set to work building the city itself.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Romulus sought the assent of the people to become their king. With Numitor's help, he addressed them and received their approval. Romulus accepted the crown after he sacrificed and prayed to <a href="/wiki/Jupiter_(mythology)" title="Jupiter (mythology)">Jupiter</a>, and after receiving favourable omens.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Romulus divided the populace into three <a href="/wiki/Roman_tribe" title="Roman tribe">tribes</a>, known as the <i>Ramnes</i>, <i>Titienses</i>, and <i>Luceres</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;v&#93;</a></sup> for taxation and military purposes. Each tribe was presided over by an official known as a <a href="/wiki/Tribune" title="Tribune">tribune</a>, and was further divided into ten <a href="/wiki/Curia" title="Curia">curia</a>, or wards, each presided over by an official known as a <i>curio</i>. Romulus also allotted a portion of land to each ward, for the benefit of the people.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Nothing is known of the manner in which the tribes and curiae were taxed, but for the military levy, each curia was responsible for providing one hundred foot soldiers, a unit known as a <i>century</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;vi&#93;</a></sup> and ten cavalry. Each Romulean tribe thus provided about one thousand infantry, and one century of cavalry; the three hundred cavalry became known as the <a href="/wiki/Celeres" title="Celeres">Celeres</a>, "the swift", and formed the royal bodyguard.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Choosing one hundred men from the leading families, Romulus established the <a href="/wiki/Roman_senate" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman senate">Roman senate</a>. These men he called <i>patres</i>, the city fathers; their descendants came to be known as "<a href="/wiki/Patrician_(ancient_Rome)" title="Patrician (ancient Rome)">patricians</a>", forming one of the two major social classes at Rome. The other class, known as the "<a href="/wiki/Plebs" class="mw-redirect" title="Plebs">plebs</a>" or "plebeians", consisted of the servants, freedmen, fugitives who sought asylum at Rome, those captured in war, and others who were granted Roman citizenship over time.<sup id="cite_ref-Livy,_i._9_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Livy,_i._9-21">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>To encourage the growth of the city, Romulus outlawed infanticide, and established an <a href="/wiki/Asylum_(antiquity)" title="Asylum (antiquity)">asylum</a> for fugitives on the <a href="/wiki/Capitoline_Hill" title="Capitoline Hill">Capitoline Hill</a>. Here freemen and slaves alike could claim protection and seek Roman citizenship.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Rape_of_the_Sabine_Women">The Rape of the Sabine Women</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: The Rape of the Sabine Women">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/The_Rape_of_the_Sabine_Women" title="The Rape of the Sabine Women">The Rape of the Sabine Women</a></div>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_the_Lacus_Curtius" title="Battle of the Lacus Curtius">Battle of the Lacus Curtius</a></div>
<p>The new city was filled with colonists, most of whom were young, unmarried men. While fugitives seeking asylum helped the population grow, single men greatly outnumbered women. With no intermarriage taking place between Rome and neighboring communities, the new city would eventually fail. Romulus sent envoys to neighboring towns, appealing to them to allow intermarriage with Roman citizens, but his overtures were rebuffed. Romulus formulated a plan to acquire women from other settlements. He announced a momentous <a href="/wiki/Consualia" title="Consualia">festival and games</a>, and invited the people of the neighboring cities to attend. Many did, in particular the <a href="/wiki/Sabines" title="Sabines">Sabines</a>, who came in droves. At a prearranged signal, the Romans began to <a href="/wiki/Bride_abduction" class="mw-redirect" title="Bride abduction">snatch and carry off</a> the marriageable women among their guests.<sup id="cite_ref-Livy,_i._9_21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Livy,_i._9-21">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The aggrieved cities prepared for war with Rome, and might have defeated Romulus had they been fully united. But impatient with the preparations of the Sabines, the Latin towns of <a href="/wiki/Caenina_(town)" title="Caenina (town)">Caenina</a>, <a href="/wiki/Crustumerium" title="Crustumerium">Crustumerium</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Antemnae" title="Antemnae">Antemnae</a> took action without their allies. Caenina was the first to attack; its army was swiftly put to flight, and the town taken. After personally defeating and slaying the prince of Caenina in single combat, Romulus stripped him of his armour, becoming the first to claim the <a href="/wiki/Spolia_opima" title="Spolia opima">spolia opima</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Temple_of_Jupiter_Feretrius" title="Temple of Jupiter Feretrius">vowed a temple</a> to <a href="/wiki/Jupiter_(mythology)" title="Jupiter (mythology)">Jupiter Feretrius</a>. Antemnae and Crustumerium were conquered in turn. Some of their people, chiefly the families of the abducted women, were allowed to settle at Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Following the defeat of the Latin towns, the Sabines, under the leadership of <a href="/wiki/Titus_Tatius" title="Titus Tatius">Titus Tatius</a>, marshalled their forces and advanced upon Rome. They gained control of the citadel by bribing <a href="/wiki/Tarpeia" title="Tarpeia">Tarpeia</a>, the daughter of the Roman commander charged with its defense. Without the advantage of the citadel, the Romans were obliged to meet the Sabines on the battlefield. The Sabines advanced from the citadel, and fierce fighting ensued. The nearby <a href="/wiki/Lacus_Curtius" title="Lacus Curtius">Lacus Curtius</a> is said to be named after Mettius Curtius, a Sabine warrior who plunged his horse into its muck to stymie his Roman pursuers as he retreated. At a critical juncture in the fighting, the Romans began to waver in the face of the Sabine advance. Romulus vowed a <a href="/wiki/Temple_of_Jupiter_Stator_(8th_century_BC)" class="mw-redirect" title="Temple of Jupiter Stator (8th century BC)">temple to Jupiter Stator</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;vii&#93;</a></sup> to keep his line from breaking. The bloodshed finally ended when the Sabine women interposed themselves between the two armies, pleading on the one hand with their fathers and brothers, and on the other with their husbands, to set aside their arms and come to terms. The leaders of each side met and made peace. They formed one community, to be jointly ruled by Romulus and Tatius.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Subsequent_events">Subsequent events</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Subsequent events">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The two kings presided over the growing city of Rome for a number of years, before Tatius was slain in a riot at <a href="/wiki/Lavinium" title="Lavinium">Lavinium</a>, where he had gone to make a sacrifice. Shortly before, a group of envoys from <a href="/wiki/Laurentum" title="Laurentum">Laurentum</a> had complained of their treatment by Tatius' kinsmen, and he had decided the matter against the ambassadors. Romulus resisted calls to avenge the Sabine king's death, instead reaffirming the Roman alliance with Lavinium, and perhaps preventing his city from splintering along ethnic lines.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the years following the death of Tatius, Romulus is said to have conquered the city of <a href="/wiki/Fidenae" title="Fidenae">Fidenae</a>, which, alarmed by the rising power of Rome, had begun raiding Roman territory. The Romans lured the Fidenates into an ambush, and routed their army; as they retreated into their city, the Romans followed before the gates could be shut, and captured the town. The <a href="/wiki/Etruscan_civilization" title="Etruscan civilization">Etruscan</a> city of <a href="/wiki/Veii" title="Veii">Veii</a>, nine miles up the Tiber from Rome, also raided Roman territory, foreshadowing that city's role as the chief rival to Roman power over the next three centuries. Romulus defeated Veii's army, but found the city too well defended to besiege, and instead ravaged the countryside.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_succession">Death and succession</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Death and succession">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>After a reign of thirty-seven years,<sup id="cite_ref-:0_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-30">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:1_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-31">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Romulus is said to have disappeared in a <a href="/wiki/Whirlwind" title="Whirlwind">whirlwind</a> during a sudden and violent storm, as he was reviewing his troops on the <a href="/wiki/Campus_Martius" title="Campus Martius">Campus Martius</a>. Livy says that Romulus was either murdered by the senators, torn apart out of jealousy, or was raised to heaven by Mars, god of war. Livy believes the last theory regarding the legendary king's death, as it allows the Romans to believe that the gods are on their side, a reason for them to continue expansion under Romulus' name.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Romulus acquired a cult following, which later became assimilated with the cult of <a href="/wiki/Quirinus" title="Quirinus">Quirinus</a>, perhaps originally the indigenous god of the Sabine population. As the Sabines had not had a king of their own since the death of Titus Tatius, the next king of Rome, <a href="/wiki/Numa_Pompilius" title="Numa Pompilius">Numa Pompilius</a>, was chosen from among the Sabines.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Primary_sources">Primary sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Primary sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Livy" title="Livy">Livy</a>, <a href="/wiki/Dionysius_of_Halicarnassus" title="Dionysius of Halicarnassus">Dionysius</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Plutarch" title="Plutarch">Plutarch</a> rely on <a href="/wiki/Quintus_Fabius_Pictor" title="Quintus Fabius Pictor">Quintus Fabius Pictor</a> as a source. Other significant sources include <a href="/wiki/Ovid" title="Ovid">Ovid</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Fasti_(poem)" title="Fasti (poem)">Fasti</a></i>, and <a href="/wiki/Virgil" title="Virgil">Virgil</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Aeneid" title="Aeneid">Aeneid</a></i>. Greek historians had traditionally claimed that Rome was founded by Greeks, a claim dating back to the <a href="/wiki/Logographer_(history)" title="Logographer (history)">logographer</a> <a href="/wiki/Hellanicus_of_Lesbos" title="Hellanicus of Lesbos">Hellanicus of Lesbos</a> of 5th-century BC, who named Aeneas as its founder. Roman historians connect Romulus to Aeneas by ancestry and mention a previous settlement on the <a href="/wiki/Palatine_Hill" title="Palatine Hill">Palatine Hill</a>, sometimes attributing it to <a href="/wiki/Evander_of_Pallene" class="mw-redirect" title="Evander of Pallene">Evander</a> and his Greek colonists. To the Romans, Rome was the institutions and traditions they credit to their legendary founder, the first "Roman".<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The legend as a whole encapsulates Rome's ideas of itself, its origins and moral values. For modern scholarship, it remains one of the most complex and problematic of all foundation myths. Ancient historians had no doubt that Romulus gave his name to the city. Most modern historians believe his name is a <a href="/wiki/Back-formation" title="Back-formation">back-formation</a> from the name of the city. Roman historians dated the city's foundation to between 758 and 728 BC, and <a href="/wiki/Plutarch" title="Plutarch">Plutarch</a> reports the calculation of <a href="/wiki/Marcus_Terentius_Varro" title="Marcus Terentius Varro">Varro</a>'s friend Tarutius that 771 BC was the birth year of Romulus and his twin.<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> The tradition that gave Romulus a distant ancestor in the semi-divine <a href="/wiki/Troy" title="Troy">Trojan</a> prince <a href="/wiki/Aeneas" title="Aeneas">Aeneas</a> was further embellished, and Romulus was made the direct ancestor of <a href="/wiki/Julio-Claudian_dynasty" title="Julio-Claudian dynasty">Rome's first Imperial dynasty</a>. It is unclear whether or not the tale of Romulus or that of the twins are original elements of the foundation myth, or whether both or either were added.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Romulus-Quirinus">Romulus-Quirinus</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Romulus-Quirinus">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Denarius_C._Memmius_C._F._Romulus.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/Denarius_C._Memmius_C._F._Romulus.jpg/220px-Denarius_C._Memmius_C._F._Romulus.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="108" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/Denarius_C._Memmius_C._F._Romulus.jpg/330px-Denarius_C._Memmius_C._F._Romulus.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/Denarius_C._Memmius_C._F._Romulus.jpg/440px-Denarius_C._Memmius_C._F._Romulus.jpg 2x" data-file-width="500" data-file-height="246" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Denarius_C._Memmius_C._F._Romulus.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Roman Denarius with Romulus as Quirinus</div></div></div>
<p><a href="/wiki/Ennius" title="Ennius">Ennius</a> (fl. 180s BC) refers to Romulus as a divinity in his own right, without reference to <a href="/wiki/Quirinus" title="Quirinus">Quirinus</a>. Roman mythographers identified the latter as an originally Sabine war-deity, and thus to be identified with Roman <a href="/wiki/Mars_(mythology)" title="Mars (mythology)">Mars</a>. <a href="/wiki/Lucilius" class="mw-redirect" title="Lucilius">Lucilius</a> lists Quirinus and Romulus as separate deities, and <a href="/wiki/Varro" class="mw-redirect" title="Varro">Varro</a> accords them different temples. Images of Quirinus showed him as a bearded warrior wielding a spear as a god of war, the embodiment of Roman strength and a deified likeness of the city of Rome. He had a <a href="/wiki/Flamen" title="Flamen">Flamen Maior</a> called the <a href="/wiki/Flamen_Quirinalis" title="Flamen Quirinalis">Flamen Quirinalis</a>, who oversaw his worship and rituals in the ordainment of Roman religion attributed to Romulus's royal successor, <a href="/wiki/Numa_Pompilius" title="Numa Pompilius">Numa Pompilius</a>. There is however no evidence for the conflated Romulus-Quirinus before the 1st century BC.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Ovid" title="Ovid">Ovid</a> in <i><a href="/wiki/Metamorphoses" title="Metamorphoses">Metamorphoses</a></i> XIV (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://ovid.lib.virginia.edu/trans/Metamorph14.htm#487618622">lines 805-828</a>) gives a description of the <a href="/wiki/Apotheosis" title="Apotheosis">deification</a> of Romulus and his wife <a href="/wiki/Hersilia" title="Hersilia">Hersilia</a>, who are given the new names of Quirinus and Hora respectively. Mars, the father of Romulus, is given permission by <a href="/wiki/Jupiter_(mythology)" title="Jupiter (mythology)">Jupiter</a> to bring his son up to Olympus to live with the <a href="/wiki/Twelve_Olympians" title="Twelve Olympians">Olympians</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>One theory regarding this tradition proposes the emergence of two mythical figures from an earlier, singular hero. While Romulus is a founding hero, Quirinus may have been a god of the harvest, and the <a href="/wiki/Fornacalia" title="Fornacalia">Fornacalia</a> a festival celebrating a staple crop (<a href="/wiki/Spelt" title="Spelt">spelt</a>). Through the traditional dates from the tales and the festivals, they are each associated with one another. A legend of the murder of such a founding hero, the burying of the hero's body in the fields (found in some accounts), and a festival associated with that hero, a god of the harvest, and a food staple is a pattern recognized by <a href="/wiki/Cultural_anthropology" title="Cultural anthropology">anthropologists</a>. Called a "<i>dema</i> archetype", this pattern suggests that in a prior tradition, the god and the hero were in fact the same figure and later evolved into two.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Historicity">Historicity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Historicity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Possible historical bases for the broad mythological narrative remain unclear and disputed.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;viii&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Modern scholarship approaches the various known stories of the myth as cumulative elaborations and later interpretations of Roman <a href="/wiki/Founding_myth" class="mw-redirect" title="Founding myth">foundation myth</a>. Particular versions and collations were presented by Roman historians as authoritative, an official history trimmed of contradictions and untidy variants to justify contemporary developments, genealogies and actions in relation to <a href="/wiki/Mos_maiorum" title="Mos maiorum">Roman morality</a>. Other narratives appear to represent popular or folkloric tradition; some of these remain inscrutable in purpose and meaning. <a href="/wiki/T.P._Wiseman" class="mw-redirect" title="T.P. Wiseman">T.P. Wiseman</a> sums up the whole issue as the <a href="/wiki/Mythography" class="mw-redirect" title="Mythography">mythography</a> of an unusually problematic foundation and early history.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Momigliano_42-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Momigliano-42">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The unsavoury elements of many of the myths concerning Romulus have led some scholars to describe them as "shameful" or "disreputable".<sup id="cite_ref-Cornell_60–62_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cornell_60–62-43">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 60–2">&#58;&#8202;60–2&#8202;</span></sup> In antiquity such stories became part of anti-Roman and anti-pagan propaganda. More recently, the historian Hermann Strasburger postulated that these were never part of authentic Roman tradition, but were invented and popularized by Rome's enemies, probably in <a href="/wiki/Magna_Graecia" title="Magna Graecia">Magna Graecia</a>, during the latter part of the fourth century BC.<sup id="cite_ref-Cornell_60–62_43-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cornell_60–62-43">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 60–2">&#58;&#8202;60–2&#8202;</span></sup> This hypothesis is rejected by other scholars, such as Tim Cornell (1995),<sup id="cite_ref-Cornell_60–62_43-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cornell_60–62-43">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> who notes that by this period, the story of Romulus and Remus had already assumed its standard form, and was widely accepted at Rome. Other elements of the Romulus mythos clearly resemble common elements of folk tale and legend, and thus strong evidence that the stories were both old and indigenous.<sup id="cite_ref-Cornell_60–62_43-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cornell_60–62-43">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 60–2">&#58;&#8202;60–2&#8202;</span></sup> Likewise, Momigliano finds Strasburger's argument well-developed, but entirely implausible; if the Romulus myths were an exercise in mockery, they were a signal failure.<sup id="cite_ref-Momigliano_42-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Momigliano-42">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Depictions_in_art">Depictions in art</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Depictions in art">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The episodes which make up the legend, most significantly that of <a href="/wiki/The_rape_of_the_Sabine_women" class="mw-redirect" title="The rape of the Sabine women">the rape of the Sabine women</a>, the tale of <a href="/wiki/Tarpeia" title="Tarpeia">Tarpeia</a>, and the death of Tatius have been a significant part of <a href="/wiki/Classical_studies" class="mw-redirect" title="Classical studies">ancient Roman scholarship</a> and the frequent subject of art, literature and philosophy since ancient times.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Palazzo_Magnani">Palazzo Magnani</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romulus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Palazzo Magnani">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In the late 16th century, the wealthy <a href="/wiki/Magnani" title="Magnani">Magnani</a> family from Bologna commissioned a series of artworks based on the Roman foundation myth. The artists contributing works included a sculpture of Hercules with the infant twins by Gabriele Fiorini, featuring the patron's own face. The most important works were an elaborate series of frescoes collectively known as <i>Histories of the Foundation of Rome</i> by the Brothers Carracci: <a href="/wiki/Ludovico_Carracci" title="Ludovico Carracci">Ludovico</a>, <a href="/wiki/Annibale_Carracci" title="Annibale Carracci">Annibale</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Agostino_Carracci" title="Agostino Carracci">Agostino</a>.
</p>
