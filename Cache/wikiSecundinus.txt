<p><b>Secundinus</b> (fl. 5th century), or <b>Sechnall</b> (<a href="/wiki/Modern_Irish" class="mw-redirect" title="Modern Irish">Modern Irish</a>: <i>Seachnall</i>) as he was known in Irish, was founder and patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Dunshaughlin" title="Dunshaughlin">Domhnach Sechnaill</a>, County Meath, who went down in medieval tradition as a disciple of <a href="/wiki/St_Patrick" class="mw-redirect" title="St Patrick">St Patrick</a> and one of the first <a href="/wiki/Bishops_of_Armagh" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishops of Armagh">bishops of Armagh</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNB_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Historians have suggested, however, that the connection with St Patrick was a later tradition invented by Armagh historians in favour of their patron saint and that Secundinus is more likely to have been a separate missionary, possibly a companion of <a href="/wiki/Palladius_(bishop_of_Ireland)" title="Palladius (bishop of Ireland)">Palladius</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNB_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background_and_sources">Background and sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Secundinus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Background and sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Little is known about the saint and his cult. His foundation is <a href="/wiki/St._Seachnall%27s_Church" title="St. Seachnall&#39;s Church">Domnach Sechnaill</a> ('Church of Sechnall'),<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FO_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FO-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> now Dunshaughlin (County Meath), not far from <a href="/wiki/Hill_of_Tara" title="Hill of Tara">Tara</a>, and to judge by the use of the toponymic element <i>domnach</i> (from Latin <i>dominicum</i>), the church is likely to be early.<sup id="cite_ref-ECI233-4_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ECI233-4-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> T.M. Charles-Edwards suggests that the site may have belonged originally to the province of Leinster rather than Mide, but that the political geography had changed by the 8th century, when much of southern Brega was divided between different septs of the <a href="/wiki/S%C3%ADl_n%C3%81edo_Sl%C3%A1ine" title="Síl nÁedo Sláine">Síl nÁedo Sláine</a>. By that time, Domnach Sechnaill lay in the kingdom of the <a href="/wiki/U%C3%AD_Chernaig" class="mw-redirect" title="Uí Chernaig">Uí Chernaig</a>, close to the royal crannóg seat in <a href="/wiki/Loch_Gabhair" class="mw-redirect" title="Loch Gabhair">Loch nGabor</a>, as did the churches of Trevet and Kilbrew.<sup id="cite_ref-ECI233-4_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ECI233-4-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Linguistic arguments in favour of the early date of the saint's arrival and his foundation have also been advanced with respect to the saint's name in Latin and Irish. The <a href="/wiki/Late_Latin" title="Late Latin">Late Latin</a> name Secundinus was a common one across Latin-speaking parts of Europe. His name was borrowed into the vernacular as Sechnall, according to a pattern for which <a href="/wiki/David_N._Dumville" class="mw-redirect" title="David N. Dumville">David N. Dumville</a> proposes the following stages of development: <i>Secundinus</i> &gt; *<i>Sechundinus</i> &gt; *<i>Sechundīnəs</i> &gt; *<i>Sechundīn</i> &gt; *<i>Sechndən</i> &gt; *<i>Sechnən</i> and finally by the 8/9th century, &gt; *<i>Sechnəl</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> If correct, this pattern lends further credence to a 5th-century floruit of the saint.<sup id="cite_ref-Dumville100_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dumville100-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Traditions about the saint are witnessed by variety of sources, including <a href="/wiki/Irish_annals" title="Irish annals">Irish annals</a>, the <i><a href="/wiki/F%C3%A9lire_%C3%93engusso" class="mw-redirect" title="Félire Óengusso">Félire Óengusso</a></i> and other martyrologies, the <i><a href="/wiki/Tripartite_Life_of_St_Patrick" class="mw-redirect" title="Tripartite Life of St Patrick">Tripartite Life of St Patrick</a></i> and a list of the coarbs of St Patrick. Secundinus is also the ascribed author of an early Latin hymn in praise of St Patrick, known as <i>Audite Omnes Amantes</i> ('Hear ye, All lovers') or the <i>Hymn of Secundinus</i> written in <a href="/wiki/Trochaic_septenarius" title="Trochaic septenarius">trochaic septenarius</a>, the earliest copy of which is found in the late 7th-century <a href="/wiki/Antiphonary_of_Bangor" title="Antiphonary of Bangor">Antiphonary of Bangor</a>. The ascription to Secundinus, whether true or false, is commonplace in medieval sources, occurring as early as in the <i>Félire Óengusso</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and notably appears in the Irish preface preserved in some manuscript copies of the <i>Hymn</i>. This preface adds some biographical detail, including a legend about Sechnall's quarrel and reconciliation with Patrick leading up to the composition of the hymn. A hagiographical <i>Life</i> was written for the saint, but it comes down to us only in a 17th-century manuscript compilation donated by Irish Jesuit <a href="/wiki/Henry_Fitzsimon" title="Henry Fitzsimon">Henry FitzSimon</a> to the <a href="/wiki/Bollandist" title="Bollandist">Bollandists</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNB_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Sharpe375_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Sharpe375-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The manuscript is found in the Bollandist collection of the Royal Library of Brussels under the shelfmark MS 8957–8.<sup id="cite_ref-Sharpe375_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Sharpe375-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Secundinus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1023981488">@media all and (max-width:720px){.mw-parser-output .rquote{width:auto!important;float:none!important}}</style><style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote rquote" style="float: right; width: 33%;"><p><i>Srúaim n-ecnai co n-áni,<br />Sechnall mind ar flathae,<br />ro gab ceol, sóer solad,<br />molad Pátric Machae.</i>
A stream of wisdom with splendour,<br />Sechnall diadem of our lords,<br />has chanted a melody — noble profit! —<br />a praise of Patrick of Armagh</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite>— <i><a href="#FO">Félire Óengusso</a></i> (27 November)</cite></div></blockquote>
<p>The Irish annals report that in 439, bishops Secundinus, <a href="/wiki/Auxilius_of_Ireland" title="Auxilius of Ireland">Auxilius</a> and <a href="/wiki/Iserninus" title="Iserninus">Iserninus</a> arrived in Ireland to the aid of St Patrick.<sup id="cite_ref-TwoPatricks21_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TwoPatricks21-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Muirch%C3%BA" class="mw-redirect" title="Muirchú">Muirchú</a> also tells of the involvement of Auxilius and Iserninus, both possibly from Auxerre, but does not name Secundinus.<sup id="cite_ref-TwoPatricks21_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TwoPatricks21-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Later tradition, which is of uncertain provenance, appears to suggest that Secundinus and Auxilius were of Italian origin. Details to this effect are first given in the Irish preface to the <i>Hymn of Secundinus</i> as found in some manuscript versions of the <i><a href="/wiki/Liber_Hymnorum" class="mw-redirect" title="Liber Hymnorum">Liber Hymnorum</a></i>. It states that Secundinus was a son of Restitutus and St Patrick's sister <a href="/wiki/Darerca_of_Ireland" title="Darerca of Ireland">Dar Ercae</a>; in the <i>Chronicon Scotorum</i> the latter is named Culmana.<sup id="cite_ref-FO_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FO-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The preface cites a stanza by Armagh scholar <a href="/wiki/Eochaid_ua_Flannacain" class="mw-redirect" title="Eochaid ua Flannacain">Eochaid ua Flannacain</a> (d. 1005) to assert that Restitutus belonged to the Lombards of <i>Letha</i>, a place-name which referred to Gaul but was sometimes confused with <a href="/wiki/Latium" title="Latium">Latium</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-TwoPatricks27_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TwoPatricks27-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> In the stanza, Sechnall receives the paternal family name <i>moccu Baird</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Although the presence of Lombards in Italy would be an anachronism, <a href="/wiki/T._F._O%27Rahilly" title="T. F. O&#39;Rahilly">Thomas F. O'Rahilly</a> considers it possible that Secundinus – and perhaps Auxilius, too – came from northern Italy.<sup id="cite_ref-TwoPatricks27_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TwoPatricks27-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Like the saint's own name, Restitutus was a popular Late Latin name in Christian Europe, but in this case there is no way of telling whether Patrician historians were using genuine information or filling in gaps in the saint's genealogical dossier.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Some scholars have suggested that Secundinus preceded Saint Patrick in Ireland. In his lecture <i>The Two Patricks</i>, O'Rahilly argues that Secundinus, possibly a native of northern Italy (see above), was one of three bishops who arrived in Ireland in 439 to assist <a href="/wiki/Palladius_(bishop_of_Ireland)" title="Palladius (bishop of Ireland)">Palladius</a>, whose mission had begun in 431 and who was known in Ireland as <i>Patricius</i> (leading to confusion with the later <a href="/wiki/Saint_Patrick" title="Saint Patrick">Saint Patrick</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-TwoPatricks27_12-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TwoPatricks27-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> In 441 Palladius was recalled to Rome to be examined by the newly elected <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_I" title="Pope Leo I">Pope Leo I</a>, leaving Secundinus in charge of the Church in Ireland. He became known as the first Christian bishop to die on Irish soil.<sup id="cite_ref-TwoPatricks27_12-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TwoPatricks27-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Dumville allows for the possibility that Secundinus participated in the Palladian mission, but is more hesitant.<sup id="cite_ref-Dumville100_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dumville100-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The development of Patrician legend also saw Secundinus becoming gradually more involved in the process whereby the see of Armagh received the relics of Saints Peter and Paul. St Patrick, according to his <i>Tripartite Life</i>, entrusted his see to Secundinus when he went to Rome to obtain the relics, while the preface to the <i>Hymn</i> tells that Patrick had sent him off to obtain them in person.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Secundinus is said to have died in 447 or 448, aged 75.<sup id="cite_ref-TwoPatricks27_12-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TwoPatricks27-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Commemoration">Commemoration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Secundinus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Commemoration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The saint's name was familiar enough in Mide to give rise to a number of derivative personal names, notably <a href="/wiki/M%C3%A1el_Sechnaill" title="Máel Sechnaill">Máel Sechnaill</a> (attested since the 9th century) and later also Gilla Sechnaill.<sup id="cite_ref-Dumville100_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dumville100-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Despite the evidence for a medieval <i>Life</i>, there is little in the sources to suggest that Sechnall was the subject of a flourishing cult during much of the Middle Ages. His feast-day is 27 November.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNB_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
