<p><b>Marcella</b> (325–410) is a <a href="/wiki/Christian_saint" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian saint">saint</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a> and <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Orthodox Church</a>. She was a <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christian</a> ascetic in the Byzantine Era. 
</p><p><i>The Oxford Dictionary of the Christian Church</i> reports similarly, "She suffered bodily ill-treatment at the hands of the Goths when they captured Rome in 410 and died from its effects."<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> She is commemorated on 31 January. 
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Marcella&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>She came from a noble family who lived in a palace on the <a href="/wiki/Aventine_Hill" title="Aventine Hill">Aventine Hill</a>. Growing up in Rome, she was influenced by her pious mother, Albina, an educated woman of wealth and benevolence. Marcella was but a child when the exiled bishop <a href="/wiki/Athanasius_of_Alexandria" title="Athanasius of Alexandria">Athanasius of Alexandria</a> visited.<sup id="cite_ref-Epistolae_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Epistolae-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After her husband's early death, Marcella decided to devote the rest of her life to <a href="/wiki/Charity_(virtue)" class="mw-redirect" title="Charity (virtue)">charity</a>, <a href="/wiki/Prayer" title="Prayer">prayer</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Mortification_of_the_flesh" title="Mortification of the flesh">mortification of the flesh</a>. According to Butler, "Having lost her husband in the seventh month of her marriage, she rejected the suit of Cerealis the consul, uncle of <a href="/wiki/Constantius_Gallus" title="Constantius Gallus">Gallus Cæsar</a>, and resolved to imitate the lives of the ascetics of the East. She abstained from wine and flesh, employed all her time in pious reading, prayer, and visiting the churches of the apostles and martyrs, and never spoke with any man alone."<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>   
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Pammachius" title="Pammachius">Pammachius</a>, a close friend and correspondent of <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>'s, was her cousin.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He was also a cousin of <a href="/wiki/Paula_of_Rome" title="Paula of Rome">Paula of Rome</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Pammachius married Paula's second daughter, <a href="/wiki/Paulina_(wife_of_Pammachius)" title="Paulina (wife of Pammachius)">Paulina</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Bacchus_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bacchus-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Marcella's palatial home became a center of Christian activity. She and her mother Albina formed a group of religious women in their home, inspired by eastern monks. Paula's third daughter, <a href="/wiki/Eustochium" title="Eustochium">Eustochium</a>, was part of this group.  The house is supposed to have stood close to the present site of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Sabina" title="Santa Sabina">Santa Sabina</a> and became a refuge for weary pilgrims and for the poor. An associate of Marcella named <a href="/wiki/Saint_Lea" title="Saint Lea">Lea</a> was also a wealthy widow and supported the house run by Marcella.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 382, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Damasus_I" title="Pope Damasus I">Pope Damasus I</a> called <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> to Rome, where he became the pope's confidential secretary. Damasus arranged lodging for him at Marcella’s hospitality house. Jerome gave readings and lectures to Marcella's community and friends.<sup id="cite_ref-Epistolae_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Epistolae-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> It was at the home of Marcella that Jerome first met Paula.
</p><p>When Paula and her daughter <a href="/wiki/Eustochium" title="Eustochium">Eustochium</a> left Rome for the Holy Land, they asked Marcella to join them, but she chose to remain in Rome to tend to her growing community. She and her student Principia moved from the palace to a smaller house on the Aventine.<sup id="cite_ref-Dunbar_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dunbar-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When the <a href="/wiki/Goths" title="Goths">Goths</a> invaded in 410, she was brutalized. Convinced that she had hidden treasure, which she had long before distributed among the poor, she was scourged and beaten with cudgels. Other soldiers arrived who had "some reverence for holy things". They escorted Marcella and Principia to the church of St. Paul,--one of those which had been named by <a href="/wiki/Alaric_I" title="Alaric I">Alaric</a> as a sanctuary for all who chose to take advantage of it. Exhausted and injured, Marcella died of her wounds shortly thereafter.<sup id="cite_ref-Dunbar_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dunbar-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>  
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Correspondence_from_Jerome">Correspondence from Jerome</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Marcella&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Correspondence from Jerome">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In modern collections of Jerome's letters, we find many letters to Marcella (Letters 23, 25, 26, 29, 34, 127). Almost a third of all the extant letters from Jerome were addressed to women. Thomas Lawler, notes, “Marcella is by far the woman most frequently addressed, quite likely because of her leading position in that celebrated circle of religious-minded women that met at her house on the Aventine.”<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Most of what we know about Marcella is from the letters of Jerome, most famously his letter 127 to Principia.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> It was written on the occasion of Marcella's death, paying tribute to her life and consoling her beloved student. In it, he says the following about his relationship with Marcella:
</p><p><i>As in those days my name was held in some renown as that of a student of the Scriptures, she never came to see me without asking me some questions about them, nor would she rest content at once, but on the contrary would dispute them; this, however, was not for the sake of argument, but to learn by questioning the answers to such objections might, as she saw, be raised. How much virtue and intellect, how much holiness and purity I found in her I am afraid to say, both lest I may exceed the bounds of men's belief and lest I may increase your sorrow by reminding you of the blessings you have lost. This only will I say, that whatever I had gathered together by long study, and by constant meditation made part of my nature, she tasted, she learned and made her own.</i><sup id="cite_ref-:1_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Perhaps because she did not live long after being scourged, she was included in the Roman Martyrology. Her feast day in the west is January 31. <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>'s <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001127.htm">To Principia</a> is a biography of her life.
</p><p>Marcella of Rome is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">honored</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Lesser_Feasts_and_Fasts" class="mw-redirect" title="Lesser Feasts and Fasts">Lesser Feast</a> on the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">liturgical calendar</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church in the United States of America</a><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> on <a href="/wiki/January_31" title="January 31">January 31</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The artwork <i><a href="/wiki/The_Dinner_Party" title="The Dinner Party">The Dinner Party</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/Judy_Chicago" title="Judy Chicago">Judy Chicago</a> features a place setting for Marcella.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> 
</p>
