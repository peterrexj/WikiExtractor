<p><b>Dionysius the Great</b> (<a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Greek language">Ancient Greek</a>: <span lang="grc">Διονύσιος Ἀλεξανδρείας</span>) was the 14th <a href="/wiki/List_of_Patriarchs_of_Alexandria" class="mw-redirect" title="List of Patriarchs of Alexandria">Pope and Patriarch of Alexandria</a> from 28 December 248 until his death on 22 March 264. Most information known about him comes from his large surviving correspondence. Only one original letter survives to this day; the remaining letters are excerpted in the works of <a href="/wiki/Eusebius" title="Eusebius">Eusebius</a>.
</p><p>Called "the Great" by Eusebius, <a href="/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea" title="Basil of Caesarea">Basil of Caesarea</a> and others, he was characterized by the <i><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia" title="Catholic Encyclopedia">Catholic Encyclopedia</a></i> as "undoubtedly, after <a href="/wiki/Cyprian" title="Cyprian">St. Cyprian</a>, the most eminent bishop of the third century... like St. Cyprian, less a great theologian than a great administrator."<sup id="cite_ref-cathen_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathen-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Dionysius_of_Alexandria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Dionysius was born to a wealthy polytheistic family sometime in the late 2nd, or early 3rd century. He spent most of his life reading books and carefully studying the traditions of polytheists. He converted to Christianity at a mature age and discussed his conversion experience with Philemon, a presbyter of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Sixtus_II" title="Pope Sixtus II">Pope Sixtus II</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-cathen_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathen-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Dionysius converted to Christianity when he received a vision sent from God; in it he was commanded to vigorously study the heresies facing the Church so that he could refute them through doctrinal study. After his conversion, he joined the <a href="/wiki/Catechetical_School_of_Alexandria" title="Catechetical School of Alexandria">Catechetical School of Alexandria</a> and was a student of <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pope_Heraclas_of_Alexandria" title="Pope Heraclas of Alexandria">Pope Heraclas</a>. He eventually became leader of the school and presbyter of the church, succeeding Pope Heraclas in 231. Later he became Pope of the church of Alexandria &amp; Patriarch of the See of St. Mark in 248 after the death of Pope Heraclas.<sup id="cite_ref-cathen_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathen-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Work_as_Bishop_of_Alexandria">Work as Bishop of Alexandria</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Dionysius_of_Alexandria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Work as Bishop of Alexandria">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Dionysius was more an able administrator than a great theologian.<sup id="cite_ref-cathen_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathen-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Information on his work as Bishop of Alexandria is found in Dionysius' correspondence with other bishops and clergymen of the third century Catholic Church.  Dionysius’ correspondences included interpretations on the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_Luke" title="Gospel of Luke">Gospel of Luke</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_John" title="Gospel of John">Gospel of John</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Revelation" title="Book of Revelation">Book of Revelation</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (December 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>During 249, a major persecution was carried out in Alexandria by a polytheist mob, and hundreds were assaulted, stoned, burned or cut down on account of their refusal to deny their faith. Dionysius managed to survive this persecution and the civil war that followed. In January 250 the new emperor <a href="/wiki/Decius" title="Decius">Decius</a> issued a decree of <a href="/wiki/Decian_persecution" title="Decian persecution">legal persecution</a>. Out of fear many Christians denied their faith by offering a token polytheist sacrifice, while others attempted to obtain false documents affirming their sacrifice. Others who refused to sacrifice faced public ridicule and shame among their family and friends, and, if found by the authorities, brutal torture and execution. Many fled from the city into the desert, where most succumbed to exposure, hunger, thirst, or attacks by bandits or wild animals.<sup id="cite_ref-college_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-college-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Dionysius himself was pursued by the prefect <a href="/wiki/Aurelius_Appius_Sabinus" class="mw-redirect" title="Aurelius Appius Sabinus">Aurelius Appius Sabinus</a>, who had sent out an assassin to murder him on sight. Dionysius spent three days in hiding before departing on the fourth night of the Decian decree with his servants and other loyal brethren. After a brush with a group of soldiers, he managed to escape with two of his followers, and set up a residence in the Libyan desert until the end of the persecution the following year.<sup id="cite_ref-college_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-college-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He supported <a href="/wiki/Pope_Cornelius" title="Pope Cornelius">Pope Cornelius</a> in the controversy of 251, arising when <a href="/wiki/Novatian" title="Novatian">Novatian</a>, a learned presbyter of the Church at Rome, set up a schismatic church with a rigorist position on the readmittance of Christians who had <a href="/wiki/Lapsi_(Christianity)" title="Lapsi (Christianity)">apostasized during the persecution</a>. In opposition to Novatian's teaching, Dionysius ordered that the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a> should be refused to no one who asked it at the hour of death, even those who had previously lapsed.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 252 an outbreak of plague ravaged Alexandria, and Dionysius, along with other priests and deacons, took it upon themselves to assist the sick and dying.<sup id="cite_ref-college_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-college-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The persecutions subsided somewhat under <a href="/wiki/Trebonianus_Gallus" title="Trebonianus Gallus">Trebonianus Gallus</a>, but were renewed under <a href="/wiki/Valerian_(emperor)" title="Valerian (emperor)">Valerian</a> who replaced Gallus. Dionysius was imprisoned and then exiled. When <a href="/wiki/Gallienus" title="Gallienus">Gallienus</a>, took over the empire he released all the believers who were in prison and brought back those in exile. Gallienus wrote to Dionysius and the bishops a letter to assure their safety in opening the churches.<sup id="cite_ref-coptic_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-coptic-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During the debate with <a href="/wiki/Pope_Stephen_I" title="Pope Stephen I">Pope Stephen</a> and <a href="/wiki/Cyprian" title="Cyprian">Cyprian</a>, Dionysius supported the position of the Roman Bishop but he still supported the autonomy of the African churches.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Dionysius_of_Alexandria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea" title="Basil of Caesarea">Basil of Caesarea</a> writes to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Damasus_I" title="Pope Damasus I">Pope Damasus I</a> about aid sent by Dionysius, to the church at <a href="/wiki/Caesarea" title="Caesarea">Caesarea</a>. This correspondence is cited by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_IX" title="Pope Pius IX">Pope Pius IX</a> in his encyclical <i><a href="/wiki/Praedecessores_nostros" title="Praedecessores nostros">Praedecessores Nostros</a> (On Aid For Ireland)</i> of 25 March 1847.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
