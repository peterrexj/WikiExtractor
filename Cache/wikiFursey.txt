<p><b>Saint Fursey</b> (also known as <b>Fursa</b>,<b> Fursy</b>,<b> Forseus</b>, and<b> Furseus</b>: died 650) was an <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">Irish monk</a> who did much to establish <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a> throughout the <a href="/wiki/British_Isles" title="British Isles">British Isles</a> and particularly in <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">East Anglia</a>. He reportedly experienced angelic <a href="/wiki/Vision_(spirtuality)" class="mw-redirect" title="Vision (spirtuality)">visions</a> of the afterlife. Fursey is one of the <a href="/wiki/Four_Comely_Saints" title="Four Comely Saints">Four Comely Saints</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Fursey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was born in the region of modern-day <a href="/wiki/Connacht" title="Connacht">Connacht</a> supposedly the son of Fintan and grandson of Finlog, pagan king of the area.  His mother was Gelges, the Christian daughter of Aed-Finn, king of <a href="/wiki/Connacht" title="Connacht">Connacht</a>. He was born probably amongst the Hy-Bruin, and was baptised by St <a href="/wiki/Brendan_the_Navigator" title="Brendan the Navigator">Brendan</a> the Traveller, his father's uncle, who then ruled a monastery in the Island of Oirbsen, now called <a href="/wiki/Inchagoill" title="Inchagoill">Inisquin</a> in <a href="/wiki/Lough_Corrib" title="Lough Corrib">Lough Corrib</a>. He was educated by St Brendan's monks, and when he became of the proper age he was inducted into the monastery at Inisquin (near <a href="/wiki/Galway" title="Galway">Galway</a>), under the Abbot St Meldan, his "soul-friend" (<i>anam-chura</i>), where he devoted himself to religious life. His great sanctity was early discerned, and there is a legend that here, through his prayers, twin children of a chieftain related to King Brendinus were raised from the dead.<sup id="cite_ref-mulcahy_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mulcahy-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He built his own monastery at <a href="/wiki/Killursa" title="Killursa">Killursa</a> outside the town of <a href="/wiki/Headford" title="Headford">Headford</a> in modern <a href="/wiki/County_Galway" title="County Galway">Co. Galway</a> and he became the patron saint of the Parish of Headford.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Visions">Visions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Fursey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Visions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was said to have been something of an ascetic, wearing thin clothing year round.  Aspirants came in numbers to place themselves under his rule, but he wished to secure also some of his relatives for the new monastery.  For this purpose he set out with some monks for Munster, but on coming near his father's home he was seized with an apparently mortal illness.  He fell into a trance from the ninth hour of the day to cock-crow, and while in this state received the first of the ecstatic visions which have made him famous in medieval literature.<sup id="cite_ref-mulcahy_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mulcahy-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In this vision were revealed to him the state of man in sin, the beauty of virtue. He heard the angelic choirs singing "the saints shall go from virtue to virtue, the God of Gods will appear in Sion." An injunction was laid on him by the two angels who appeared to restore him to his body to become a more zealous labourer for the Lord. Three nights later, the ecstasy was renewed. He was taken to the heavens by three angels who contended six times with demons for his soul. He saw the fires of <a href="/wiki/Hell" title="Hell">hell</a>, the strife of demons, and then heard the angel hosts sing in four choirs "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God of hosts." Among the spirits of those just made perfect he recognized Saints <a href="/wiki/Meldan" title="Meldan">Meldan</a> and <a href="/wiki/Beoan" class="mw-redirect" title="Beoan">Beoan</a>. They entertained him with much spiritual instruction concerning the duties of ecclesiastics and monks, the dreadful effects of pride and disobedience, and the heinousness of spiritual and internal sins. They also predicted famine and pestilence. As he returned through the fire the demon hurled a tortured sinner at him, burning him, and the angel of the Lord said to him, "Because thou didst receive the mantle of this man when dying in his sin the fire consuming him hath scarred thy body also." Fursey's body bore the mark from that day forward. Exactly twelve months later he received a third vision. This time, the angel remained with him a whole day, instructed him for his preaching, and prescribed for him twelve years of apostolic labour. This he faithfully fulfilled in Ireland, and then stripping himself of all earthly goods he retired for a time to a small island in the ocean.<sup id="cite_ref-mulcahy_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mulcahy-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>  
</p><p>After some years he founded a monastery at Rathmat on the shore of <a href="/wiki/Lough_Corrib" title="Lough Corrib">Lough Corrib</a> which Colgan identifies as Killursa, in the <a href="/wiki/Deanery" title="Deanery">deanery</a> of <a href="/wiki/Annaghdown" title="Annaghdown">Annadown</a>. His brothers <a href="/wiki/Foillan" title="Foillan">Foillan</a> and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Ultan" title="Saint Ultan">Ultan</a> then joined the community at Rathmat, but Fursey seems to have renounced the administration of the monastery and to have devoted himself to preaching throughout the land, frequently exorcising evil spirits.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mission_to_East_Anglia">Mission to East Anglia</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Fursey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Mission to East Anglia">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Fursey was the first recorded Irish missionary to Anglo-Saxon England. He arrived in East Anglia with his brothers, Foillan and Ultan, during the 630s shortly before St Aidan founded his monastery on Holy Island.<sup id="cite_ref-norwich_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwich-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The conversion of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">Kingdom of East Anglia</a> to Christianity began under <a href="/wiki/Raedwald_of_East_Anglia" class="mw-redirect" title="Raedwald of East Anglia">Raedwald</a>, but halted with the martyrdom of Raedwald's successor, his son <a href="/wiki/Eorpwald_of_East_Anglia" title="Eorpwald of East Anglia">Eorpwald</a>. <a href="/wiki/Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia" title="Sigeberht of East Anglia">Sigeberht of East Anglia</a> was already Christian when he took the throne around 630. By 633, <a href="/wiki/Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia" title="Sigeberht of East Anglia">Sigeberht of East Anglia</a> had established the first East Anglian bishopric at <a href="/wiki/Dommoc" title="Dommoc">Dommoc</a> and appointed a Burgundian Bishop named Felix.<sup id="cite_ref-norwich_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwich-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Fursey arrived with his brothers Foillan and Ultan, as well as other brethren, bearing the relics of Saints Meldan and Beoan, he had been welcomed by the king, who gave him land to establish an abbey at <i><a href="/wiki/Cnobheresburg" title="Cnobheresburg">Cnobheresburg</a></i>, where there was an abandoned Roman fort, traditionally identified with <a href="/wiki/Burgh_Castle" title="Burgh Castle">Burgh Castle</a> in <a href="/wiki/Norfolk" title="Norfolk">Norfolk</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Here he laboured for some years converting the Picts and Saxons. After Sigeberht was slain by an army led by <a href="/wiki/Penda_of_Mercia" title="Penda of Mercia">Penda of Mercia</a>, it is recorded that his successor King <a href="/wiki/Anna_of_East_Anglia" title="Anna of East Anglia">Anna of East Anglia</a>, and his nobles, further endowed the monastery at Cnobheresburg. Three miracles are recorded of Fursey's life in this monastery. He then retired for a year to live with Ultan the life of an <a href="/wiki/Anchorite" title="Anchorite">anchorite</a>. However, as great numbers continued to visit him, and as war threatened in East Anglia, he left Foillan as <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> and proceeded to Lagny, in France around 644.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mission_in_Neustria">Mission in Neustria</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Fursey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Mission in Neustria">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He arrived in <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a> in 648. Passing through <a href="/wiki/Ponthieu" title="Ponthieu">Ponthieu</a>, in a village near <a href="/wiki/M%C3%A9zerolles" title="Mézerolles">Mézerolles</a> he found grief and lamentation on all sides, for the only son of Duke Hayson, the lord of that area, was dead. At the prayer of Fursey the body was restored. Pursuing his journey to <a href="/wiki/Neustria" title="Neustria">Neustria</a> he cured many infirmities on the way. He converted a robber, who had attacked the monks in a wood near <a href="/wiki/Corbie" title="Corbie">Corbie</a>, and his family through miracles. He also cured the inhospitable worldling Ermelinda, who had refused to harbour the weary travellers. His fame preceded him to <a href="/wiki/P%C3%A9ronne,_Somme" title="Péronne, Somme">Péronne</a>, where he was joyfully received by <a href="/wiki/Erchinoald" title="Erchinoald">Erchinoald</a>, and through his prayers obtained the reprieve of six criminals. He was offered any site in the king's dominions for a monastery. He selected Latiniacum (<a href="/wiki/Lagny-sur-Marne" title="Lagny-sur-Marne">Lagny</a>), close to <a href="/wiki/Chelles,_Seine-et-Marne" title="Chelles, Seine-et-Marne">Chelles</a> and about six miles from <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a>, a spot beside the <a href="/wiki/Marne_(river)" title="Marne (river)">Marne</a>, at that time covered with shady woods and abounding in fruitful vineyards. Here he built his monastery and three chapels, one dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">Jesus Christ the Saviour</a>, one to <a href="/wiki/Saint_Peter" title="Saint Peter">St Peter</a>, and the third, an unpretending structure, was later dedicated to St Fursey himself. Many of his Irish countrymen were attracted to his rule at Lagny, including Emilian, Eloquius, Mombulus, Adalgisius, Etto, Bertuin, Fredegand, Lactan, and Malguil.
</p><p>His journeys continued and many churches in Picardy are dedicated to him.<sup id="cite_ref-pilgrims_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pilgrims-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He received some premonitions of his end, and set out to visit his brothers Foillan and Ultan who had by this time recruited the scattered monks of Cnobheresburg and re-established that monastery.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Fursey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He died about 650 at <a href="/wiki/M%C3%A9zerolles" title="Mézerolles">Mézerolles</a> while on a journey. The village was for some time called Forsheim, which translated as the house of Fursey. He was buried in a church (built specially by Earconwald) in Péronne which has claimed him as patron ever since.<sup id="cite_ref-pilgrims_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pilgrims-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Many unusual events attended the transmission of his remains, and his body was eventually buried in the <a href="/wiki/Portico" title="Portico">portico</a> of the church of St Peter where Fursey had earlier placed the relics of Saints Meldan and Beoan. His body lay unburied for thirty days pending the dedication of the church, and was during that time visited by pilgrims from all parts, incorrupt and emitting a <a href="/wiki/Odour_of_Sanctity" class="mw-redirect" title="Odour of Sanctity">sweet odour</a>. At the end of that time, it was buried near the altar of the church. Four years later, on 9 February, his remains were moved from their earlier location by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Eligius" title="Saint Eligius">Saint Eligius</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Noyon" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Noyon">Bishop of Noyon</a>, and Cuthbert, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Cambrai" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Cambrai">Bishop of Cambrai</a>, to a new chapel specifically built to hold the remains to the east of the main altar. The city would later become a great centre of devotion to him.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconography_and_veneration">Iconography and veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Fursey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Iconography and veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In art St Fursey is represented with two oxen at his feet in commemoration of the prodigy by which, according to legend, Erkinoald's claim to his body was made good; or he is represented striking water from the soil at Lagny with the point of his staff; or beholding a vision of angels, or gazing at the flames of <a href="/wiki/Purgatory" title="Purgatory">purgatory</a> and hell.  It is disputed whether he was a bishop; he may have been a <a href="/wiki/Chorepiscopus" class="mw-redirect" title="Chorepiscopus">chorepiscopus</a>.  A litany attributed to him is among the manuscripts in <a href="/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin" title="Trinity College Dublin">Trinity College Dublin</a>. An Irish prophecy is attributed to him by Harris. His feast day is 16 January.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_popular_culture">In popular culture</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Fursey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: In popular culture">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<ul><li><a href="/wiki/James_Joyce" title="James Joyce">James Joyce</a> mentions S. Fursa in his book <i><a href="/wiki/Ulysses_(novel)" title="Ulysses (novel)">Ulysses</a></i>, among a list of mostly Irish heroes and heroines.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>The Unfortunate Fursey</i>, a popular comic novel by <a href="/wiki/Mervyn_Wall" title="Mervyn Wall">Mervyn Wall</a> describes the saint as a hapless poor soul tormented by visions of buxom women of a type that could only be imagined by the sex-starved Irish ecclesiastics, and bargaining with demons to escape the torments of religion.</li></ul>

The inaugural Fursey Lecture was given by Michelle Brown in 2000. She gathers together the current state of knowledge about Fursey, following him from Ireland - to become the first Irish leader in the English church - to France, where he died; he became the object of veneration shortly afterwards. Brown reviews the sources for our knowledge of Fursey, both textual and from place-name studies. For Brown, Fursey represents 'charity and a sense of compassion', and is an ideal model for 'the ecumenical celebration of the Christian faith'.
