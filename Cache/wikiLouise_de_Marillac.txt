<p><b>Louise de Marillac <span class="noexcerpt nowraplinks" style="font-size:85%; font-weight:normal;"><a href="/wiki/Daughters_of_Charity_of_Saint_Vincent_de_Paul" title="Daughters of Charity of Saint Vincent de Paul">D.C.</a></span></b>, also known as <b>Louise Le Gras</b>, (August 12, 1591 &#8211; March 15, 1660) was the co-founder, with <a href="/wiki/Vincent_de_Paul" title="Vincent de Paul">Vincent de Paul</a>, of the <a href="/wiki/Daughters_of_Charity_of_Saint_Vincent_de_Paul" title="Daughters of Charity of Saint Vincent de Paul">Daughters of Charity</a>. She is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church in the United States of America</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Louise_de_Marillac&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Louise de Marillac was born <a href="/wiki/Out_of_wedlock" class="mw-redirect" title="Out of wedlock">out of wedlock</a> on August 12, 1592<sup id="cite_ref-pittsburgh_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pittsburgh-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> near <a href="/wiki/Le_Meux" title="Le Meux">Le Meux</a>, now in the <a href="/wiki/Departments_of_France" title="Departments of France">department</a> of <a href="/wiki/Oise" title="Oise">Oise</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Picardy" title="Picardy">Picardy</a>. She never knew her mother. Louis de Marillac, Lord of Ferrires (1556-1604),<sup id="cite_ref-glass_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-glass-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> claimed her as his natural daughter yet not his legal heir. Louis was a member of the prominent de Marillac family and was a widower at the time of Louise's birth. Her uncle, Michel de Marillac, was a major figure in the court of Queen <a href="/wiki/Marie_de%27_Medici" title="Marie de&#39; Medici">Marie de' Medici</a> and, though Louise was not a member of the Queen's court, she lived and worked among the French aristocracy.  When her father married his new wife, Antoinette Le Camus, she refused to accept Louise as part of their family. Thus Louise grew up amid the affluent society of Paris, but without a stable home life. Nevertheless, she was cared for and received an excellent education at the royal monastery of <a href="/wiki/Poissy" title="Poissy">Poissy</a> near <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a>, where her aunt was a <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican</a> <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nun</a>.
</p><p>Louise remained at Poissy until her father's death, when she was twelve years old. She then stayed with a devout spinster, from whom she learned household management skills as well as the secrets of herbal medicine.<sup id="cite_ref-famvin_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-famvin-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Around the age of fifteen, Louise felt drawn to the <a href="/wiki/Enclosed_religious_order" class="mw-redirect" title="Enclosed religious order">cloistered</a> life. She later made application to the <a href="/wiki/Poor_Clares" title="Poor Clares">Capuchin nuns</a> in Paris but was refused admission. It is not clear if her refusal was for her continual poor health or other reasons, but her spiritual director assured her that God had "other plans" for her.
</p><p>Devastated by this refusal, Louise was at a loss as to her next step. When she was 22, her family convinced her that marriage was the best alternative. Her uncle arranged for her to marry Antoine Le Gras, secretary to Queen Marie. Antoine was an ambitious young man who seemed destined for great accomplishments. Louise and Antoine were wed in the fashionable <a href="/wiki/St-Gervais-et-St-Protais_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="St-Gervais-et-St-Protais Church">Church of St. Gervaise</a> on February 5, 1613. In October, the couple had their only child, Michel. Louise grew to love Antoine and was an attentive mother to their son. Along with being devoted to her family, Louise was also active in ministry in her parish. She had a leading role in the Ladies of Charity, an organization of wealthy women dedicated to assisting those suffering from poverty and disease.<sup id="cite_ref-famvin_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-famvin-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Family_and_personal_troubles">Family and personal troubles</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Louise_de_Marillac&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Family and personal troubles">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>During civil unrest, her two uncles who held high rank within the government were imprisoned. One was publicly executed, and the other died in prison. Around 1621, Antoine contracted a chronic illness and eventually became bedridden. Louise nursed and cared for him and their child. In 1623, when illness was wasting Antoine, depression was overcoming Louise<sup id="cite_ref-famvin_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-famvin-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> In addition, she suffered for years with internal doubt and guilt for having not pursued the religious calling she had felt as a young woman. She was fortunate to have a wise and sympathetic counsellor, <a href="/wiki/Francis_de_Sales" title="Francis de Sales">Francis de Sales</a>, then in Paris,<sup id="cite_ref-glass_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-glass-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and then his friend, the bishop of <a href="/wiki/Belley" title="Belley">Belley</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Decision_on_life">Decision on life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Louise_de_Marillac&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Decision on life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1623, at 32, she wrote,
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>On the feast of <a href="/wiki/Pentecost" title="Pentecost">Pentecost</a> during Holy <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">Mass</a> or while I was praying in the church, my mind was completely freed of all doubt. I was advised that I should remain with my husband and that the time would come when I would be in the position to make vows of poverty, chastity and obedience and that I would be in a small community where others would do the same...I felt that it was God who was teaching me these things and that, believing there is a God, I should not doubt the rest.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>She vowed not to remarry if her husband died before her.<sup id="cite_ref-glass_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-glass-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> She also believed that she had received the insight that she would be guided to a new <a href="/wiki/Spiritual_director" class="mw-redirect" title="Spiritual director">spiritual director</a> whose face she was shown. When she happened to meet <a href="/wiki/Vincent_de_Paul" title="Vincent de Paul">Vincent de Paul</a>, she recognized him as the priest from her vision.<sup id="cite_ref-famvin_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-famvin-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Three years after this experience, Antoine died. Being a woman of energy, intelligence, determination and devotion, Louise wrote her own "Rule of Life in the World" that detailed a structure for her day. Time was set aside for reciting the <a href="/wiki/Little_Office_of_Our_Lady" class="mw-redirect" title="Little Office of Our Lady">Little Office of the Blessed Virgin Mary</a>, attending Mass, receiving <a href="/wiki/Holy_Communion" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Communion">Holy Communion</a>, <a href="/wiki/Meditation" title="Meditation">meditation</a>, spiritual reading, <a href="/wiki/Fasting" title="Fasting">fasting</a>, penance, reciting the <a href="/wiki/Rosary" title="Rosary">rosary</a> and special prayers. Still, Louise managed to find time to maintain her household, entertain guests and nurture Michel, her 13-year-old son, with <a href="/wiki/Special_needs" title="Special needs">special needs</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Meeting_Vincent_de_Paul">Meeting Vincent de Paul</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Louise_de_Marillac&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Meeting Vincent de Paul">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Paris_Saint-Laurent_Glasfenster45.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ad/Paris_Saint-Laurent_Glasfenster45.JPG/220px-Paris_Saint-Laurent_Glasfenster45.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ad/Paris_Saint-Laurent_Glasfenster45.JPG/330px-Paris_Saint-Laurent_Glasfenster45.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ad/Paris_Saint-Laurent_Glasfenster45.JPG/440px-Paris_Saint-Laurent_Glasfenster45.JPG 2x" data-file-width="3888" data-file-height="2592" /></a><figcaption>Vincent de Paul and Louise de Marillac</figcaption></figure>
<p>Antoine died in 1625. Widowed and lacking financial means, she had to move. Vincent lived near her new dwelling.<sup id="cite_ref-dc_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dc-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> At first, he was reluctant to be her confessor, as he was busy with his Confraternities of Charity. Members were aristocratic ladies of charity, who were helping him nurse the poor and look after neglected children, a real need of the day, but the ladies were busy with many of their own concerns and duties. His work needed many more helpers, especially ones who were peasants themselves and so would be closer to the poor. He also needed someone who could teach and organize them.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Over the next four years, Vincent and Louise often met and communicated by letters. Vincent guided Louise to  a greater balance in a life of moderation, peace and calm. In 1629, Vincent invited Louise to become involved in his work with the Confraternities of Charity.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> She found great success in these endeavors. Then, in 1632, Louise made a <a href="/wiki/Retreat_(spiritual)" title="Retreat (spiritual)">spiritual retreat</a>. Her intuition led her to understand that it was time to intensify her ministry with poor and needy persons. Louise, now forty-two years old, communicated this objective to Monsieur Vincent.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Company_of_the_Daughters_of_Charity_of_St._Vincent_de_Paul">Company of the Daughters of Charity of St. Vincent de Paul</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Louise_de_Marillac&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Company of the Daughters of Charity of St. Vincent de Paul">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Daughters_of_Charity_of_Saint_Vincent_de_Paul" title="Daughters of Charity of Saint Vincent de Paul">Daughters of Charity of Saint Vincent de Paul</a></div>
<p>In 17th-century France, the charitable care of the poor was completely unorganized. The Ladies of Charity, founded by Vincent years earlier, provided some care and monetary resources, but it was far from enough. They had the funds to aid poor people, but they did not have the time or temperament to live a life of service among the poor.
</p><p>Vincent and Louise realized that direct service of the poor was not easy for the nobility or the bourgeoisie because of <a href="/wiki/Social_class" title="Social class">social class</a>. The women took meals, distributed clothing and gave care and comfort. They visited the slums dressed in beautiful dresses next to people considered to be peasants. The tension, between the ideal of service and social constraints, was real. Besides, the families of the ladies often opposed the works.<sup id="cite_ref-dc_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dc-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> It soon became clear that many of the ladies were unfit to cope with the actual conditions.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:S%C5%93ur_Jean_Gabriel_Batke.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/S%C5%93ur_Jean_Gabriel_Batke.jpg/220px-S%C5%93ur_Jean_Gabriel_Batke.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="228" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/S%C5%93ur_Jean_Gabriel_Batke.jpg/330px-S%C5%93ur_Jean_Gabriel_Batke.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/S%C5%93ur_Jean_Gabriel_Batke.jpg/440px-S%C5%93ur_Jean_Gabriel_Batke.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1541" data-file-height="1595" /></a><figcaption>Until 1964, the traditional <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">religious habit</a> included a large, starched <i>cornette</i>.</figcaption></figure>
<p>While the aristocratic ladies were better suited to the work of raising money and dealing with correspondence, the practical work of nursing the poor in their own homes, and caring for neglected children was best accomplished by women of a similar social status to those served.<sup id="cite_ref-oxford_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oxford-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The need of organization in work for the poor suggested to de Paul the forming of a confraternity among the women of his parish in <a href="/wiki/Ch%C3%A2tillon-les-Dombes" class="mw-redirect" title="Châtillon-les-Dombes">Châtillon-les-Dombes</a>. It was so successful that it spread from the rural districts to Paris, where noble ladies often found it hard to give personal care to the needs of the poor. The majority sent their servants to minister to those in need, but often, the work was considered unimportant. Vincent de Paul remedied it by referring young women who inquired about serving persons in need to go to Paris and devote themselves to the ministry under the direction of the Ladies of Charity. These young girls formed the nucleus of the <a href="/wiki/Daughters_of_Charity_of_St._Vincent_de_Paul" class="mw-redirect" title="Daughters of Charity of St. Vincent de Paul">Daughters of Charity of St. Vincent de Paul</a>.
</p><p>Louise found the help she needed in young, humble country women, who had the energy and the proper attitude to deal with people weighed down by destitution and suffering. She began working with a group of them and saw a need for common life and formation. Consequently, she invited four country girls to live in her home in the Rue des Fosses‐Saint‐Victor and began training them to care for those in need.<sup id="cite_ref-randolph_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-randolph-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Mobility was a major innovation.<sup id="cite_ref-dc_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dc-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The Daughters of Charity were unlike other established religious communities, whose religious women were behind cloister walls in a monastery and performed a ministry of contemplative prayer.<sup id="cite_ref-mo_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mo-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> "Love the poor and honor them as you would honor Christ Himself," Louise explained.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2018)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> That was the foundation of the Company of the Daughters of Charity, which received official approbation in 1655. Their distinctive habit, a grey wool tunic with a large headdress or <a href="/wiki/Cornette" title="Cornette">cornette</a> of white linen, was the usual dress of <a href="/wiki/Breton_people" class="mw-redirect" title="Breton people">Breton</a> peasant women of the 17th century and later.<sup id="cite_ref-oxford_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oxford-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At first, the Company served the needs of the sick and poor in their homes. Louise's work with these young women developed into a system of pastoral care at the <a href="/wiki/H%C3%B4tel-Dieu_de_Paris" class="mw-redirect" title="Hôtel-Dieu de Paris">Hôtel-Dieu</a>, the oldest and largest hospital in Paris. Their work became well-known, and the Daughters were invited to <a href="/wiki/Angers" title="Angers">Angers</a> to take over management of the nursing services of the hospital there.<sup id="cite_ref-randolph_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-randolph-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> As it was the first ministry outside Paris for the fledgling community, Louise made the arduous journey there in the company of three nuns.
</p><p>After completing negotiations with the city officials and the hospital managers, Louise instituted collaboration among the doctors, nurses and others to form a comprehensive team. The model was highly successful and is still in use today by the Daughters of Charity. Under her guidance, they expanded their scope of service to include orphanages, institutions for the elderly and mentally ill, prisons and the battlefield.
</p><p>In working with her <a href="/wiki/Religious_sister" title="Religious sister">sisters</a>, Louise emphasized a balanced life, as Vincent de Paul had taught her. It was the integration of contemplation and activity that made Louise's work so successful. She wrote near the end of her life, "Certainly it is the great secret of the spiritual life to abandon to God all that we love by abandoning ourselves to all that He wills."<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2018)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Louise led the Company of Daughters until her death. Nearing her death, she wrote to her nuns: "Take good care of the service of the poor. Above all, live together in great union and cordiality, loving one another in imitation of the union and life of our Lord. Pray earnestly to the <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Virgin" class="mw-redirect" title="Blessed Virgin">Blessed Virgin</a>, that she might be your only Mother."<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2018)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>After increasingly ill health, Louise de Marillac died six months before the death of her dear friend and mentor, Vincent de Paul.<sup id="cite_ref-randolph_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-randolph-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> She was 68, and the Daughters of Charity had more than 40 houses in France. The nuns have always been held in high repute and have made foundations in all parts of the world.<sup id="cite_ref-oxford_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oxford-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Spirituality">Spirituality</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Louise_de_Marillac&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Spirituality">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Mlle_le_Gras.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/8/83/Mlle_le_Gras.jpg/220px-Mlle_le_Gras.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="261" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/8/83/Mlle_le_Gras.jpg/330px-Mlle_le_Gras.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/8/83/Mlle_le_Gras.jpg 2x" data-file-width="349" data-file-height="414" /></a><figcaption>Louise de Marillac</figcaption></figure>
<p>Aided by her directors, the young Louise had entered into profound prayer in the tradition of the Rhenish-Flemish spiritualists, and had been introduced to the <a href="/wiki/French_school_of_spirituality" title="French school of spirituality">French school of spirituality</a> of Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Pierre_de_B%C3%A9rulle" title="Pierre de Bérulle">Pierre de Bérulle</a>. Louise, like <a href="/wiki/Duns_Scotus" title="Duns Scotus">Duns Scotus</a>, viewed the <a href="/wiki/Incarnation_(Christianity)" title="Incarnation (Christianity)">Incarnation</a> as the moment in which men and women were saved. In the 17th century in France, there was discussion about the condemnation of <a href="/wiki/Quietism_(Christian_philosophy)" class="mw-redirect" title="Quietism (Christian philosophy)">Quietism</a> so from the time of her death, mysticism was viewed with suspicion. In light of this, her biographer, Nicholas Gobillon, removed any traces of mysticism from Louise's writings and rewrote her meditations.<sup id="cite_ref-betanzos_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-betanzos-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Louise_de_Marillac&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Louisedemarillac.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d9/Louisedemarillac.jpg/240px-Louisedemarillac.jpg" decoding="async" width="240" height="180" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d9/Louisedemarillac.jpg/360px-Louisedemarillac.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d9/Louisedemarillac.jpg/480px-Louisedemarillac.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1024" data-file-height="768" /></a><figcaption>The effigy of Saint Louise de Marillac in the <a href="/wiki/Chapel_of_Our_Lady_of_the_Miraculous_Medal" title="Chapel of Our Lady of the Miraculous Medal">Chapel of Our Lady of the Miraculous Medal</a> at 140 Rue du Bac, in <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a>, <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Louise de Marillac was <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XV" title="Pope Benedict XV">Pope Benedict XV</a> in 1920 and, on March 11, 1934, she was canonized by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XI" title="Pope Pius XI">Pope Pius XI</a>. Her <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is May 9 (March 15 until 2016). Her remains are enshrined in the <a href="/wiki/Chapel_of_Our_Lady_of_the_Miraculous_Medal" title="Chapel of Our Lady of the Miraculous Medal">chapel</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Motherhouse" title="Motherhouse">motherhouse</a> of the Daughters of Charity at 140 rue du Bac, Paris. She is mistakenly referred to as an incorrupt saint; the body enshrined in the chapel is actually a wax effigy, containing her bones. She was declared Patroness of Christian Social Workers by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_XXIII" title="Pope John XXIII">Pope John XXIII</a>, in 1960.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Louise de Marillac is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">honored</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Lesser_Feasts_and_Fasts" class="mw-redirect" title="Lesser Feasts and Fasts">Lesser Feast</a> on the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">liturgical calendar</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church in the United States of America</a><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> on <a href="/wiki/March_15" title="March 15">March 15</a>.
</p>
