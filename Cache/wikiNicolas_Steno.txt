<p><b>Niels Steensen</b> (<a href="/wiki/Danish_language" title="Danish language">Danish</a>: <i lang="da">Niels Steensen</i>; <a href="/wiki/Latinization_(literature)" class="mw-redirect" title="Latinization (literature)">Latinized</a> to <i>Nicolaus Steno</i> or <i>Nicolaus Stenonius</i><sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;notes 2&#93;</a></sup>; 1 January 1638&#160;– 25 November 1686<sup id="cite_ref-CathEncy_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CathEncy-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-JS_Aber_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-JS_Aber-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> <span style="font-size:85%;">[<a href="/wiki/Adoption_of_the_Gregorian_calendar" title="Adoption of the Gregorian calendar">NS</a>: 11 January 1638 – 5 December 1686]<sup id="cite_ref-CathEncy_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CathEncy-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></span>) was a <a href="/wiki/Danish_people" class="mw-redirect" title="Danish people">Danish</a> <a href="/wiki/Scientist" title="Scientist">scientist</a>, a pioneer in both <a href="/wiki/Anatomy" title="Anatomy">anatomy</a> and <a href="/wiki/Geology" title="Geology">geology</a> who became a <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic</a> bishop in his later years. 
</p><p>Steensen was trained in the classical texts on science; however, by 1659 he seriously questioned accepted knowledge of the natural world.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Importantly he questioned explanations for tear production, the idea that fossils grew in the ground and explanations of rock formation. His investigations and his subsequent conclusions on fossils and rock formation have led scholars to consider him one of the founders of modern <a href="/wiki/Stratigraphy" title="Stratigraphy">stratigraphy</a> and modern geology.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Woods_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Woods-15">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The importance of Steensen's foundational contributions to geology may be gauged from the fact that half of the twenty papers in a recent miscellany volume on <i>The Revolution in Geology from the Renaissance to the Enlightenment</i> focus on Steensen, the "preeminent Baroque polymath and founder of modern geologic thought".<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Born to a <a href="/wiki/Lutheranism" title="Lutheranism">Lutheran</a> family, Steensen converted to Catholicism in 1667. After his conversion, his interest in the natural sciences rapidly waned giving way to his interest in theology.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> At the beginning of 1675, he decided to become a priest. Four months later, he was <a href="/wiki/Priesthood_(Catholic_Church)" class="mw-redirect" title="Priesthood (Catholic Church)">ordained</a> in the Catholic clergy on Easter Sunday in 1675. As a clergyman, he was later appointed <a href="/wiki/Apostolic_Vicariate_of_Northern_Germany" title="Apostolic Vicariate of Northern Germany">Vicar Apostolic of Nordic Missions</a> and <a href="/wiki/Titopolis" class="mw-redirect" title="Titopolis">Titular Bishop of Titopolis</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Innocent_XI" title="Pope Innocent XI">Pope Innocent XI</a>. Steensen played an active role in the <a href="/wiki/Counter-Reformation" title="Counter-Reformation">Counter-Reformation</a> in Northern Germany. 
</p><p>His <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a> process began in 1938 and <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> Steensen in 1988.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_career">Early life and career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicolas_Steno&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life and career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:172px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Portrait_of_Nicolas_Stenonus.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Portrait_of_Nicolas_Stenonus.jpg/170px-Portrait_of_Nicolas_Stenonus.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="227" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Portrait_of_Nicolas_Stenonus.jpg/255px-Portrait_of_Nicolas_Stenonus.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Portrait_of_Nicolas_Stenonus.jpg/340px-Portrait_of_Nicolas_Stenonus.jpg 2x" data-file-width="944" data-file-height="1262" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Portrait_of_Nicolas_Stenonus.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Portrait of Niels Steensen (1666–1677). Unsigned but attributed to court painter <a href="/wiki/Justus_Sustermans" title="Justus Sustermans">Justus Sustermans</a>. (<a href="/wiki/Uffizi_Gallery" class="mw-redirect" title="Uffizi Gallery">Uffizi Gallery</a>, Florence, Italy)<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup></div></div></div>
<p>Niels Steensen was born in <a href="/wiki/Copenhagen" title="Copenhagen">Copenhagen</a> on New Year's Day 1638 (<a href="/wiki/Julian_calendar" title="Julian calendar">Julian calendar</a>), the son of a <a href="/wiki/Lutheran" class="mw-redirect" title="Lutheran">Lutheran</a> <a href="/wiki/Goldsmith" title="Goldsmith">goldsmith</a> who worked regularly for King <a href="/wiki/Christian_IV_of_Denmark" title="Christian IV of Denmark">Christian IV of Denmark</a>. He became ill at age three, suffering from an unknown disease, and grew up in isolation during his childhood. In 1644 his father died, after which his mother married another goldsmith. In 1654–1655, 240 pupils of his school died due to the <a href="/wiki/Black_Death" title="Black Death">plague</a>. Across the street lived <a href="/wiki/Peder_Griffenfeld" title="Peder Griffenfeld">Peder Schumacher</a> (who would offer Steensen a post as professor in Copenhagen in 1671). At the age of 19, Steensen entered the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Copenhagen" title="University of Copenhagen">University of Copenhagen</a> to pursue medical studies.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> After completing his university education, Steensen set out to travel through Europe; in fact, he would be on the move for the rest of his life. In the Netherlands, France, Italy and Germany he came into contact with prominent physicians and scientists. These influences led him to use his own powers of observation to make important scientific discoveries.
</p><p>At the urging of <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Bartholin" title="Thomas Bartholin">Thomas Bartholin</a>, Steensen first travelled to <a href="/wiki/Rostock" title="Rostock">Rostock</a>, then to <a href="/wiki/Amsterdam" title="Amsterdam">Amsterdam</a>, where he studied anatomy under and lodged with <a href="/wiki/Gerard_Blasius" title="Gerard Blasius">Gerard Blasius</a>, focusing on the <a href="/wiki/Lymphatic_system" title="Lymphatic system">lymphatic system</a>.  Within a few months Steensen moved to Leiden, where he met the students <a href="/wiki/Jan_Swammerdam" title="Jan Swammerdam">Jan Swammerdam</a>, <a href="/wiki/Frederik_Ruysch" title="Frederik Ruysch">Frederik Ruysch</a>, <a href="/wiki/Reinier_de_Graaf" class="mw-redirect" title="Reinier de Graaf">Reinier de Graaf</a>, <a href="/wiki/Franciscus_Sylvius" title="Franciscus Sylvius">Franciscus de le Boe Sylvius</a>, a famous professor, and <a href="/wiki/Baruch_Spinoza" title="Baruch Spinoza">Baruch Spinoza</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Steensen doubted <a href="/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Descartes" title="René Descartes">Descartes's</a> recently published explanation of the origin of tears<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> as produced by the brain. Invited to Paris by <a href="/wiki/Henri_Louis_Habert_de_Montmor" title="Henri Louis Habert de Montmor">Henri Louis Habert de Montmor</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pierre_Bourdelot" title="Pierre Bourdelot">Pierre Bourdelot</a>, he there met <a href="/wiki/Ole_Borch" title="Ole Borch">Ole Borch</a> and <a href="/wiki/Melchis%C3%A9dech_Th%C3%A9venot" title="Melchisédech Thévenot">Melchisédech Thévenot</a> who were interested in new research and in demonstrations of his skills. In 1665 Steensen travelled to <a href="/wiki/Saumur" title="Saumur">Saumur</a>, Bordeaux and <a href="/wiki/Montpellier" title="Montpellier">Montpellier</a>, where he met <a href="/wiki/Martin_Lister" title="Martin Lister">Martin Lister</a> and <a href="/wiki/William_Croone" title="William Croone">William Croone</a>, who introduced Steensen's work to the <a href="/wiki/Royal_Society" title="Royal Society">Royal Society</a>.
</p><p>After travelling through France, he settled in Italy in 1666 – at first as professor of anatomy at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Padua" title="University of Padua">University of Padua</a> and then in Florence as in-house physician of <a href="/wiki/Ferdinando_II_de%27_Medici,_Grand_Duke_of_Tuscany" title="Ferdinando II de&#39; Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany">Grand Duke of Tuscany Ferdinando II de' Medici</a>, who supported arts and science and whom Steensen had met in <a href="/wiki/Pisa" title="Pisa">Pisa</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Steensen was invited to live in the <a href="/wiki/Palazzo_Vecchio" title="Palazzo Vecchio">Palazzo Vecchio</a>; in return he had to gather a <a href="/wiki/Cabinet_of_curiosities" title="Cabinet of curiosities">cabinet of curiosities</a>. Steensen went to Rome and met <a href="/wiki/Pope_Alexander_VII" title="Pope Alexander VII">Pope Alexander VII</a> and <a href="/wiki/Marcello_Malpighi" title="Marcello Malpighi">Marcello Malpighi</a>, whom he admired. On his way back he watched a <a href="/wiki/Corpus_Christi_(feast)" class="mw-redirect" title="Corpus Christi (feast)">Corpus Christi</a> procession in <a href="/wiki/Livorno" title="Livorno">Livorno</a> and wondered if he had the right belief.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Scientific_contributions">Scientific contributions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicolas_Steno&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Scientific contributions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Anatomy">Anatomy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicolas_Steno&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Anatomy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Parotid_duct" title="Parotid duct">Parotid duct</a></div>
<p>During his stay in Amsterdam, Steensen discovered a previously undescribed structure, the "<i><a href="/wiki/Parotid_duct" title="Parotid duct">ductus Stenonis</a></i>" (the duct of the <a href="/wiki/Parotid_gland" title="Parotid gland">parotid salivary gland</a>) in sheep, dog and rabbit heads. A dispute with Blasius over credit for the discovery arose, but Steensen's name remained associated with this structure known today as the <a href="/wiki/Parotid_duct" title="Parotid duct">Stensen's duct</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> In Leiden, Steensen studied the boiled <a href="/wiki/Heart" title="Heart">heart</a> of a cow, and determined that it was an ordinary <a href="/wiki/Muscle" class="mw-redirect" title="Muscle">muscle</a><sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> and not the center of warmth as <a href="/wiki/Galenus" class="mw-redirect" title="Galenus">Galenus</a> and Descartes believed.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> In <a href="/wiki/Florence" title="Florence">Florence</a> Steensen focused on the <a href="/wiki/Muscular_system" title="Muscular system">muscular system</a> and the nature of <a href="/wiki/Muscle_contraction" title="Muscle contraction">muscle contraction</a>. He became a member of <a href="/wiki/Accademia_del_Cimento" title="Accademia del Cimento">Accademia del Cimento</a> and had long discussions with <a href="/wiki/Francesco_Redi" title="Francesco Redi">Francesco Redi</a>. Like <a href="/wiki/Vincenzo_Viviani" title="Vincenzo Viviani">Vincenzo Viviani</a>, Steensen proposed a geometrical model of muscles to show that a contracting muscle changes its shape but not its <a href="/wiki/Volume" title="Volume">volume</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div style="clear:left;"></div><p> Steensen was the first to describe the <a href="/wiki/Lateral_line" title="Lateral line">lateral line</a> system in fish.
</p><h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Paleontology">Paleontology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicolas_Steno&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Paleontology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: <a href="/wiki/History_of_paleontology" title="History of paleontology">History of paleontology</a></div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:172px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Steensen_-_Elementorum_myologiae_specimen,_1669_-_4715289.tif" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/75/Steensen_-_Elementorum_myologiae_specimen%2C_1669_-_4715289.tif/lossy-page1-170px-Steensen_-_Elementorum_myologiae_specimen%2C_1669_-_4715289.tif.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="278" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/75/Steensen_-_Elementorum_myologiae_specimen%2C_1669_-_4715289.tif/lossy-page1-255px-Steensen_-_Elementorum_myologiae_specimen%2C_1669_-_4715289.tif.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/75/Steensen_-_Elementorum_myologiae_specimen%2C_1669_-_4715289.tif/lossy-page1-340px-Steensen_-_Elementorum_myologiae_specimen%2C_1669_-_4715289.tif.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2209" data-file-height="3612" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Steensen_-_Elementorum_myologiae_specimen,_1669_-_4715289.tif" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Elementorum myologiae specimen</i>: Illustration from Steensen's 1667 paper comparing the teeth of a shark head with a fossil tooth.</div></div></div>
<p>In October 1666 two fishermen caught a huge female <a href="/wiki/Shark" title="Shark">shark</a> near the town of <a href="/wiki/Livorno" title="Livorno">Livorno</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Ferdinando_II_de%27_Medici" class="mw-redirect" title="Ferdinando II de&#39; Medici">Ferdinando II de' Medici</a>, Grand Duke of Tuscany, ordered its head to be sent to Steensen. Steensen <a href="/wiki/Dissection" title="Dissection">dissected</a> the head and published his findings in 1667. He noted that the <a href="/wiki/Shark_tooth" title="Shark tooth">shark's teeth</a> bore a striking resemblance to certain stony objects, found embedded within rock formations, that his learned contemporaries were calling <i>glossopetrae</i> or "tongue stones". Ancient authorities, such as the <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Rome" title="Ancient Rome">Roman</a> author <a href="/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder" title="Pliny the Elder">Pliny the Elder</a>, in his <i><a href="/wiki/Natural_History_(Pliny)" title="Natural History (Pliny)">Naturalis Historia</a></i>, had suggested that these stones fell from the sky or from the <a href="/wiki/Moon" title="Moon">Moon</a>. Others were of the opinion, also following ancient authors, that <a href="/wiki/Fossil" title="Fossil">fossils</a> naturally grew in the rocks. Steensen's contemporary <a href="/wiki/Athanasius_Kircher" title="Athanasius Kircher">Athanasius Kircher</a>, for example, attributed fossils to a "lapidifying virtue diffused through the whole body of the geocosm", considered an inherent characteristic of the earth – an <a href="/wiki/Aristotelianism" title="Aristotelianism">Aristotelian</a> approach. <a href="/wiki/Fabio_Colonna" title="Fabio Colonna">Fabio Colonna</a>, however, had already shown by burning the material to show that <i>glossopetrae</i> were organic matter (limestone) rather than soil minerals,<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> in his treatise <i>De glossopetris dissertatio</i> published in 1616.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> Steensen added to Colonna's theory a discussion on the differences in composition between glossopetrae and living sharks' teeth, arguing that the chemical composition of fossils could be altered without changing their form, using the contemporary <a href="/wiki/Corpuscularianism" title="Corpuscularianism">corpuscular theory of matter</a>.
</p><p>Steensen's work on shark teeth led him to the question of how any solid object could come to be found inside another solid object, such as a rock or a layer of rock. The "solid bodies within solids" that attracted Steensen's interest included not only fossils, as we would define them today, but minerals, crystals, encrustations, veins, and even entire rock layers or <a href="/wiki/Stratum" title="Stratum">strata</a>. He published his geologic studies in <i>De solido intra solidum naturaliter contento dissertationis prodromus</i>, or <i>Preliminary discourse to a dissertation on a solid body naturally contained within a solid</i> in 1669. This book was his last scientific work of note.<sup id="cite_ref-Garrett1916_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Garrett1916-34">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;notes 3&#93;</a></sup> Steensen was not the first to identify fossils as being from living organisms; his contemporary <a href="/wiki/Robert_Hooke" title="Robert Hooke">Robert Hooke</a> also argued that fossils were the remains of once-living organisms.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Geology_and_stratigraphy">Geology and stratigraphy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicolas_Steno&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Geology and stratigraphy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:172px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Steno_De_Solido_Dissertationis_Prodromus_1669.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Steno_De_Solido_Dissertationis_Prodromus_1669.jpg/170px-Steno_De_Solido_Dissertationis_Prodromus_1669.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="254" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Steno_De_Solido_Dissertationis_Prodromus_1669.jpg/255px-Steno_De_Solido_Dissertationis_Prodromus_1669.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Steno_De_Solido_Dissertationis_Prodromus_1669.jpg/340px-Steno_De_Solido_Dissertationis_Prodromus_1669.jpg 2x" data-file-width="606" data-file-height="904" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Steno_De_Solido_Dissertationis_Prodromus_1669.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>De solido intra solidum naturaliter contento dissertationis prodromus</i> (1669)</div></div></div>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main articles: <a href="/wiki/Law_of_superposition" title="Law of superposition">law of superposition</a>, <a href="/wiki/Principle_of_original_horizontality" title="Principle of original horizontality">principle of original horizontality</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Principle_of_lateral_continuity" title="Principle of lateral continuity">principle of lateral continuity</a></div>
<p>Steensen, in his <i>Dissertationis prodromus</i> of 1669 is credited with four of the defining principles of the science of <a href="/wiki/Stratigraphy" title="Stratigraphy">stratigraphy</a>. His words were:
</p>
<ol><li>the <a href="/wiki/Law_of_superposition" title="Law of superposition">law of superposition</a>: "At the time when a given stratum was being formed, there was beneath it another substance which prevented the further descent of the comminuted matter and so at the time when the lowest stratum was being formed either another solid substance was beneath it, or if some fluid existed there, then it was not only of a different character from the upper fluid, but also heavier than the solid sediment of the upper fluid."</li>
<li>the <a href="/wiki/Principle_of_original_horizontality" title="Principle of original horizontality">principle of original horizontality</a>: "At the time when one of the upper strata was being formed, the lower stratum had already gained the consistency of a solid."</li>
<li>the <a href="/wiki/Principle_of_lateral_continuity" title="Principle of lateral continuity">principle of lateral continuity</a>: "At the time when any given stratum was being formed it was either encompassed on its sides by another solid substance, or it covered the entire spherical surface of the earth. Hence it follows that in whatever place the bared sides of the strata are seen, either a continuation of the same strata must be sought, or another solid substance must be found which kept the matter of the strata from dispersion."</li>
<li>the <a href="/wiki/Principle_of_cross-cutting_relationships" class="mw-redirect" title="Principle of cross-cutting relationships">principle of cross-cutting relationships</a>: "If a body or discontinuity cuts across a stratum, it must have formed after that stratum."<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup></li></ol>
<p>These principles were applied and extended in 1772 by <a href="/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_L._Rom%C3%A9_de_l%27Isle" title="Jean-Baptiste L. Romé de l&#39;Isle">Jean-Baptiste L. Romé de l'Isle</a>. Steensen's ideas still form the basis of stratigraphy and were key in the development of <a href="/wiki/James_Hutton" title="James Hutton">James Hutton</a>'s theory of <a href="/wiki/James_Hutton#Search_for_evidence" title="James Hutton">infinitely repeating cycles</a> of seabed deposition, uplifting, erosion, and submersion.<sup id="cite_ref-brook_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-brook-39">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Crystallography">Crystallography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicolas_Steno&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Crystallography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information&#32;on crystallographic indices: <a href="/wiki/Crystal_system" title="Crystal system">Crystal system</a></div>
<p>Steensen gave the first accurate observations on a type of crystal in his 1669 book <i>"De solido intra solidum naturaliter contento"</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-kunz_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kunz-40">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> The principle in <a href="/wiki/Crystallography" title="Crystallography">crystallography</a>, known simply as <i>Steensen's law</i>, or <i>Steensen's law of constant angles</i> or the <i>first law of crystallography</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> states that the angles between corresponding faces on crystals are the same for all specimens of the same mineral. Steensen's seminal work paved the way for the law of the rationality of the <a href="/wiki/Crystal_system" title="Crystal system">crystallographic indices</a> of French mineralogist <a href="/wiki/Ren%C3%A9-Just_Ha%C3%BCy" class="mw-redirect" title="René-Just Haüy">René-Just Haüy</a> in 1801.<sup id="cite_ref-kunz_40-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kunz-40">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> This fundamental breakthrough formed the basis of all subsequent inquiries into <a href="/wiki/Crystal_structure" title="Crystal structure">crystal structure</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Religious_studies">Religious studies</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicolas_Steno&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Religious studies">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Steensen's questioning mind also influenced his religious views. Having been brought up in the <a href="/wiki/Lutheran" class="mw-redirect" title="Lutheran">Lutheran</a> faith, he nevertheless questioned its teachings, something which became a burning issue when confronted with Roman Catholicism while studying in <a href="/wiki/Florence" title="Florence">Florence</a>. After making comparative theological studies, including reading the Church Fathers and by using his natural observational skills, he decided that Catholicism, rather than Lutheranism, provided more sustenance for his constant inquisitiveness. In 1667, Steensen converted to Catholicism on <a href="/wiki/All_Souls%27_Day" title="All Souls&#39; Day">All Souls' Day</a>, influenced, among others, by Lavinia Cenami Arnolfini, a noblewoman of <a href="/wiki/Lucca" title="Lucca">Lucca</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-cathhier_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathhier-43">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Steensen traveled to Hungary, Austria and in Spring 1670 he arrived in Amsterdam. There he met with old friends <a href="/wiki/Jan_Swammerdam" title="Jan Swammerdam">Jan Swammerdam</a> and <a href="/wiki/Reinier_de_Graaf" class="mw-redirect" title="Reinier de Graaf">Reinier de Graaf</a>. With <a href="/wiki/Anna_Maria_van_Schurman" title="Anna Maria van Schurman">Anna Maria van Schurman</a> and <a href="/wiki/Antoinette_Bourignon" title="Antoinette Bourignon">Antoinette Bourignon</a> he discussed scientific and religious topics. The following quote is from a 1673 speech:
</p>
<dl><dd><i>Fair is what we see, Fairer what we have perceived, Fairest what is still in veil</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup></dd></dl>
<p>It is not clear if he met <a href="/wiki/Nicolaes_Witsen" title="Nicolaes Witsen">Nicolaes Witsen</a>, but he did read Witsen's book on shipbuilding. In 1671 he accepted the post of professor of anatomy in the University of Copenhagen,<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_23-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> but promised <a href="/wiki/Cosimo_III_de%27_Medici" class="mw-redirect" title="Cosimo III de&#39; Medici">Cosimo III de' Medici</a> he would return when he was appointed tutor to <a href="/wiki/Ferdinando_III_de%27_Medici" class="mw-redirect" title="Ferdinando III de&#39; Medici">Ferdinando III de' Medici</a>.
</p><p>At the beginning of 1675, Steensen decided to continue his theological studies, which he had begun even before his conversion, toward his ordination to the priesthood.<sup id="cite_ref-kraus35_46-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kraus35-46">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> After only 4 months, he was ordained priest  and celebrated his first mass on 13 April 1675 in the Basilica of the <a href="/wiki/Santissima_Annunziata,_Florence" title="Santissima Annunziata, Florence">Santissima Annunziata</a> in Florence at the age of 37.<sup id="cite_ref-NewCathEncy_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NewCathEncy-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-cathhier_43-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathhier-43">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-kraus35_46-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kraus35-46">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Athanasius_Kircher" title="Athanasius Kircher">Athanasius Kircher</a> expressly asked what were the reasons why he decided to become priest.<sup id="cite_ref-kraus35_46-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kraus35-46">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> Steensen had left natural sciences for education and theology and became one of the leading figures in the <a href="/wiki/Counter-Reformation" title="Counter-Reformation">Counter-Reformation</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Garrett1916_34-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Garrett1916-34">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> Upon request of Duke <a href="/wiki/John_Frederick,_Duke_of_Brunswick-L%C3%BCneburg" class="mw-redirect" title="John Frederick, Duke of Brunswick-Lüneburg">Johann Friedrich of Hanover</a>, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Innocent_XI" title="Pope Innocent XI">Pope Innocent XI</a> made him <a href="/wiki/Apostolic_Vicariate_of_Northern_Germany#Vicars_Apostolic_for_the_Nordic_Missions" title="Apostolic Vicariate of Northern Germany">Vicar Apostolic for the Nordic Missions</a> on 21 August 1677.  He was consecrated <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Titiopolis" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Titiopolis">titular bishop of Titiopolis</a> on 19 September by <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_Barbarigo" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinal Barbarigo">Cardinal Barbarigo</a> and moved to the Lutheran North.<sup id="cite_ref-Miniati77_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Miniati77-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the year after he was made bishop, he was probably involved in the banning of publications by <a href="/wiki/Spinoza" class="mw-redirect" title="Spinoza">Spinoza</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> There he had talks with <a href="/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz" class="mw-redirect" title="Gottfried Leibniz">Gottfried Leibniz</a>, the librarian; the two argued about <a href="/wiki/Spinoza" class="mw-redirect" title="Spinoza">Spinoza</a> and his letter to <a href="/wiki/Albert_Burgh" title="Albert Burgh">Albert Burgh</a>, then Steensen's pupil.<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> Leibniz recommended a reunification of the churches. Steensen worked at the city of <a href="/wiki/Hannover" class="mw-redirect" title="Hannover">Hannover</a> until 1680.
</p><p>After John Frederick death's, <a href="/wiki/Prince-Bishop_of_Paderborn" class="mw-redirect" title="Prince-Bishop of Paderborn">Prince-Bishop of Paderborn</a> <a href="/w/index.php?title=Ferdinand_von_F%C3%BCrstenberg_(1626%E2%80%931683)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Ferdinand von Fürstenberg (1626–1683) (page does not exist)">Ferdinand of Fürstenberg</a> appointed him as Auxiliary <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_M%C3%BCnster" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Münster">Bishop of Münster</a> (Church Saint Liudger) on 7 October 1680.<sup id="cite_ref-NewCathEncy_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NewCathEncy-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The new <a href="/wiki/Prince-elector" title="Prince-elector">prince-elector</a> <a href="/wiki/Ernest_Augustus,_Elector_of_Hanover" title="Ernest Augustus, Elector of Hanover">Ernest Augustus, Elector of Hanover</a> was a Protestant. Earlier, Augustus' wife, <a href="/wiki/Sophia_of_Hanover" title="Sophia of Hanover">Sophia of Hanover</a>, had made fun of Steensen's piousness; he had sold his bishop's ring and cross to help the needy.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2012)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> He continued zealously the work of counter reform begun by <a href="/wiki/Bernhard_von_Galen" class="mw-redirect" title="Bernhard von Galen">Bernhard von Galen</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-NewCathEncy_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NewCathEncy-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1683, Steensen resigned as auxiliary bishop after an argument about the election of the new bishop, <a href="/wiki/Maximilian_Henry_of_Bavaria" title="Maximilian Henry of Bavaria">Maximilian Henry of Bavaria</a> and moved in 1684 to <a href="/wiki/Hamburg" title="Hamburg">Hamburg</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-cathhier_43-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathhier-43">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> There Steensen became involved again in the study of the brain and the nerve system with an old friend <a href="/wiki/Dirck_Kerckring" class="mw-redirect" title="Dirck Kerckring">Dirck Kerckring</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> Steensen was invited to <a href="/wiki/Schwerin" title="Schwerin">Schwerin</a>, when it became clear he was not accepted in Hamburg. Steensen dressed like a poor man in an old cloak. He drove in an open carriage in snow and rain. Living four days a week on bread and beer, he became emaciated.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;notes 4&#93;</a></sup> When Steensen had fulfilled his mission, some years of difficult tasks, he wanted to go back to Italy. Before he could return, Steensen became severely ill, his belly swelling day by day. Steensen died in Germany, after much suffering. His corpse was shipped to Florence by Kerckring upon request of <a href="/wiki/Cosimo_III_de%27_Medici" class="mw-redirect" title="Cosimo III de&#39; Medici">Cosimo III de' Medici</a> and buried in the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_San_Lorenzo,_Florence" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica of San Lorenzo, Florence">Basilica of San Lorenzo</a> close to his protectors, the <a href="/wiki/De%27_Medici" class="mw-redirect" title="De&#39; Medici">De' Medici</a> family.<sup id="cite_ref-NewCathEncy_8-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NewCathEncy-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> In 1946 his grave was opened,<sup id="cite_ref-51" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-51">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup> and the corpse was reburied after a procession through the streets of the city.<sup id="cite_ref-52" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-52">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Beatification">Beatification</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicolas_Steno&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Beatification">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After his death in 1686, Steensen was venerated as a saint in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Hildesheim" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of Hildesheim">diocese of Hildesheim</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-NewCathEncy_8-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NewCathEncy-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Steensen's piety and virtue have been evaluated with a view to an eventual <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a>. His canonization process was begun in <a href="/wiki/Osnabr%C3%BCck" title="Osnabrück">Osnabrück</a> in 1938.<sup id="cite_ref-NewCathEncy_8-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NewCathEncy-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> In 1953 his grave in the crypt of the church of San Lorenzo was opened as part of the beatification process.<sup id="cite_ref-kermit21_53-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kermit21-53">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> His corpse was transferred to a fourth-century Christian sarcophagus found in the river <a href="/wiki/Arno" title="Arno">Arno</a> donated by the Italian state. His remains were placed in a lateral chapel of the church that received the name of <i>"Capella Stenoniana"</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-NewCathEncy_8-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NewCathEncy-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-kermit21_53-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kermit21-53">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> He was <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> in 1988. His feast day is 5 December.<sup id="cite_ref-NewCathEncy_8-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NewCathEncy-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicolas_Steno&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Steensen's life and work has been studied, in particular in relation to the developments in geology in the late nineteenth century.
</p>
<ul><li>The <a href="/wiki/Steno_Museum" class="mw-redirect" title="Steno Museum">Steensen Museum</a> in <a href="/wiki/Aarhus" title="Aarhus">Aarhus</a>, Denmark, named after Niels Steensen, holds exhibitions on the history of science and medicine.<sup id="cite_ref-54" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-54">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup> It also operates a <a href="/wiki/Planetarium" title="Planetarium">planetarium</a>, a medicinal herb garden and the greenhouses in <a href="/wiki/Aarhus_Botanical_Gardens" title="Aarhus Botanical Gardens">Aarhus Botanical Gardens</a>.</li>
<li>The <a href="/wiki/Steno_Medal" title="Steno Medal">Steno Medal</a>, awarded by the Geological Society of Denmark, honors prominent geologists who have made significant contributions to Danish and Greenland geology, and is named in his honor.<sup id="cite_ref-55" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-55">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Impact_crater" title="Impact crater">Impact craters</a> on <a href="/wiki/List_of_craters_on_Mars:_O-Z#S" class="mw-redirect" title="List of craters on Mars: O-Z">Mars</a> (<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1073938472">.mw-parser-output .geo-default,.mw-parser-output .geo-dms,.mw-parser-output .geo-dec{display:inline}.mw-parser-output .geo-nondefault,.mw-parser-output .geo-multi-punct{display:none}.mw-parser-output .longitude,.mw-parser-output .latitude{white-space:nowrap}</style><span class="plainlinks nourlexpansion"><a class="external text" href="//geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Nicolas_Steno&amp;params=68.0_S_115.6_W_globe:mars_type:landmark&amp;title=Steno"><span class="geo-nondefault"><span class="geo-dms" title="Maps, aerial photos, and other data for this location"><span class="latitude">68°00′S</span> <span class="longitude">115°36′W</span></span></span><span class="geo-multi-punct">&#xfeff; / &#xfeff;</span><span class="geo-default"><span class="vcard"><span class="geo-dec" title="Maps, aerial photos, and other data for this location">68.0°S 115.6°W</span><span style="display:none">&#xfeff; / <span class="geo">-68.0; -115.6</span></span><span style="display:none">&#xfeff; (<span class="fn org">Steno</span>)</span></span></span></a></span>) and the <a href="/wiki/Steno_(lunar_crater)" title="Steno (lunar crater)">Moon</a> are named in his honor.</li>
<li>The <a href="/wiki/Mineral" title="Mineral">mineral</a> <a href="/w/index.php?title=Stenonite&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Stenonite (page does not exist)">Stenonite</a> was named in his honour.<sup id="cite_ref-56" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-56">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-57" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-57">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>The Catholic parish church of <a href="/wiki/Grevesm%C3%BChlen" title="Grevesmühlen">Grevesmühlen</a>, North Germany, built from 1989 to 1991, is dedicated to Niels Steensen.<sup id="cite_ref-58" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-58">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>In 1950 the "Niels Steensens Gymnasium", a Catholic preparatory school, was founded by the <a href="/wiki/Jesuit_Order" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit Order">Jesuit Order</a> in Copenhagen.<sup id="cite_ref-59" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-59">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Steno_Diabetes_Center" title="Steno Diabetes Center">Steno Diabetes Center</a>, a research and teaching hospital dedicated to diabetes in <a href="/wiki/Gentofte" title="Gentofte">Gentofte</a>, Denmark, was named after Niels Steensen.</li>
<li>The Istituto Niels Stensen was founded in 1964 in Florence, Italy. Administered by the Jesuit Order, it is dedicated to his memory.</li>
<li>On 11 January 2012, Steensen was commemorated with a <a href="/wiki/Google_doodle" class="mw-redirect" title="Google doodle">Google doodle</a> as the founder of geology.<sup id="cite_ref-60" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-60">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-csm-2012_61-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-csm-2012-61">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-62" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-62">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
