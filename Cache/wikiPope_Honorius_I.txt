<p><b>Pope Honorius I</b> (died 12 October 638)<sup id="cite_ref-ce_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> was the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Rome">bishop of Rome</a> from 27 October 625 to his death. He was active in spreading <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a> among <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxons</a> and attempted to convince the <a href="/wiki/Celts" title="Celts">Celts</a> to <a href="/wiki/Easter_controversy" title="Easter controversy">calculate Easter</a> in the Roman fashion. He is chiefly remembered for his correspondence with Patriarch <a href="/wiki/Sergius_I_of_Constantinople" title="Sergius I of Constantinople">Sergius I of Constantinople</a> over the latter's <a href="/wiki/Monothelite" class="mw-redirect" title="Monothelite">monothelite</a> teachings. Honorius was posthumously <a href="/wiki/Anathema" title="Anathema">anathematized</a>, initially for subscribing to monothelitism, and later only for failing to end it. The anathema against Honorius I became one of the central arguments against the doctrine of <a href="/wiki/Papal_infallibility" title="Papal infallibility">papal infallibility</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Honorius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Honorius was a rich aristocrat who came from <a href="/wiki/Campania" title="Campania">Campania</a>. His father was the consul Petronius. Nothing is known about Honorius I's career before he <a href="/wiki/Papal_selection_before_1059" title="Papal selection before 1059">became pope</a> on 27 October 625. He was <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_consecration" class="mw-redirect" title="Episcopal consecration">consecrated</a> only two days after the death of his predecessor, <a href="/wiki/Boniface_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Boniface V">Boniface V</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Sede_vacante" title="Sede vacante">vacancy</a> was short probably because of the presence in Rome of <a href="/wiki/Isaac_the_Armenian" title="Isaac the Armenian">Isaac the Armenian</a>, who was empowered to <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_papacy" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine papacy">confirm the election</a> as the imperial <a href="/wiki/Exarch_in_Italy" class="mw-redirect" title="Exarch in Italy">exarch in Italy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Attwater_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Attwater-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Papacy">Papacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Honorius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Papacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>As pope, Honorius I looked up to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I" title="Pope Gregory I">Gregory I</a> and employed monks rather than secular clergy as staff at the <a href="/wiki/Lateran_Palace" title="Lateran Palace">Lateran Palace</a>. He initially supported <a href="/wiki/Adaloald" title="Adaloald">Adaloald</a>, the deposed Catholic <a href="/wiki/King_of_the_Lombards" class="mw-redirect" title="King of the Lombards">king of the Lombards</a>, but established cordial relations with Adoald's <a href="/wiki/Arian" class="mw-redirect" title="Arian">Arian</a> rival <a href="/wiki/Arioald" title="Arioald">Arioald</a>. He did not succeed in resolving the schism of Venetia-Istria, but took steps to appease the <a href="/wiki/Archbishops_of_Ravenna" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishops of Ravenna">archbishops of Ravenna</a>, who were dissatisfied with their subordination to Rome. Honorius actively supported the difficult <a href="/wiki/Christianisation_of_Anglo-Saxon_England" title="Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England">Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England</a> and sent <a href="/wiki/Birinus" title="Birinus">Birinus</a> to convert the <a href="/wiki/West_Saxons" class="mw-redirect" title="West Saxons">West Saxons</a>, but less successful in convincing the <a href="/wiki/Celts" title="Celts">Celts</a> to abandon <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">their system</a> of <a href="/wiki/Easter_controversy" title="Easter controversy">computing the date of Easter</a>. At the <a href="/wiki/Sixth_Council_of_Toledo" title="Sixth Council of Toledo">Sixth Council of Toledo</a>, Honorius urged the Visigothic bishops to continue <a href="/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Spain#Visigoth_rule_–_Repression_and_forced_conversions_(5th_century_to_711)" title="History of the Jews in Spain">baptizing Jews</a>, a policy instituted by Gregory I.<sup id="cite_ref-Attwater_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Attwater-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Honorius became involved in early discussions regarding the doctrine of <a href="/wiki/Monothelitism" title="Monothelitism">Monothelitism</a>, which is the teaching that Christ has only one energy and one will, in contrast with the teaching that he has two energies and two wills, both human and divine.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Patriarch <a href="/wiki/Sergius_I_of_Constantinople" title="Sergius I of Constantinople">Sergius I of Constantinople</a> wrote an initial letter informing Honorius of the <a href="/wiki/Monoenergism" title="Monoenergism">Monoenergism</a> controversy, asking Honorius to endorse a position that Church unity should not be endangered by having any discussions or disputes over Christ’s possessing one energy or two.  Sergius added that the doctrine of two energies could lead to the erroneous belief that Jesus has two conflicting wills.<sup id="cite_ref-Hefele,_pg_25_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hefele,_pg_25-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Pope Honorius’ reply in 635 endorsed this view that all discussions over energies should cease, and agreed that Jesus does not have two conflicting wills, but one will, since Jesus did not assume the vitiated human nature tainted by <a href="/wiki/Adam" title="Adam">Adam</a>'s fall, but human nature as it existed prior to Adam's fall.<sup id="cite_ref-Hefele,_pg_29-30_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hefele,_pg_29-30-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Honorius was apparently aware of the <a href="/wiki/Rise_of_Islam" class="mw-redirect" title="Rise of Islam">rise of Islam</a> and viewed this religion's tenets as closely resembling those of <a href="/wiki/Arius" title="Arius">Arius</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Honorius_I&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In the <a href="/wiki/Third_Council_of_Constantinople" title="Third Council of Constantinople">Third Council of Constantinople</a>, the monothelites were <a href="/wiki/Anathema" title="Anathema">anathematized</a> by name "and with them Honorius, who was Prelate of Rome, as having followed them in all things" in the XIII session. Citing his written correspondence with Sergius, Honorius was subsequently accused of having confirmed his impious doctrines; the XVI session reaffirmed the condemnation of the heretics explicitly stating "to Honorius, the heretic, anathema!",<sup id="cite_ref-Percival1900_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Percival1900-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Mansi,_XI,_col._622_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mansi,_XI,_col._622-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> and concluding with the decree of the XVII session that 
Honorius had not stopped provoking scandal and error in the Body of the Church; for he had "with unheard of expressions disseminated amidst the faithful people the heresy of the one will", doing so "in agreement with the insane false doctrine of the impious Apollinaire, Severus and Themistius".<sup id="cite_ref-Mansi,_XI,_col._733_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mansi,_XI,_col._733-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The Roman legates made no objection to his condemnation.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_II" title="Pope Leo II">Pope Leo II</a>'s letter of confirmation of the Council commended it for it had "perfectly preached the definition of the true faith"<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> and made reference to the condemnation of his predecessor:<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>We anathematize the inventors of the new error, that is, Theodore, Bishop of Pharan, Sergius, Pyrrhus, Paul, and Peter, betrayers rather than leaders of the Church of Constantinople, and also Honorius, who did not attempt to sanctify this Apostolic Church with the teaching of apostolic tradition, but by profane treachery permitted its purity to be polluted.<sup id="cite_ref-Mansi,_XI,_col._733_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mansi,_XI,_col._733-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Within the year a Latin translation of the Acts of the council had been disseminated and signed by the Bishops throughout the West. The condemnation of Pope Honorius was reiterated by Pope Leo's successors,<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> subsequent councils<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> and included in Breviary lessons up until the eighteenth century. As a result, Honorius would later be the subject of vigorous attacks by opponents of <a href="/wiki/Papal_infallibility" title="Papal infallibility">papal infallibility</a> in the discussions surrounding the <a href="/wiki/First_Vatican_Council" title="First Vatican Council">First Vatican Council</a> of 1870.<sup id="cite_ref-ce_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ce-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> In contemporary times, that Honorius actually agreed with Sergius on the doctrine of monothelitism has given rise to much discussion, and <a href="/wiki/John_B._Bury" class="mw-redirect" title="John B. Bury">John B. Bury</a> argues that the most reasonable conclusion is that Honorius did not really apprehend the point at issue, considering it more a question of grammar than theology, for he placed "one energy" and "two energies" on exactly the same footing; in Bury's words, "it was for the 'imprudent economy of silence' that he was condemned".<sup id="cite_ref-Bury,_pg_252_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bury,_pg_252-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
