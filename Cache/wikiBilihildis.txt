					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Benedictine abbess and Frankish saint</div>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">For the Frankish queen, see <a href="/wiki/Bilichild" title="Bilichild">Bilichild</a>.</div>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:S._Bilhiltis.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/S._Bilhiltis.jpg/170px-S._Bilhiltis.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="255" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/S._Bilhiltis.jpg/255px-S._Bilhiltis.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/S._Bilhiltis.jpg/340px-S._Bilhiltis.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1183" data-file-height="1773" /></a><figcaption></figcaption></figure>
<p><b>Bilihildis</b> (also spelled Bilihilt, Bilhild, Bilehild; died 734) was a <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frankish</a> noblewoman, remembered as the founder and abbess of the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Altm%C3%BCnster_(Mainz)" class="mw-redirect" title="Altmünster (Mainz)">Altmünster near Mainz</a>, and venerated locally as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>, on Nov. 27.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bilihildis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The biography of Saint Bilihildis is difficult to establish; firm evidence of her existence only goes back to the 12th century, according to Andreas Meier.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Her <i><a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">vitae</a></i> date from after 1060 and, in the absence of other evidence, form the basis for her biography.<sup id="cite_ref-schafer_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schafer-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> There are five distinct <i>vitae</i>, the most important of which are:
</p>
<ul><li>a short, Latin version in prose, preserved in two manuscripts from the 13th and the 14th century</li>
<li>a longer prose version likewise preserved in two manuscripts, from the 14th and the 15th century (possibly modeled on hagiographical texts on Frankish queen and saint <a href="/wiki/Radegund" title="Radegund">Radegund</a>, and certainly indebted to <a href="/wiki/Saint_Kilian" title="Saint Kilian"><i>vitae Kiliani</i></a><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>)</li>
<li>a group of locally preserved texts in the vernacular</li>
<li>a metrical (Latin) version, the <i>Vita metrica auctore Herbelone</i>, first printed at the end of the 18th century and based on a now-lost manuscript.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<p>Brigitte Flug considers the short Latin prose version (written in the <a href="/wiki/Merovingians" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingians">Merovingian</a> style) to be the earliest, though she denies it could have been written within living memory.<sup id="cite_ref-Flug_47_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Flug_47-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Hagiography">Hagiography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bilihildis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Hagiography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Bilihildis was born in the 7th century in <i>Hocheim</i>, typically identified as modern-day <a href="/wiki/Veitsh%C3%B6chheim" title="Veitshöchheim">Veitshöchheim</a> near <a href="/wiki/W%C3%BCrzburg_(district)" title="Würzburg (district)">Würzburg</a>, Bavaria, the child of Count Jberin and his wife Mathilda.<sup id="cite_ref-schafer_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schafer-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> According to Alfred Wendehorst, however, what is meant is probably <a href="/wiki/Hochheim_am_Main" title="Hochheim am Main">Hochheim am Main</a>, with the location in Würzburg and its East-Frankish connections a later "explanation" for the monastery's foundation.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Her 11th-century <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> reports that she was forced to flee the invading Huns and was moved to <a href="/wiki/W%C3%BCrzburg" title="Würzburg">Würzburg</a>, where she was raised as a Christian. Though she wanted to devote her life to Christ, her parents forced her to marry a pagan Frankish duke named Hetan around 672, whom she loved but was unable to convert.<sup id="cite_ref-Werner_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Werner-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> It is not clear whether this is Hedan I<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> or <a href="/wiki/Hedan_II" title="Hedan II">Hedan II</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-schafer_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schafer-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Hetan was called to battle and was killed; during his absence Bilihild saw an opportunity for a religious life and traveled by ship to Mainz,<sup id="cite_ref-Flug_45_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Flug_45-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> 
where she asked for and received permission from the <a href="/wiki/Elector_of_Mainz#Bishops_of_Moguntiacum.2C_80.E2.80.93745" title="Elector of Mainz">local bishop</a>, her uncle Sigibert (a misreading for Rigibert, bishop of Mainz<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>), to start a foundation for religious women. She started this foundation using her considerable wealth (having sold her possessions in Hochheim<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>) to support it.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> This was the beginning of the Altmünster monastery of which she was the first abbess.<sup id="cite_ref-Werner_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Werner-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> She was baptized later in life. She died on 27 November 734 and was buried in the abbey church; her grave soon gave off a sweet aroma and many miracles happened there.<sup id="cite_ref-Flug_45_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Flug_45-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Commentary">Commentary</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bilihildis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Commentary">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>This account, which is based on the short Latin prose version, is embellished with various details in other versions; the German version adds local geographical and historical detail. Such additional detail includes her maiden name, <i>Mathildis</i>, and the gift of a <i><a href="/wiki/Sudarium" title="Sudarium">sudarium</a></i> ("sweat cloth"), supposedly a cloth used to cover Jesus's face after the crucifixion. This <i>sudarium</i> was given by a queen Imnechild (in a different redaction, Kunegundis) and has been venerated in Altmünster since the 15th century.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bilihildis's hagiography follows a traditional (Merovingian) scheme common for saints like her since the 6th century: the saint is religious from an early age, exhibits humility and abstinence, is forced into marriage, flees, and ends up founding an abbey. Sainthood is proven by the sweet odor of the dead body and the miracles after death. Flug does not deny the possibility that Bilihildis was already considered a saint during life or shortly thereafter, but considers it unlikely that a <i>vita</i> was written so early, considering mistakes such as the bishop's name; Flug proposes that the author did not know Bilihildis and her life, and probably misread the name in the foundational deed for the monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-Flug_47_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Flug_47-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As for "Hetan", identification with Hedan I ("the elder") is difficult given the time frame; since he died (according to <a href="/wiki/Hubert_Mordek" title="Hubert Mordek">Hubert Mordek</a><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>) after 676, which would mean the founding of the abbey took place when Bilihildis was in her seventies, an unlikely proposition. For Hedan II the problem is that he was Christian and had a wife, who was proven to exist in 704 and in 716/717.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Historical_traces_and_legacy">Historical traces and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bilihildis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Historical traces and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The word/name "bilihilt" occurs in a 5th/6th manuscript containing texts by <a href="/wiki/Priscillian" title="Priscillian">Priscillian</a>, which is identified with the Bilihildis who founded Altmünster.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> A 16th-century <a href="/wiki/Missal" title="Missal">missal</a> from Mainz (containing a calendar with Rhenish saints) has a "Mass from the feast of Saint Bilhildis"; the manuscript was acquired by the <a href="/wiki/J._Paul_Getty_Museum" title="J. Paul Getty Museum">J. Paul Getty Museum</a> in 1986.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> German poet <a href="/w/index.php?title=Alois_Henninger&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Alois Henninger (page does not exist)">Alois Henninger</a>, a prolific writer of religious narrative poems, dedicated a lengthy poem to Bilihildis in his <i>Nassau in seinen sagen, geschichten und liedern fremder und eigner dichtung</i> (1845); consisting of eighteen six-line stanzas, it praised her eternal beauty and steadfast devotion to God.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>German Protestant theologian <a href="/wiki/Johannes_Heinrich_August_Ebrard" title="Johannes Heinrich August Ebrard">Johannes Heinrich August Ebrard</a> wrote a biography of Bilihildis, which was translated (or, "retold") in English by Julie Sutter and published by the <a href="/wiki/Religious_Tract_Society" title="Religious Tract Society">Religious Tract Society</a>, with a particular focus on the influence of <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">Iroscottish Christianity</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
