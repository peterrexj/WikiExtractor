<p><b>Paschasius Radbertus</b> (785–865) was a <a href="/wiki/Carolingian_Renaissance" title="Carolingian Renaissance">Carolingian</a> theologian and the abbot of <a href="/wiki/Corbie_Abbey" title="Corbie Abbey">Corbie</a>, a monastery in <a href="/wiki/Picardy" title="Picardy">Picardy</a> founded in 657 or 660 by the queen regent <a href="/wiki/Balthild_of_Chelles" title="Balthild of Chelles">Bathilde</a>  with a founding community of monks from <a href="/wiki/Luxeuil_Abbey" title="Luxeuil Abbey">Luxeuil Abbey</a>. His most well-known and influential work is an exposition on the nature of the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a> written around 831, entitled <i>De <a href="/wiki/Body_of_Christ" title="Body of Christ">Corpore</a> et <a href="/wiki/Blood_of_Christ" title="Blood of Christ">Sanguine</a> <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">Domini</a></i>. 
He was canonized in 1073 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_VII" title="Pope Gregory VII">Pope Gregory VII</a>. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is April 26. 
His works are edited in <i><a href="/wiki/Patrologia_Latina" title="Patrologia Latina">Patrologia Latina</a></i> vol. 120 (1852) and his important tract on the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a> and <a href="/wiki/Transubstantiation" title="Transubstantiation">transubstantiation</a>, <i>De Corpore et Sanguine Domini</i>, in a 1969 edition by B. Paulus, published by <a href="/wiki/Brepols" title="Brepols">Brepols</a> (<a href="/wiki/Corpus_Christianorum" title="Corpus Christianorum">Corpus Christianorum</a>, Continuatio Mediaevalis 16).
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paschasius_Radbertus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Paschasius was an orphan left on the steps of the convent of <a href="/wiki/Notre-Dame_de_Soissons" title="Notre-Dame de Soissons">Notre-Dame de Soissons</a>.  He was raised by the nuns there, and became very fond of the abbess, Theodrara.  Theodrara was sister of <a href="/wiki/Adalard_of_Corbie" title="Adalard of Corbie">Adalard of Corbie</a> and <a href="/wiki/Wala_of_Corbie" title="Wala of Corbie">Wala of Corbie</a>, two monks whom he admired greatly.  At a fairly young age, Paschasius left the convent to serve as a monk under Abbot Adalard, at <a href="/wiki/Corbie" title="Corbie">Corbie</a>.  There he also met Wala, Adalard's brother and successor.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopedia_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopedia-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>  
</p><p>Through the abbotship of both Adalard and Wala, Paschasius focused on the monastic life, spending his time studying and teaching. In 822 he accompanied abbot Adalard into Saxony for the purpose of founding the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Princely_Abbey_of_Corvey" title="Princely Abbey of Corvey">New Corvey</a> in Westphalia. When Adalard died in 826, Paschasius helped ensure Wala would become Abbot in his place. After Wala's death in 836 Heddo became abbot. <a href="/wiki/Ratramnus" title="Ratramnus">Ratramnus</a>, who may have held the position of teaching master,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> held opposing views to Paschasius on a number of ecclesiastical issues. Ratramnus wrote a refutation of Paschasius' treatise on the Eucharist, <i>De Corpore et Sanguine Domini</i>, using the same title.  
</p><p>In 843 Paschasius succeeded Abbot Isaac, however he resigned his title ten years later to return to his studies.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  He left Corbie for the nearby monastery of <a href="/wiki/Saint-Riquier" title="Saint-Riquier">Saint-Riquier</a>, where he lived in voluntary exile for some years. Why he resigned is unknown, however it is likely that his actions were motivated by factional disputes within his monastic community; misunderstandings between himself and the younger monks were likely factors in his decision. 
He returned to Corbie late in life, and resided in his old monastery until his death in 865.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Paschasius' body was first buried at the Church of St. John in Corbie.  After numerous reported miracles, the Pope ordered his remains to be removed, and interred in the Church of St. Peter, Corbie.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopedia_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopedia-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paschasius_Radbertus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="De_Corpore_et_Sanguine_Domini"><i>De Corpore et Sanguine Domini</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paschasius_Radbertus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: De Corpore et Sanguine Domini">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The most well-known and influential work of Paschasius, <i>De Corpore et Sanguine Domini</i> (written between 831 and 833), is an exposition on the nature of the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a>. It was originally written as an instructional manual for the monks under his care at Corbie, and is the first lengthy treatise on the sacrament of the Eucharist in the Western world.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> In it, Paschasius agrees with <a href="/wiki/Ambrose" title="Ambrose">Ambrose</a> in affirming that the Eucharist contains the true, historical body of Jesus Christ. According to Paschasius, God is truth itself, and therefore, his words and actions must be true.  Christ's proclamation at the <a href="/wiki/Last_Supper" title="Last Supper">Last Supper</a> that the bread and wine were his body and blood must be taken literally, since God is truth.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>  He thus believes that the <a href="/wiki/Transubstantiation" title="Transubstantiation">transubstantiation</a> of the bread and wine offered in the Eucharist really occurs.  Only if the Eucharist is the actual body and blood of Christ can a Christian know it is salvific.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>  
</p><p>Paschasius believed that the presence of the historical blood and body of Christ allows the partaker a real union with Jesus in a direct, personal, and physical union by joining a person's flesh with Christ's and Christ's flesh with his.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>  To Paschasius, the Eucharist's transformation into the flesh and blood of Christ is possible <a href="/wiki/Divine_simplicity" title="Divine simplicity">because of the principle that God is truth</a>;  God is able to manipulate nature, as he created it.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The book was given to <a href="/wiki/Charles_the_Bald" title="Charles the Bald">Charles the Bald</a>, the Frankish king, as a present in 844, with the inclusion of a special introduction. The view Paschasius expressed in this work was met with some hostility; <a href="/wiki/Ratramnus" title="Ratramnus">Ratramnus</a> wrote a rebuttal by the same name, by order of <a href="/wiki/Charles_the_Bald" title="Charles the Bald">Charles the Bald</a>, who did not agree with some of the views Paschasius held.  Ratramnus believed that the Eucharist was strictly metaphorical; he focused more on the relationship between faith and the newly emerging science. Shortly thereafter, a third monk joined the debate, <a href="/wiki/Rabanus_Maurus" title="Rabanus Maurus">Rabanus Maurus</a>, which initiated the Carolingian Eucharist Controversy.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Ultimately, however, the king accepted Paschasius' assertion, and the <a href="/wiki/Real_presence_of_Christ_in_the_Eucharist" title="Real presence of Christ in the Eucharist">substantial presence of Christ in the Eucharist</a> became the authoritative belief in the Roman Catholic faith.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Vitae_Adalhardi_et_Walae"><i>Vitae Adalhardi et Walae</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paschasius_Radbertus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Vitae Adalhardi et Walae">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Written in 826 and 836, respectively, <i>Vita Adalhardi</i> and <i>Vita Walae</i> are spiritual biographies of Paschasius' role-models.  They are personal tributes, written for the memory of two fathers, and the patterns of life depicted in them are intended to be followed.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><i>Vita Adalhardi</i> is rather brief; it is a fairly conventional representation of a saint's life. However, the style that Paschasius uses is unique for the time in which it was written. Written in mourning for the loss of his friend, Paschasius compares Adalard to the painter <a href="/wiki/Zeuxis_(painter)" title="Zeuxis (painter)">Zeuxis</a>. As described by <a href="/wiki/Cicero" title="Cicero">Cicero</a>, artists study models to perfect their art; Zeuxis' challenge was to paint the woman, <a href="/wiki/Helen_of_Troy" title="Helen of Troy">Helen of Troy</a>.  Paschasius states that just as Zeuxis studied forms in order to perfect his art, so too does Adalard in trying to reform the image of God in himself. In making this comparison, Paschasius was identified with being a <a href="/wiki/Humanistic" class="mw-redirect" title="Humanistic">humanistic</a> writer of the Carolingian period, as he compared classic and ancient literature with contemporary literature.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Paschasius depicts Adalard as a mirror image of Christ, emphasizing the elements of infinite love and descent into suffering.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>  He also parallels Adalard's role in the church to that of a mother, which is a concept attributed to <a href="/wiki/Cistercian" class="mw-redirect" title="Cistercian">Cistercian</a> spirituality in the 12th century, three hundred years after Paschasius' death.  The grief felt over the death of Adalard is extremely strong in the book – although Paschasius knows that suffering should give way to joy, as depicted by his forefathers, such as <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>, Paschasius' grief for the loss of his friend surpassed that of his literary models.  This style of writing is also not seen elsewhere prior to the 12th century.  Paschasius' justification of excess mourning is his most distinctive contribution to the tradition of consolation literature.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><i>Vita Walae</i> is much longer (about twice as long as <i>Vita Adalhardi</i>), and is structured as a dialogue. In total, there are eight characters represented, presumably monks of Corbie.  These characters are given pseudonyms, probably nor with the intention of masking identities. It is more likely that these pseudonyms were employed to further support Paschasius' interpretation of Wala, as the names were taken from classical texts.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Phrases and passages from a variety of sources are woven into the text (<a href="/w/index.php?title=Acts_of_St._Sebastien&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Acts of St. Sebastien (page does not exist)">Acts of St. Sebastien</a>, <a href="/wiki/The_Book_of_Job" class="mw-redirect" title="The Book of Job">The Book of Job</a>, various comedies of <a href="/wiki/Terence" title="Terence">Terence</a>). Although not displaying information about Wala, these additions reflect Paschasius' own beliefs and his skill at writing.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>  While <i>Vita Adalhardi</i> was written to be in part a funeral eulogy, <i>Vita Walae</i> was  written as a (fairly) accurate depiction of Wala.  Paschasius used sources in writing this biography, a handbook written by Wala, and treatises of the time, probably to show his own views through his depiction of Wala.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_works">Other works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paschasius_Radbertus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Other works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Paschasius has an extensive collection of works, including many <a href="/wiki/Exegeses" class="mw-redirect" title="Exegeses">exegeses</a> on various books of the <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">Bible</a>.  He wrote commentaries on the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_Matthew" title="Gospel of Matthew">Gospel of Matthew</a>, <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Lamentations" title="Book of Lamentations">Lamentations</a>, and an exposition of <a href="/wiki/Psalm_45" title="Psalm 45">Psalm 45</a>, which he dedicated to the nuns at St. Mary at Soissons.  <i>De Partu Virginis</i>, written for his friend Emma, Abbess of St. Mary at Soissons and daughter of Theodrara, describes the lifestyle of nuns.  He also wrote a treatise, titled <i>De Nativitae Sanctae Mariae</i>, regarding the nature of the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a> and the birth of <a href="/wiki/Jesus_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesus Christ">Jesus Christ</a>. Paschasius probably wrote much more, but none of it has survived through the centuries.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Paschasius wrote a commentary on <i>Revelation</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Theological_contributions">Theological contributions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paschasius_Radbertus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Theological contributions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Understanding_of_the_human_body">Understanding of the human body</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paschasius_Radbertus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Understanding of the human body">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In opposition to other <a href="/wiki/Carolingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Carolingian">Carolingian</a> authors, Paschasius locates the <i><a href="/wiki/Imago_Dei" class="mw-redirect" title="Imago Dei">Imago Dei</a></i> (the "Image of God") in the whole human being – body as well as soul. This view is in alignment with that of the second-century <a href="/wiki/Church_Father" class="mw-redirect" title="Church Father">Church Father</a> <a href="/wiki/Irenaeus" title="Irenaeus">Irenaeus</a>. Irenaeus believed that Jesus was the physical embodiment of God; the son is the image of the father. As such, humans represent the image of God not only in soul, but in flesh as well. This view is in opposition to the more accepted view of <a href="/wiki/Origen_of_Alexandria" class="mw-redirect" title="Origen of Alexandria">Origen of Alexandria</a>, who believed that the physical body had no part in the image-relationship.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Unlike other theologians of the time, Paschasius does not equate the <a href="/wiki/Sanctification" title="Sanctification">sanctification</a> process with a metaphysical detachment of the body and the soul. Instead, he believes that the <a href="/wiki/Human_condition" title="Human condition">human condition</a> (existing in a physical form) can contribute positively to achieving sanctification. However, he did believe in a form of mitigated dualism, in which the soul plays a larger part in the process than the body.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> Paschasius believes that life is an opportunity to practice for death; however, the concept that the body is a prison for the soul is practically non-existent in his work, and probably only occurs due to pressure from his peers. Even though he believed that the body has a role in one's sanctification process, he also acknowledged that flesh struggles against God, and thus has the ability to be corrupted.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Understanding_of_Christ.27s_body"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Understanding_of_Christ's_body">Understanding of Christ's body</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paschasius_Radbertus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Understanding of Christ&#039;s body">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Paschasius believed in a distinction between <i>veritas</i> (truth) and <i>figura</i> (form, or appearance). Christ's descent from heaven to earth was a declension from truth to appearance, from the realm of perfection to the realm of imperfection.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> This would imply that Jesus in flesh is false, and imperfect; however, Paschasius asserted that not every figure is false. Christ is simultaneously both truth and figure because his external, physical self is the figure of the truth, the physical manifestation of the truth that exists in the soul.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> The person that was Jesus was subject to human needs, just like the rest of humanity. He required to eat, to sleep, and to be in company with others. In addition to this, however, he also performed miracles. These behaviours which Jesus exhibited imply a duality in the concept of "Word made flesh". Miracles, until then only performed by God, the non-physical Truth or Word, were suddenly performed by a physical human being.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> The relationship between Jesus' humanity and his divinity is rather convoluted; however, it is analogous to the relation of figures (written letters) of words to their spoken counterparts. Therefore, Jesus in physical form is the visual representation, T-R-U-T-H, while his divinity is the spoken sound of those written letters together as a word.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
