<p><b>Peter Nolasco</b>, O. de M. (<i>Pere Nolasc</i> in <a href="/wiki/Catalan_language" title="Catalan language">Catalan</a>, <i>Pierre Nolasque</i> in French and <i>Pedro Nolasco</i> in <a href="/wiki/Spanish_language" title="Spanish language">Spanish</a>; 1189 – 6 May 1256) was a <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic</a> priest known for founding the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_the_Blessed_Virgin_Mary_of_Mercy" title="Order of the Blessed Virgin Mary of Mercy">Royal and Military Order of Our Lady of Mercy of the Redemption of the Captives</a> (the Mercedarians) with approval by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_IX" title="Pope Gregory IX">Pope Gregory IX</a> in 1230.
</p><p>Though there is debate about whether Nolasco was born in France or Spain, is clear that he was in <a href="/wiki/Barcelona" title="Barcelona">Barcelona</a> when he was a teenager, became part of an army fighting <a href="/wiki/Moors" title="Moors">the Moors</a> in the Iberian peninsula, and was appointed tutor to the young king, <a href="/wiki/James_I_of_Aragon" title="James I of Aragon">James I of Aragon</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background">Background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Nolasco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Between the eighth and the fifteenth centuries, medieval Europe was in a state of intermittent warfare between the Christian kingdoms of southern Europe and the Muslim polities of North Africa, Southern France, Sicily and portions of Spain. According to James W. Brodman, the threat of capture, whether by pirates or coastal raiders, or during one of the region's intermittent wars, was a continuous threat to residents of <a href="/wiki/Catalonia" title="Catalonia">Catalonia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Languedoc" title="Languedoc">Languedoc</a>, and the other coastal provinces of medieval Christian Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-brodman_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-brodman-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Raids by militias, bands, and armies from both sides was an almost annual occurrence.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Alfonso VIII's incursions into Andalusia in 1182 are said to have brought him over 2,000 captives and thousands in ransom,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> while in 1191 the governor of Córdoba, took 3,000 prisoners and 15,000 head of cattle in an attack on Silves.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> For over six hundred years, these constant armed confrontations produced numerous war prisoners on both sides. Any Christian or Muslim near the ever-shifting territorial borders was in danger of capture. Captives were considered war booty. Those not ransomed were sold as slaves. In the lands of Visigothic Spain, both Christian and Moslem societies had become accustomed to the buying and selling of captives. In the thirteenth century, in addition to spices, slaves constituted one of the goods of the flourishing trade between Christian and Moslem ports.<sup id="cite_ref-friars_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-friars-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Nolasco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Visi%C3%B3n_de_San_Pedro_Nolasco.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/53/Visi%C3%B3n_de_San_Pedro_Nolasco.jpg/250px-Visi%C3%B3n_de_San_Pedro_Nolasco.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="200" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/53/Visi%C3%B3n_de_San_Pedro_Nolasco.jpg/375px-Visi%C3%B3n_de_San_Pedro_Nolasco.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/53/Visi%C3%B3n_de_San_Pedro_Nolasco.jpg/500px-Visi%C3%B3n_de_San_Pedro_Nolasco.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3051" data-file-height="2438" /></a><figcaption>San Pedro Nolasco has a vision of <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Sources for the origins of the Mercedarians are scant and almost nothing is known of the founder, Peter Nolasco. A narrative developed between the fifteenth and early seventeenth centuries that culminated in Nolasco's canonization as a saint in 1628. The two earliest accounts, those written by the mid-fifteenth-century Mercedarian chroniclers Nadal Gaver and Pedro Cijar, declare the founder, the son of a merchant, to be from the French village of Mas-Saintes-Puelles, near the town of Castelnaudary,<sup id="cite_ref-allaria_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-allaria-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> in the modern department of Aude. A fuller account of his life by Francisco Zumel appeared in 1588 and is the basis for the biography given in the Acta sanctorum. 
</p><p>According to Butler, Nolasco followed <a href="/wiki/Simon_de_Montfort,_5th_Earl_of_Leicester" title="Simon de Montfort, 5th Earl of Leicester">Simon de Montfort</a> in the war against the <a href="/wiki/Albigensians" class="mw-redirect" title="Albigensians">Albigensians</a>. In the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Muret" title="Battle of Muret">Battle of Muret</a> Montfort had defeated and killed King <a href="/wiki/Peter_II_of_Aragon" title="Peter II of Aragon">Peter II of Aragon</a>, and took his son James prisoner, a child of six years old and sent him back to Aragon with Nolasco, then twenty-five years old, appointed his tutor.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After making a pilgrimage to <a href="/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Montserrat" class="mw-redirect" title="Our Lady of Montserrat">Our Lady of Montserrat</a>, Nolasco went to Barcelona where he began to practice various works of charity. Nolasco became concerned with the plight of Christians captured in Moorish raids and decided to establish a religious order to succor these unfortunates.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Ransomer">Ransomer</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Nolasco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Ransomer">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Pedro_Nolasco_-_Capilla_de_Santa_Teresa_-_La_Mezquita_-_C%C3%B3rdoba.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/St._Pedro_Nolasco_-_Capilla_de_Santa_Teresa_-_La_Mezquita_-_C%C3%B3rdoba.JPG/220px-St._Pedro_Nolasco_-_Capilla_de_Santa_Teresa_-_La_Mezquita_-_C%C3%B3rdoba.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="331" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/St._Pedro_Nolasco_-_Capilla_de_Santa_Teresa_-_La_Mezquita_-_C%C3%B3rdoba.JPG/330px-St._Pedro_Nolasco_-_Capilla_de_Santa_Teresa_-_La_Mezquita_-_C%C3%B3rdoba.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/St._Pedro_Nolasco_-_Capilla_de_Santa_Teresa_-_La_Mezquita_-_C%C3%B3rdoba.JPG/440px-St._Pedro_Nolasco_-_Capilla_de_Santa_Teresa_-_La_Mezquita_-_C%C3%B3rdoba.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1360" data-file-height="2048" /></a><figcaption>St. Pedro Nolasco – Capilla de Santa Teresa – La Catedral – Córdoba</figcaption></figure>
<p>Nolasco began ransoming Christian captives in 1203. In 1218 <a href="/wiki/Raymond_of_Penyafort" title="Raymond of Penyafort">Raymond of Penyafort</a> started a lay confraternity for ransoming slaves from the Moors and Peter became the procurator for this.<sup id="cite_ref-duffy_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-duffy-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Peter's plan, was to establish a well-structured and stable redemptive religious order under the patronage of Mary.<sup id="cite_ref-allaria_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-allaria-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1230 Nolasco became the first Superior and also held the position of Ransomer, the order being concerned with the freeing of Christian prisoners from the Moors. He worked first in the Kingdom of Valencia and then in Granada. He made several other journeys to the coasts of Spain, besides a voyage to Algiers.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Raymond_Nonnatus" title="Raymond Nonnatus">Raymond Nonnatus</a> later succeeded to this position.
</p><p>The order originally attracted young noblemen whose heritage equipped them to practically address the matter of ransom,<sup id="cite_ref-stevens_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stevens-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> and friars who were in holy orders, and attended the choir. The knights were to guard the coasts against the Saracens, but were obliged to choir when not on duty. Nolasco himself was never ordained priest; and the first seven generals or commanders were chosen out of the knights, though the friars were always more numerous.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The founder required of himself and his followers a special vow in addition to the usual three-to devote their "whole substance and very liberty to the ransoming of slaves," even to the point of acting as hostages in order to free others. According to records, the Order of the Blessed Virgin Mary for the Ransom of Captives accomplished approximately 70,000 rescues-some 2,700 during the founder's lifetime.<sup id="cite_ref-stevens_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stevens-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The order elected a habit of white, signifying innocence. Some histories claim that Mary provided such guidance during her appearance to Nolasco. An enthusiastic King James authorized the members to wear on their and long scapulars his own distinguished arms of Aragon.<sup id="cite_ref-stevens_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stevens-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Nolasco died in 1256 in Barcelona, seven years after having resigned as Superior. According to tradition he died on 25 December, but recent studies of the <a href="/wiki/Royal_Archives_of_Barcelona" class="mw-redirect" title="Royal Archives of Barcelona">Royal Archives of Barcelona</a> have indicated that he died on 6 May.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Nolasco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nolasco was canonized by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_VIII" title="Pope Urban VIII">Pope Urban VIII</a>. His festival was appointed by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_VIII" title="Pope Clement VIII">Pope Clement VIII</a> to be kept on January 31, which was later moved to 28 January, when the former date was assigned to the liturgical celebration of <a href="/wiki/John_Bosco" title="John Bosco">John Bosco</a> (see <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar_as_in_1954" class="mw-redirect" title="General Roman Calendar as in 1954">General Roman Calendar as in 1954</a>). He is presently inscribed in the Roman <a href="/wiki/Martyrology" title="Martyrology">Martyrology</a>, the official list of saints, on 6 May, the day of his death.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Nolasco&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Order spread through most of Spain and was closely associated with the "Reconquista" of the southern provinces under Ferdinand and Isabella. The order flourished in France, England, Germany, Portugal, and Spain. From Spain, they provided a missionary presence in the New World.<sup id="cite_ref-duffy_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-duffy-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
