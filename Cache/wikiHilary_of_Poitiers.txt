<p><b>Hilary of Poitiers</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Hilarius Pictaviensis</i>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;310</span>&#160;– c.<span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;367</span>)<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> was <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Poitiers" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Poitiers">Bishop of Poitiers</a> and  a <a href="/wiki/Doctor_of_the_Church" title="Doctor of the Church">Doctor of the Church</a>. He was sometimes referred to as the "Hammer of the <a href="/wiki/Arians" class="mw-redirect" title="Arians">Arians</a>" (<span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Malleus Arianorum</i></span>) and the "<a href="/wiki/Athanasius_of_Alexandria" title="Athanasius of Alexandria">Athanasius</a> of the West".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His name comes from the Latin word for happy or cheerful. In addition to his important work as bishop, Hilary was married and the father of <a href="/wiki/Abra_of_Poitiers" title="Abra of Poitiers">Abra of Poitiers</a>, a nun and saint who became known for her charity.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Hilary was born at <a href="/wiki/Poitiers" title="Poitiers">Poitiers</a> either at the end of the 3rd or beginning of the 4th century A.D.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010302_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010302-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> His parents were <a href="/wiki/Pagan" class="mw-redirect" title="Pagan">pagans</a> of distinction. He received a good pagan education,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> which included a high level of Greek.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He studied, later on, the Old and New Testament writings, with the result that he abandoned his <a href="/wiki/Neo-Platonism" class="mw-redirect" title="Neo-Platonism">Neo-Platonism</a> for <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a>, and with his wife and his daughter (traditionally named <a href="/wiki/Saint_Abra" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Abra">Saint Abra</a>), was <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptized</a> and received into the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Church</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Christians of Poitiers so respected Hilary that about 350 or 353,<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> they unanimously elected him their bishop. At that time <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a> threatened to overrun the Western Church; Hilary undertook to repel the disruption. One of his first steps was to secure the <a href="/wiki/Excommunication" title="Excommunication">excommunication</a>, by those of the Gallican hierarchy who still remained orthodox Christians, of Saturninus, the <a href="/wiki/Arian" class="mw-redirect" title="Arian">Arian</a> <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Arles" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Arles">Bishop of Arles</a>, and of <a href="/wiki/Ursacius_of_Singidunum" title="Ursacius of Singidunum">Ursacius of Singidunum</a> and <a href="/wiki/Valens_of_Mursa" title="Valens of Mursa">Valens of Mursa</a>, two of his prominent supporters.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>About the same time, Hilary wrote to Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantius_II" title="Constantius II">Constantius II</a> a remonstrance against the persecutions by which the Arians had sought to crush their opponents (<i>Ad Constantium Augustum liber primus</i>, of which the most probable date is 355).<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (July 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> Other Historians refer to this first book to Constantius as "Book Against Valens," of which only fragments are extant.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> His efforts did not succeed at first, for at the <a href="/wiki/Synod" title="Synod">synod</a> of Biterrae (<a href="/wiki/B%C3%A9ziers" title="Béziers">Béziers</a>), summoned by the emperor in 356 with the professed purpose of settling the longstanding dispute, an imperial <a href="/wiki/Rescript" title="Rescript">rescript</a> banished the new bishop, along with Rhodanus of Toulouse, to <a href="/wiki/Phrygia" title="Phrygia">Phrygia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458–459_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458–459-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hilary spent nearly four years in exile, although the reasons for this banishment remain obscure. The traditional explanation is that Hilary was exiled for refusing to subscribe to the condemnation of Athanasius and the Nicene faith. More recently several scholars have suggested that political opposition to Constantius and support of the usurper <a href="/wiki/Claudius_Silvanus" class="mw-redirect" title="Claudius Silvanus">Silvanus</a> may have led to Hilary's exile.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010302_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010302-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_exile">In exile</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: In exile">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>While in <a href="/wiki/Phrygia" title="Phrygia">Phrygia</a>, however, he continued to govern his diocese, as well as writing two of the most important of his contributions to dogmatic and polemical theology: the <i>De synodis</i> or <i>De fide Orientalium</i>, an epistle addressed in 358 to the Semi-Arian bishops in <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a>, <a href="/wiki/Germania" title="Germania">Germania</a> and <a href="/wiki/Roman_Britain" title="Roman Britain">Britain</a>, analyzing the views of the Eastern bishops on the <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Nicaea" title="First Council of Nicaea">Nicene</a> controversy.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> In reviewing the professions of faith of the Oriental bishops in the Councils of <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Ancyra" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Ancyra">Ancyra</a>, <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Antioch" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Antioch">Antioch</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Councils_of_Sirmium" title="Councils of Sirmium">Sirmium</a>, he sought to show that sometimes the difference between certain doctrines and orthodox beliefs was rather in the words than in the ideas, which led to his counseling the bishops of the West to be more reserved in their condemnation.<sup id="cite_ref-Clugnet_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Clugnet-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>De trinitate libri XII</i>, composed in 359 and 360, was the first successful expression in Latin of that Council's theological subtleties originally elaborated in Greek. Although some members of Hilary's own party thought the first had shown too great a forbearance towards the Arians, Hilary replied to their criticisms in the <i>Apologetica ad reprehensores libri de synodis responsa</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In his classic introduction to the works of Hilary, Watson summarizes Hilary's points:
</p>
<dl><dd>“They were the forerunners of Antichrist. . . . They bear themselves not as bishops of Christ but as priests of Antichrist. This is not random abuse, but sober recognition of the fact, stated by St. John, that there are many Antichrists. For these men assume the cloak of piety, and pretend to preach the Gospel, with the one object of inducing others to deny Christ. It was the misery and folly of the day that men endeavoured to promote the cause of God by human means and the favour of the world. Hilary asks bishops, who believe in their office, whether the Apostles had secular support when by their preaching they converted the greater part of mankind. . . .</dd>
<dd>“The Church seeks for secular support, and in so doing insults Christ by the implication that His support is insufficient. She in her turn holds out the threat of exile and prison.  It was her endurance of these that drew men to her; now she imposes her faith by violence.  She craves for favours at the hand of her communicants; once it was her consecration that she braved the threatenings of persecutors.  Bishops in exile spread the Faith; now it is she that exiles bishops.  She boasts that the world loves her; the world's hatred was the evidence that she was Christ's. . . . The time of Antichrist, disguised as an angel of light, has come.  The true Christ is hidden from almost every mind and heart.  Antichrist is now obscuring the truth that he may assert falsehood hereafter."<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup></dd></dl>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:162px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Constantius_II_-_solidus_-_antioch_RIC_viii_025.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e5/Constantius_II_-_solidus_-_antioch_RIC_viii_025.jpg/160px-Constantius_II_-_solidus_-_antioch_RIC_viii_025.jpg" decoding="async" width="160" height="77" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e5/Constantius_II_-_solidus_-_antioch_RIC_viii_025.jpg/240px-Constantius_II_-_solidus_-_antioch_RIC_viii_025.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e5/Constantius_II_-_solidus_-_antioch_RIC_viii_025.jpg/320px-Constantius_II_-_solidus_-_antioch_RIC_viii_025.jpg 2x" data-file-width="500" data-file-height="240" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Constantius_II_-_solidus_-_antioch_RIC_viii_025.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Constantius II coin.</div></div></div><p>Hilary also attended several synods during his time in exile, including the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Seleucia" title="Council of Seleucia">council at Seleucia</a> (359) which saw the triumph of the <i>homoion</i> party and the forbidding of all discussion of the divine substance.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> In 360, Hilary tried unsuccessfully to secure a personal audience with <a href="/wiki/Constantius_II" title="Constantius II">Constantius</a>, as well as to address the council which met at <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> in 360. When this council ratified the decisions of <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Rimini" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Rimini">Ariminum</a> and Seleucia, Hilary responded with the bitter <i>In Constantium</i>, which attacked the Emperor Constantius as Antichrist and persecutor of orthodox Christians.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010302_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010302-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Hilary's urgent and repeated requests for public debates with his opponents, especially with Ursacius and Valens, proved at last so inconvenient that he was sent back to his diocese, which he appears to have reached about 361, within a very short time of the accession of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Julian_the_Apostate" class="mw-redirect" title="Julian the Apostate">Julian</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459_11-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Later_life">Later life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Later life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On returning to his diocese in 361, Hilary spent most of the first two or three years trying to persuade the local clergy that the <i>homoion</i> confession was merely a cover for traditional Arian subordinationism. Thus, a number of synods in Gaul condemned the creed promulgated at <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Rimini" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Rimini">Council of Ariminium</a> (359).<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010303_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010303-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In about 360 or 361, with Hilary's encouragement, <a href="/wiki/Martin_of_Tours" title="Martin of Tours">Martin</a>, the future <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Tours">bishop of Tours</a>, founded a <a href="/wiki/Ligug%C3%A9_Abbey" title="Ligugé Abbey">monastery at Ligugé</a> in his diocese.
</p><p>In 364, Hilary extended his efforts once more beyond Gaul. He impeached <a href="/wiki/Auxentius_of_Milan" title="Auxentius of Milan">Auxentius</a>, <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Milan" title="Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Milan">bishop of Milan</a>, a man high in the imperial favour, as heterodox. Emperor <a href="/wiki/Valentinian_I" title="Valentinian I">Valentinian I</a> accordingly summoned Hilary to <a href="/wiki/Milan" title="Milan">Milan</a> to there maintain his charges. However, the supposed heretic gave satisfactory answers to all the questions proposed. Hilary denounced Auxentius as a hypocrite as he himself was ignominiously expelled from Milan. Upon returning home, Hilary in 365, published the <i>Contra Arianos vel Auxentium Mediolanensem liber,</i> describing his unsuccessful efforts against Auxentius. He also (but perhaps at a somewhat earlier date) published the <i>Contra Constantium Augustum liber</i>, accusing the lately deceased emperor as having been the Antichrist, a rebel against <a href="/wiki/God" title="God">God</a>, "a tyrant whose sole object had been to make a gift to the devil of that world for which <a href="/wiki/Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Christ">Christ</a> had suffered."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459_11-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>, Hilary died in Poitiers in 367.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Divi_Hilarii_Pictavorum_episcopi_De_Trinitate.tif" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/Divi_Hilarii_Pictavorum_episcopi_De_Trinitate.tif/lossy-page1-220px-Divi_Hilarii_Pictavorum_episcopi_De_Trinitate.tif.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="347" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/Divi_Hilarii_Pictavorum_episcopi_De_Trinitate.tif/lossy-page1-330px-Divi_Hilarii_Pictavorum_episcopi_De_Trinitate.tif.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/Divi_Hilarii_Pictavorum_episcopi_De_Trinitate.tif/lossy-page1-440px-Divi_Hilarii_Pictavorum_episcopi_De_Trinitate.tif.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3561" data-file-height="5614" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Divi_Hilarii_Pictavorum_episcopi_De_Trinitate.tif" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Opera omnia</i> (1523)</div></div></div>
<p>While Hilary closely followed the two great Alexandrians, <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a> and <a href="/wiki/Athanasius_of_Alexandria" title="Athanasius of Alexandria">Athanasius</a>, in exegesis and Christology respectively, his work shows many traces of vigorous independent thought.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459_11-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Exegetical">Exegetical</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Exegetical">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Among Hilary's earliest writings, completed some time before his exile in 356, is his <i>Commentarius in Evangelium Matthaei</i>, an allegorical exegesis of the first <a href="/wiki/Gospel" title="Gospel">Gospel</a>. This is the first Latin <a href="/wiki/Biblical_commentary" class="mw-redirect" title="Biblical commentary">commentary</a> on <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_Matthew" title="Gospel of Matthew">Matthew</a> to have survived in its entirety. Hilary's commentary was strongly influenced by Tertullian and Cyprian, and made use of several classical writers, including Cicero, Quintilian, Pliny and the Roman historians.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010303_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010303-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hilary's expositions of the <a href="/wiki/Psalms" title="Psalms">Psalms</a>, <i>Tractatus super Psalmos</i>, largely follow <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>, and were composed some time after Hilary returned from exile in 360.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459_11-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Since Jerome found the work incomplete,<sup id="cite_ref-Vir_Ill_100_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vir_Ill_100-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> no one knows whether Hilary originally commented on the whole Psalter. Now extant are the commentaries on Psalms 1, 2, 9, 13, 14, 51–69, 91, and 118–150.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010303_15-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010303-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The third surviving exegetical writing by Hilary is the <i>Tractatus mysteriorum</i>, preserved in a single manuscript first published in 1887.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010303_15-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010303-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Because <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine</a> cites part of the commentary on <a href="/wiki/Epistle_to_the_Romans" title="Epistle to the Romans">Romans</a> as by "Sanctus Hilarius" it has been ascribed by various critics at different times to almost every known Hilary.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Theological">Theological</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Theological">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Hilary's major theological work was the twelve books now known as <i>De Trinitate</i>. This was composed largely during his exile, though perhaps not completed until his return to Gaul in 360.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010304_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010304-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Another important work is <i>De synodis</i>, written early in 359 in preparation for the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Rimini" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Rimini">councils of Ariminium</a> and Seleucia.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010304_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010304-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Historical_works_and_hymns">Historical works and hymns</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Historical works and hymns">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Various writings comprise Hilary's 'historical' works. These include the <i>Liber II ad Constantium imperatorem</i>, the <i>Liber in Constantium inperatorem</i>, <i>Contra Arianos vel Auxentium Mediolanensem liber</i>, and the various documents relating to the Arian controversy in <i>Fragmenta historica</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHunter2010304_18-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunter2010304-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Some consider Hilary as the first Latin Christian hymn writer, because Jerome said Hilary produced a <i>liber hymnorum</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-Vir_Ill_100_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vir_Ill_100-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Three hymns are attributed to him, though none are indisputable.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Reputation_and_veneration">Reputation and veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Reputation and veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Hilary is the pre-eminent Latin writer of the 4th century (before <a href="/wiki/Ambrose" title="Ambrose">Ambrose</a>). <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</a> called him "the illustrious doctor of the churches", and his works continued to be highly influential in later centuries. <a href="/wiki/Venantius_Fortunatus" title="Venantius Fortunatus">Venantius Fortunatus</a> wrote a <i><a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">vita</a></i> of Hilary by 550, but few now consider it reliable. More trustworthy are the notices in <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Saint Jerome</a> (<a href="/wiki/Jerome%27s_De_Viris_Illustribus" class="mw-redirect" title="Jerome&#39;s De Viris Illustribus">De vir. illus.</a> 100), <a href="/wiki/Sulpicius_Severus" title="Sulpicius Severus">Sulpicius Severus</a> (<i>Chron.</i> ii. 39–45) and in Hilary's own writings.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459_11-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911459-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_IX" title="Pope Pius IX">Pope Pius IX</a> formally recognized him as a <a href="/wiki/Doctor_of_the_Church" title="Doctor of the Church">Doctor of the Church</a> in 1851.
</p><p>In the Roman calendar of saints, Hilary's feast day is on 13 January, 14 January in the pre-1970 form of the calendar.  The spring terms of the English and Irish Law Courts and <a href="/wiki/University_of_Oxford" title="University of Oxford">Oxford</a> and <a href="/wiki/University_of_Dublin" title="University of Dublin">Dublin</a> Universities are called the <a href="/wiki/Hilary_term" title="Hilary term">Hilary term</a> since they begin on approximately this date.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>  Some consider Saint Hilary of Poitiers the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Lawyer" title="Lawyer">lawyers</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hilary is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Lesser_Festival_(Anglicanism)" title="Lesser Festival (Anglicanism)">Lesser Festival</a> on <a href="/wiki/January_13" title="January 13">13 January</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconography">Iconography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Iconography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>From his writing St. Hilary's symbol came to be three books and a quill pen.<sup id="cite_ref-hisperia_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hisperia-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Dedications">Dedications</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilary_of_Poitiers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Dedications">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><a href="/wiki/Sulpicius_Severus" title="Sulpicius Severus">Sulpicius Severus</a>' <i>Vita Sancti Martini</i> led to a cult of Saint Hilary as well as of <a href="/wiki/St._Martin_of_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Martin of Tours">St. Martin of Tours</a> which spread early to western <a href="/wiki/Great_Britain" title="Great Britain">Britain</a>. The villages of <a href="/wiki/St_Hilary,_Cornwall" title="St Hilary, Cornwall">St Hilary</a> in <a href="/wiki/Cornwall" title="Cornwall">Cornwall</a> and <a href="/wiki/Glamorgan" title="Glamorgan">Glamorgan</a> and that of <a href="/wiki/Llanilar" title="Llanilar">Llanilar</a> in <a href="/wiki/Ceredigion" title="Ceredigion">Ceredigion</a> bear his name.
</p><p>In France most <a href="/wiki/Saint-Hilaire_(disambiguation)" class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" title="Saint-Hilaire (disambiguation)">dedications to Saint Hilary</a> are styled "Saint-Hilaire" and lie west (and north) of the <a href="/wiki/Massif_Central" title="Massif Central">Massif Central</a>; the cult in this region eventually extended to <a href="/wiki/Canada" title="Canada">Canada</a>.
</p><p>In northwest <a href="/wiki/Italy" title="Italy">Italy</a> the church of Sant’Ilario at <a href="/wiki/Casale_Monferrato" title="Casale Monferrato">Casale Monferrato</a> was dedicated to St. Hilary as early as 380.
</p><p>In southern <a href="/wiki/Spain" title="Spain">Spain</a> the feast of San Hilario is celebrated in the village of <a href="/wiki/Comares" title="Comares">Comares</a> with a mass, a procession and local <a href="/wiki/Verdiales" title="Verdiales">Verdiales</a> dances.
</p>
