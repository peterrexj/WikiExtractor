<p><b>William of Norwich</b> (2 February 1132 – <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 22 March 1144) was an English boy whose disappearance and killing was, at the time, attributed to the <a href="/wiki/Jews" title="Jews">Jewish</a> community of <a href="/wiki/Norwich" title="Norwich">Norwich</a>. It is the first known <a href="/wiki/Medieval" class="mw-redirect" title="Medieval">medieval</a> accusation against Jews of <a href="/wiki/Blood_libel" title="Blood libel">ritual murder</a>. These accusations occurred at a time that religious centres had begun to compete for visitors and their donations by setting up local shrines, after martyrdoms and other mysterious events. In addition, the monks of Norwich which promoted the supposed martyrdom lacked a patron saint and associated cult; it has been suggested that they exploited the accusations for their own institutional benefit.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>William was an apprentice <a href="/wiki/Tanning_(leather)" title="Tanning (leather)">tanner</a> who regularly came into contact with Jews and visited their homes as part of his trade. His murder remains unsolved; the local community of Norwich attributed the boy's death to the Jews, though the local authorities would not convict them for lack of proof. William was <a href="/wiki/Folk_saint" title="Folk saint">acclaimed as a saint</a> in Norwich, with <a href="/wiki/Miracle" title="Miracle">miracles</a> attributed to him. 
</p><p>William's story was told in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Life_and_Miracles_of_St_William_of_Norwich" title="The Life and Miracles of St William of Norwich">The Life and Miracles of St William of Norwich</a></i>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMonmouth2011_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMonmouth2011-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Global_net_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Global_net-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> a multi-volume <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> work by <a href="/wiki/Thomas_of_Monmouth" title="Thomas of Monmouth">Thomas of Monmouth</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> in the Norwich <a href="/wiki/Benedictine_monastery" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine monastery">Benedictine monastery</a>. Monmouth started <i>The Life</i> in 1149/50, after interest in the events had died down, most likely because the monks needed to give the cult new impetus. He completed volume seven by 1173.<sup id="cite_ref-Langmuir_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Langmuir-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Augustus_Jessopp" title="Augustus Jessopp">Augustus Jessopp</a> (1823–1914), one of the editors of the first printed edition of Monmouth's work, describes Monmouth as belonging to the class of those who are "deceivers and being deceived."<sup id="cite_ref-Halsall_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Halsall-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Accounts_of_his_life_and_death">Accounts of his life and death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Norwich&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Accounts of his life and death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> contains an account of the murder which places it c. 1137, several years earlier than Thomas of Monmouth. The entire account in the Chronicle is:
</p>
<dl><dd><i>In his reign the Jews of Norwich bought a Christian child before Easter, and tortured him after the same manner as our Lord was tortured; and on Long-Friday hanged him on a rood, in mockery of our Lord, and afterwards buried him.  They supposed that it would be concealed, but our Lord showed that he was a holy martyr.  And the monks took him, and buried him with high honour in the minster.  And through our Lord he worketh wonderful and manifold miracles, and is called St. William.</i><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></dd></dl><p>
Since most information about William's life comes from Thomas of Monmouth, it is difficult to distinguish the facts of the case from the story of martyrdom created around it by Monmouth. </p><figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Death_of_William_of_Norwich.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d1/Death_of_William_of_Norwich.jpg/220px-Death_of_William_of_Norwich.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="322" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d1/Death_of_William_of_Norwich.jpg/330px-Death_of_William_of_Norwich.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d1/Death_of_William_of_Norwich.jpg 2x" data-file-width="388" data-file-height="568" /></a><figcaption>The crucifixion of William depicted on a <a href="/wiki/Rood_screen" title="Rood screen">rood screen</a> in Holy Trinity church, <a href="/wiki/Loddon,_Norfolk" title="Loddon, Norfolk">Loddon, Norfolk</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>Thomas of Monmouth arrived in Norwich around 1150. At the urging of the Bishop, he decided to investigate the killing by interviewing surviving witnesses. He also spoke to people whom he identified as "converted Jews" who provided him with inside information about events within the Jewish community. He wrote an <a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_Latin" title="Ecclesiastical Latin">Ecclesiastical Latin</a> account of the crime in his book <i>The Life and Miracles of St William of Norwich</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Monmouth, William was born on 2 February 1132 to a local couple and was apprenticed to a <a href="/wiki/Skinner_(profession)" title="Skinner (profession)">skinner</a> and tanner of hides, often dealing with local Jews. Monmouth attributes the working relationship between William and the Jewish community alternately to William's skill and to Jewish "avarice."<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Monmouth writes that shortly before his murder, William's mother was approached by a man who claimed to be a cook working for the <a href="/wiki/Archdeacon_of_Norwich" title="Archdeacon of Norwich">Archdeacon of Norwich</a>. He offered William a job in the Archdeacon's kitchens. William's mother was paid three shillings to let her son go. William later visited his aunt in the company of this man. His aunt was apparently suspicious, and asked her daughter to follow them after they left. They were then seen entering the house of a local Jew. This was the last time William was seen alive; it was <a href="/wiki/Holy_Tuesday" title="Holy Tuesday">Holy Tuesday</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Monmouth describes in detail the ritual torture and murder of William, as a reenactment of the crucifixion of Jesus. In his account of the murder, Monmouth writes that "having shaved his head, they stabbed it with countless thornpoints, and made the blood come horribly from the wounds they made...some of those present ad judged him to be fixed to a cross in mockery of the Lord's Passion...”<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> William's body was later said to have been found in Thorpe Wood with a crown of thorns atop his head. 
</p><p>Monmouth writes that William was crucified, "in mockery of the Lord's passion." Monmouth also claims "they inflicted a frightful wound in his left side," similar to that which was described in the biblical account of the death of Jesus.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>According to Monmouth, the twelve-year-old William's body was found on <a href="/wiki/Holy_Saturday" title="Holy Saturday">Holy Saturday</a> in <a href="/wiki/Mousehold_Heath" title="Mousehold Heath">Mousehold Heath</a>, part of <a href="/wiki/Thorpe_Wood" title="Thorpe Wood">Thorpe Wood</a>, outside Norwich.<sup id="cite_ref-Langmuir_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Langmuir-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-jew_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jew-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> A local nun saw the body, but did not initially contact anyone. A forester named Henry de Sprowston then came across it. He noted injuries which suggested a violent death and the fact that the boy appeared to have been gagged with a wooden <a href="/wiki/Carding" title="Carding">teasel</a>. William was wearing a jacket and shoes.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After consultation with the <a href="/wiki/Parish_priest" class="mw-redirect" title="Parish priest">parish priest</a>, it was decided to bury the body on <a href="/wiki/Easter_Monday" title="Easter Monday">Easter Monday</a>. In the meanwhile, local people came to look at it, and William was recognised. The body was then buried at the murder site, and the following day, members of William's family, one of whom was the priest, Godwin Stuart, arrived to confirm the identity of the body. They exhumed it and then reburied it with proper ceremony.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>William's family and their fellow English quickly blamed the local Jewish community for the crime and demanded justice from the <a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_court" title="Ecclesiastical court">ecclesiastical court</a> of Bishop <a href="/wiki/William_de_Turbeville" title="William de Turbeville">William de Turbeville</a>. Members of the Jewish community were asked by the Bishop to attend the court and submit to a <a href="/wiki/Trial_by_ordeal" title="Trial by ordeal">trial by ordeal</a>, but the local Norman <a href="/wiki/Sheriff_of_Norfolk_and_Suffolk" title="Sheriff of Norfolk and Suffolk">sheriff</a>, <a href="/wiki/Robert_fitz_Walter_of_Horsham#Marriage_and_issue" title="Robert fitz Walter of Horsham">John de Chesney</a>, advised them that the ecclesiastical court had no jurisdiction over them, as they were not Christians.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He then took the Jews into protection in <a href="/wiki/Norwich_Castle" title="Norwich Castle">Norwich Castle</a>. After the situation had calmed down, they returned to their homes. The issue was revived two years later, when a member of the Jewish community was murdered in an unrelated incident. <a href="/wiki/Stephen,_King_of_England" title="Stephen, King of England">King Stephen</a> agreed to look into the matter, but later decided not to pursue it.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the meanwhile, William's body had been moved to the monks' cemetery. Bishop de Turbeville and other members of the local clergy attempted to create a <i><a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cultus</a></i> around him as a <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyr" title="Christian martyr">Christian martyr</a>, but this plan did not succeed. There was no evidence in the initial accusations against the Jews that the murder was related to religious activity of any kind, but as the cult developed, so did a story of how and why William was killed.<sup id="cite_ref-Langmuir_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Langmuir-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Thomas of Monmouth's account is attributed to the testimony of a monk and former Jew named Theobald of Cambridge. Theobald alleged that the murder was a <a href="/wiki/Human_sacrifice" title="Human sacrifice">human sacrifice</a> and that the "ancient writings of his fathers" required the yearly killing of a Christian. This was allegedly for two reasons: to one day return to the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Land" title="Holy Land">Holy Land</a> during the <a href="/wiki/Messianic_Age" title="Messianic Age">Messianic Age</a> and to punish <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">Jesus</a> Christ for the persecution that the <a href="/wiki/Jewish_people" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish people">Jewish people</a> continued to experience at the hands of his followers. There is, however, no such commandment for <a href="/wiki/Human_sacrifice" title="Human sacrifice">human sacrifice</a> anywhere in <a href="/wiki/Jewish_theology" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish theology">Jewish theology</a> or religious literature. Theobald also alleged, however, that the murderers were not practitioners of <a href="/wiki/Orthodox_Judaism" title="Orthodox Judaism">Orthodox Judaism</a>. The murder was instead ordered at <a href="/wiki/Narbonne" title="Narbonne">Narbonne</a>, by a <a href="/wiki/Cult" title="Cult">cult</a> leader who had <a href="/wiki/List_of_Jewish_messiah_claimants" title="List of Jewish messiah claimants">declared himself</a> to be the <a href="/wiki/Jewish_Messiah" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish Messiah">Jewish Messiah</a> and who had cast lots to select where in Europe his followers were to commit the murder.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Monmouth supports this claim by saying that a converted former member of the Messianic cult told him that there was an argument over how to dispose of the body. Monmouth also says that a Christian servant woman glimpsed the child through a chink in a door. Another man is said to have confessed on his deathbed, years after the events, that he saw a group of Jews transporting a body on a horse in the woods.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Context">Context</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Norwich&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Context">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Jews_in_Norwich">The Jews in Norwich</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Norwich&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: The Jews in Norwich">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The Jewish community is thought to have been established in Norwich by 1135, only nine years before the murder (though one Jew called 'Isaac' is recorded in the <a href="/wiki/Domesday_Book" title="Domesday Book">Domesday Book</a> of 1086). Most lived in a Jewish quarter or "Jewry", located in what is now the Haymarket and White Lion Street.<sup id="cite_ref-Ayers_1994_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ayers_1994-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_France" title="History of the Jews in France">Jews were a French-speaking community</a>, like the recently established <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Normans" title="Anglo-Normans">Anglo-Normans</a>, and were closely associated with them. The "Jewry" was very close to the Castle, a pattern seen in other English towns where Jews were under the protection of the Normans.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>William and his family, on the other hand, were of <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxon</a> descent and several of his relatives were <a href="/wiki/Clerical_marriage" title="Clerical marriage">married priests</a> following local tradition.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Conflicts between local Anglo-Saxons and Normans may well have lent themselves to <a href="/wiki/Conspiracy_theory" title="Conspiracy theory">conspiracy theories</a> regarding capital crimes by French-speaking Jews being covered up by French-speaking Normans. Tensions were particularly high during the reign of King Stephen when the murder occurred. Thomas of Monmouth alleges that the sheriff was bribed by the Norwich Jewish community to protect them.<sup id="cite_ref-jew_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-jew-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There may also have been background conflicts between the cathedral, the sheriff and local people about rights in the city and suburbs. Monmouth repeatedly invokes God as the sole source of justice for Anglo-Saxons against corrupt Norman sheriffs. He also claims that John de Chesney, the sheriff who protected the city's Jews, was later punished with internal bleeding.<sup id="cite_ref-paul_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-paul-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Cult">Cult</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Norwich&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Cult">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:The_site_of_St_William%27s_chapel_in_Mousehold_Heath_(geograph_2062004).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/The_site_of_St_William%27s_chapel_in_Mousehold_Heath_%28geograph_2062004%29.jpg/310px-The_site_of_St_William%27s_chapel_in_Mousehold_Heath_%28geograph_2062004%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="310" height="233" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/The_site_of_St_William%27s_chapel_in_Mousehold_Heath_%28geograph_2062004%29.jpg/465px-The_site_of_St_William%27s_chapel_in_Mousehold_Heath_%28geograph_2062004%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/The_site_of_St_William%27s_chapel_in_Mousehold_Heath_%28geograph_2062004%29.jpg/620px-The_site_of_St_William%27s_chapel_in_Mousehold_Heath_%28geograph_2062004%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="640" data-file-height="480" /></a><figcaption>The site of the chapel consecrated to William on <a href="/wiki/Mousehold_Heath" title="Mousehold Heath">Mousehold Heath</a> (2010). The chapel was demolished during the <a href="/wiki/English_Reformation" title="English Reformation">English Reformation</a>; its remains are listed as a <a href="/wiki/Scheduled_monument" title="Scheduled monument">scheduled monument</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-EH_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EH-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup></figcaption></figure>
<p>The wish of the local clergy – in particular, of Bishop William de Turbeville, to establish a <i>cultus</i> may at least in part have been partly financially motivated. The Bishop encouraged Thomas of Monmouth to question local people and to write his book.<sup id="cite_ref-Langmuir_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Langmuir-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After being buried in the monk's cemetery, the body of William was moved to progressively more prestigious places in the church, being placed in the <a href="/wiki/Chapter_house" title="Chapter house">chapter house</a> in 1150 and close to the High Altar in 1151.<sup id="cite_ref-sim_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sim-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Monmouth devotes most of his book not to the murder, but to the evidence for William's sanctity, including mysterious lights seen around the body itself and miraculous cures effected on local devotees. Monmouth admits that some of the clergy, notably the Prior, Elias, were opposed to the cult on the grounds that there was little evidence of William's piety or martyrdom. Monmouth promoted the claims by providing evidence of visions of William and miracles.<sup id="cite_ref-sim_21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sim-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Historian Paul Dalton states that the cult of William was predominantly "protective and pacificatory" in character, having similarities to that of another child saint, <a href="/wiki/Saint_Faith" title="Saint Faith">Faith of Conques</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-paul_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-paul-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Despite its origins, the cult itself was not associated with the promotion of anti-Jewish activity. The cult was a minor one even at its height. There is little evidence of a flourishing cult of William in Norwich – surviving financial records listing offerings made at his shrine at <a href="/wiki/Norwich_Cathedral" title="Norwich Cathedral">Norwich Cathedral</a> suggest that, although its fortunes waxed and waned, for much of its history there were few pilgrims, although offerings continued to be made until at least 1521.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENilson1998157_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENilson1998157-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> A temporary boost to the shrine's popularity occurred after 1376, when William was adopted by the Norwich Peltier's Guild, whose annual service at the Cathedral included a child who played the part of William.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWallace199795_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWallace199795-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> There was also a scholars' guild dedicated to St William in the <a href="/wiki/Norfolk" title="Norfolk">Norfolk</a> town of <a href="/wiki/King%27s_Lynn" title="King&#39;s Lynn">Bishop's Lynn</a>.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_John%27s_church_-_north_aisle_screen_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1708264.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/St_John%27s_church_-_north_aisle_screen_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1708264.jpg/260px-St_John%27s_church_-_north_aisle_screen_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1708264.jpg" decoding="async" width="260" height="203" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/St_John%27s_church_-_north_aisle_screen_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1708264.jpg/390px-St_John%27s_church_-_north_aisle_screen_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1708264.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/St_John%27s_church_-_north_aisle_screen_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1708264.jpg/520px-St_John%27s_church_-_north_aisle_screen_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1708264.jpg 2x" data-file-width="640" data-file-height="499" /></a><figcaption>The <a href="/wiki/Rood_screen" title="Rood screen">rood screen</a> of St John's Church, <a href="/wiki/Garboldisham" title="Garboldisham">Garboldisham</a>, Norfolk</figcaption></figure>
<p>Images of William as a martyr were created for some churches, generally in the vicinity of Norwich. A panel of painted oak, depicting William and <a href="/wiki/Agatha_of_Sicily" title="Agatha of Sicily">Agatha of Sicily</a>, is in the collection of the <a href="/wiki/Victoria_and_Albert_Museum" title="Victoria and Albert Museum">Victoria and Albert Museum</a> in London; William is shown holding a hammer and with three nails in his head. The panel was formerly part of a <a href="/wiki/Rood_screen" title="Rood screen">rood screen</a> at the Norwich <a href="/wiki/Church_of_St_John_Maddermarket" class="mw-redirect" title="Church of St John Maddermarket">Church of St John Maddermarket</a>. The screen was commissioned by Ralph Segrym (died 1472), a merchant who became a Member of Parliament and Mayor of Norwich.<sup id="cite_ref-Victoria_and_Albert_Museum_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Victoria_and_Albert_Museum-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>William is depicted on the rood screens of a number of other Norfolk churches: St Mary's Church, <a href="/wiki/Worstead" title="Worstead">Worstead</a><sup id="cite_ref-auto_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-auto-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> and St John's Church, <a href="/wiki/Garboldisham" title="Garboldisham">Garboldisham</a><sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> depict William holding nails; the screen in Holy Trinity Church, <a href="/wiki/Loddon,_Norfolk" title="Loddon, Norfolk">Loddon</a> depicts William being crucified.<sup id="cite_ref-auto_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-auto-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Aftermath">Aftermath</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Norwich&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Aftermath">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>As a result of the feelings generated by the William ritual murder story and subsequent intervention by the authorities on behalf of the accused, the growing suspicion of collusion between corrupt sheriffs and nobles and Jews fuelled the general anti-Jewish and anti-Norman mood of the population. After Thomas of Monmouth's version of William's death circulated a number of other unsolved child murders were attributed to Jewish conspiracies, including <a href="/wiki/Harold_of_Gloucester" title="Harold of Gloucester">Harold of Gloucester</a> (d.&#160;1168) and <a href="/wiki/Robert_of_Bury" title="Robert of Bury">Robert of Bury</a> (d.&#160;1181).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESkinner200331,_130_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESkinner200331,_130-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> The best-known of these was <a href="/wiki/Little_Saint_Hugh_of_Lincoln" title="Little Saint Hugh of Lincoln">Little Saint Hugh of Lincoln</a> (d.&#160;1255).<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> This became known as the <a href="/wiki/Blood_libel" title="Blood libel">blood libel</a>.
</p><p>By the reign of <a href="/wiki/Richard_I_of_England" title="Richard I of England">Richard the Lionheart</a> attitudes towards <a href="/wiki/English_Jew" class="mw-redirect" title="English Jew">English Jews</a> had become less tolerant. This, in conjunction with the increase in national opinion in favour of a <a href="/wiki/Crusade" class="mw-redirect" title="Crusade">Crusade</a>, and the conflation of all non-Christians in the Medieval Christian imagination, led to the Jewish deputation attending the coronation of Richard in 1189 being attacked by the crowd.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> A widespread attack began on the Jewish population, leading to <a href="/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_England_(1066%E2%80%931290)#Massacres_at_London_and_York_(1189–1190)" title="History of the Jews in England (1066–1290)">massacres of Jews at London and York</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESkinner200331_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESkinner200331-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The attacks were followed by others throughout England.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESkinner200329–32_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESkinner200329–32-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> When the Norman nobility of Norwich attempted to suppress these activities, the <a href="/wiki/Yeoman" title="Yeoman">yeomanry</a> and peasants revolted against the lords and attacked their supporters, especially Norwich's Jewish community. On 6 February 1190, the Jews who were found in their own houses at Norwich were killed; others had taken refuge in <a href="/wiki/Norwich_Castle" title="Norwich Castle">Norwich Castle</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESkinner200330_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESkinner200330-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During the development of <a href="/wiki/Chapelfield" class="mw-redirect" title="Chapelfield">Chapelfield</a>, Norwich, in 2004 a well with the remains of the remains of at least 6 adults and 11 children was discovered. Analysis showed strong affinities to living Ashkenazi Jewish groups.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hostility against Jews continued until, in 1290, Jews were <a href="/wiki/Edict_of_Expulsion" title="Edict of Expulsion">expelled from England</a> by <a href="/wiki/Edward_I_of_England" title="Edward I of England">King Edward I</a>. Jews were not officially allowed to resettle in England until after 1655, when <a href="/wiki/Lord_Protector" title="Lord Protector">Lord Protector</a> <a href="/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell" title="Oliver Cromwell">Oliver Cromwell</a> commissioned the <a href="/wiki/Whitehall_Conference" title="Whitehall Conference">Whitehall Conference</a> to debate the proposals made by <a href="/wiki/Menasseh_ben_Israel" class="mw-redirect" title="Menasseh ben Israel">Menasseh ben Israel</a>. While the Conference reached no verdict, it is seen as the beginning of <a href="/wiki/Resettlement_of_the_Jews_in_England" title="Resettlement of the Jews in England">resettlement of the Jews in England</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Modern_theories_of_the_crime">Modern theories of the crime</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=William_of_Norwich&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Modern theories of the crime">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Map_of_medieval_Norwich_(c1144).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/71/Map_of_medieval_Norwich_%28c1144%29.jpg/220px-Map_of_medieval_Norwich_%28c1144%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="287" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/71/Map_of_medieval_Norwich_%28c1144%29.jpg/330px-Map_of_medieval_Norwich_%28c1144%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/71/Map_of_medieval_Norwich_%28c1144%29.jpg/440px-Map_of_medieval_Norwich_%28c1144%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="649" data-file-height="848" /></a><figcaption>A map of medieval Norwich reproduced in Jessopp and James' edition of the <i>Life</i>. The "Jewry" is to the left of the castle in the centre, part of the "New Town" area on the other side of the town from the monastery and the wood.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The story of William's supposed martyrdom in a Jewish conspiracy persisted for many centuries. As late as 1853, the author Susan Swain Madders, in her book on the history of Norwich, attributes William's death to a murderous conspiracy of "the Jews, then the leading doctors, merchants and scholars of the day". She also repeats the story that they escaped punishment "by some <a href="/wiki/Economic_antisemitism" title="Economic antisemitism">clever monetary arrangement</a> with the authorities".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMadders185344_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMadders185344-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Thomas of Monmouth's account of William's life was published in 1896 in an edition by <a href="/wiki/Augustus_Jessopp" title="Augustus Jessopp">Augustus Jessopp</a> and <a href="/wiki/M._R._James" title="M. R. James">M. R. James</a>. James's introduction to the book is the first modern analysis of the evidence provided by Monmouth. James notes that Monmouth is keen to prove the truth of his version of events by citing witnesses to build up a consistent account. He argues that some testimonies seem to be pure invention, others are unreliable, but that some appear to describe real events, though facts are being manipulated to fit the story. James dismisses the claim of planned ritual murder as a fantasy, which only emerges some years after the crime, promoted by the convert Theobald, keen to ingratiate himself with the Christian community. Independent support is very flimsy, such as the servant who is supposed to have glimpsed a child through a crack in the door, but did not report this until interviewed by Monmouth years later.<sup id="cite_ref-mrj_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mrj-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>James suggests several possibilities: 1. an accident in the woods; 2. a murder by a Christian who arranged the scene to cast blame on Jews; 3. a murder by an unknown person that was blamed on Jews for reasons unrelated to the crime itself; 4. accidental or deliberate killing by a Jew that was then covered up by the Jewish community who feared they would all be blamed.<sup id="cite_ref-mrj_35-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mrj-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> James thinks that all these are possible, including that a "deranged or superstitious" Jew might have killed William in a quasi-ritual way. He says that the convert Theobald himself is a possible suspect.
</p><p>In an 1897 review of James' book <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Jacobs" title="Joseph Jacobs">Joseph Jacobs</a> in the <i><a href="/wiki/Jewish_Quarterly_Review" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish Quarterly Review">Jewish Quarterly Review</a></i> argued that William's own family were the most likely suspects, speculating that they had held a mock crucifixion over Easter during which William fell into a "cataleptic" trance and died as a result of burial. Jacobs argues that it would make no sense for Jews to hide the body in Thorpe Wood, as they would have had to carry it through the whole of the Christian part of the town to get there.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> According to a 2005 paper by Raphael Langham, Jacobs provided "no evidence" for his speculation about a family crucifixion.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1933 <a href="/wiki/Cecil_Roth" title="Cecil Roth">Cecil Roth</a> argued that a different type of mock crucifixion may have led to the accusations against Jews, because of a masquerade involving the <a href="/wiki/Purim#Burning_of_Haman&#39;s_effigy" title="Purim">mock execution of Haman</a> enacted by the Jews at <a href="/wiki/Purim" title="Purim">Purim</a>. In 1964 Marion Anderson developed this idea, combining it with Monmouth's original arguments. She suggests that William had been told not to associate with Jews following one such masquerade; he was then <a href="/wiki/Kidnap" class="mw-redirect" title="Kidnap">kidnapped</a> and tortured by the Jews to find out why they were being ostracised. He died as a result and the body was disposed of.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1984, <a href="/wiki/Gavin_I._Langmuir" title="Gavin I. Langmuir">Gavin I. Langmuir</a> endorsed a 1967 theory by Vivian Lipman that the murder was a <a href="/wiki/Sex_crime" class="mw-redirect" title="Sex crime">sex crime</a> on the basis that Monmouth's description of William wearing a "jacket" and "shoes" implied that the boy's body was naked below the waist. It was probably perpetrated by the man who represented himself as a cook, and had enticed William away from his family to commit the crime. This man was never identified by Monmouth and disappears from the story without explanation.
</p><p>Langmuir dismissed Anderson's theories and criticised both James' and Jacobs' speculations, adding that Theobald was an unlikely suspect as he appears to have been in Cambridge when the murder was committed.<sup id="cite_ref-Langmuir_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Langmuir-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> In 1988, Zefirah Rokeah revived James' suggestion that Theobald was the killer.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> In 1997, John McCulloh followed Lipman in arguing that it was a sadistic sex crime.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> Raphael Langham, writing in 2005, believed that Theobald was a disturbed individual with a hatred of his own community and thus the most likely killer.<sup id="cite_ref-soc_10-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-soc-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 2015, <a href="/wiki/E._M._Rose" title="E. M. Rose">E. M. Rose</a>'s investigation of the subject, <i>The Murder of William of Norwich</i><sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> received the 2016 <a href="/wiki/Ralph_Waldo_Emerson_Award" title="Ralph Waldo Emerson Award">Ralph Waldo Emerson Award</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Phi_Beta_Kappa_Society" class="mw-redirect" title="Phi Beta Kappa Society">Phi Beta Kappa Society</a> for "a scholarly study that contributes significantly to interpretation of the intellectual and cultural condition of humanity"<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> and was named a "Top Ten Book in History" by <i><a href="/wiki/The_Sunday_Times" title="The Sunday Times">The Sunday Times</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-sundaytimes_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sundaytimes-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> Rose points out that road robberies and kidnappings gone wrong were a frequent cause of death in the region during Stephen's reign, when the Crown struggled to safeguard the roads, and could offer another explanation of William's death.
</p>
