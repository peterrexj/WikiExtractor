<p><b>Olof Skötkonung</b>, (<a href="/wiki/Old_Norse" title="Old Norse">Old Norse</a>: <i>Óláfr skautkonungr</i>) sometimes stylized as <b>Olaf the Swede</b> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;980</span>&#8211;1022), was King of Sweden, son of <a href="/wiki/Eric_the_Victorious" title="Eric the Victorious">Eric the Victorious</a> and, according to Icelandic sources, <a href="/wiki/Sigrid_the_Haughty" title="Sigrid the Haughty">Sigrid the Haughty</a>. He succeeded his father in c. 995. He stands at the threshold of recorded history, since he is the first Swedish ruler about whom there is substantial knowledge.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He is regarded as the first king known to have ruled both the <a href="/wiki/Swedes_(Germanic_tribe)" class="mw-redirect" title="Swedes (Germanic tribe)">Swedes</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Geats" title="Geats">Geats</a>. In Sweden, the reign of king Olov Skötkonung (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;995–1022</span>) is considered to be the transition from the Viking age to the Middle Ages, because he was the first Christian king of the <a href="/wiki/Swedes_(Germanic_tribe)" class="mw-redirect" title="Swedes (Germanic tribe)">Swedes</a>, who were the last to adopt Christianity in <a href="/wiki/Scandinavia" title="Scandinavia">Scandinavia</a>. He is associated with a growing influence of the church in what is today southwestern and central Sweden. Norse beliefs persisted in parts of <a href="/wiki/Sweden" title="Sweden">Sweden</a> until the 12th century.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Olof was victorious alongside <a href="/wiki/Sweyn_Forkbeard" title="Sweyn Forkbeard">Sweyn Forkbeard</a> when the kings created an alliance to defeat the Norwegian king <a href="/wiki/Olaf_Tryggvason" title="Olaf Tryggvason">Olaf Tryggvason</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Svolder" title="Battle of Svolder">Battle of Svolder</a>. After the battle, the victorious leaders split Norway into areas of control. <a href="/wiki/Heimskringla" title="Heimskringla">Heimskringla</a> gives the most detailed account of the division. Olof received four districts in Trondheim as well as Møre, Romsdal and Rånrike.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Etymology">Etymology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Etymology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>One of many explanations for the name <i>Skötkonung</i> is that it is derived from the Swedish word "skatt", which can mean either "taxes" or "treasure". The latter meaning has been interpreted as "tributary king" and one English scholar speculates about a tributary relationship to the Danish king <a href="/wiki/Sweyn_Forkbeard" title="Sweyn Forkbeard">Sweyn Forkbeard</a>, who was his stepfather.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> That explanation, however, is not supported by evidence or historical sources. Another possible explanation of the name refers to the fact that he was the first Swedish king to mint coins.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> An ancient land ownership ceremony which placed a parcel of earth in someone's <i>lap</i> (Swedish: <i>sköte</i>) was called <i>scotting</i> and may have been involved in this epithet.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Old Norse "Óláfr sœnski" means "Olaf the Swedish", an epithet used to distinguish him from the Norwegian kings <a href="/wiki/Olaf_Tryggvasson" class="mw-redirect" title="Olaf Tryggvasson">Olaf Tryggvasson</a> and <a href="/wiki/Olaf_II_of_Norway" title="Olaf II of Norway">Olaf Haraldsson</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>General knowledge of Olof's life is mostly based on <a href="/wiki/Snorri_Sturluson" title="Snorri Sturluson">Snorri Sturluson</a>'s and <a href="/wiki/Adam_of_Bremen" title="Adam of Bremen">Adam of Bremen</a>'s accounts, which have been subject to criticism from some scholars. The eldest account by the German ecclesiastic chronicler Adam of Bremen (c. 1075), relates that Sweyn Forkbeard was expelled from his Danish realm by the Swedish King Eric the Victorious in the late 10th century. When Eric died (c. 995), Sweyn returned and regained his kingdom, marrying Eric's widow. Meanwhile, however, Olof had succeeded his father Eric, gathered an army, and launched a surprise attack against Sweyn. The Danish king was once again expelled while Olof occupied his lands. After this, however, the conflict was resolved. Since Sweyn had married Olof's mother, he was reinstated on the Danish throne and the two kings were thereafter allies.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Snorri Sturluson (c. 1230) and the other Icelandic saga writers likewise say that Sweyn married Olof's mother after the death of Eric the Victorious, but without mentioning any conflict. Also, Snorri describes Sweyn and Olof as equal allies when they defeated the Norwegian king <a href="/wiki/Olav_Tryggvason" class="mw-redirect" title="Olav Tryggvason">Olav Tryggvason</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Svolder" title="Battle of Svolder">battle of Svolder</a> in 1000, and thereafter divided Norway between themselves (see below).<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> It is commonly believed that Adam's account about Sweyn's defeats at the hands of Eric and Olof is partial and might have been misinterpreted; the marriage to Olof's mother may in fact have sealed Sweyn's senior position.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Stadshuset_Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/Stadshuset_Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung.jpg/250px-Stadshuset_Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="344" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/Stadshuset_Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung.jpg/375px-Stadshuset_Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/Stadshuset_Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung.jpg/500px-Stadshuset_Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2292" data-file-height="3152" /></a><figcaption>Olof Skötkonung as imagined by Ansgar Almquist in the 1920s, statue at <a href="/wiki/Stockholm_City_Hall" title="Stockholm City Hall">Stockholm City Hall</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Viking_expedition_to_Wendland">Viking expedition to Wendland</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Viking expedition to Wendland">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>According to Snorri, Olof Skötkonung led a Viking expedition to <a href="/wiki/L%C3%BCchow-Dannenberg" title="Lüchow-Dannenberg">Wendland</a> early in his reign. He captured <a href="/wiki/Edla" title="Edla">Edla</a>, the daughter of a Wendish chieftain, and kept her as mistress. She gave him the son <a href="/wiki/Emund_the_Old" title="Emund the Old">Emund</a> (who was to become king of Sweden), and the daughters <a href="/wiki/Astrid_Olofsdotter" class="mw-redirect" title="Astrid Olofsdotter">Astrid</a> (later wife of <a href="/wiki/Olaf_II_of_Norway" title="Olaf II of Norway">Olaf II of Norway</a>) and Holmfrid (married to Sven Jarl of Norway).<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> He later married <a href="/wiki/Estrid_of_the_Obotrites" title="Estrid of the Obotrites">Estrid of the Obotrites</a>, and they had a son, <a href="/wiki/Anund_Jacob" title="Anund Jacob">Anund Jacob</a>, and a daughter, <a href="/wiki/Ingegerd_Olofsdotter" class="mw-redirect" title="Ingegerd Olofsdotter">Ingegerd Olofsdotter</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Alliance_with_Sweyn_Forkbeard">Alliance with Sweyn Forkbeard</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Alliance with Sweyn Forkbeard">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>While Adam of Bremen praises Olof as a good Christian, Icelandic authors paint an unfavourable picture of the king as haughty and prickly. Olof is said to have preferred royal <a href="/wiki/Sport" title="Sport">sports</a> to war, which may explain the ease with which <a href="/wiki/Sweyn_Forkbeard" title="Sweyn Forkbeard">Sweyn Forkbeard</a> retook the Danish lands Olof's father Eric had conquered.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Olof may also have lost the right to tribute which his predecessors had preserved in what is now <a href="/wiki/Estonia" title="Estonia">Estonia</a> and <a href="/wiki/Latvia" title="Latvia">Latvia</a>.
</p><p>In 1000, he joined forces with <a href="/wiki/Sweyn_Forkbeard" title="Sweyn Forkbeard">Sweyn Forkbeard</a> and with the Norwegian <a href="/wiki/Jarl_(title)" class="mw-redirect" title="Jarl (title)">Jarls</a> <a href="/wiki/Eir%C3%ADkr_H%C3%A1konarson" title="Eiríkr Hákonarson">Eric</a> and <a href="/wiki/Sveinn_H%C3%A1konarson" class="mw-redirect" title="Sveinn Hákonarson">Sven</a>, against the Norwegian King <a href="/wiki/Olaf_Tryggvason" title="Olaf Tryggvason">Olaf Tryggvason</a>. The circumstances have been much debated in modern historical research, but a contemporary poem confirms that Eric Jarl gathered auxiliaries in Sweden: "The belligerent jarl / gathered much manpower / in <a href="/wiki/Svithiod" class="mw-redirect" title="Svithiod">Svithiod</a>, the chief went / southward to the battle."<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Olaf Tryggvason was attacked by the allied fleets in the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Svolder" title="Battle of Svolder">Battle of Svolder</a>, the location of which is uncertain. It may have been either in <a href="/wiki/%C3%98resund" title="Øresund">Øresund</a> or <a href="/wiki/Pomerania" title="Pomerania">Pomerania</a>. Olaf Tryggvason disappeared during the battle and Norway was appropriated by the allied lords. The bulk of the conquests went to Sweyn Forkbeard while Olof gained a part of <a href="/wiki/Tr%C3%B8ndelag" title="Trøndelag">Trøndelag</a> as well as modern <a href="/wiki/Bohusl%C3%A4n" title="Bohuslän">Bohuslän</a>. These lands were placed under Sven Jarl, son-in-law of the king.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Norwegian-Swedish_War">Norwegian-Swedish War</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Norwegian-Swedish War">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>When the Norwegian kingdom was reestablished by <a href="/wiki/Olaf_II_of_Norway" title="Olaf II of Norway">Olaf II of Norway</a> (Olaf the Saint) in 1015, a new war erupted between Norway and Sweden. There is a circumstantial account of this in Snorri Sturluson's work. As he writes, many men in both <a href="/wiki/Sweden" title="Sweden">Sweden</a> and <a href="/wiki/Norway" title="Norway">Norway</a> tried to reconcile the kings. In 1018, Olof's cousin, the <a href="/wiki/Earl" title="Earl">earl</a> of <a href="/wiki/V%C3%A4sterg%C3%B6tland" title="Västergötland">Västergötland</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ragnvald_Ulfsson" title="Ragnvald Ulfsson">Ragnvald Ulfsson</a> and the Norwegian king's emissaries <a href="/wiki/Bj%C3%B6rn_Stallare" class="mw-redirect" title="Björn Stallare">Björn Stallare</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hjalti_Skeggiason" title="Hjalti Skeggiason">Hjalti Skeggiason</a> had arrived at the <a href="/wiki/Thing_(assembly)" title="Thing (assembly)">thing</a> of <a href="/wiki/Uppsala" title="Uppsala">Uppsala</a> in an attempt to sway the Swedish king to accept peace and as a warrant marry his daughter <a href="/wiki/Ingegerd_Olofsdotter" class="mw-redirect" title="Ingegerd Olofsdotter">Ingegerd Olofsdotter</a> to the king of Norway. The Swedish king was greatly angered and threatened to banish Ragnvald from his kingdom, but Ragnvald was supported by his foster-father <a href="/wiki/Thorgny_Lawspeaker" class="mw-redirect" title="Thorgny Lawspeaker">Thorgny the Lawspeaker</a>
</p><p>Thorgny delivered a powerful speech in which he reminded the king of the great Viking expeditions in the East that predecessors such as <a href="/wiki/Erik_Anundsson" class="mw-redirect" title="Erik Anundsson">Erik Anundsson</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bj%C3%B6rn_(III)_Eriksson" class="mw-redirect" title="Björn (III) Eriksson">Björn</a> had undertaken, without having the hubris not to listen to their men's advice. Thorgny himself had taken part in many successful pillaging expeditions with Olof's father <a href="/wiki/Eric_the_Victorious" title="Eric the Victorious">Eric the Victorious</a> and even Eric had listened to his men. The present king wanted nothing but Norway, which no Swedish king before him had desired. This displeased the Swedish people, who were eager to follow the king on new ventures in the East to win back the kingdoms that paid tribute to his ancestors, but it was the wish of the people that the king make peace with the king of Norway and give him his daughter Ingegerd as queen.
</p><p>Thorgny finished his speech by saying: "if you do not desire to do so, we shall assault you and kill you and not brook any more of your warmongering and obstinacy. Our ancestors have done so, who at <a href="/wiki/Mula_thing" class="mw-redirect" title="Mula thing">Mula thing</a> threw five kings in a well, kings who were too arrogant as you are against us."<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Upon hearing this, King Olof complied with the demands of the peasantry for the time being.
</p><p>However, far from keeping his promise Olof married his daughter <a href="/wiki/Ingegerd_Olofsdotter" class="mw-redirect" title="Ingegerd Olofsdotter">Ingegerd-Irene</a> to <a href="/wiki/Yaroslav_I_the_Wise" class="mw-redirect" title="Yaroslav I the Wise">Yaroslav I the Wise</a> instead. When Olaf of Norway heard about the arrangement he was furious and intent on attacking Olof Skötkonung. However, the <a href="/wiki/Geatish" class="mw-redirect" title="Geatish">Geatish</a> jarl <a href="/wiki/Ragnvald_Ulfsson" title="Ragnvald Ulfsson">Ragnvald Ulfsson</a>, colluding with Olaf II's skald <a href="/wiki/Sigvat_Thordarson" class="mw-redirect" title="Sigvat Thordarson">Sigvat Thordarson</a>, managed to avert the impending war. Olof's other daughter <a href="/wiki/Astrid_Olofsdotter_of_Sweden" title="Astrid Olofsdotter of Sweden">Astrid</a> stayed with Ragnvald at the time, and it was agreed that she would take Ingegerd's place. Unbeknownst to Olof, she traveled to Norway and married Olaf II. That happened c. 1019.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Olof Skötkonung was highly upset, but soon ran into trouble at home. Both the Swedes and Geats were displeased with the self-willed rule of the king. The <a href="/wiki/Lawspeaker" title="Lawspeaker">lawspeaker</a> of <a href="/wiki/V%C3%A4sterg%C3%B6tland" title="Västergötland">Västergötland</a>, Emund, traveled to <a href="/wiki/Uppsala" title="Uppsala">Gamla Uppsala</a> and spoke to Olof's councilors, and a settlement was made. Olof agreed to share his power with his son <a href="/wiki/Anund_Jacob" title="Anund Jacob">Anund Jacob</a> who was 10 or 12 years at the time. Olof was also forced to accept a settlement with <a href="/wiki/Olaf_II_of_Norway" title="Olaf II of Norway">Olaf II of Norway</a> at <a href="/wiki/Kungah%C3%A4lla" title="Kungahälla">Kungahälla</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> The veracity of Snorri Sturluson's account of Olof Skötkonung, written more than two centuries later, is difficult to assess; however, he quotes several probably genuine scaldic verses which allude to some of the related events.
</p><p>One result of the hostilities between Olof Skötkonung and Olaf of Norway was, according to Snorri Sturluson, that the people of <a href="/wiki/J%C3%A4mtland" title="Jämtland">Jämtland</a> and <a href="/wiki/H%C3%A4lsingland" title="Hälsingland">Hälsingland</a> came under the Swedish rather than Norwegian king. Previously the Jämtlanders and part of the Hälsinglanders had adhered to Norway since the days of <a href="/wiki/Hakon_the_Good" class="mw-redirect" title="Hakon the Good">Hakon the Good</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> The veracity of this is not known, but the medieval provincial laws of Jämtland show Swedish influences, and there are indications that Christianity arrived from Central Sweden in the 11th century.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Jämtland reverted to the Norwegian king in 1111, while Hälsingland was henceforth under Sweden.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Diplomacy">Diplomacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Diplomacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Olof's ally Sweyn Forkbeard occupied <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a> in 1013, but died shortly afterwards, and the Anglo-Saxon ruler <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_the_Unready" title="Æthelred the Unready">Æthelred the Unready</a> was able to return. According to Adam of Bremen, "the son of the king, <a href="/wiki/Cnut_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Cnut the Great">Cnut</a>, returned home with the army and prepared a new war against the English. Olav [II], whom the Norwegians had chosen as their commander, now separated from the Danish kingdom. Cnut, who felt threatened from two directions, then entered an alliance with his brother Olof Eriksson who ruled in Sweden, and planned to take power in England, and then in Norway,  with his assistance. Equipped with a thousand large ships, Cnut thus traversed the British Sea".<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> From Swedish rune stones it also appears that many people joined the Danish Viking expeditions of the early 11th century. After <a href="/wiki/Cnut_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Cnut the Great">Cnut the Great</a> became <a href="/wiki/King_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="King of England">King of England</a> in 1016, he sent the two sons of the deceased King <a href="/wiki/Edmund_Ironside" title="Edmund Ironside">Edmund Ironside</a> to Olof (who was either Canute's half-brother or stepbrother), supposedly with instructions to have the children murdered. Instead of having them killed, the two boys were secretly sent either to <a href="/wiki/Kiev" class="mw-redirect" title="Kiev">Kiev</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> where Olof's daughter <a href="/wiki/Ingigerd_Olofsdotter" class="mw-redirect" title="Ingigerd Olofsdotter">Ingigerd</a> was the Queen, or to Poland, where Canute's uncle <a href="/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_I_Chrobry" class="mw-redirect" title="Bolesław I Chrobry">Bolesław I Chrobry</a> was duke.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Christian_King">Christian King</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Christian King">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Included in the <a href="/wiki/Westrogothic_law" class="mw-redirect" title="Westrogothic law">Westrogothic law</a> from c. 1240 is the first brief Swedish chronicle, which begins with Olof Skötkonung. It relates that Olof was baptized in <a href="/wiki/Husaby" title="Husaby">Husaby</a> in <a href="/wiki/V%C3%A4sterg%C3%B6tland" title="Västergötland">Västergötland</a> by the missionary <a href="/wiki/Sigfrid_of_Sweden" title="Sigfrid of Sweden">Sigfrid</a>, and made generous donations on spot.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> At Husaby parish church, there is a sign commemorating his baptism; nearby is a well thought to be the same sacred spring where Olof was baptized.
</p><p>He was the first Swedish king to remain a Christian until his death. However, the circumstances about his baptism are not clear. A document from 1008 says that a certain bishop, dispatched by Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Bruno_of_Querfurt" title="Bruno of Querfurt">Bruno of Querfurt</a>, visited the Suigi tribe and managed to baptize the king, whose queen was already Christian. 1,000 people and seven communities followed his example. The Suigi have sometimes been identified as the Swedes, though this has been rejected by several other scholars.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> On the other hand, Olof's coinage (see below) indicates that he was a Christian already at the time of his accession in c. 995.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/Adam_of_Bremen" title="Adam of Bremen">Adam of Bremen</a>, Olof planned to tear down the <a href="/wiki/Uppsala_temple" class="mw-redirect" title="Uppsala temple">Uppsala temple</a>, which was allegedly an important cult centre.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> The fact that a large part of the Swedes were still pagan forced him to abandon this aim. The pagans, weary of his plans, made an agreement with Olof to the effect that he, if he wished to be a Christian, must exercise his royal authority in a province of his choice. If he founded a church, he was not supposed to force anyone to convert. Olof was content with this and installed a bishopric in the province of <a href="/wiki/V%C3%A4sterg%C3%B6tland" title="Västergötland">Västergötland</a>, closer to Denmark and Norway. On the wishes of Olof, the Archbishop of Hamburg-Bremen anointed Thurgot as the first Bishop in <a href="/wiki/Skara" title="Skara">Skara</a>. This Thurgot was successful in disseminating Christianity among the West Geats and east Geats.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The legend of St. <a href="/wiki/Sigfrid" class="mw-redirect" title="Sigfrid">Sigfrid</a>, known since the 13th century, relates that the still pagan Olof called in the English <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_York" title="Archbishop of York">Archbishop of York</a>, Sigfrid, to teach the new faith in his realm. On his way, Sigfrid and his three nephews came to <a href="/wiki/V%C3%A4rend" title="Värend">Värend</a> in southern <a href="/wiki/Sm%C3%A5land" title="Småland">Småland</a> where the twelve local tribes endorsed Christianity at a <a href="/wiki/Thing_(assembly)" title="Thing (assembly)">Thing</a>. Sigfrid left his nephews to tend matters in Värend and proceeded to Olof's court where the king and his family were baptised. Meanwhile, a heathen reaction in Värend cost the lives of the nephews, whose heads were sunk in the <a href="/wiki/V%C3%A4xj%C3%B6_Lake" title="Växjö Lake">Växjö Lake</a>. Hearing about this, Sigfrid returned to Värend where the heads were discovered through a miracle. King Olof then appeared in Värend with a force, punished the murderers, and forced the locals to yield properties to the Church. Whether the legend reflects the expansion of Olof's realm to the south is unsure. The account seems to incorporate various elements in order to legitimise the establishment of the Bishopric of <a href="/wiki/V%C3%A4xj%C3%B6" title="Växjö">Växjö</a> in c. 1170.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> It is nevertheless known from Adam of Bremen that an English missionary called Sigfrid preached among the Swedes and Geats in the first half of the 11th century.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Coinage_and_extent_of_the_realm">Coinage and extent of the realm</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Coinage and extent of the realm">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>When he stamped coins in <a href="/wiki/Sigtuna" title="Sigtuna">Sigtuna</a> in the province of <a href="/wiki/Uppland" title="Uppland">Uppland</a> Olof used the word <i>rex</i> for <i>king</i>--<i>OLUF REX</i> as in the coin displayed above or <i>OLAF REX</i>. The use of Latin seems to suggest that he was already baptised at this time but on the other hand the coins were imitating English pennies in type and style. Sigtuna is written <i>SITUN</i>, <i>ZINT</i> (in the coin above), <i>ZTNETEI</i>, or <i>SIDEI</i>. The two last have been deciphered as <i>Si(gtuna) Dei</i> meaning <i>God's Sigtuna</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> The earliest of Olof's coins merely depict him as "King in Sigtuna", while the later ones have "King of the Swedes". 
</p><p>It has been suggested that this change in nomenclature relates to a widening of Olof's base of power around 1000. Sigtuna may be understood as the area in <a href="/wiki/Uppland" title="Uppland">Uppland</a> ruled from the town of this name, while rulership over the Swedes may indicate a more extensive realm. Contemporary scaldic poetry indicates Olof as the ruler of the Swedes as well as the <a href="/wiki/Geats" title="Geats">Geats</a> (<a href="/wiki/G%C3%B6tar" class="mw-redirect" title="Götar">Götar</a>), and the same goes for the account of Adam of Bremen.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> The exact nature of the relation between the Swedes and Geats, and the process by which a unified kingdom was created, has been intensively debated. While the unification has traditionally been thought to depart from the provinces around lake <a href="/wiki/M%C3%A4laren" title="Mälaren">Mälaren</a> some scholars hold that the Geatic provinces were leading the process, and that the construction of a Swedish kingdom was a long process that was only concluded in the 13th century.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id=".C3.93l.C3.A1fsdr.C3.A1pa_s.C3.A6nska"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Óláfsdrápa_sænska">Óláfsdrápa sænska</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Óláfsdrápa sænska">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Iceland" title="Iceland">Icelandic</a> <a href="/wiki/Skald" title="Skald">skald</a> <a href="/wiki/%C3%93ttarr_svarti" title="Óttarr svarti">Óttarr svarti</a> spent some time at Olof's court and composed the poem <i><a href="/wiki/%C3%93l%C3%A1fsdr%C3%A1pa_s%C3%A6nska" title="Óláfsdrápa sænska">Óláfsdrápa sænska</a></i> describing Olof's war expeditions in the east. The poem is interesting, since it gives a view of the reign of Olof: "The warrior guards his land, few kings are as mighty as him; Olof pleases the eagle, the Swedish king is outstanding".<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> Other skalds who served Olof were <a href="/wiki/Gunnlaugr_ormstunga" title="Gunnlaugr ormstunga">Gunnlaugr ormstunga</a>, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Hrafn_%C3%96nundarson&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Hrafn Önundarson (page does not exist)">Hrafn Önundarson</a> and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Gizurr_svarti&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Gizurr svarti (page does not exist)">Gizurr svarti</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Olaf_Scotking_of_Sweden_alleged_grave_2009_Husaby_G%C3%B6tene.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Olaf_Scotking_of_Sweden_alleged_grave_2009_Husaby_G%C3%B6tene.jpg/220px-Olaf_Scotking_of_Sweden_alleged_grave_2009_Husaby_G%C3%B6tene.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="164" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Olaf_Scotking_of_Sweden_alleged_grave_2009_Husaby_G%C3%B6tene.jpg/330px-Olaf_Scotking_of_Sweden_alleged_grave_2009_Husaby_G%C3%B6tene.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Olaf_Scotking_of_Sweden_alleged_grave_2009_Husaby_G%C3%B6tene.jpg/440px-Olaf_Scotking_of_Sweden_alleged_grave_2009_Husaby_G%C3%B6tene.jpg 2x" data-file-width="892" data-file-height="664" /></a><figcaption>The alleged <i>Olaf Grave</i> at Husaby Church</figcaption></figure>
<p>Judging from Snorri Sturluson's chronology of events, Olof died a natural death in the winter of 1021–1022.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Adam of Bremen asserts that he died at approximately the same time as Cnut the Great (1035), which is certainly too late.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Claims that he was martyred after refusing to sacrifice to pagan gods are likely due to a mix-up with the king <a href="/wiki/Olof_(Swedish_king_852)" title="Olof (Swedish king 852)">Olof</a> from <i><a href="/wiki/Vita_Ansgari" class="mw-redirect" title="Vita Ansgari">Vita Ansgari</a></i> and <a href="/wiki/Olof_Tr%C3%A4t%C3%A4lja" title="Olof Trätälja">Olof Trätälja</a> from Norse sources.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Since the 1740s, it has been claimed that he was buried in <a href="/wiki/Husaby" title="Husaby">Husaby</a> in the Christian part of his kingdom, but such identifications are controversial.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Family">Family</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Olof_Sk%C3%B6tkonung&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Family">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Olof was the son of <a href="/wiki/Eric_the_Victorious" title="Eric the Victorious">Eric the Victorious</a> (Erik Segersäll) and a woman whose identity is debated. According to Adam of Bremen she was the sister or daughter of <a href="/wiki/Boleslaw_I_Chrobry" class="mw-redirect" title="Boleslaw I Chrobry">Boleslaw I Chrobry</a> of <a href="/wiki/Poland" title="Poland">Poland</a>, but, according to Icelandic sources, she was <a href="/wiki/Sigrid_the_Haughty" title="Sigrid the Haughty">Sigrid the Haughty</a> (Sigrid Storråda), a daughter of the Viking chief <a href="/wiki/Skoglar_Toste" title="Skoglar Toste">Skoglar Toste</a>. Certain sources say that Olof had a brother called Emunde.
</p><p>With his mistress <a href="/wiki/Edla" title="Edla">Edla</a>, daughter of a Slavic chief, he first had three children:
</p>
<ul><li><a href="/wiki/Emund_the_Old" title="Emund the Old">Emund the Old</a>, King of Sweden in c. 1050–1060</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Astrid_Olofsdotter_of_Sweden" title="Astrid Olofsdotter of Sweden">Astrid</a>, d. after 1035, married to <a href="/wiki/Olaf_II_of_Norway" title="Olaf II of Norway">Olaf II of Norway</a> (Olaf the Saint)</li>
<li>Holmfrid (possibly sister of Olof), married to <a href="/wiki/Sweyn_Haakonsson" title="Sweyn Haakonsson">Sven Jarl</a> of Norway</li></ul>
<p>With his spouse Queen <a href="/wiki/Estrid_of_the_Obotrites" title="Estrid of the Obotrites">Estrid</a> he had two children:
</p>
<ul><li><a href="/wiki/Anund_Jacob" title="Anund Jacob">Anund Jacob</a>, King of Sweden in 1022–c. 1050</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Ingegerd_Olofsdotter_of_Sweden" title="Ingegerd Olofsdotter of Sweden">Ingegerd</a>, d. 1050, married to <a href="/wiki/Yaroslav_I_of_Kiev" class="mw-redirect" title="Yaroslav I of Kiev">Yaroslav I of Kiev</a></li></ul>
