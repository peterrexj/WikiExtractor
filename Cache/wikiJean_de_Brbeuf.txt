<p><b>Jean de Brébeuf</b> (<small>French:&#160;</small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="fr-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/French" title="Help:IPA/French">[ʒɑ̃ də bʁe.bœf]</a></span>) (25 March 1593&#160;&#8211;&#32; 16 March 1649) was a French <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a> <a href="/wiki/Missionary" title="Missionary">missionary</a> who travelled to <a href="/wiki/New_France" title="New France">New France</a> (<a href="/wiki/Canada" title="Canada">Canada</a>) in 1625. There he worked primarily with the Huron (<a href="/wiki/Wyandot_people" title="Wyandot people">Wyandot people</a>) for the rest of his life, except for a few years in <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a> from 1629 to 1633. He learned their language and culture, writing extensively about each to aid other missionaries.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1649, Brébeuf and another missionary were captured when an <a href="/wiki/Iroquois" title="Iroquois">Iroquois</a> raid took over a Huron village (referred to in French as St. Louis). Together with Huron captives, the <a href="/wiki/Canadian_Martyrs" title="Canadian Martyrs">missionaries</a> were ritually tortured and killed on 16 March 1649. Brébeuf was <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> in 1925 and among eight Jesuit missionaries <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> as <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saints</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> in 1930.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_years">Early years</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early years">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Brébeuf was born 25 March 1593 in <a href="/wiki/Cond%C3%A9-sur-Vire" title="Condé-sur-Vire">Condé-sur-Vire</a>, <a href="/wiki/Normandy" title="Normandy">Normandy</a>, <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_France" title="Kingdom of France">France</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECampbell1907_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECampbell1907-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> (He was the uncle of poet <a href="/wiki/Georges_de_Br%C3%A9beuf" title="Georges de Brébeuf">Georges de Brébeuf</a>). He joined the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">Society of Jesus</a> in 1617 at the age of 24,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETalbot19497_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETalbot19497-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> spending the next two years under the direction of Lancelot Marin. Between 1619 and 1621, he was a teacher at the college of <a href="/wiki/Rouen_Cathedral" title="Rouen Cathedral">Rouen</a>. Brébeuf was nearly expelled from the Society when he contracted <a href="/wiki/Tuberculosis" title="Tuberculosis">tuberculosis</a> in 1620—a severe and usually fatal illness that prevented his studying and teaching for the traditional periods.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His record as a student was not particularly distinguished, but Brébeuf was already beginning to show an aptitude for languages. Later in New France, he would teach <a href="/wiki/Native_American_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Native American language">Native American languages</a> to missionaries and French traders.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995106_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995106-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Brébeuf was ordained as a <a href="/wiki/Priesthood_in_the_Catholic_Church" title="Priesthood in the Catholic Church">priest</a> at <a href="/wiki/Pontoise_Cathedral" title="Pontoise Cathedral">Pontoise Cathedral</a> in February 1622.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Missionary">Missionary</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Missionary">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:248px;"><a href="/wiki/File:North_American_Martyrs.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/43/North_American_Martyrs.jpg" decoding="async" width="246" height="300" class="thumbimage" data-file-width="246" data-file-height="300" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:North_American_Martyrs.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>North American Martyrs</div></div></div>
<p>After three years as Steward at the College of Rouen, Brébeuf was chosen by the Provincial of France, Father <a href="/wiki/Pierre_Coton" title="Pierre Coton">Pierre Coton</a>, to embark on the missions to <a href="/wiki/New_France" title="New France">New France</a>.
</p><p>In June 1625, Brébeuf arrived in <a href="/wiki/Quebec_City" title="Quebec City">Québec</a> with Fathers <a href="/wiki/Charles_Lalemant" class="mw-redirect" title="Charles Lalemant">Charles Lalemant</a> and <a href="/wiki/%C3%89nemond_Mass%C3%A9" title="Énemond Massé">Énemond Massé</a>, together with the lay brothers Francois Charton and Gilbert Burel. He worked at the <a href="/wiki/Sainte-Marie_among_the_Hurons" title="Sainte-Marie among the Hurons">Sainte-Marie among the Hurons</a>. For about five months Brébeuf lived with a tribe of <a href="/wiki/Innu_people" class="mw-redirect" title="Innu people">Montagnais</a>, who spoke an <a href="/wiki/Algonquian_languages" title="Algonquian languages">Algonquian language</a>. He was later assigned in 1626 to the <a href="/wiki/Wyandot_people" title="Wyandot people">Huron</a> with Father Anne Nouée. From then on Brébeuf worked mostly as a missionary to the Huron, who spoke an <a href="/wiki/Iroquoian" class="mw-redirect" title="Iroquoian">Iroquoian</a> language. Brébeuf briefly took up residence with the Bear Tribe at <a href="/wiki/Toanche,_Ontario" class="mw-redirect" title="Toanche, Ontario">Toanché</a>, but met with no success in trying to convert them to Catholicism. He was summoned to Québec because of the danger to which the entire colony was then exposed by the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_England" title="Kingdom of England">English</a>. He reached Québec on 17 July 1628 after an absence of two years. On 19 July 1629, <a href="/wiki/Samuel_de_Champlain" title="Samuel de Champlain">Samuel de Champlain</a> surrendered, and the missionaries returned to France.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECampbell1907_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECampbell1907-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In Rouen, Brébeuf served as a preacher and <a href="/wiki/Confessor" title="Confessor">confessor</a>, taking his final <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a> vows in 1630.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Between 1631 and 1633, Brébeuf worked at the College of <a href="/wiki/Eu,_Seine-Maritime" title="Eu, Seine-Maritime">Eu, Seine-Maritime</a> in northern France as a steward, minister and confessor. He returned to New France in 1633, where he lived and worked for the rest of his life.
</p><p>Along with <a href="/wiki/Antoine_Daniel" title="Antoine Daniel">Antoine Daniel</a> and Ambroise Davost, Brébeuf chose <i>Ihonatiria</i> (Saint-Joseph I) as the centre for missionary activity with the Huron.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECampbell1907_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECampbell1907-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> At the time, the Huron suffered epidemics of new Eurasian diseases contracted from the Europeans. Their death rates were high, as they had no <a href="/wiki/Immunity_(medical)" title="Immunity (medical)">immunity</a> to the diseases long <a href="/wiki/Endemic" class="mw-redirect" title="Endemic">endemic</a> in Europe. They, with our hindsight, rightly blamed the Europeans for the deaths, with none of the parties understanding the causes.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETrigger1986229_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETrigger1986229-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Called <i>Échon</i> by the Hurons,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Brébeuf was personally involved with teaching. His lengthy conversations with Huron friends left him with a good knowledge of their culture and spirituality.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGreer200037_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreer200037-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He learned their language and taught it to other missionaries and colonists.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995119_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995119-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Fellow Jesuits such as <a href="/wiki/Paul_Ragueneau" title="Paul Ragueneau">Paul Ragueneau</a> describe his ease and adaptability to the Huron way of life.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995119_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995119-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His efforts to develop a complete ethnographic record of the Huron has been described as "the longest and most ambitious piece of ethnographic description in all <i><a href="/wiki/The_Jesuit_Relations" title="The Jesuit Relations">The Jesuit Relations</a></i>".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGreer200038_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreer200038-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Brébeuf tried to find parallels between the Huron religion and Christianity, so as to facilitate conversion of the Huron to the European religion.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGreer200041,_61_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreer200041,_61-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Brébeuf was known by the Huron for his apparent <a href="/wiki/Shaman" class="mw-redirect" title="Shaman">shamanistic</a> skills, especially in rainmaking.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETrigger1986202_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETrigger1986202-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Despite his efforts to learn their ways, he considered Huron spiritual beliefs to be undeveloped and "foolish delusions"; he was determined to convert them to Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGreer200037_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreer200037-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Brébeuf did not enjoy universal popularity with the Huron, as many believed he was a sorcerer.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETrigger1986290_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETrigger1986290-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> By 1640, nearly half the Huron had died of <a href="/wiki/Smallpox" title="Smallpox">smallpox</a> and the losses disrupted their society. Many children and elders died. With their loved ones dying before their eyes, many Huron began to listen to the words of Jesuit missionaries who, unaffected by the disease, appeared to be men of great power.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrébeuf2008121_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrébeuf2008121-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Brébeuf's progress as a missionary in achieving conversions was slow. Not until 1635 did some Huron agree to be <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptized</a> as Christians. He claimed to have made 14 converts as of 1635 and, by the next year, he claimed 86. He wrote a detailed account in 1636 of <a href="/wiki/The_Huron_Feast_of_the_Dead" title="The Huron Feast of the Dead">The Huron Feast of the Dead</a>, a mass reburial of remains of loved ones after a community moved the location of its village. It was accompanied by elaborate ritual and gift-giving.
</p><p>In 1638, Brébeuf turned over direction of the mission at Saint-Joseph I to <a href="/wiki/J%C3%A9r%C3%B4me_Lalemant" title="Jérôme Lalemant">Jérôme Lalemant</a>; he was called to become Superior at his newly founded Saint-Joseph II.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> In 1640, after an unsuccessful mission into <a href="/wiki/Neutral_Nation" title="Neutral Nation">Neutral Nation</a> territory, Brébeuf broke his collarbone. He was sent to Québec to recover, and worked there as a mission procurator. He taught the Huron, acting as confessor and advisor to the <a href="/wiki/Ursulines" title="Ursulines">Ursulines</a> and <a href="/wiki/Religious_Hospitallers_of_St._Joseph" title="Religious Hospitallers of St. Joseph">Religious Hospitallers</a>. On Sundays and feast days, he preached to French colonists.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979_5-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Brébeuf is credited with composing the "<a href="/wiki/Huron_Carol" title="Huron Carol">Huron Carol</a>", Canada's oldest Christmas song, written around 1642.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGee198513_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGee198513-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> He wrote the lyrics in the <a href="/wiki/Wyandot_language" title="Wyandot language">native language</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Wyandot_people" title="Wyandot people">Huron</a>/<a href="/wiki/Wyandot_people" title="Wyandot people">Wendat</a> people. The song's melody is based on a traditional <a href="/wiki/French_language" title="French language">French</a> <a href="/wiki/Folk_music" title="Folk music">folk</a> song, "<a href="/wiki/Une_Jeune_Pucelle" class="mw-redirect" title="Une Jeune Pucelle">Une Jeune Pucelle</a>" (A Young Maid).
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Linguistic_work">Linguistic work</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Linguistic work">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The educational rigor of the Jesuit <a href="/wiki/Seminary" title="Seminary">seminaries</a> prepared missionaries to acquire native languages.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995119_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995119-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> But, as they had learned the <a href="/wiki/Classical_language" title="Classical language">classical</a> and <a href="/wiki/Romance_languages" title="Romance languages">Romance languages</a>, they likely had difficulty with the very different conventions of the New World indigenous languages.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995109_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995109-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Brébeuf's study of the languages was also shaped by his religious training. Current Catholic theology tried to reconcile knowledge of world languages with accounts in the <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">Bible</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Tower_of_Babel" title="Tower of Babel">tower of Babel</a>, as this was the basis of European history. This influence can be seen in his discussion of language in his accounts collected in <i>The Jesuit Relations.</i><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGreer200038_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreer200038-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Jean de Brébeuf's remarkable facility with language was one of the reasons he was chosen for the Huron mission in 1626.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995112_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995112-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> He is distinguished for his commitment to learning the <a href="/wiki/Wyandot_language" title="Wyandot language">Huron (Wyandot) language</a>. People with a strong positive attitude towards the language often learn the language much more easily.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995113_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995113-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Brébeuf was widely acknowledged to have best mastered the Native oratory style, which used <a href="/wiki/Metaphor" title="Metaphor">metaphor</a>, <a href="/wiki/Circumlocution" title="Circumlocution">circumlocution</a> and <a href="/wiki/Repetition_(rhetorical_device)" title="Repetition (rhetorical device)">repetition</a>. Learning the language was still onerous, and he wrote to warn other missionaries of the difficulties.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlackburn200088_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlackburn200088-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>To explain the low number of converts, Brébeuf noted that missionaries first had to master the Huron language.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlackburn2000111_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlackburn2000111-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> His commitment to this work demonstrates he understood that mutual intelligibility was vital for communicating complex and abstract religious ideas. He believed learning native languages was imperative for the Jesuit missions, but noted that it was so difficult a task that it consumed most of the priest's time. Brébeuf felt his primary goal in his early years in New France was to learn the language.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlackburn2000117_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlackburn2000117-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>With increasing proficiency in the Wyandot language, Brébeuf became optimistic about advancing his missionary goals. By understanding Huron religious beliefs and communicating Christian fundamentals, he could secure converts to Christianity. He realized the people would not give up all their traditional beliefs.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlackburn200088_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlackburn200088-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Brébeuf worked tirelessly to record his findings for the benefit of other missionaries. He built on the work of <a href="/wiki/Recollects" title="Recollects">Recollects</a> priests but significantly advanced the study, particularly in his representations of sounds.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995116_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995116-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> He discovered and reported the feature of <a href="/wiki/Compound_words" class="mw-redirect" title="Compound words">compound words</a> in Huron, which may have been his major <a href="/wiki/Linguistic" class="mw-redirect" title="Linguistic">linguistic</a> contribution.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995122_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995122-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> This breakthrough had enormous consequences for further study, becoming the foundation for all subsequent Jesuit linguistic work.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995129_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995129-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He translated Ledesma's <a href="/wiki/Catechism" title="Catechism">catechism</a> from French into Huron, and arranged to have it printed. It was the first printed text in that language (with French orthography).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELeahey1995115–116_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELeahey1995115–116-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> He also compiled a dictionary of Huron words, emphasizing translation of religious phrases such as from prayers and the Bible.
</p>

<p>Brébeuf was killed at St. Ignace in <a href="/wiki/Huronia_(region)" title="Huronia (region)">Huronia</a> on 16 March 1649.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson200896_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson200896-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> He had been taken captive with <a href="/wiki/Gabriel_Lalemant" title="Gabriel Lalemant">Gabriel Lalemant</a> when the <a href="/wiki/Iroquois" title="Iroquois">Iroquois</a> destroyed the Huron mission village at Saint-Louis. The Iroquois took the priests to the occupied village of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Taenhatenteron&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Taenhatenteron (page does not exist)">Taenhatenteron</a> (also known as St. Ignace), where they subjected the missionaries and native converts to ritual torture before killing them.
</p><p>Three priests had been killed in <a href="/wiki/Mohawk_people" title="Mohawk people">Mohawk</a> country at <a href="/wiki/Ossernenon" class="mw-redirect" title="Ossernenon">Ossernenon</a> in 1642 and 1646. <a href="/wiki/Antoine_Daniel" title="Antoine Daniel">Antoine Daniel</a> had been killed in a similar Iroquois raid in 1648.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Charles_Garnier_(missionary)" title="Charles Garnier (missionary)">Charles Garnier</a> was killed by Iroquois in December 1649 in a <a href="/wiki/Petun" title="Petun">Petun</a> (Tobacco People) village,<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> and <a href="/wiki/Noel_Chabanel" class="mw-redirect" title="Noel Chabanel">Noel Chabanel</a> was also martyred that year in the conflict between the Mohawk and other tribes.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> The Jesuits considered the priests' martyrdom as proof that the mission to the Native Americans was blessed by God and would be successful.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson200868_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson200868-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Throughout the torture, Brébeuf was reported to have been more concerned for the fate of the other Jesuits and of the captive Native converts than for himself. As part of the ritual, the Iroquois drank his blood and ate his heart, as they wanted to absorb Brébeuf's courage in enduring the pain.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson200898_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson200898-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> The Iroquois mocked baptism by pouring boiling water over his head.<sup id="cite_ref-Magni_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Magni-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Jesuits Christophe Regnault and Paul Ragueneau provided the two accounts of the deaths of Jean de Brébeuf and Gabriel Lalemant. According to Regnault, they learned of the tortures and deaths from Huron refugee witnesses who had escaped from Saint-Ignace.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEParkman1888101_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEParkman1888101-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Regnault went to see the bodies to verify the accounts, and his superior Ragueneau's account was based on his report.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEParkman188896–97_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEParkman188896–97-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> The main accounts of Brébeuf's death come from <i>The Jesuit Relations.</i> Jesuit accounts of his torture emphasize his stoic nature and acceptance, claiming that he suffered silently without complaining.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETalbot1949297_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETalbot1949297-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Potential martyrdom was a central component of the Jesuit missionary identity.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson200874_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson200874-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Missionaries going to Canada knew they were at risk from harsh conditions, as well as from confronting alien cultures. They expected to die in the name of God; they believed the missionary life and its risks were a chance to save converts and be saved.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson200872_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson200872-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Relics.2C_beatification_and_canonization"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Relics,_beatification_and_canonization">Relics, beatification and canonization</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Relics, beatification and canonization">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:ON_Midland4_tango7174.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/ON_Midland4_tango7174.jpg/220px-ON_Midland4_tango7174.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/ON_Midland4_tango7174.jpg/330px-ON_Midland4_tango7174.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/ON_Midland4_tango7174.jpg/440px-ON_Midland4_tango7174.jpg 2x" data-file-width="800" data-file-height="600" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:ON_Midland4_tango7174.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Statue of Jean de Brébeuf on the site of the <a href="/wiki/Martyrs%27_Shrine" title="Martyrs&#39; Shrine">Martyrs' Shrine</a>, <a href="/wiki/Midland,_Ontario" title="Midland, Ontario">Midland, Ontario</a></div></div></div>
<p>Fathers Brébeuf and Lalement were recovered and buried together in a Sainte Marie cemetery.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEParkman1888391_38-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEParkman1888391-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> Brébeuf's relics later became important religious objects within  Catholic New France. Historian Allan Greer notes that "his death seemed to fit the profile of a perfect martyr's end" and was preceded by what were considered religious signs pointing to correspondences with the <a href="/wiki/Passion_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Passion of Christ">Passion of Christ</a>, which added to the significance of Brébeuf.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGreerApril_2000333_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreerApril_2000333-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> On 21 March 1649, Jesuit inspectors found the bodies of Brébeuf and Lalement.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008101_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008101-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> In the late spring of 1649, Christophe Regnault prepared the skeletal remains of Brébeuf and Lalemant for transportation to Québec for safekeeping. Regnault boiled away the remaining flesh and reburied it in the mission church, scraped the bones and dried them in an oven, wrapped each relic in separate silk, deposited them in two small chests, and sent them to Québec.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008112_41-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008112-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Brébeuf's family later donated his skull in a silver <a href="/wiki/Reliquary" title="Reliquary">reliquary</a> to the Catholic church orders in Québec.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEParkman1888391_38-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEParkman1888391-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> It was held by the women of the <a href="/wiki/H%C3%B4tel-Dieu_de_Qu%C3%A9bec" title="Hôtel-Dieu de Québec">Hôtel-Dieu de Québec</a> and the Ursuline convent from 1650 until 1925, when the relics were moved to the Québec Seminary for a ceremony to celebrate Brébeuf's <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatification</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008123_42-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008123-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> According to Catholic belief, these relics provide physical access to the influence of the saint of whom they are a part.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008128_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008128-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1652 Paul Raguenau went through the <i>Relations</i> and pulled out material relating to the martyrs of New France. He formalized this material in a document, to be used as the foundation of canonization proceedings, entitled <i>Memoires touchant la mort et les vertus (des Pères Jesuits),</i> or the <i>Manuscript of 1652.</i><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008116_44-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008116-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> The religious communities in New France considered the Jesuit martyrs as imitators of previous saints in the Catholic Church.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008123_42-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008123-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> In this sense, Brébeuf in particular, and others like him, reinforced the notion that "...Canada was a land of saints".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008165_45-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008165-45">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Catherine_de_Saint-Augustin" class="mw-redirect" title="Catherine de Saint-Augustin">Catherine de Saint-Augustin</a> said that Brébeuf appeared to her in a vision at the Québec Hôtel-Dieu while she was in a state of "mystical ecstasy," and he acted as her spiritual advisor.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008123_42-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008123-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> According to one account, Catherine de Saint-Augustin ground up part of Brébeuf's relic bone and gave it in a drink to a heretical and mortally ill man. It is said that the man was cured of his disease.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008124_46-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008124-46">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> In another instance, in 1660–61, a possessed woman was <a href="/wiki/Exorcism" title="Exorcism">exorcised</a> by the aid of one of Brébeuf's ribs, again while under the care of Catherine de Saint-Augustin. The exact circumstances of this event are disputed.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPearson2008125_47-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearson2008125-47">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup> Brébeuf's relics were also used by nuns who were treating wounded <a href="/wiki/Huguenot" class="mw-redirect" title="Huguenot">Huguenot</a> (Protestant) soldiers, and they "reported that his assistance [bone slivers put in soldiers' drinks] helped rescue these patients from <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heresy</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGreerApril_2000333_39-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreerApril_2000333-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Jean de Brébeuf was canonized by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XI" title="Pope Pius XI">Pope Pius XI</a> on 29 June 1930, and proclaimed one of the patron saints of Canada by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII" title="Pope Pius XII">Pope Pius XII</a> on 16 October 1940.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979_5-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELatourelle1979-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> A contemporary newspaper account of the canonization declares: "Brébeuf, the '<a href="/wiki/Ajax_(missionary)" title="Ajax (missionary)">Ajax</a> of the mission', stands out among them [others made saints with him] because of his giant frame, a man of noble birth, of vigorous passions tamed by religion," describing both the man and his defining drive according to formal terms of hagiography.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENY_Times30_June_1930_48-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENY_Times30_June_1930-48">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Modern_era">Modern era</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Modern era">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:StatueStJeanDeBrebeuf1.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/StatueStJeanDeBrebeuf1.jpg/220px-StatueStJeanDeBrebeuf1.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="364" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/08/StatueStJeanDeBrebeuf1.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="302" data-file-height="499" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:StatueStJeanDeBrebeuf1.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Statue of Jean de Brébeuf at Trois-Rivières</div></div></div>
<p>It is said that the modern name of the <a href="/wiki/First_Nations_in_Canada" title="First Nations in Canada">Native North American</a> sport of <a href="/wiki/Lacrosse" title="Lacrosse">lacrosse</a> was first coined by Brébeuf who thought that the sticks used in the game reminded him of a bishop's <a href="/wiki/Crosier" title="Crosier">crosier</a> (<i>crosse</i> in <a href="/wiki/French_language" title="French language">French</a>, and with the feminine definite article, <i>la crosse</i>).
</p><p>He is buried in the Church of St. Joseph at the reconstructed Jesuit mission of <a href="/wiki/Sainte-Marie_among_the_Hurons" title="Sainte-Marie among the Hurons">Sainte-Marie among the Hurons</a> across <a href="/wiki/Highway_12_(Ontario)" class="mw-redirect" title="Highway 12 (Ontario)">Highway 12</a> from the <a href="/wiki/Martyrs%27_Shrine" title="Martyrs&#39; Shrine">Martyrs' Shrine</a> Catholic Church near <a href="/wiki/Midland,_Ontario" title="Midland, Ontario">Midland, Ontario</a>. A plaque near the grave of Jean de Brébeuf and Gabriel Lalemant was unearthed during excavations at Ste Marie in 1954. The letters read "P. Jean de Brébeuf /brusle par les Iroquois /le 17 de mars l'an/1649" (Father Jean de Brébeuf, burned by the Iroquois, 17 March 1649).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGray2004_49-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGray2004-49">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In September 1984, <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> prayed over Brébeuf's skull before fully joining in an outdoor ecumenical service on the grounds of the nearby Martyrs' Shrine. The service was attended by an estimated 75,000 and mixed pre-Christian first-nation ritual with Catholic liturgy.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Numerous schools are named in his honour:
</p>
<ul><li>St. Jean Brebeuf School in <a href="/wiki/Calgary" title="Calgary">Calgary, Alberta</a><sup id="cite_ref-51" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-51">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>St. John Brebeuf Regional Secondary School in <a href="/wiki/Abbotsford,_British_Columbia" title="Abbotsford, British Columbia">Abbotsford, British Columbia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-52" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-52">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>St. John Brebeuf School in <a href="/wiki/Winnipeg" title="Winnipeg">Winnipeg, Manitoba</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-53" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-53">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> which is part of the St. John Brebeuf Catholic Parish</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Br%C3%A9beuf_College_School" class="mw-redirect" title="Brébeuf College School">Brebeuf College School</a> in <a href="/wiki/Toronto" title="Toronto">Toronto, Ontario</a></li>
<li>St. Jean de Brébeuf Catholic Secondary School in <a href="/wiki/Hamilton,_Ontario" title="Hamilton, Ontario">Hamilton, Ontario</a><sup id="cite_ref-54" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-54">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>St. Jean de Brébeuf Catholic Elementary School in <a href="/wiki/Brantford" title="Brantford">Brantford, Ontario</a> (Closed 2009)<sup id="cite_ref-55" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-55">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/St._Jean_de_Brebeuf_Catholic_High_School" title="St. Jean de Brebeuf Catholic High School">St. Jean de Brebeuf Catholic High School</a> in <a href="/wiki/Vaughan" title="Vaughan">Vaughan, Ontario</a></li>
<li>St. Jean Brebeuf Catholic School in <a href="/wiki/Brampton" title="Brampton">Brampton, Ontario</a><sup id="cite_ref-56" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-56">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>St. John Brebeuf Catholic School in <a href="/wiki/Erin,_Ontario" title="Erin, Ontario">Erin, Ontario</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-57" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-57">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup> which is part of St. John Brebeuf Catholic Parish, part of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Hamilton,_Ontario" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of Hamilton, Ontario">Roman Catholic Diocese</a> in Hamilton, Ontario</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Coll%C3%A8ge_Jean-de-Br%C3%A9beuf" title="Collège Jean-de-Brébeuf">Collège Jean-de-Brébeuf</a> in <a href="/wiki/Montreal" title="Montreal">Montreal, Quebec</a></li>
<li>École Jean-de-Brébeuf in <a href="/wiki/Gatineau" title="Gatineau">Gatineau, Québec</a><sup id="cite_ref-58" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-58">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Brebeuf_Jesuit_Preparatory_School" title="Brebeuf Jesuit Preparatory School">Brebeuf Jesuit Preparatory School</a> in Indianapolis, Indiana</li></ul>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf_mosbo6.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c4/St_Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf_mosbo6.jpg/220px-St_Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf_mosbo6.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c4/St_Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf_mosbo6.jpg/330px-St_Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf_mosbo6.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c4/St_Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf_mosbo6.jpg/440px-St_Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf_mosbo6.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3879" data-file-height="5173" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Jean_de_Br%C3%A9beuf_mosbo6.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Statue in Parc Brébeuf, Gatineau</div></div></div>
<p>There is Eglise St-Jean de Brebeuf in <a href="/wiki/Greater_Sudbury" title="Greater Sudbury">Sudbury, Ontario</a>, and a St. John Brebeuf Catholic Parish in <a href="/wiki/Niles,_Illinois" title="Niles, Illinois">Niles, Illinois, USA</a>. There is also a unit at <a href="/wiki/Camp_Ondessonk" title="Camp Ondessonk">Camp Ondessonk</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Shawnee_National_Forest" title="Shawnee National Forest">Shawnee National Forest</a> named after Jean de Brébeuf. The Catholic camp is named for all of the <a href="/wiki/Canadian_Martyrs" title="Canadian Martyrs">North American Martyrs</a> and those who helped them.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Parish_municipality_(Quebec)" class="mw-redirect" title="Parish municipality (Quebec)">parish municipality</a> of <a href="/wiki/Br%C3%A9beuf,_Quebec" title="Brébeuf, Quebec">Brébeuf, Quebec</a>, is named after him, as is rue de Brébeuf on the <a href="/wiki/Le_Plateau-Mont-Royal" title="Le Plateau-Mont-Royal">Plateau Mont-Royal</a> in Montreal. Parc Brébeuf in the <a href="/wiki/Hull,_Quebec" title="Hull, Quebec">Hull</a> region of Gatineau, Quebec is named in his honour, and features a statue.
</p><p>The character of Christophe in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Orenda" title="The Orenda">The Orenda</a></i>, a 2013 novel by Joseph Boyden, is based on Jean de Brebeuf.<sup id="cite_ref-59" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-59">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-60" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-60">&#91;60&#93;</a></sup> The novel won the 2014 <a href="/wiki/Canada_Reads" title="Canada Reads">Canada Reads</a> competition, a reality show with elimination-style voting on CBC Radio.
</p><p>Jean de Brébeuf is the subject of <i>Brébeuf and his Brethren</i>, a blank-verse <a href="/wiki/Epic_poetry" title="Epic poetry">epic poem</a> by the Canadian poet <a href="/wiki/E._J._Pratt" title="E. J. Pratt">E. J. Pratt</a>, <a href="/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society_of_Canada" title="Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada">FRSC</a>, for which Pratt was awarded one of his three <a href="/wiki/1940_Governor_General%27s_Awards" title="1940 Governor General&#39;s Awards">Governor General's Awards</a> for Poetry in 1940.<sup id="cite_ref-online_61-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-online-61">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
