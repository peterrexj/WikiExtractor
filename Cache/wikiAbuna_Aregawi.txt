<p><b>Abuna Aregawi</b> (also called <b>Za-Mika'el Aragawi</b>) was a sixth-century Syrian monk<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and canonized by the <a href="/wiki/Ethiopian_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church" title="Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church">Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church</a>, as well as by the <a href="/wiki/Ethiopian_Catholic_Church" title="Ethiopian Catholic Church">Ethiopian Catholic Church</a>, as well as the <a href="/wiki/Eritrean_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church" title="Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church">Eritrean Orthodox Church</a>. He is one of the <a href="/wiki/Nine_Saints" title="Nine Saints">Nine Saints</a>, who came from the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman Empire</a> to <a href="/wiki/Ethiopia" title="Ethiopia">Ethiopia</a>, and are credited for founding many monasteries and churches and was the main force behind installing monasticism in Ethiopia <sup id="cite_ref-Buxton_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Buxton-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Tradition holds that Abuna Aregawi founded the monastery <a href="/wiki/Debre_Damo" title="Debre Damo">Debre Damo</a> in <a href="/wiki/Tigray_Region" title="Tigray Region">Tigray Region</a> of Ethiopia, which is said to have been commissioned by Emperor <a href="/wiki/Lalibela_(Emperor_of_Ethiopia)" class="mw-redirect" title="Lalibela (Emperor of Ethiopia)">Gebre Mesqel</a> of Axum.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Abuna_Aregawi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He is one of the <a href="/wiki/Nine_Saints" title="Nine Saints">Nine Saints</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Ethiopian_Orthodox_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Ethiopian Orthodox Church">Ethiopian Orthodox Church</a>. These learned monks came from various parts of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman Empire</a> to escape persecution after the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Chalcedon" title="Council of Chalcedon">Council of Chalcedon</a> (451). Once in Ethiopia, they revitalized Christianity in <a href="/wiki/Ethiopia" title="Ethiopia">Ethiopia</a>, and to whom the <a href="/wiki/Ge%27ez" class="mw-redirect" title="Ge&#39;ez">Ge'ez</a> version of the <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a> is attributed. Besides Abba Aregawi, the Nine Saints included Abba Aftsé, Abba 'Aléf, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Abba_G%C3%A4rima&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Abba Gärima (page does not exist)">Abba Gärima</a>, Abba Guba, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Abba_Liqanos&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Abba Liqanos (page does not exist)">Abba Liqanos</a>, <a href="/wiki/Abba_Pantelewon" title="Abba Pantelewon">Abba Pantelewon</a>, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Abba_Sehma&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Abba Sehma (page does not exist)">Abba Sehma</a>, and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Abba_Y%C3%A4m%27ata&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Abba Yäm&#39;ata (page does not exist)">Abba Yäm'ata</a> (Yemata).
</p><p>Aregawi was a disciple of the famous Coptic monk <a href="/wiki/Pachomius_the_Great" title="Pachomius the Great">Pachomius the Great</a> who is regarded a founder as <a href="/wiki/Cenobitic_Monasticism" class="mw-redirect" title="Cenobitic Monasticism">Cenobitic Monasticism</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After spending twelve years at the court of king <a href="/wiki/Ella_Amida" class="mw-redirect" title="Ella Amida">Ella Amida</a> of <a href="/wiki/Aksumite_Kingdom" class="mw-redirect" title="Aksumite Kingdom">Axum</a>, he set out with his companion, the nun Edna, to found Debre Damo.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Later in his life, king <a href="/wiki/Kaleb_of_Axum" title="Kaleb of Axum">Kaleb</a> is said to have sought his advice before setting out to <a href="/wiki/South_Arabia" title="South Arabia">south Arabia</a> against the <a href="/wiki/Jewish" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish">Jewish</a> king <a href="/wiki/Dhu_Nuwas" title="Dhu Nuwas">Dhu Nuwas</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Debre_Demo_monastery">Debre Demo monastery</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Abuna_Aregawi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Debre Demo monastery">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to legend, God provided a large <a href="/wiki/Snake" title="Snake">snake</a> to aid Aregawi in climbing the <i>amba</i>, or steep-sided mountain, so he could build Debre Damo in <a href="/wiki/Tigray_Region" title="Tigray Region">Tigray</a>. As <a href="/wiki/David_Roden_Buxton" title="David Roden Buxton">David Buxton</a> recounts the story, "when Abba Aragawi, the founder of the monastery, came to the foot of the cliff a great serpent appeared. As <a href="/wiki/Michael_(archangel)" title="Michael (archangel)">Michael the Archangel</a> stood by to give directions, the serpent folded Abba Aregawi in his coils and drew him to the top of the mountain."<sup id="cite_ref-Buxton_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Buxton-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The monastery is built with curved wood panels, painted ceilings and walls dedicated to the legend of Abuna Aregawi. Many books have been written there and distributed to churches throughout Ethiopia. There are a collection of some of the best manuscripts presently existing in Ethiopia.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>His feast day is on Ṭəqəmt 14 in the Ethiopian calendar (24 or 25 October in Gregorian calendar).<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
