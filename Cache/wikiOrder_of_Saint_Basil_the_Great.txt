<p>The <b>Order of Saint Basil the Great</b> (<a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_language" title="Ukrainian language">Ukrainian</a>: <span lang="uk">Чин Святого Василія Великого</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Ukrainian" title="Romanization of Ukrainian">romanized</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Ukrainian-language romanization"><i lang="uk-Latn">Chyn Sviatoho Vasyliia Velykoho</i></span>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Ordo Sancti Basilii Magni</i>, abbreviated <b>OSBM</b>),<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> also known as the <b>Basilian Order of Saint Josaphat</b>, is a <a href="/wiki/Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Greek Catholic Church">Greek Catholic</a> monastic order of pontifical right that works actively among <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church">Ukrainian Catholics</a> and other Greek-Catholic churches in central and Eastern Europe. The order received approbation on August 20, 1631, and is based at the <a href="/wiki/Monastery_of_the_Holy_Trinity,_Vilnius" title="Monastery of the Holy Trinity, Vilnius">Monastery of the Holy Trinity, Vilnius</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Order_of_Saint_Basil_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Revival">Revival</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Order_of_Saint_Basil_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Revival">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In the 16th century, with the efforts of <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_of_Kiev" class="mw-redirect" title="Metropolitan of Kiev">Metropolitan of Kiev</a> <a href="/wiki/Josyf_Veliamyn_Rutsky" class="mw-redirect" title="Josyf Veliamyn Rutsky">Josyf Veliamyn Rutsky</a> and Archbishop of Polotsk <a href="/wiki/Josaphat_Kuntsevych" title="Josaphat Kuntsevych">Josaphat Kuntsevych</a>, the monastic order was revived on territory of the <a href="/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth" title="Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth">Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth</a>. Following <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">World War II</a>, the order was eliminated by the Russian Orthodox from its original territory and forced into exile. With the <a href="/wiki/Fall_of_the_Soviet_Union" class="mw-redirect" title="Fall of the Soviet Union">fall of the Soviet Union</a>, it was reestablished again in modern Ukraine as part of the <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church">Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church</a>.
</p><p>Besides the Order of Saint Basil the Great, there is a smaller order of <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Studite_Monks" title="Ukrainian Studite Monks">Studite Monks</a> that was revived at the end of 19th century by Metropolitan of Galicia <a href="/wiki/Andrey_Sheptytsky" title="Andrey Sheptytsky">Andrey Sheptytsky</a> and is based in the <a href="/wiki/Univ_Lavra" title="Univ Lavra">Univ Lavra</a>.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Antony_and_feodosy.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/10/Antony_and_feodosy.jpg/220px-Antony_and_feodosy.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="276" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/10/Antony_and_feodosy.jpg/330px-Antony_and_feodosy.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/10/Antony_and_feodosy.jpg/440px-Antony_and_feodosy.jpg 2x" data-file-width="671" data-file-height="841" /></a><figcaption>Sts. Anthony and Theodosius</figcaption></figure>
<p>The order is based upon the ascetic writings of <a href="/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea" title="Basil of Caesarea">Basil of Caesarea</a> (329-379, in accordance with the <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_St_Basil" class="mw-redirect" title="Rule of St Basil">Rule of St Basil</a> laid down by him and later developed by <a href="/wiki/Theodore_the_Studite" title="Theodore the Studite">Theodore the Studite</a> (760-826), <a href="/wiki/Theodosius_of_Kiev" title="Theodosius of Kiev">Theodosius of Kiev</a> (†1074), <a href="/wiki/Josaphat_Kuntsevych" title="Josaphat Kuntsevych">Josaphat Kuntsevych</a> (1580-1623), and the <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_of_Kiev" class="mw-redirect" title="Metropolitan of Kiev">Metropolitan of Kiev</a> <a href="/wiki/Josyf_Veliamyn_Rutsky" class="mw-redirect" title="Josyf Veliamyn Rutsky">Joseph Benjamin Rutsky</a> (1574-1637).
</p><p>Monastic life began to develop in Ukraine in the time of <a href="/wiki/Vladimir_the_Great" title="Vladimir the Great">Vladimir the Great</a> (980-1015), when the first monks settled in caves near <a href="/wiki/Kiev" class="mw-redirect" title="Kiev">Kiev</a>, led by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Anthony_of_Kiev" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Anthony of Kiev">Anthony</a> and <a href="/wiki/Theodosius_of_Kiev" title="Theodosius of Kiev">Theodosius</a>. After the Mongol invasions in the 13th century, the monks fled west to <a href="/wiki/Halych-Volhynia" class="mw-redirect" title="Halych-Volhynia">Halych-Volhynia</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Lithuania" title="Grand Duchy of Lithuania">Grand Duchy of Lithuania</a>, spreading Eastern Monasticism there. The <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church">Eastern Rite Ruthenian Church</a> (in modern-day <a href="/wiki/Belarus" title="Belarus">Belarus</a> and Ukraine) has re-affirmed its communion with the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Union_of_Brest" title="Union of Brest">Union of Brest</a> in 1596. The monasteries living according to the rules of St. Basil and St. Theodore Studite, previously undergoing a period of laxity and decline, were reformed by the initiative of <a href="/wiki/Josaphat_Kuntsevych" title="Josaphat Kuntsevych">Josaphat Kuntsevych</a> and Joseph Benjamin Rutsky, beginning with the <a href="/wiki/Church_and_monastery_of_the_Holy_Trinity,_Vilnius" class="mw-redirect" title="Church and monastery of the Holy Trinity, Vilnius">monastery of the Holy Trinity</a> in <a href="/wiki/Vilnius" title="Vilnius">Vilnius</a>. Following this reform in 1617 the individual monasteries united into a single congregation under a Protarchimandrite directly subject to the Metropolitan, similar to the path Western Rite monasticism took during the Middle Ages. In 1739 a second congregation was formed by monasteries in Halychyna and in 1744 both congregations were united in the Ruthenian Order of St. Basil the Great by Pope <a href="/wiki/Benedict_XIV" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedict XIV">Benedict XIV</a>.
</p><p>The Order of Saint Basil the Great spread and flourished across modern day Belarus and Ukraine and played a key role in the education both of laity and clergy, and helped to preserve the distinctiveness of the Ruthenian culture in the predominantly Polish and Roman Catholic <a href="/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth" title="Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth">Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth</a> until the <a href="/wiki/Partitions_of_Poland" title="Partitions of Poland">partitions of Poland</a> at the end of the 18th century. In 1772 the Order had over 200 monasteries and over 1000 monks, six <a href="/wiki/Seminary" title="Seminary">seminaries</a>, twenty schools and colleges, and four printing houses.
</p><p>In the last years of the 18th century most of the <a href="/wiki/Ruthenians" title="Ruthenians">Ruthenian</a> lands came under the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Empire" title="Russian Empire">Russian Empire</a>, where the Order along with the whole Ruthenian Church was persecuted. Eventually the monasteries were subjected to the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodox Church</a>. A small part of modern-day Ukraine came under <a href="/wiki/Austrian_Empire" title="Austrian Empire">Austrian</a> rule where the fate of the Ruthenian Church was much better. However, the Order suffered under the policies of <a href="/wiki/Joseph_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor#Religion" title="Joseph II, Holy Roman Emperor">Emperor Joseph II</a> directed against all religious orders. In the second half of the 19th century efforts were undertaken to renew the Order. By 1882 it was reduced to just 60 monks in 14 monasteries. With permission from Pope <a href="/wiki/Leo_XIII" class="mw-redirect" title="Leo XIII">Leo XIII</a> the Basilian Constitution was updated with help from the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">Society of Jesus</a> starting with the <a href="/wiki/Dobromyl" title="Dobromyl">Dobromyl</a> monastery, by which it became less sedentary and more missionary, among other things allowing the monks to work with the <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_diaspora" title="Ukrainian diaspora">Ukrainian diaspora</a> overseas. The Basilians reached Brazil (1897), Canada (1902), United States (1907), and <a href="/wiki/Argentina" title="Argentina">Argentina</a> (1934). New provinces were established covering <a href="/wiki/Carpathian_Ruthenia" title="Carpathian Ruthenia">Transcarpathia</a>, Hungary, and <a href="/wiki/Yugoslavia" title="Yugoslavia">Yugoslavia</a> and Romania. By 1939 the number of monks rose to over 650.  In 1944 the order purchased the <a href="/wiki/John_E._Aldred_Estate" title="John E. Aldred Estate">John E. Aldred Estate</a> at <a href="/wiki/Lattingtown,_New_York" title="Lattingtown, New York">Lattingtown, New York</a>, now known as St. Josaphat's Monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-nrhpinv_ny_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-nrhpinv_ny-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Following the <a href="/wiki/Second_World_War" class="mw-redirect" title="Second World War">Second World War</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Soviets" class="mw-redirect" title="Soviets">Soviets</a> entered further into Europe and forced the <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church">Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church</a> underground. In all of the Soviet-controlled territories only a single Basilian monastery was left open, in the Polish capital of <a href="/wiki/Warsaw" title="Warsaw">Warsaw</a>. Nonetheless, the Order survived among the Ukrainian diaspora in the free world (and in communist Yugoslavia where the regime was relatively benign) and in Ukraine itself where the monks secretly prayed and catechized.
</p><p>After the <a href="/wiki/Collapse_of_the_Soviet_Union" class="mw-redirect" title="Collapse of the Soviet Union">collapse of the Soviet Union</a>, the Order was reestablished in independent Ukraine and other Central and Eastern European countries such as Hungary, <a href="/wiki/Romania" title="Romania">Romania</a>, <a href="/wiki/Slovakia" title="Slovakia">Slovakia</a> and <a href="/wiki/Portugal" title="Portugal">Portugal</a>. Some old monasteries have been restored and new ones established. In 2001 there were over 600 monks, 300 of them in Ukraine.
</p>
