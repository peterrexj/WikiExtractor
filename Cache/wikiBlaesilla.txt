					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Roman saint</div>
<p class="mw-empty-elt">
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1066479718">.mw-parser-output .infobox-subbox{padding:0;border:none;margin:-3px;width:auto;min-width:100%;font-size:100%;clear:none;float:none;background-color:transparent}.mw-parser-output .infobox-3cols-child{margin:auto}.mw-parser-output .infobox .navbar{font-size:100%}body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-header,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-subheader,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-above,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-title,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-image,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-full-data,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-below{text-align:center}</style><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div class="honorific-prefix" style="display:inline;font-size: 77%; font-weight: normal;">Saint</div><br /><div style="display:inline;" class="fn">Blaesilla</div></th></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Born</th><td class="infobox-data">364<br /><a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Died</th><td class="infobox-data">384 (aged&#160;19&#8211;20)</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Feast</a></th><td class="infobox-data">22 January</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">Patronage</a></th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>,
<a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Orthodox Church</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Influences</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Saint Jerome</a></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p><b>Blaesilla</b>, also known as <b>Blesilla</b><sup id="cite_ref-cruz_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cruz-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> (364–384), was a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman</a> <a href="/wiki/Widow" title="Widow">widow</a> and disciple of <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>. She was born into a wealthy senatorial family in Rome, the eldest daughter of <a href="/wiki/Paula_of_Rome" title="Paula of Rome">Paula of Rome</a> and sister of <a href="/wiki/Eustochium" title="Eustochium">Eustochium</a>, who were members of a group of wealthy Christian women who followed the teachings of Jerome.  Blaesilla was widowed at the age of 18; at first, she enjoyed her freedom as a widow, but after a life-threatening fever, became "a changed woman"<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-32_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-32-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and a severe <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetic</a>, practicing <a href="/wiki/Fasting" title="Fasting">fasting</a> as a spiritual discipline. Her fasts dramatically weakened her, and she died within four months, at the age of 20.
</p><p>Blaesilla's death caused  "bitter controversy" in Rome;<sup id="cite_ref-letters_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-letters-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> many Romans blamed Jerome for her death and demanded that he be removed from Rome. Eventually, Jerome left Rome, with Blaesilla's mother and sister, to live as an ascetic in the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Land" title="Holy Land">Holy Land</a>. Most of the knowledge about Blaesilla's life comes from the writings of Jerome, in which he described her piety and virtue. She also inspired Jerome to translate the book of <a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastes" title="Ecclesiastes">Ecclesiastes</a>. Modern writers and researchers have connected Blaesilla with the eating disorder <a href="/wiki/Anorexia_nervosa" title="Anorexia nervosa">anorexia nervosa</a>; she has been called "this ancient woman who starved herself in death in the name of Christ"<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-33_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-33-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and "the first victim of anorexia nervosa, described in the literature",<sup id="cite_ref-anorexia-26_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-anorexia-26-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> and has been compared to modern patients with the disorder.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Blaesilla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/06/Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg/220px-Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="320" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/06/Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg/330px-Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/06/Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg/440px-Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2024" data-file-height="2948" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Saint Jerome, Saint Paula, and Saint Eustochium, at National Gallery of Art in Washington.</div></div></div>
<p>Blaesilla was born in Rome in 364, into a wealthy aristocratic family, the eldest daughter of <a href="/wiki/Paula_of_Rome" title="Paula of Rome">Paula of Rome</a>, the widow of Toxotius, a wealthy Roman senator.  Toxicus' brother was a former <a href="/wiki/Vicarius" title="Vicarius">vicarius</a> of Rome and <a href="/wiki/Proconsul" title="Proconsul">proconsul</a> of Africa.<sup id="cite_ref-matthews-264_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-matthews-264-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>  Blaesilla had three sisters, one of which was <a href="/wiki/Eustochium" title="Eustochium">Eustochium</a>.  Both Paula and Eustochium were part of "the vigorous Christian community" in Rome during the 4th century that was made up of wealthy women like Paula and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Asella" title="Saint Asella">Asella</a>, and advised by <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-32_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-32-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  Blaesilla was described as "a beautiful, talented young girl who loved the merry life of aristocratic Rome";<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-32_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-32-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> she enjoyed the theater, dinner parties, and socializing with other young people.<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-32_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-32-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Blaesilla was 18, she married Furius, a son of Titiana, who was devoutly Christian like Blaesilla's mother.<sup id="cite_ref-cruz_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cruz-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>  Blaesilla's husband died seven months after they were married, leaving her a wealthy widow. She mourned his death, but as historian Joyce E. Salisbury stated, Blaesilla was too young to "forego the exciting life she loved",<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-32_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-32-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> living as other young widows did at the time, lavishly spending her time and money on her dress, appearance, and parties, and enjoying her freedom as a widow.<sup id="cite_ref-matthews-264_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-matthews-264-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>  Jerome, who advocated <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a> and extreme <a href="/wiki/Fasting" title="Fasting">fasting</a> as a spiritual practice because he believed that it helped his followers control their flesh and focus more on God, chastised her for "her frivolous life".<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-32_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-32-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Shortly after her husband's death, Blaesilla became ill with a fever; she recovered and became "a changed woman".<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-32_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-32-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  Writer Joan Carroll Cruz said that Blaesilla had "yield[ed] to the promptings of grace"<sup id="cite_ref-cruz_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cruz-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> and chose to spend "the rest of her short life in great austerity".<sup id="cite_ref-cruz_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cruz-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>  She studied scripture, learned to speak Greek with a perfect accent, learned Hebrew in a few months, carried books with her wherever she went, and demanded that Jerome write commentaries for her to study.<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-33_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-33-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>  Jerome, speaking about her intellectual talent, said: "Who can recall without a sigh the earnestness of her prayers, the brilliancy of her conversation, the tenacity of her memory, and the quickness of her intellect?"<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>  She became an ascetic, wearing plain clothes and participating in extreme fasts that dramatically weakened her.<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-32_2-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-32-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  Historian John Matthews conjectured that Blaesilla's behavior was due to depression and a response to a life-threatening illness.<sup id="cite_ref-matthews-264_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-matthews-264-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>  Her family grew alarmed about her declining health. She was not able to withstand the demands that her asceticism had on her body, though, and she died within four months, in 384, at the age of 20.<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-32_2-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-32-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  Blaesiila's feast day is 22 January.<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-123_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-123-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Blaesilla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Historians Finley Hooper and Matthew Schwartz reported that Blaesilla's death caused "bitter controversy" in Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-letters_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-letters-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  Salisbury reported that many in Rome were outraged at Blaesilla's premature death and blamed Jerome.<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-33_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-33-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>  According to Jerome in one of his letters to Paula, in which he described Blaesella's death, Paula became so overwhelmed by grief during Blaesilla's funeral that she collapsed during the procession and had to be taken away unconscious.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  Historian John Matthews stated that Paula's response was due to her own asceticism, and that she had been weakened by the lack of food.<sup id="cite_ref-matthews-264_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-matthews-264-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>  Jerome, in his attempt to console her, proclaimed Blaesilla "the victor in the struggle against Satan".<sup id="cite_ref-letters_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-letters-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Much of the Roman populace was outraged at Jerome for causing the premature death of such a lively young woman, and his insistence to Paula that Blaesilla should not be mourned, and complaints that her grief was excessive, were seen as heartless, polarising Roman opinion against him.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> If it were not for the writings of Jerome, in which he described her piety and virtue, we would know very little about Blaesilla.<sup id="cite_ref-cruz_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cruz-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-123_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-123-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>  Cruz reported that Jerome translated the book of <a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastes" title="Ecclesiastes">Ecclesiastes</a> at Blaesilla's request.<sup id="cite_ref-cruz_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cruz-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Blaesilla's death polarized Roman opinion against Jerome. They blamed him for her death, stated that she had been killed by fasting, and demanded that monks be expelled from Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-matthews-264_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-matthews-264-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> While in Rome, Jerome served as secretary to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Damasus_I" title="Pope Damasus I">Pope Damasus I</a>. Jerome's criticism of the Roman clergy of Rome brought increased hostility towards against him from the clergy and their supporters. Damasus died in December 384. Shortly after the pope's death, an inquiry was brought questioning the relationship between Jerome and Paula and he was forced to leave his position. Jerome left Rome for Antioch, before heading for Jerusalem. Paula and Blaesilla's younger sister Eustochium soon followed; they did not seem to blame him for Blaesilla's death but were certain that "she had died in a state of grace".<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-33_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-33-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>  Jerome spent the last 34 years of his life living as an ascetic supported by Paula, who founded a double monastery near Bethlehem.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Modern writers and researchers have connected Blaesilla with the eating disorder <a href="/wiki/Anorexia_nervosa" title="Anorexia nervosa">anorexia nervosa</a>.  Salisbury called Blaesilla "this ancient woman who starved herself in death in the name of Christ",<sup id="cite_ref-salisbury-33_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-salisbury-33-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and writer Leonard Shlain stated that she "died from anorexia".<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>  Hooper and Schwartz were critical of Jerome, stating that his beliefs about asceticism and monasticism demonstrated an attitude that would prevail in Christianity for centuries.<sup id="cite_ref-letters_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-letters-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  Researchers Martine Docx and Paul Govaert called Blaesilla "probably the first recorded case of death due to anorexia"<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> and "the first victim of anorexia nervosa, described in the literature".<sup id="cite_ref-anorexia-26_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-anorexia-26-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>  They compared modern patients with anorexia to religious ascetics of the past like Blaesilla, stating that the "refusal of food both in antiquity and in modern society by the anorexic patient is an extreme form of self-discipline to pursue a new, better identity".<sup id="cite_ref-anorexia-27_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-anorexia-27-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>  They also stated that the refusal of food offered ascetics like Blaesella "a spiritual transcendence",<sup id="cite_ref-anorexia-27_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-anorexia-27-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> while the goal for modern patients was the pursuit of the "perfect body".<sup id="cite_ref-anorexia-27_14-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-anorexia-27-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>  They suggested that Blaesilla's motivation might also have included a desire to earn the love and approval of Jerome, her mother, and her sister, much like the way in which modern patients become anorexic to earn the love and approval of their families and society.<sup id="cite_ref-anorexia-26_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-anorexia-26-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
