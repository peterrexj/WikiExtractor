<p><b>Saint Dunstan</b><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> (c. 909 – 19 May 988)<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELapidge2004_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELapidge2004-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> was an English <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a>. He was successively <a href="/wiki/Abbot_of_Glastonbury" title="Abbot of Glastonbury">Abbot of Glastonbury Abbey</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Worcester" title="Bishop of Worcester">Bishop of Worcester</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_London" title="Bishop of London">Bishop of London</a> and <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, later <a href="/wiki/Canonised" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonised">canonised</a> as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His work restored monastic life in England and reformed the English Church. His 11th-century biographer <a href="/wiki/Osbern_of_Canterbury" title="Osbern of Canterbury">Osbern</a>, himself an artist and scribe, states that Dunstan was skilled in "making a picture and forming letters", as were other clergy of his age who reached senior rank.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAlexander19929_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlexander19929-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
Dunstan served as an important minister of state to several English kings. He was the most popular saint in England for nearly two centuries, having gained fame for the many stories of his greatness, not least among which were those concerning his famed cunning in defeating the <a href="/wiki/Devil_in_Christianity" title="Devil in Christianity">Devil</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span id="Early_life_.28909.E2.80.93943.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_(909–943)">Early life (909–943)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life (909–943)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Birth_and_relatives">Birth and relatives</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Birth and relatives">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>According to Dunstan's earliest biographer, known only as 'B',<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup>  his parents were called Heorstan and Cynethryth and they lived near <a href="/wiki/Glastonbury" title="Glastonbury">Glastonbury</a>. B states that Dunstan <i>"oritur"</i> in the days of King Æthelstan, 924 to 939. <i>"Oritur"</i> has often been taken to mean "born", but this is unlikely as another source states that he was ordained during Æthelstan's reign, and he would have been under the minimum age of 30 if he was born no earlier than 924. It is more likely that <i>"oritur"</i> should be taken as "emerged", and that he was born around 910. B states that he was related to <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfheah_the_Bald" title="Ælfheah the Bald">Ælfheah the Bald</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Winchester" title="Bishop of Winchester">Bishop of Winchester</a> and <a href="/wiki/Cynesige_of_Lichfield" title="Cynesige of Lichfield">Cynesige</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Lichfield" title="Bishop of Lichfield">Bishop of Lichfield</a>. According to a later biographer, <a href="/wiki/Adelard_of_Ghent" title="Adelard of Ghent">Adelard of Ghent</a>, he was a nephew of <a href="/wiki/Athelm" title="Athelm">Athelm</a>, <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, but this is less certain as it is not mentioned by B, who should have known as he had been a member of Dunstan's household.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELapidge2004_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELapidge2004-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="School_to_the_king.27s_court"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="School_to_the_king's_court">School to the king's court</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: School to the king&#039;s court">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>As a young boy, Dunstan studied under the <a href="/wiki/Hiberno-Scottish_mission" title="Hiberno-Scottish mission">Irish monks</a> who then occupied the ruins of <a href="/wiki/Glastonbury_Abbey" title="Glastonbury Abbey">Glastonbury Abbey</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-co_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-co-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Accounts tell of his youthful optimism and of his vision of the abbey being restored. While still a boy, Dunstan was stricken with a near-fatal illness and effected a seemingly miraculous recovery. Even as a child, he was noted for his devotion to learning and for his mastery of many kinds of artistic craftsmanship. With his parents' consent he was <a href="/wiki/Tonsure" title="Tonsure">tonsured</a>, received <a href="/wiki/Minor_orders" title="Minor orders">minor orders</a> and served in the ancient church of St Mary. He became so well known for his devotion to learning that he is said to have been summoned by Athelm to enter his service.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He was later appointed to the court of King <a href="/wiki/Athelstan" class="mw-redirect" title="Athelstan">Athelstan</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Dunstan2.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/d/dd/Dunstan2.jpg/220px-Dunstan2.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="261" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/d/dd/Dunstan2.jpg/330px-Dunstan2.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/d/dd/Dunstan2.jpg/440px-Dunstan2.jpg 2x" data-file-width="492" data-file-height="584" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Dunstan2.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Dunstan playing his harp as the Devil is paying a visit</div></div></div>
<p>Dunstan soon became a <a href="/wiki/Favourite" title="Favourite">favourite</a> of the king and was the envy of other members of the court.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> A plot was hatched to disgrace him and Dunstan was accused of being involved with <a href="/wiki/Witchcraft" title="Witchcraft">witchcraft</a> and <a href="/wiki/Black_magic" title="Black magic">black magic</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The king ordered him to leave the court and as Dunstan was leaving the palace his enemies physically attacked him, beat him severely, bound him, and threw him into a <a href="/wiki/Cesspit" title="Cesspit">cesspool</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-cw_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cw-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> He managed to crawl out and make his way to the house of a friend. From there, he journeyed to Winchester and entered the service of <a href="/wiki/Alphege_the_Bald" class="mw-redirect" title="Alphege the Bald">Ælfheah</a>, Bishop of Winchester.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The bishop tried to persuade him to become a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a>, but Dunstan was doubtful whether he had a vocation to a <a href="/wiki/Clerical_celibacy" title="Clerical celibacy">celibate</a> life. The answer came in the form of an attack of swelling tumours all over Dunstan's body. This ailment was so severe that it was thought to be <a href="/wiki/Leprosy" title="Leprosy">leprosy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> It was more probably some form of <a href="/wiki/Bacteremia" class="mw-redirect" title="Bacteremia">blood poisoning</a> caused by being beaten and thrown in the cesspool.<sup id="cite_ref-cw_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cw-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Whatever the cause, it changed Dunstan's mind. He took <a href="/wiki/Holy_Orders" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Orders">Holy Orders</a> in 943, in the presence of Ælfheah, and returned to live the life of a hermit at Glastonbury.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Against the old church of St Mary he built a small cell 5 feet (1.5&#160;m) long and <style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1020198016">.mw-parser-output .frac{white-space:nowrap}.mw-parser-output .frac .num,.mw-parser-output .frac .den{font-size:80%;line-height:0;vertical-align:super}.mw-parser-output .frac .den{vertical-align:sub}.mw-parser-output .sr-only{border:0;clip:rect(0,0,0,0);height:1px;margin:-1px;overflow:hidden;padding:0;position:absolute;width:1px}</style><span class="frac" role="math">2<span class="sr-only">+</span><span class="num">1</span>&#8260;<span class="den">2</span></span> feet (0.76&#160;m) deep. It was there that Dunstan studied, worked at his art, and played on his harp.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> It is at this time, according to a late 11th-century legend, that the <a href="/wiki/Devil_in_Christianity" title="Devil in Christianity">Devil</a> is said to have tempted Dunstan and to have been held by the face with Dunstan's tongs.<sup id="cite_ref-cw_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cw-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Monk_and_abbot_.28943.E2.80.93957.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Monk_and_abbot_(943–957)">Monk and abbot (943–957)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Monk and abbot (943–957)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Life_as_a_monk">Life as a monk</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Life as a monk">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:DunstanLarge.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/DunstanLarge.jpg/220px-DunstanLarge.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="273" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/DunstanLarge.jpg/330px-DunstanLarge.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/DunstanLarge.jpg/440px-DunstanLarge.jpg 2x" data-file-width="512" data-file-height="636" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:DunstanLarge.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Possibly Dunstan praying before Christ</div></div></div>
<p>Dunstan worked as a <a href="/wiki/Silversmith" title="Silversmith">silversmith</a> and in the <a href="/wiki/Scriptorium" title="Scriptorium">scriptorium</a> while he was living at Glastonbury. It is thought likely that he was the artist who drew the well-known image of Christ with a small kneeling monk beside him in the <i>Glastonbury Classbook</i>, "one of the first of a series of outline drawings which were to become a special feature of <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_art" title="Anglo-Saxon art">Anglo-Saxon art</a> of this period."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAlexander19929_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlexander19929-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Dunstan became famous as a musician, <a href="/wiki/Illuminated_manuscript" title="Illuminated manuscript">illuminator</a>, and metalworker.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Lady Æthelflaed, King <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelstan" title="Æthelstan">Æthelstan's</a> niece, made Dunstan a trusted adviser and on her death, she left a considerable fortune to him.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He used this money later in life to foster and encourage a monastic revival in England. About the same time, his father Heorstan died and Dunstan inherited his fortune as well. He became a person of great influence, and on the death of King Æthelstan in 940, the new King, <a href="/wiki/Edmund_I_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Edmund I of England">Edmund</a>, summoned him to his court at <a href="/wiki/Cheddar,_Somerset" title="Cheddar, Somerset">Cheddar</a> and made him a minister.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Again, royal favour fostered jealousy among other courtiers and again Dunstan's enemies succeeded in their plots. The King was prepared to send Dunstan away.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> There were then at Cheddar certain envoys from the "Eastern Kingdom", which probably meant <a href="/wiki/East_Anglia" title="East Anglia">East Anglia</a>. Dunstan implored the envoys to take him with them when they returned to their homes. They agreed to do so, but it never happened. The story is recorded:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>... the king rode out to hunt the <a href="/wiki/Stag" class="mw-redirect" title="Stag">stag</a> in <a href="/wiki/Mendip_Hills" title="Mendip Hills">Mendip Forest</a>. He became separated from his attendants and followed a stag at great speed in the direction of the Cheddar cliffs. The stag rushed blindly over the precipice and was followed by the hounds. Eadmund endeavoured vainly to stop his horse; then, seeing death to be imminent, he remembered his harsh treatment of St Dunstan and promised to make amends if his life was spared. At that moment his horse was stopped on the very edge of the cliff. Giving thanks to God, he returned forthwith to his palace, called for St. Dunstan and bade him follow, then rode straight to Glastonbury. Entering the church, the king first knelt in prayer before the altar, then, taking St. Dunstan by the hand, he gave him the kiss of peace, led him to the <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a>'s throne and, seating him thereon, promised him all assistance in restoring Divine worship and regular observance.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8201;<cite><a href="#CITEREFToke1909">Toke 1909</a></cite></div></blockquote>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Abbot_of_Glastonbury">Abbot of Glastonbury</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Abbot of Glastonbury">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Dunstan, now Abbot of Glastonbury, went to work at once on the task of reform.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He had to re-create monastic life and to rebuild the abbey. He began by establishing <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> monasticism at Glastonbury.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The Rule of St. Benedict was the basis of his restoration according to the author of 'Edgar's Establishment of the Monasteries' (written in the 960s or 970s) and according to Dunstan's first biographer, who had been a member of the community at Glastonbury.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWhitelock1979920_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWhitelock1979920-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Their statements are also in accordance with the nature of his first measures as abbot, with the significance of his first buildings, and with the Benedictine leanings of his most prominent disciples.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Nevertheless, not all the members of Dunstan's community at Glastonbury were monks who followed the <a href="/wiki/Benedictine_Rule" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine Rule">Benedictine Rule</a>. In fact, Dunstan's first biographer, 'B.', was a cleric who eventually joined a community of <a href="/wiki/Canon_(priest)" class="mw-redirect" title="Canon (priest)">canons</a> at <a href="/wiki/Li%C3%A8ge" title="Liège">Liège</a> after leaving Glastonbury.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWinterbottomLapidge2011lxix_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWinterbottomLapidge2011lxix-13">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Glastonburyabbey.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Glastonburyabbey.jpg/220px-Glastonburyabbey.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="151" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Glastonburyabbey.jpg/330px-Glastonburyabbey.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Glastonburyabbey.jpg/440px-Glastonburyabbey.jpg 2x" data-file-width="581" data-file-height="399" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Glastonburyabbey.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Remains of the <a href="/wiki/Choir_(architecture)" title="Choir (architecture)">choir</a> of Glastonbury Abbey church</div></div></div>
<p>Dunstan's first care was to rebuild the Church of St. Peter, rebuild the cloister, and re-establish the monastic enclosure. The secular affairs of the house were committed to his brother, Wulfric, "so that neither himself nor any of the professed monks might break enclosure."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> A school for the local youth was founded and soon became the most famous of its time in England.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> A substantial extension of the irrigation system on the surrounding <a href="/wiki/Somerset_Levels" title="Somerset Levels">Somerset Levels</a> was also completed.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilliams197050_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilliams197050-14">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Within two years of Dunstan's appointment, in 946, King Edmund was assassinated. His successor was <a href="/wiki/Eadred_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Eadred of England">Eadred</a>. The policy of the new government was supported by the <a href="/wiki/Queen_mother" title="Queen mother">Queen mother</a>, <a href="/wiki/Eadgifu_of_Kent" title="Eadgifu of Kent">Eadgifu of Kent</a>, by the Archbishop of Canterbury, <a href="/wiki/Oda_the_Severe" class="mw-redirect" title="Oda the Severe">Oda</a>, and by the East Anglian nobles, at whose head was the powerful <a href="/wiki/Ealdorman" title="Ealdorman">ealdorman</a> <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelstan_Half-King" title="Æthelstan Half-King">Æthelstan the "Half-king"</a>. It was a policy of unification and conciliation with the Danish half of the kingdom.<sup id="cite_ref-co_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-co-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The goal was a firm establishment of royal authority. In ecclesiastical matters it favoured the spread of <a href="/wiki/Catholicism" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholicism">Catholic</a> observance, the rebuilding of churches, the moral reform of the clergy and laity, and the end of the <a href="/wiki/Norse_mythology" title="Norse mythology">religion of the Danes</a> in England.<sup id="cite_ref-cw_11-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cw-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
Against all these reforms were the nobles of Wessex, who included most of Dunstan's own relatives, and who had an interest in maintaining established customs.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> For nine years Dunstan's influence was dominant, during which time he twice refused the office of bishop (that of Winchester in 951 and <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Exeter" title="Bishop of Exeter">Crediton</a> in 953), affirming that he would not leave the king's side so long as the king lived and needed him.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Changes_in_fortune">Changes in fortune</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Changes in fortune">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:282px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Eadwig_-_MS_Royal_14_B_VI.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Eadwig_-_MS_Royal_14_B_VI.jpg/280px-Eadwig_-_MS_Royal_14_B_VI.jpg" decoding="async" width="280" height="257" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Eadwig_-_MS_Royal_14_B_VI.jpg/420px-Eadwig_-_MS_Royal_14_B_VI.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Eadwig_-_MS_Royal_14_B_VI.jpg/560px-Eadwig_-_MS_Royal_14_B_VI.jpg 2x" data-file-width="656" data-file-height="601" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Eadwig_-_MS_Royal_14_B_VI.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Eadwig" title="Eadwig">King Eadwig</a>'s reign was marred by conflicts with his family and with Dunstan.</div></div></div>
<p>In 955, Eadred died, and the situation was at once changed. <a href="/wiki/Eadwig" title="Eadwig">Eadwig</a>, the elder son of Edmund, who then came to the throne, was a headstrong youth wholly devoted to the reactionary nobles. According to one legend, the feud with Dunstan began on the day of Eadwig's coronation, when he failed to attend a meeting of nobles. When Dunstan eventually found the young monarch, he was cavorting with a noblewoman named Ælfgifu and her mother, and refused to return with the bishop. Infuriated by this, Dunstan dragged Eadwig back and forced him to renounce the girl as a "strumpet". Later realising that he had provoked the king, Dunstan fled to the apparent sanctuary of his cloister, but Eadwig, incited by Ælfgifu, whom he married, followed him and plundered the monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Although Dunstan managed to escape, he saw that his life was in danger. He fled England and crossed the channel to Flanders, where he found himself ignorant of the language and of the customs of the locals.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Counts_of_Flanders" class="mw-redirect" title="Counts of Flanders">count of Flanders</a>, <a href="/wiki/Arnulf_I,_Count_of_Flanders" title="Arnulf I, Count of Flanders">Arnulf I</a>, received him with honour and lodged him in the Abbey of <a href="/wiki/Blandijnberg" title="Blandijnberg">Mont Blandin</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Ghent" title="Ghent">Ghent</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> This was one of the centres of the Benedictine revival in that country, and Dunstan was able for the first time to observe the strict observance that had seen its rebirth at <a href="/wiki/Cluny_Abbey" title="Cluny Abbey">Cluny</a> at the beginning of the century. His exile was not of long duration. Before the end of 957, the <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercians</a> and <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbrians</a> revolted and drove out Eadwig, choosing his brother <a href="/wiki/Edgar_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Edgar of England">Edgar</a> as king of the country north of the Thames.<sup id="cite_ref-co_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-co-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The south remained faithful to Eadwig. At once Edgar's advisers recalled Dunstan.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Bishop_and_archbishop_.28957.E2.80.93978.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop_and_archbishop_(957–978)">Bishop and archbishop (957–978)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Bishop and archbishop (957–978)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop_of_Worcester_and_of_London">Bishop of Worcester and of London</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Bishop of Worcester and of London">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>On Dunstan's return, Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Oda_the_Severe" class="mw-redirect" title="Oda the Severe">Oda</a> consecrated him a bishop and, on the death of <a href="/wiki/Coenwald" class="mw-redirect" title="Coenwald">Coenwald</a> of <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Diocese_of_Worcester" title="Anglican Diocese of Worcester">Worcester</a> at the end of 957, Oda appointed Dunstan to the see.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFrydePowicke1961260_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrydePowicke1961260-15">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the following year the see of <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_London" title="Diocese of London">London</a> became vacant and was conferred on Dunstan, who held it simultaneously with Worcester.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFrydePowicke1961260_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrydePowicke1961260-15">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> In October 959, Eadwig died and his brother Edgar was readily accepted as ruler of Wessex. One of Eadwig's final acts had been to appoint a successor to Archbishop Oda, who died on 2 June 958. The chosen candidate was <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfsige" title="Ælfsige">Ælfsige</a> of Winchester, but he died of cold in the Alps as he journeyed to <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Rome</a> for the <a href="/wiki/Pallium" title="Pallium">pallium</a>. In his place Eadwig then nominated the Bishop of Wells, <a href="/wiki/Byrhthelm_(Bishop_of_Wells)" class="mw-redirect" title="Byrhthelm (Bishop of Wells)">Byrhthelm</a>. As soon as <a href="/wiki/Edgar_the_Peaceful" class="mw-redirect" title="Edgar the Peaceful">Edgar</a> became king, he reversed this second choice on the ground that Byrhthelm had not been able to govern even his first diocese properly.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The archbishopric was then conferred on Dunstan.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Archbishop_of_Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Archbishop of Canterbury">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:MS._Hatton_30_Expositio_Augustini_in_Apocalypsin_73v.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c9/MS._Hatton_30_Expositio_Augustini_in_Apocalypsin_73v.jpg/220px-MS._Hatton_30_Expositio_Augustini_in_Apocalypsin_73v.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="348" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c9/MS._Hatton_30_Expositio_Augustini_in_Apocalypsin_73v.jpg/330px-MS._Hatton_30_Expositio_Augustini_in_Apocalypsin_73v.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c9/MS._Hatton_30_Expositio_Augustini_in_Apocalypsin_73v.jpg/440px-MS._Hatton_30_Expositio_Augustini_in_Apocalypsin_73v.jpg 2x" data-file-width="456" data-file-height="722" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:MS._Hatton_30_Expositio_Augustini_in_Apocalypsin_73v.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Theological manuscript from Glastonbury Abbey (<a href="/wiki/Bodleian_Library" title="Bodleian Library">Bodleian Library</a>):<i>Abbot Dunstan ordered the writing of this book</i>.</div></div></div>
<p>Dunstan went to Rome in 960, and received the pallium from <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_XII" title="Pope John XII">Pope John XII</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> On his journey there, Dunstan's acts of charity were so lavish as to leave nothing for himself and his attendants. His steward complained, but Dunstan seems to have suggested that they trust in <a href="/wiki/Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Christ">Jesus Christ</a>.
</p><p>On his return from Rome, Dunstan at once regained his position as virtual prime minister of the kingdom. By his advice <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfstan_(bishop_of_London)" title="Ælfstan (bishop of London)">Ælfstan</a> was appointed to the Bishopric of London, and <a href="/wiki/Oswald_of_Worcester" title="Oswald of Worcester">Oswald</a> to that of Worcester. In 963, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester" title="Æthelwold of Winchester">Æthelwold</a>, the Abbot of <a href="/wiki/Abingdon_Abbey" title="Abingdon Abbey">Abingdon</a>, was appointed to the See of Winchester. With their aid and with the ready support of King Edgar, Dunstan pushed forward his reforms in the English Church.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The monks in his communities were taught to live in a spirit of self-sacrifice, and Dunstan actively enforced the law of celibacy whenever possible.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHollister196661_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollister196661-16">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> He forbade the practices of <a href="/wiki/Simony" title="Simony">simony</a> (selling ecclesiastical offices for money) and ended the custom of <a href="/wiki/Cleric" class="mw-redirect" title="Cleric">clerics</a> appointing relatives to offices under their jurisdiction. Monasteries were built, and in some of the great cathedrals, monks took the place of the secular canons; in the rest the canons were obliged to live according to rule. The parish priests were compelled to be qualified for their office; they were urged to teach parishioners not only the truths of the Christian faith, but also trades to improve their position.<sup id="cite_ref-cw_11-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cw-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The state saw reforms as well.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Good order was maintained throughout the realm and there was respect for the law. Trained bands policed the north, and a navy guarded the shores from Viking raids. There was a level of peace in the kingdom unknown in living memory.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 973, Dunstan's statesmanship reached its zenith when he officiated at the coronation of King Edgar. Edgar was crowned at Bath in an imperial ceremony planned not as the initiation, but as the culmination of his reign (a move that must have taken a great deal of preliminary diplomacy).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChurchill1966134_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChurchill1966134-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> This service, devised by Dunstan himself and celebrated with a poem in the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> forms the basis of the present-day <a href="/wiki/Coronation_of_the_British_monarch" title="Coronation of the British monarch">British coronation ceremony</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChurchill1966134_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChurchill1966134-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> There was a second symbolic coronation held later. This was an important step, as other kings of Britain came and gave their allegiance to Edgar at <a href="/wiki/Chester" title="Chester">Chester</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESchama201165_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESchama201165-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Six kings in Britain, including the kings of Scotland and of <a href="/wiki/Strathclyde" title="Strathclyde">Strathclyde</a>, pledged their faith that they would be the king's liege-men on sea and land.
</p><p>Edgar ruled as a strong and popular king for 16 years. In 975 he was succeeded by his eldest son <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Martyr" title="Edward the Martyr">Edward "the Martyr"</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> His accession was disputed by his stepmother, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfthryth,_Queen_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Ælfthryth, Queen of England">Ælfthryth</a>, who wished her own son <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_the_Unready" title="Æthelred the Unready">Æthelred</a> to reign. Through the influence of Dunstan, Edward was chosen and crowned at Winchester.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Edgar's death had encouraged the reactionary nobles, and at once there was a determined attack upon the monks, the protagonists of reform. Throughout Mercia they were persecuted and deprived of their possessions. Their cause, however, was supported by <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelstan_Half-King#Family" title="Æthelstan Half-King">Æthelwine</a>, the ealdorman of East Anglia, and the realm was in serious danger of civil war. Three meetings of the <a href="/wiki/Witenagemot" class="mw-redirect" title="Witenagemot">Witan</a> were held to settle these disputes, at <a href="/wiki/Oxfordshire" title="Oxfordshire">Kyrtlington</a>, at <a href="/wiki/Calne" title="Calne">Calne</a>, and at <a href="/wiki/Amesbury" title="Amesbury">Amesbury</a>. At the second of them the floor of the hall where the Witan was sitting gave way, and all except Dunstan, who clung to a beam, fell into the room below; several men were killed.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="Final_years_.28978.E2.80.9388.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Final_years_(978–88)">Final years (978–88)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Final years (978–88)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In March 978, King Edward was assassinated at <a href="/wiki/Corfe_Castle" title="Corfe Castle">Corfe Castle</a>, possibly at the instigation of his stepmother, and Æthelred the Unready became king. His coronation on <a href="/wiki/Low_Sunday" class="mw-redirect" title="Low Sunday">Low Sunday</a> 31 March 978, was the last state event in which Dunstan took part.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/wiki/William_of_Malmesbury" title="William of Malmesbury">William of Malmsesbury</a>, writing over a century later, when the young king took the usual oath to govern well, Dunstan addressed him in solemn warning. He criticised the violent act whereby he became king and prophesied the misfortunes that were shortly to fall on the kingdom,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChurchill1966139_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChurchill1966139-19">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> but Dunstan's influence at court was ended.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514_10-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone1997514-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Dunstan retired to Canterbury, to teach at the <a href="/wiki/Cathedral_school" title="Cathedral school">cathedral school</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Only three more public acts are known. In 980, Dunstan joined <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfhere,_Ealdorman_of_Mercia" title="Ælfhere, Ealdorman of Mercia">Ælfhere of Mercia</a> in the solemn <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">translation of the relics</a> of King Edward, soon to be regarded as a saint, from their grave at <a href="/wiki/Wareham,_Dorset" title="Wareham, Dorset">Wareham</a> to a shrine at <a href="/wiki/Shaftesbury_Abbey" title="Shaftesbury Abbey">Shaftesbury Abbey</a>. In 984, in obedience to a vision of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Andrew" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Andrew">St Andrew</a>, he persuaded King Æthelred to appoint <a href="/wiki/Alphege" class="mw-redirect" title="Alphege">Ælfheah</a> as Bishop of Winchester in succession to Æthelwold. In 986, Dunstan induced the king, by a donation of 100 pounds of silver, to stop his persecution of the See of <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Rochester" title="Diocese of Rochester">Rochester</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Dunstan's retirement at Canterbury consisted of long hours, both day and night, spent in private prayer, as well as his regular attendance at Mass and the <a href="/wiki/Canonical_hours" title="Canonical hours">daily office</a>. He visited the shrines of <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury" title="Augustine of Canterbury">St Augustine</a> and <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelberht_of_Kent" title="Æthelberht of Kent">St Æthelberht</a>, and there are reports of a vision of angels who sang to him heavenly canticles.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He worked to improve the spiritual and temporal well-being of his people, to build and restore churches, to establish schools, to judge suits, to defend widows and orphans, to promote peace, and to enforce respect for purity.<sup id="cite_ref-co_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-co-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> He practised his crafts, made bells and organs and corrected the books in the cathedral library. He encouraged and protected European scholars who came to England, and was active as a teacher of boys in the cathedral school. On the vigil of <a href="/wiki/Ascension_of_Jesus_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascension of Jesus Christ">Ascension</a> Day 988, it is recorded that a vision of angels warned he would die in three days.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> On the feast day itself, Dunstan said Mass and preached three times to the people: at the <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)#Liturgy_of_the_Word" title="Mass (liturgy)">Gospel</a>, at the <a href="/wiki/Benediction#Christianity" title="Benediction">benediction</a>, and after the <a href="/wiki/Agnus_Dei_(liturgy)" class="mw-redirect" title="Agnus Dei (liturgy)">Agnus Dei</a>. In this last address, he announced his impending death and wished his congregation well.<sup id="cite_ref-msc_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-msc-20">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> That afternoon he chose the spot for his tomb, then went to his bed. His strength failed rapidly, and on Saturday morning, 19 May, he caused the clergy to assemble. Mass was celebrated in his presence, then he received <a href="/wiki/Extreme_Unction" class="mw-redirect" title="Extreme Unction">Extreme Unction</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Viaticum" title="Viaticum">Viaticum</a>, and died. Dunstan's final words are reported to have been, "He hath made a remembrance of his wonderful works, being a merciful and gracious Lord: He hath given food to them that fear Him."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The English people accepted him as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> shortly thereafter. He was formally <a href="/wiki/Canonised" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonised">canonised</a> in 1029. That year at the Synod of Winchester, St Dunstan's feast was ordered to be kept solemnly throughout England.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Until <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Becket" title="Thomas Becket">Thomas Becket</a>'s fame overshadowed Dunstan's, he was the favourite saint of the English people. Dunstan had been buried in his <a href="/wiki/Canterbury_Cathedral#Later_Saxon_and_Viking" title="Canterbury Cathedral">cathedral</a>. In 1880 his relics were translated to a tomb on the south side of the high altar, when that building was restored after being partially destroyed by a fire in 1174.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The monks of Glastonbury used to claim that during the sack of Canterbury by the <a href="/wiki/Viking" class="mw-redirect" title="Viking">Danes</a> in 1012, Dunstan's body had been carried for safety to their abbey. This story was disproved by Archbishop <a href="/wiki/William_Warham" title="William Warham">William Warham</a>, who opened the tomb at Canterbury in 1508. They found Dunstan's relics still to be there. Within a century, however, his shrine was destroyed during the <a href="/wiki/English_Reformation" title="English Reformation">English Reformation</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEToke1909_6-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEToke1909-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage_and_feast_day">Patronage and feast day</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Patronage and feast day">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Dunstan became <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of English <a href="/wiki/Goldsmith" title="Goldsmith">goldsmiths</a> and silversmiths because he worked as a silversmith making church plate. The <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a> mark his <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a> on 19 May.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998_4-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunsonBunsonBunson1998-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Dunstan is also honoured in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">Church of England</a> and in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church</a> on 19 May.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="In_literature_and_folklore">In literature and folklore</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: In literature and folklore">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><a href="/wiki/English_literature" title="English literature">English literature</a> contains many references to him: for example, in <i><a href="/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol" title="A Christmas Carol">A Christmas Carol</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/Charles_Dickens" title="Charles Dickens">Charles Dickens</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup> and in this folk rhyme: 
</p>
<div class="poem">
<p>St Dunstan, as the story goes,<br />
Once pull'd the devil by the nose<br />
With red-hot tongs, which made him roar,<br />
That he was heard three miles or more.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHone1825670_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHone1825670-26">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
</div>
<p>This <a href="/wiki/Folklore" title="Folklore">folk</a> story is already shown in an <a href="/wiki/Initial" title="Initial">initial</a> in the <i>Life of Dunstan</i> in the Canterbury <i>Passionale</i>, from the second quarter of the 12th century (<a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>, Harley MS 315, f. 15v.).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDunning2016_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunning2016-27">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:262px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Dunstan_and_the_Devil_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_13978.png" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Dunstan_and_the_Devil_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_13978.png/260px-Dunstan_and_the_Devil_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_13978.png" decoding="async" width="260" height="294" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Dunstan_and_the_Devil_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_13978.png/390px-Dunstan_and_the_Devil_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_13978.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Dunstan_and_the_Devil_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_13978.png/520px-Dunstan_and_the_Devil_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_13978.png 2x" data-file-width="700" data-file-height="791" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Dunstan_and_the_Devil_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_13978.png" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Dunstan shoeing the Devil's hoof, as illustrated by <a href="/wiki/George_Cruikshank" title="George Cruikshank">George Cruikshank</a></div></div></div>
<p>Daniel Anlezark has tentatively suggested that Dunstan may be the medieval author of the poem <i><a href="/wiki/Solomon_and_Saturn" title="Solomon and Saturn">Solomon and Saturn</a></i>, citing the style, word choice, and <a href="/wiki/Hiberno-Latin" title="Hiberno-Latin">Hiberno-Latin</a> used in the texts.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAnlezark2009_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnlezark2009-28">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> However, Clive Tolley examines this claim from a linguistic point-of-view and disagrees with Anlezark's claim.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETolley2010166–168_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETolley2010166–168-29">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Another story relates how Dunstan nailed a <a href="/wiki/Horseshoe" title="Horseshoe">horseshoe</a> to the Devil's foot when he was asked to re-shoe the Devil's cloven hoof. This caused the Devil great pain, and Dunstan only agreed to remove the shoe and release the Devil after he promised never to enter a place where a horseshoe is over the door. This is claimed as the origin of the lucky horseshoe.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELawrence189991_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELawrence189991-30">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A further legend relating to Dunstan and the Devil seeks to explain the phenomena of Franklin nights, late frosts which occur around his Feast Day. The story goes that Dunstan was a great <a href="/wiki/Brewing" title="Brewing">brewer</a> and negotiated an agreement whereby the Devil could blast the blossom of local apple trees with frost, damaging the <a href="/wiki/Cider_apple" title="Cider apple">cider</a> crop so that the saint's own beer would sell more readily.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="An_East_London_saint">An East London saint</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dunstan&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: An East London saint">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>As Bishop of London, Dunstan was also Lord of the <a href="/wiki/Stepney#Manor_and_Ancient_Parish" title="Stepney">Manor of Stepney</a>, and may, like subsequent bishops, have lived there. Dunstan is recorded as having founded (or rebuilt<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>) <a href="/wiki/St_Dunstan%27s,_Stepney" title="St Dunstan&#39;s, Stepney">Stepney's church</a>, in 952 AD. This church was dedicated to All Saints, but was rededicated to Dunstan after his <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonisation</a> in 1029, making Dunstan the Patron Saint of Stepney.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
