<p><b>Richard Langhorne</b> (c. 1624 – 14 July 1679) was an English <a href="/wiki/Barrister" title="Barrister">barrister</a> and Catholic <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyr" title="Christian martyr">martyr</a>, who was executed on a false charge of treason as part of the fabricated <a href="/wiki/Popish_Plot" title="Popish Plot">Popish Plot</a>. He fell under suspicion because he was a Roman Catholic and because he had acted as legal adviser to the <a href="/wiki/Jesuits" title="Jesuits">Jesuits</a> at a time of acute anti-Catholic hysteria.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background_and_early_life">Background and early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Richard_Langhorne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Background and early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was the third son of William Langhorne, a <a href="/wiki/Barrister" title="Barrister">barrister</a>, and his wife Lettice Needham, of <a href="/wiki/Little_Wymondley" title="Little Wymondley">Little Wymondley</a> in <a href="/wiki/Hertfordshire" title="Hertfordshire">Hertfordshire</a>. He was admitted to the <a href="/wiki/Inner_Temple" title="Inner Temple">Inner Temple</a> in May 1647 and called to the bar in November 1654. He was a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a>, and provided legal and financial advice for the <a href="/wiki/Jesuits" title="Jesuits">Jesuits</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNB_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> During the wave of anti-Catholic hysteria which followed the <a href="/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London" title="Great Fire of London">Great Fire of London</a> of 1666, he was briefly arrested but quickly released.
</p><p>His wife, Dorothy Legatt, was a Protestant from <a href="/wiki/Havering" class="mw-redirect" title="Havering">Havering</a> in <a href="/wiki/Essex" title="Essex">Essex</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-CathEncy_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CathEncy-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> His sons Charles and Francis were both priests. When, in October 1677, <a href="/wiki/Titus_Oates" title="Titus Oates">Titus Oates</a> was expelled from the <a href="/wiki/College_of_St._Omer" class="mw-redirect" title="College of St. Omer">English College at St Omer</a> "for serious moral lapses", Charles Langhorne entrusted Oates with a letter to his father. Oates returned to St Omer with a letter from Richard thanking the Jesuits for all they had done for his sons.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNB_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Arrest">Arrest</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Richard_Langhorne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Arrest">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>When Oates and <a href="/wiki/Israel_Tonge" title="Israel Tonge">Israel Tonge</a> unleashed their entirely fictitious Popish Plot, a non-existent Catholic conspiracy to kill King <a href="/wiki/Charles_II_of_England" title="Charles II of England">Charles II</a>, in September 1678, three Jesuits and a <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> were arrested. Following a detailed search of their papers (which failed to uncover any evidence of treason), Langhorne's role as legal adviser to the Jesuits was discovered almost at once: he was arrested a week after the four priests, although there was no evidence in the priests' papers that he had committed any crime. He was imprisoned at <a href="/wiki/Newgate" title="Newgate">Newgate</a> and charged with <a href="/wiki/Treason" title="Treason">treason</a>. Oates claimed, and was corroborated by the notorious <a href="/wiki/Informer" class="mw-redirect" title="Informer">informer</a> and confidence trickster <a href="/wiki/William_Bedloe" title="William Bedloe">William Bedloe</a>, that Langhorne's earlier correspondence dealt with the conspiracy to kill the King.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNB_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Trial_and_execution">Trial and execution</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Richard_Langhorne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Trial and execution">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was tried on 14 June 1679.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was forced to defend himself, as a person charged with treason had no right then to <a href="/wiki/Defence_counsel" class="mw-redirect" title="Defence counsel">defence counsel</a> (this rule was not changed until the passage of the <a href="/wiki/Treason_Act_1695" title="Treason Act 1695">Treason Act 1695</a>). His main defence consisted of an attack on the character of the Crown's principal witnesses, Oates and Bedloe, but since the judges were well aware of the deplorable past lives of both men, this seems to have made little impression.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He also called a number of students from St Omer to prove that Oates had been at the college on the crucial dates when he claimed to be in London, but the public mood was so hostile to Catholics that the witnesses were barely able to make themselves heard over the roar of the crowd, and some of them were assaulted as they left the Court.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Ironically, some of the same witnesses appeared for the prosecution at Oates' own trial for <a href="/wiki/Perjury" title="Perjury">perjury</a> in 1685, where the crowd treated them courteously, and the jury was told to weigh their evidence with the greatest seriousness.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/William_Scroggs" title="William Scroggs">William Scroggs</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Lord_Chief_Justice" class="mw-redirect" title="Lord Chief Justice">Lord Chief Justice</a>, although violently prejudiced against <a href="/wiki/Catholic_priests" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic priests">Catholic priests</a>, was relatively tolerant of Catholic laymen; his summing up was reasonably fair by the standards of the time, and he did warn the jury that on no account should an innocent man's life be taken away.<sup id="cite_ref-Kenyon_p.188_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kenyon_p.188-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Nonetheles, Langhorne was found guilty of <a href="/wiki/High_Treason" class="mw-redirect" title="High Treason">High Treason</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Kenyon_p.188_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kenyon_p.188-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As the result of a petition by his wife, a ‘true Protestant’, he received a month's reprieve to tidy the affairs of his clients. <a href="/wiki/John_Philipps_Kenyon" title="John Philipps Kenyon">Kenyon</a> suggests that the Crown was still hoping that he would confess, and it seems that he was offered a <a href="/wiki/Royal_pardon" class="mw-redirect" title="Royal pardon">royal pardon</a> if he did so. Langhorne was prepared, presumably with the consent of the Jesuit fathers, to give the Crown a list of all the Jesuit properties in England, (which turned out to be much less extensive than the Crown, misled by Oates's wild exaggeration of the Jesuits' wealth, had expected) but he steadily maintained his innocence.<sup id="cite_ref-Kenyon_p.191_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kenyon_p.191-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He also wrote a lengthy religious meditation in verse, which was later published.<sup id="cite_ref-Kenyon_p.191_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kenyon_p.191-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He was executed at <a href="/wiki/Tyburn" title="Tyburn">Tyburn</a>, <a href="/wiki/London" title="London">London</a>, on 14 July 1679.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNB_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Public opinion was slowly turning against the Plot, and Langhorne's courageous death made a favourable impression on the watching crowd.<sup id="cite_ref-Kenyon_p.191_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kenyon_p.191-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Richard_Langhorne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On 15 December 1929, he was beatified by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XI" title="Pope Pius XI">Pope Pius XI</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODNB_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> His feast day is 14 July, the day of his death.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There is a stained glass window of Langhorne in Our Lady of Lourdes in Harpenden, Hertfordshire.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
