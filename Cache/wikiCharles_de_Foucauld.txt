<p><b>Charles Eugène de Foucauld de Pontbriand</b>, Viscount of Foucauld (15 September 1858 – 1 December 1916) was a <a href="/wiki/French_people" title="French people">French</a> soldier, explorer, <a href="/wiki/Geographer" title="Geographer">geographer</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ethnographer" class="mw-redirect" title="Ethnographer">ethnographer</a>, <a href="/wiki/Priesthood_in_the_Catholic_Church" title="Priesthood in the Catholic Church">Catholic priest</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a> who lived among the <a href="/wiki/Tuareg_people" title="Tuareg people">Tuareg people</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Sahara" title="Sahara">Sahara</a> in Algeria. He was assassinated in 1916. His inspiration and writings led to the founding of the <a href="/wiki/Little_Brothers_of_Jesus" title="Little Brothers of Jesus">Little Brothers of Jesus</a> among other <a href="/wiki/Religious_congregation" title="Religious congregation">religious congregations</a>. He was <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Francis" title="Pope Francis">Pope Francis</a> in 2022.
</p><p>Orphaned at the age of six, de Foucauld was brought up by his maternal grandfather, Colonel Beaudet de Morlet. He joined the <a href="/wiki/Saint-Cyr_Military_Academy" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint-Cyr Military Academy">Saint-Cyr Military Academy</a>. Upon leaving the academy he opted to join the cavalry. He thus went to the <a href="/wiki/%C3%89cole_de_cavalerie,_Saumur" title="École de cavalerie, Saumur">Saumur Cavalry School</a>, where he was known for his childish sense of humour, whilst living a life of debauchery enabled by an inheritance he received after his grandfather's death. He was assigned to the 4th <a href="/wiki/Chasseurs_d%27Afrique" title="Chasseurs d&#39;Afrique">Chasseurs d'Afrique</a> Regiment. At the age of twenty-three, he decided to resign in order to explore <a href="/wiki/Morocco" title="Morocco">Morocco</a> by impersonating a <a href="/wiki/Jew" class="mw-redirect" title="Jew">Jew</a>. The quality of his works earned him a gold medal from the <a href="/wiki/Soci%C3%A9t%C3%A9_de_G%C3%A9ographie" title="Société de Géographie">Société de Géographie</a>, as well as fame following publication of his book <i>Reconnaissance au Maroc</i> (1888).
</p><p>Once back in France, he rekindled his <a href="/wiki/Catholic_faith" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic faith">Catholic faith</a> and joined the <a href="/wiki/Trappist_order" class="mw-redirect" title="Trappist order">Trappist order</a> on 16 January 1890. Still with the Trappists, he then went to <a href="/wiki/Syria" title="Syria">Syria</a>. His quest of an even more radical ideal of poverty, <a href="/wiki/Altruism" title="Altruism">altruism</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Penitence" class="mw-redirect" title="Penitence">penitence</a>, led him to leave the Trappists in order to become a hermit in 1897. He was then living in <a href="/wiki/Palestine_(region)" title="Palestine (region)">Palestine</a> as a porter at convents of the <a href="/wiki/Poor_Clares" title="Poor Clares">Poor Clares</a> in <a href="/wiki/Nazareth" title="Nazareth">Nazareth</a> and in <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>, writing his meditations that became the cornerstone of his spirituality.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Ordained" class="mw-redirect" title="Ordained">Ordained</a> in <a href="/wiki/Viviers,_Ard%C3%A8che" title="Viviers, Ardèche">Viviers</a> in 1901,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> he decided to settle in the Algerian Sahara at <a href="/wiki/B%C3%A9ni_Abb%C3%A8s" title="Béni Abbès">Béni Abbès</a>. His ambition was to form a new congregation, but nobody joined him. Taking the <a href="/wiki/Religious_name" title="Religious name">religious name</a> "Brother Charles of Jesus", he lived with the <a href="/wiki/Berbers" title="Berbers">Berbers</a>, adopting a new apostolic approach, preaching not through sermons, but through his example. In order to become more familiar with the <a href="/wiki/Tuareg_people" title="Tuareg people">Tuareg</a>, he studied their culture for over twelve years, using a pseudonym to publish the first <a href="/wiki/Tuareg_languages" title="Tuareg languages">Tuareg</a>-French dictionary. He collected hundreds of Tuareg poems (paying a few sous to anyone who would bring poems to his hermitage) which he translated into French. He censored nothing in the poems, and never changed anything that might not conform to Catholic morality. De Foucauld's works are a reference point for the understanding of Tuareg culture.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>On 1 December 1916, de Foucauld was assassinated at his hermitage. He was quickly considered to be a martyr of faith <sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and was the object of veneration following the success of the biography written by <a href="/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Bazin" title="René Bazin">René Bazin</a> (1923). New religious congregations, spiritual families, and a renewal of eremitic life are inspired by Charles de Foucauld's life and writings.
</p><p>His beatification process started in 1927 eleven years after his death. He was declared <a href="/wiki/Venerable" class="mw-redirect" title="Venerable">Venerable</a> on 24 April 2001 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a>, then <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">Blessed</a> on 13 November 2005 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a>. On 27 May 2020, the Vatican announced that a miracle had been attributed to de Foucauld's intercession.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> In his 2020 encyclical <i><a href="/wiki/Fratelli_tutti" title="Fratelli tutti">Fratelli tutti</a></i>, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Francis" title="Pope Francis">Pope Francis</a> wrote that "Blessed Charles directed his ideal of total surrender to God towards an identification with the poor, abandoned in the depths of the African desert. ... he expressed his desire to feel himself a brother to every human being, and asked a friend to 'pray to God that I truly be the brother of all'. He wanted to be, in the end, 'the universal brother'. Yet only by identifying with the least did he come at last to be the brother of all."<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> De Foucauld was <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> as a saint by Pope Francis on 15 May 2022 in Rome.
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Childhood">Childhood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_de_Foucauld&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Childhood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>De Foucauld's family was originally from the <a href="/wiki/P%C3%A9rigord" title="Périgord">Périgord</a> region of France and part of the old <a href="/wiki/French_nobility" title="French nobility">French nobility</a>; their motto being <i>Jamais arrière</i> ("Never behind").<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Several of his ancestors took part in the <a href="/wiki/Crusades" title="Crusades">crusades</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> a source of prestige within the French nobility. His great-great-uncle, Armand de Foucauld de Pontbriand, a vicar general and first cousin of the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop" title="Archbishop">archbishop</a> of <a href="/wiki/Arles" title="Arles">Arles</a>, Monseigneur <a href="/wiki/Jean_Marie_du_Lau" title="Jean Marie du Lau">Jean Marie du Lau d'Allemans</a>, as well as the archbishop himself, were victims of the <a href="/wiki/September_massacres" class="mw-redirect" title="September massacres">September massacres</a> that took place during the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> His mother, Élisabeth de Morlet, was from the <a href="/wiki/Lorraine" title="Lorraine">Lorraine</a> aristocracy<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> whilst his grandfather had made a fortune during the revolution as a republican.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Élisabeth de Morlet married the viscount Édouard de Foucauld de Pontbriand, a forest inspector, in 1855.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> On 17 July 1857, their first child Charles was born, and died one month later.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Their second son, whom they named Charles Eugène, was born in <a href="/wiki/Strasbourg" title="Strasbourg">Strasbourg</a> on 15 September 1858<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> in the family house on <a href="/wiki/Place_Broglie" title="Place Broglie">Place Broglie</a> at what was previously mayor Dietrich's mansion, where <a href="/wiki/La_Marseillaise" title="La Marseillaise">La Marseillaise</a> was sung for the first time, in 1792.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A few months after his birth, his father was transferred to <a href="/wiki/Wissembourg" title="Wissembourg">Wissembourg</a>. In 1861, Charles was three and a half years old when his sister, Marie-Inès-Rodolphine, was born.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> His profoundly religious mother educated him in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_faith" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic faith">Catholic faith</a>, steeped in acts of devotion and piety.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> She died following miscarriage<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> on 13 March 1864, followed by her husband who suffered from <a href="/wiki/Neurasthenia" title="Neurasthenia">neurasthenia</a>, on 9 August.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The now orphaned Charles (age 6) and his sister Marie (age 3) were put in the care of their paternal grandmother, <a href="/wiki/Viscountess" class="mw-redirect" title="Viscountess">Viscountess</a> Clothilde de Foucauld, who died of a heart attack shortly afterwards.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:1_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The children were then taken in by their maternal grandparents, Colonel Beaudet de Morlet and his wife, who lived in Strasbourg. De Morlet, an alumnus of the <a href="/wiki/%C3%89cole_Polytechnique" class="mw-redirect" title="École Polytechnique">École Polytechnique</a> and engineering officer, provided his grandchildren with an affectionate upbringing. Charles wrote of him: "My grandfather whose beautiful intelligence I admired, whose infinite tenderness surrounded my childhood and youth with an atmosphere of love, the warmth of which I still feel emotionally."<sup id="cite_ref-:1_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Charles pursued his studies at the Saint-Arbogast episcopal school, and went to Strasbourg high school in 1868.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> At the time an introvert and short-tempered,<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> he was often ill and pursued his education thanks to private tuition.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He spent the summer of 1868 with his aunt, <a href="/wiki/Portrait_of_Madame_Moitessier" title="Portrait of Madame Moitessier">Inès Moitessier</a>, who felt responsible for her nephew. Her daughter, Marie Moitessier (later Marie de Bondy), eight years older than Charles, became fast friends with him.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> She was a fervent churchgoer who was very close to Charles, sometimes acting as a maternal figure for him.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1870 the de Morlet family fled the <a href="/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War" title="Franco-Prussian War">Franco-Prussian War</a> and found refuge in <a href="/wiki/Bern" title="Bern">Bern</a>. Following the French defeat, the family moved to <a href="/wiki/Nancy,_France" title="Nancy, France">Nancy</a> in October 1871.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Charles had four years of secular highschool left.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Jules_Duvaux" title="Jules Duvaux">Jules Duvaux</a> was a teacher of his,<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and he bonded with fellow student Gabriel Tourdes.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Both students had a passion for classical literature,<sup id="cite_ref-:1_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> and Gabriel remained, according to Charles, one of the "two incomparable friends" of his life.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_9-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> His education in a secular school developed nurtured patriotic sentiment, alongside a mistrust for the <a href="/wiki/German_Empire" title="German Empire">German Empire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> His <a href="/wiki/First_Communion" title="First Communion">First Communion</a> took place on 28 April 1872, and his confirmation at the hands of Monseigneur <a href="/wiki/Joseph-Alfred_Foulon" title="Joseph-Alfred Foulon">Joseph-Alfred Foulon</a> in <a href="/wiki/Nancy,_France" title="Nancy, France">Nancy</a> followed shortly thereafter.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_9-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In October 1873, when he was 15, whilst in a <a href="/wiki/Rhetoric" title="Rhetoric">Rhetoric</a> class, he began to distance himself from the <a href="/wiki/Faith_in_Christianity" title="Faith in Christianity">faith</a> before becoming agnostic.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> He later affirmed, "The philosophers are all in discord. I spent twelve years not denying and believing nothing, despairing of the truth, not even believing in God. No proof to me seemed evident."<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> This loss of the faith was accompanied by uneasiness; Charles found himself to be "all selfishness, all impiousness, all evil desire, I was as though distraught".<i><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:1_9-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></i>
</p><p>On 11 April 1874, his cousin Marie married Olivier de Bondy.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> A few months later, on 12 August 1874, Charles obtained his <a href="/wiki/Baccalaur%C3%A9at" title="Baccalauréat">baccalauréat</a> with the distinction "mention bien" (equivalent to <a href="/wiki/Magna_cum_laude" class="mw-redirect" title="Magna cum laude">magna cum laude</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="A_dissipated_youth">A dissipated youth</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_de_Foucauld&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: A dissipated youth">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Charles was sent to the Sainte-Geneviève school (now located in <a href="/wiki/Versailles,_Yvelines" title="Versailles, Yvelines">Versailles</a>), run by the <a href="/wiki/Jesuites" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuites">Jesuits</a>, at that time located in the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Quarter,_Paris" title="Latin Quarter, Paris">Latin Quarter</a> of Paris, in order to prepare the admission test for the <a href="/wiki/Saint-Cyr_Military_Academy" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint-Cyr Military Academy">Saint-Cyr Military Academy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Charles was opposed to the strictness of the boarding school and decided to abandon all religious practice. He obtained his second baccalauréat in August 1875.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> He led a dissipated lifestyle at that point in time and was expelled from the school for being "lazy and undisciplined"<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> in March 1876.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He then returned to <a href="/wiki/Nancy,_France" title="Nancy, France">Nancy</a>, where he studied tutoring whilst secretly perusing light readings.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> During his readings with Gabriel Tourdes, he wanted to "completely enjoy that which is pleasant to the mind and body".<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> This reading introduced the two students to the works of <a href="/wiki/Aristotle" title="Aristotle">Aristotle</a>, <a href="/wiki/Voltaire" title="Voltaire">Voltaire</a>, <a href="/wiki/Erasmus" title="Erasmus">Erasmus</a>, <a href="/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Rabelais" title="François Rabelais">Rabelais</a> and <a href="/wiki/Laurence_Sterne" title="Laurence Sterne">Laurence Sterne</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_9-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In June 1876, he applied for entrance to the <a href="/wiki/Saint-Cyr_Military_Academy" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint-Cyr Military Academy">Saint-Cyr Military Academy</a>, and was accepted eighty-second out of four hundred and twelve.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_7-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> He was one of the youngest in his class.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_10-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> His record at Saint-Cyr was a mixed one and he graduated 333rd out of a class of 386.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The death of Foucauld's grandfather and the receipt of a substantial inheritance, was followed by his entry into the French cavalry school at <a href="/wiki/Saumur" title="Saumur">Saumur</a>. Continuing to lead an extravagant life style, Foucauld was posted to the 4th Regiment of <a href="/wiki/Chasseurs_d%27Afrique" title="Chasseurs d&#39;Afrique">Chasseurs d'Afrique</a> in Algeria. Bored with garrison service he travelled in Morocco (1883–84), the Sahara (1885), and Palestine (1888–89). While reverting to being a wealthy young socialite when in Paris, Foucauld became an increasingly serious student of the geography and culture of Algeria and Morocco. In 1885 the <i>Societe de Geographie de Paris</i> awarded him its gold medal in recognition of his exploration and research.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Religious_life">Religious life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_de_Foucauld&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Religious life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>On 14 January 1890, de Foucauld entered the <a href="/wiki/Trappists" title="Trappists">Trappist</a> monastery of <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Notre-Dame_des_Neiges" title="Abbey of Notre-Dame des Neiges">Notre-Dame des Neiges</a>, where he received, as a <a href="/wiki/Novice" title="Novice">novice</a>, the religious name <i>Marie-Albéric</i> on the feast of this saint 26 January. According to a plea which he sent to the abbot prior to his entrance in Notre-Dame des Neiges, after some months of <a href="/wiki/Novitiate" title="Novitiate">novitiate</a> Br. Marie-Albéric was sent to the abbey of La Trappe at <a href="/wiki/Akbez" title="Akbez">Akbès</a> on the Syrian-Turkish border.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> But despite the strict life of the Trappists according to their <a href="/wiki/Vow_of_poverty" class="mw-redirect" title="Vow of poverty">vow of poverty</a>, de Foucauld considered the life of the residents in the surrounding villages to be more miserable. In 1897, after seven years, he therefore left the order began to lead a life of prayer near a convent of <a href="/wiki/Poor_Clares" title="Poor Clares">Poor Clares</a> in <a href="/wiki/Nazareth" title="Nazareth">Nazareth</a> where he worked as a porter and servant.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> After some time, it was suggested to him that he be <a href="/wiki/Ordained" class="mw-redirect" title="Ordained">ordained</a>, so he returned to Akbès for some time in order to prepare for the ordination to the priesthood. On 9 June 1901, at the age of 43, he received the ordination in <a href="/wiki/Viviers,_Ard%C3%A8che" title="Viviers, Ardèche">Viviers</a>, France.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (December 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>After that, he went to the <a href="/wiki/Sahara" title="Sahara">Sahara</a> in <a href="/wiki/French_Algeria" title="French Algeria">French Algeria</a> and continued to live an <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">heremitical</a> lifestyle. At that time he adopted the religious name <i>Charles of Jesus</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> He first settled in <a href="/wiki/B%C3%A9ni_Abb%C3%A8s" title="Béni Abbès">Béni Abbès</a>, near the Moroccan border, building a small <a href="/wiki/Hermitage_(religious_retreat)" title="Hermitage (religious retreat)">hermitage</a> for "adoration and hospitality", which he soon referred to as the "fraternity" and both himself and the future members as <a href="/wiki/Little_Brothers_of_Jesus" title="Little Brothers of Jesus">"little brothers" of Jesus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>De Foucauld moved to be with the <a href="/wiki/Tuareg_people" title="Tuareg people">Tuareg people</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Tamanghasset" class="mw-redirect" title="Tamanghasset">Tamanghasset</a> in southern Algeria. This region is the central part of the Sahara with the <a href="/wiki/Hoggar_Mountains" title="Hoggar Mountains">Ahaggar Mountains</a> (the Hoggar) immediately to the west. Foucauld used the highest point in the region, the <a href="/wiki/Assekrem" title="Assekrem">Assekrem</a>, as a place of retreat. Living close to the Tuareg and sharing their life and hardships, he made a ten-year study of their language and cultural traditions. He learned the <a href="/wiki/Tuareg_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Tuareg language">Tuareg language</a> and worked on a dictionary and grammar. His dictionary manuscript was published posthumously in four volumes and has become known among <a href="/wiki/Berber_languages" title="Berber languages">Berberologists</a> for its rich and apt descriptions.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (December 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_de_Foucauld&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Grave_of_Charles_de_Foucauld.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Grave_of_Charles_de_Foucauld.jpg/220px-Grave_of_Charles_de_Foucauld.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Grave_of_Charles_de_Foucauld.jpg/330px-Grave_of_Charles_de_Foucauld.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Grave_of_Charles_de_Foucauld.jpg/440px-Grave_of_Charles_de_Foucauld.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1280" data-file-height="960" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Grave_of_Charles_de_Foucauld.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Tomb of Charles de Foucauld in <a href="/wiki/El_M%C3%A9nia" class="mw-redirect" title="El Ménia">El Ménia</a>, Algeria</div></div></div>
<p>On 1 December 1916, de Foucauld was dragged from his hermitage by a group of tribal raiders led by El Madani ag Soba, who was connected with the <a href="/wiki/Senussi" class="mw-redirect" title="Senussi">Senussi</a> Bedouin. They intended to kidnap de Foucauld. However they were interrupted by two <a href="/wiki/M%C3%A9hariste" title="Méhariste">Méharistes</a> of the French Camel Corps. One startled bandit (15-year-old Sermi ag Thora) shot de Foucauld through the head, killing him instantly. The Méharistes were also shot dead.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> The murder was witnessed by <a href="/wiki/Sacristan" title="Sacristan">sacristan</a> and servant Paul Embarek, an African Arab former slave liberated and instructed by de Foucauld.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The French authorities continued for years searching for the bandits involved. In 1943 El Madani fled French forces in Libya to the remote South <a href="/wiki/Fezzan" title="Fezzan">Fezzan</a>. Sermi ag Thora was apprehended and executed at <a href="/wiki/Djanet" title="Djanet">Djanet</a> in 1944.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In April 1929, the mortal remains of Charles de Foucauld were transferred to the oasis of <a href="/wiki/El_Meniaa" class="mw-redirect" title="El Meniaa">El Meniaa</a> to a tomb in the cemetery near the local parish of St. Joseph.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (December 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_de_Foucauld&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>De Foucauld was <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> by Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Saraiva_Martins" title="José Saraiva Martins">José Saraiva Martins</a> on 13 November 2005, on behalf of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-cathnews.acu.edu.au_2011_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathnews.acu.edu.au_2011-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-AsiaNews.it_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AsiaNews.it-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 27 May 2020, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Francis" title="Pope Francis">Pope Francis</a> issued a decree which approved a second miracle clearing the way for de Foucauld to be canonized.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> On 4 March 2022, a <a href="/wiki/Papal_consistory" title="Papal consistory">papal consistory</a> opened the way for the canonization and set the date for the canonization ceremony to 15 May 2022, together with a number of others including <a href="/wiki/Titus_Brandsma" title="Titus Brandsma">Titus Brandsma</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> His feast is on 1 December, the <a href="/wiki/Liturgical_colour" class="mw-redirect" title="Liturgical colour">liturgical colour</a> is white.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Religious_communities_inspired_by_de_Foucauld">Religious communities inspired by de Foucauld</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_de_Foucauld&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Religious communities inspired by de Foucauld">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>De Foucauld inspired and helped to organize a <a href="/wiki/Confraternity" title="Confraternity">confraternity</a> within France in support of his ideas. This organization, the Association of the Brothers and Sisters of the Sacred Heart of Jesus, consisted of 48 lay and ordained members at the time of his death. Members of this group, notably <a href="/wiki/Louis_Massignon" title="Louis Massignon">Louis Massignon</a>, a scholar of <a href="/wiki/Islam" title="Islam">Islam</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Bazin" title="René Bazin">René Bazin</a>, author of a biography, <i>La Vie de Charles de Foucauld Explorateur en Maroc, Ermite du Sahara</i> (1923), kept his memory alive and inspired the family of lay and religious fraternities. Though French in origin, these groups have expanded to include many cultures and their languages on every populated continent. The Charles de Foucauld Spiritual Family Association brings together the Little Brothers of Jesus, the Little Sisters of Jesus and 18 other religious orders and associations for priests, religious and laypeople which were inspired by him.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_de_Foucauld&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The 1936 French film <i><a href="/wiki/The_Call_of_Silence" title="The Call of Silence">The Call of Silence</a></i> depicted his life.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1950, the colonial Algerian government issued a postage stamp with his image. The French government did the same in 1959.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (December 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>In 1959 Dupuis published a comic book about the life of Charles de Foucauld, art and text by the Belgian comic artist <a href="/wiki/Jij%C3%A9" title="Jijé">Jijé</a> (Joseph Gillain), who also made comic books about other saints, like Bernadette Soubirous and Don Bosco.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (December 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Antonello Padovano wrote and directed the film "The Four Doors of the Desert" based on Charles de Foucauld life and his friendship with the Tuareg Amenokal Moussa Ag Amastan.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Charles de Foucauld is honored in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">Church of England</a> and in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church</a> on <a href="/wiki/December_1" title="December 1">1 December</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
