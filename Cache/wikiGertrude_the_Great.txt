<p><b>Gertrude the Great</b>, <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">OSB</a> (or <b>Saint Gertrude of Helfta</b>; <a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Santa Gertrude</i>, <a href="/wiki/German_language" title="German language">German</a>: <i lang="de">Gertrud die Große von Helfta</i>, <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Sancta Gertrudis</i>; January 6, 1256 – November 17, 1302<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>) was a German <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> nun and mystic from the <a href="/wiki/Monastery_of_Helfta" title="Monastery of Helfta">monastery of Helfta</a>. She is recognized as a saint by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> and figures in the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> on November 16 for optional celebration as a memorial throughout the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Rite" title="Roman Rite">Roman Rite</a>.
</p><p>In recent times the <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church</a> has added Gertude to the Episcopal <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Calendar of Saints</a> on November 21.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Little is known of the early life of Gertrude who was born on the <a href="/wiki/Feast_of_the_Epiphany" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast of the Epiphany">Feast of the Epiphany</a>, January 6, 1256, in <a href="/wiki/Eisleben" title="Eisleben">Eisleben</a>, <a href="/wiki/Thuringia" title="Thuringia">Thuringia</a> (within the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire" title="Holy Roman Empire">Holy Roman Empire</a>). At age four,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> she entered the monastery school at St. Mary at Helfta (variously described both as Benedictine and as Cistercian),<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> under the direction of its abbess, <a href="/wiki/Gertrude_of_Hackeborn" title="Gertrude of Hackeborn">Gertrude of Hackeborn</a>. It is speculated that her devout parents offered her as a child <a href="/wiki/Oblate" title="Oblate">oblate</a> to the church. However, given that Gertrude implies in the <i>Herald</i> that her parents were long dead at the time of writing,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> it is also possible that she entered the monastery school as an orphan.
</p><p>Gertrude was entrusted to the care of <a href="/wiki/Mechthild_of_Hackeborn" title="Mechthild of Hackeborn">Mechtilde</a>, younger sister of the Abbess Gertrude, and joined the monastic community in 1266.<sup id="cite_ref-casanova_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-casanova-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> It is clear from her own writings that she received a thorough education in a range of subjects. She, and the nun who authored Books 1 and 3-5 of the <i>Herald</i>, are thoroughly familiar with scripture, the <a href="/wiki/Church_Fathers" title="Church Fathers">Church Fathers</a> such as <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</a> and <a href="/wiki/Gregory_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Gregory the Great">Gregory the Great</a>, and also more contemporary spiritual writers such as <a href="/wiki/Richard_of_St._Victor" class="mw-redirect" title="Richard of St. Victor">Richard</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hugh_of_St._Victor" class="mw-redirect" title="Hugh of St. Victor">Hugh of St. Victor</a>, <a href="/wiki/William_of_St-Thierry" title="William of St-Thierry">William of St Thierry</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Bernard_of_Clairvaux" title="Bernard of Clairvaux">Bernard of Clairvaux</a>. Moreover, Gertrude's writing demonstrates that she was well-versed in rhetoric, and her Latin is very fluent.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1281, at age 25, she experienced the first of a series of visions<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> that continued for the rest of her days, and which changed the course of her life. Her priorities shifted away from secular knowledge and toward the study of scripture and theology. Gertrude devoted herself strongly to personal prayer and meditation, and began writing spiritual treatises for the benefit of her fellow nuns.<sup id="cite_ref-cna_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cna-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Gertrude became one of the great mystics of the 13th century. Together with her friend and teacher Mechtilde, she practiced a spirituality called "nuptial mysticism," that is, she came to see herself as the <a href="/wiki/Bride_of_Christ" title="Bride of Christ">Bride of Christ</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gertrude died at Helfta, near <a href="/wiki/Eisleben" title="Eisleben">Eisleben</a>, <a href="/wiki/Saxony" title="Saxony">Saxony</a>, around 1302. Her feast day is celebrated on November 16, but the exact date of her death is unknown; the November date stems from a confusion with Abbess Gertrude of Hackeborn. One of her biographers, Gasparo Antonio Campaccio, diligently produced his own ‘Discorso Cronologico’ (pp 154–160) on her life, stating that the exact date of her death was November 17, 1334.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Merazhofen_Pfarrkirche_Chorgest%C3%BChl_links_Gertrud_von_Helfta.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/Merazhofen_Pfarrkirche_Chorgest%C3%BChl_links_Gertrud_von_Helfta.jpg/240px-Merazhofen_Pfarrkirche_Chorgest%C3%BChl_links_Gertrud_von_Helfta.jpg" decoding="async" width="240" height="367" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/Merazhofen_Pfarrkirche_Chorgest%C3%BChl_links_Gertrud_von_Helfta.jpg/360px-Merazhofen_Pfarrkirche_Chorgest%C3%BChl_links_Gertrud_von_Helfta.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/Merazhofen_Pfarrkirche_Chorgest%C3%BChl_links_Gertrud_von_Helfta.jpg/480px-Merazhofen_Pfarrkirche_Chorgest%C3%BChl_links_Gertrud_von_Helfta.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1951" data-file-height="2984" /></a><figcaption>Gertrude of Helfta: in Merazhofen Parish Church, Choir</figcaption></figure>
<p>Gertrude produced numerous writings, though only some survive today. The longest survival is the <i>Legatus Memorialis Abundantiae Divinae Pietatis </i>(known in English today as <i>The Herald of Divine Love </i>or <i>The Herald of God's Loving-Kindness</i>, and sometimes previously known as <i>Life and Revelations</i>), partly written by other nuns. There also remains her collection of <i>Spiritual Exercises. </i>A work known as <i>Preces Gertrudianae </i>(<i>Gertrudian Prayers</i>) is a later compilation, made up partly of extracts from the writings of Gertrude and partly of prayers composed in her style.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMarnau199311_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarnau199311-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> It is also very possible that Gertrude was the author of a part of the revelations of Mechthild of Hackeborn, the <i>Book of Special Grace</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMarnau199311_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarnau199311-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Herald</i> is composed of five books. Book 2 forms the core of the work, and was written by Gertrude herself; she states that she began the work on Maundy Thursday, 1289. Books 3, 4, and 5 were written by another nun, or possibly by more than one, during Gertrude's lifetime and probably at least in part at her dictation. Book 1 was written shortly before or after Gertrude's death as an introduction to the whole collection; it is possible it was written by Gertrude's confessor, but more probably by another Helfta nun.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The importance of the <i>Spiritual Exercises</i> extends to the present day because they are grounded in the themes and rites of Catholic liturgy for occasions such as baptism, conversion, commitment, discipleship, union with God, praise of God, and preparation for death. Gertrude's <i>Spiritual Exercises</i> can still be used by anyone who seeks to deepen spirituality through prayer and meditation.<sup id="cite_ref-bossert_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bossert-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Devotion_to_the_Sacred_Heart">Devotion to the Sacred Heart</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Devotion to the Sacred Heart">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>One of the most esteemed women saints of the Christian West, she was a notable early devotee of the <a href="/wiki/Sacred_Heart" title="Sacred Heart">Sacred Heart</a> of <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">Jesus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-cna_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cna-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Book 2 of the <i>Herald of Divine Love</i> is notable within the history of Christian devotion because its vivid descriptions of Gertrude's visions show a considerable elaboration on the long-standing but ill-defined veneration of Christ's heart. This veneration was present in the belief that Christ's heart poured forth a redemptive fountain through the wound in his side, an image culminating in its most famous articulation by <a href="/wiki/Bernard_of_Clairvaux" title="Bernard of Clairvaux">Bernard of Clairvaux</a> in his commentary on the Song of Songs. The women of Helfta—Gertrude foremost, who surely knew Bernard's commentary, and to a somewhat lesser extent the two Mechthilds, <a href="/wiki/Mechthild_of_Magdeburg" title="Mechthild of Magdeburg">Mechthild of Magdeburg</a> and Mechthild of Hackeborn—made this devotion central to their mystical visions.<sup id="cite_ref-Jenkins_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jenkins-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Gertrude reported a vision on the Feast of <a href="/wiki/John_the_Evangelist" title="John the Evangelist">John the Evangelist</a>. She was resting her head near the wound in the Christ's side and hearing the beating of his heart. She asked John if on the night of the Last Supper he had felt these pulsations, why he had never spoken of the fact. John replied that this revelation had been reserved for subsequent ages when the world, having grown cold, would have need of it to rekindle its love.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Later_reputation_and_influence">Later reputation and influence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Later reputation and influence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After her death, Gertrude's works seem to have vanished almost without trace. Only five manuscripts of the <i>Herald</i> have survived, the earliest one being written in 1412, and only two of these manuscripts are complete. With the invention of printing, Gertrude became far more prominent, with Latin, Italian and German editions being published in the sixteenth century. She was popular in seventeenth-century France, where her trust in and burning love for God were potent antidotes to <a href="/wiki/Jansenism" title="Jansenism">Jansenism</a>.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Philip_Neri" title="Philip Neri">Philip Neri</a> and <a href="/wiki/Francis_de_Sales" title="Francis de Sales">Francis de Sales</a> both used her prayers and recommended them to others.
</p><p>In Spain, Bishop Diego of Tarragona, the confessor to <a href="/wiki/Philip_II_of_Spain" title="Philip II of Spain">Philip II</a>, read the revelations of Gertrude aloud to the king as he lay dying in the <a href="/wiki/Escorial" class="mw-redirect" title="Escorial">Escorial</a>.
</p><p>Her works were also popular with the Discalced Carmelites in the sixteenth century. <a href="/wiki/Francisco_Ribera" title="Francisco Ribera">Francisco Ribera</a>, the confessor to <a href="/wiki/Teresa_of_%C3%81vila" title="Teresa of Ávila">Teresa of Ávila</a>, recommended that she take Gertrude as spiritual mistress and guide. At the height of Spanish female mysticism, the Spanish Jesuit Alonso de Andrade published a biography of Gertrude, giving Teresa a clear medieval antecedent.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>More recently, <a href="/wiki/Dom_Prosper_Gu%C3%A9ranger" class="mw-redirect" title="Dom Prosper Guéranger">Dom Prosper Guéranger</a>, the restorer of Benedictine monasticism in France, was influenced by Gertrude. His <a href="/wiki/Solesmes_Congregation" title="Solesmes Congregation">Congregation of Solesmes</a> was responsible for most of the work done on Gertrude in the nineteenth century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMarnau199343_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarnau199343-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Miguel_Cabrera,_Santa_Gertrudis,_1763_Mexico.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Miguel_Cabrera%2C_Santa_Gertrudis%2C_1763_Mexico.jpg/220px-Miguel_Cabrera%2C_Santa_Gertrudis%2C_1763_Mexico.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="269" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Miguel_Cabrera%2C_Santa_Gertrudis%2C_1763_Mexico.jpg/330px-Miguel_Cabrera%2C_Santa_Gertrudis%2C_1763_Mexico.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Miguel_Cabrera%2C_Santa_Gertrudis%2C_1763_Mexico.jpg/440px-Miguel_Cabrera%2C_Santa_Gertrudis%2C_1763_Mexico.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1633" data-file-height="2000" /></a><figcaption><i>Saint Gertrude</i> by Miguel Cabrera, 1763</figcaption></figure>
<p>Gertrude was never solemnly canonized, but a liturgical office of prayer, readings, and hymns in her honor was approved by Rome in 1606, considered the equivalent of a canonization. The feastday of Saint Gertrude was extended to the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Church" title="Latin Church">Latin Church</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_XII" title="Pope Clement XII">Pope Clement XII</a> and is nowadays celebrated on November 16, her presumed <i>dies natalis</i>, or date of death. Some religious communities, including some Benedictines, celebrate her liturgically on November 17, which had originally been chosen, but was already occupied by Gregory Thaumaturgus. It is said that Benedict XIV deemed it unfitting for him to be unseated by a woman. In the event, the Pope established November 15 as the date for Gertrude,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> since become November 16. Pope Benedict XIV gave her the title "the Great" to distinguish her from <a href="/wiki/Gertrude_of_Hackeborn" title="Gertrude of Hackeborn">Gertrude of Hackeborn</a> and to recognize the depth of her spiritual and theological insight.<sup id="cite_ref-bossert_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bossert-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 2022, <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">The Episcopal Church</a> of the United States added Gertrude, along with Mechtilde, to its <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">calendar of saints</a> with a feast day on November 21.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gertrude showed "tender sympathy towards the souls in Purgatory" and urged prayers for them.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> She is therefore invoked in aid of the suffering souls in Purgatory. The following prayer is attributed to Gertrude, and is often depicted on her <a href="/wiki/Prayer_card" class="mw-redirect" title="Prayer card">prayer card</a>:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>Eternal Father, I offer you the Most Precious Blood of your Divine Son, Jesus, in union with the Masses said throughout the world today, for all the Holy Souls in Purgatory, for sinners everywhere, for sinners in the universal Church, for those in my own home and in my family. Amen.</p></blockquote>
<p>Perhaps for that reason, her name has been attached to a prayer that, according to a legend of uncertain origin and date (neither are found in the <i>Revelations of Saint Gertrude the Great</i>), Christ promised to release a thousand souls from Purgatory each time it was said; despite the fact that practices relative to alleged promises to free one or more souls from Purgatory by the recitation of some particular prayer were prohibited by Pope Leo XIII.<sup id="cite_ref-prayer_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-prayer-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Nonetheless, the material that is found in her <i>Revelations,</i> such as the celebration of <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_Masses" title="Gregorian Masses">Gregorian Masses</a> for the departed, reflects the devotions approved by the Catholic Church.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconography">Iconography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Iconography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Images of Gertrude often show her gazing up to heaven, clothed as a nun with the ample sleeves typical of the choir habit. In the Baroque period it became a widespread artistic practice for the habit to be clearly depicted as that of a Benedictine, though this detail is not historically certain. At times she is also shown as an abbess, carrying a copy of the Rule of St Benedict in one hand and often also a crosier in the other hand. This latter detail is certainly mistaken and arose from confusing Gertrude the Great with <a href="/wiki/Gertrude_of_Hackeborn" title="Gertrude of Hackeborn">Gertrude of Hackeborn</a>, the abbess of St Mary at Helfta. Other artistic attributes distinguishing images of Gertrude are commonly a cross held in her hand  and on occasion a heart in the other. Sometimes the heart is displayed on her breast, surrounded by golden rays, and containing within it either a cross, or an image of the <a href="/wiki/Sacred_Heart" title="Sacred Heart">Sacred Heart of Jesus</a> or a small figure of the <a href="/wiki/Christ_Child" title="Christ Child">Child Jesus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage">Patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In compliance with a petition from King <a href="/wiki/Philip_IV_of_Spain" title="Philip IV of Spain">Philip IV of Spain</a> she was declared Patroness of the West Indies; in Peru her feast is celebrated with great pomp, and in New Mexico the town <a href="/wiki/Mora,_New_Mexico" title="Mora, New Mexico">Santa Gertrudis de lo de Mora</a> was built in her honor and bears her name.<sup id="cite_ref-casanova_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-casanova-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<li id="cite_note-3"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-3">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">This point of contention in Gertrude studies reflects the lack of clear distinction between the orders in her time. Many monasteries of nuns following the Rule of St Benedict were influenced by Cistercian customs, as was the one at Helfta. Founded in 1258, it could not officially be Cistercian because in 1228 the General Chapter of Citeaux had forbidden acceptance of more monasteries of nuns into their order so as to relieve the monks already overburdened by the number of nuns under their care. Still, the customs at Helfta seem to have been those of Citeaux and the works of Bernard of Clairvaux certainly were influential there. Whether the nuns wore a black 'Benedictine' or white 'Cistercian' habit is unclear, though both Gertrude and Mechtilde are almost universally represented in black. The spiritual directors of the monastery were neither Benedictines nor Cistercians, but Dominicans. See Sr Maximilian Marnau, 'Introduction', in Gertrude of Helfta, <i>The Herald of Divine Love</i>, (New York: Paulist Press, 1993), p10; Caroline Bynum Walker, <i>Jesus as Mother,</i> (Berkeley and Los Angeles, 1982), pp174-5.</span>
