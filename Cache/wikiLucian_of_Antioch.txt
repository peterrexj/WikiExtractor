<p><b>Lucian of Antioch</b> (c. 240 – January 7, 312),<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> known as <b>Lucian the Martyr</b>, was a <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> presbyter, <a href="/wiki/Theology" title="Theology">theologian</a> and <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a>. He was noted for both his scholarship and <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetic piety</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lucian_of_Antioch&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to <a href="/wiki/Suda" title="Suda">Suidas</a>, Lucian was born at <a href="/wiki/Samosata" class="mw-redirect" title="Samosata">Samosata</a>, <a href="/wiki/Kommagene" class="mw-redirect" title="Kommagene">Kommagene</a>, <a href="/wiki/Syria" title="Syria">Syria</a>, to Christian parents, and was educated in the neighbouring city of <a href="/wiki/Edessa,_Mesopotamia" class="mw-redirect" title="Edessa, Mesopotamia">Edessa</a>, <a href="/wiki/Mesopotamia" title="Mesopotamia">Mesopotamia</a>, at the school of <a href="/wiki/Macarius" title="Macarius">Macarius</a>. However, this tradition might be due to a conflation with his famous namesake, <a href="/wiki/Lucian" title="Lucian">Lucian of Samosata</a>, the pagan satirist of the second century.<sup id="cite_ref-CathEn_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CathEn-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At <a href="/wiki/Early_centers_of_Christianity#Antioch" class="mw-redirect" title="Early centers of Christianity">Antioch</a>, Lucian was ordained <a href="/wiki/Presbyter" title="Presbyter">presbyter</a>. <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea" class="mw-redirect" title="Eusebius of Caesarea">Eusebius of Caesarea</a> notes his theological learning<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and Lucian's vita (composed after 327) reports that he founded a <i>Didaskaleion</i>, a school. Scholars following <a href="/wiki/Adolf_von_Harnack" title="Adolf von Harnack">Adolf von Harnack</a> see him as the first head of the <a href="/wiki/School_of_Antioch" class="mw-redirect" title="School of Antioch">School of Antioch</a>, with links to later theologians <a href="/wiki/Diodorus_of_Tarsus" title="Diodorus of Tarsus">Diodorus of Tarsus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Theodore_of_Mopsuestia" title="Theodore of Mopsuestia">Theodore of Mopsuestia</a>, but that contention is unrecorded in the extant sources.<sup id="cite_ref-ThRE_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ThRE-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After the deposition of Antioch's bishop <a href="/wiki/Paul_of_Samosata" title="Paul of Samosata">Paul of Samosata</a>, he fell under suspicion for heresy, and was excommunicated. According to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Alexander_I_of_Alexandria" title="Pope Alexander I of Alexandria">Alexander of Alexandria</a>, he remained in schism during the episcopates of three <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishops</a>, Domnus, Timaeus and Cyril, whose administration extended from 268 to 303. Lucian was reconciled with the Church either early in the episcopate of Cyril (perhaps about 285), which seems more likely, or under Cyril's successor Tyrannus.
</p><p>During the persecution of <a href="/wiki/Maximinus_Daia" class="mw-redirect" title="Maximinus Daia">Maximinus Daia</a>, Lucian was arrested at Antioch and sent to Nicomedia, where he endured many tortures over nine years of imprisonment. He was twice brought up for examination, and both times defended himself ably and refused to renounce his Christian faith.
</p><p>His death is uncertain. He might have been starved to death. Another, more likely, possibility is that he was beheaded. The traditional date ascribed to his execution is January 7, 312, in <a href="/wiki/Nicomedia" title="Nicomedia">Nicomedia</a>. There is a late tradition of uncertain origin that he had been drowned in the sea and that his body was returned to land by a <a href="/wiki/Dolphin" title="Dolphin">dolphin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Attwater_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Attwater-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He was buried at <a href="/wiki/Helenopolis,_Bithynia" class="mw-redirect" title="Helenopolis, Bithynia">Drepanum</a> on the Gulf of Nicomedia, which was later renamed Helenopolis to honour <a href="/wiki/Helena_(Empress)" class="mw-redirect" title="Helena (Empress)">Helena</a>, mother of <a href="/wiki/Constantine_the_Great" title="Constantine the Great">Constantine the Great</a>.
</p><p>He is also commemorated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>, with a <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> of January 7 in the Roman Catholic Church<sup id="cite_ref-Attwater_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Attwater-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> and October 15 in the Orthodox Church.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Theology">Theology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lucian_of_Antioch&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Theology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Lucian's theological position is a matter of contention. Attempts to reconstruct his theology from the extant sources have led to contradictory results.
</p><p>Because <a href="/wiki/Arius" title="Arius">Arius</a> in a letter addressed <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Nicomedia" title="Eusebius of Nicomedia">Eusebius of Nicomedia</a> as "sylloukianistes" ("Fellow-Lucianist"), Lucian's theology came to be associated with the <a href="/wiki/Arian_controversy" title="Arian controversy">Arian controversy</a>. Following <a href="/wiki/Adolf_von_Harnack" title="Adolf von Harnack">Adolf von Harnack</a>, many scholars have interpreted the word (which only appears in this instance) as denoting a theological school and have therefore seen not only Eusebius but also Arius and other Arian leaders (among them <a href="/wiki/Maris_(bishop)" title="Maris (bishop)">Maris</a>, <a href="/wiki/Theognis_of_Nicaea" title="Theognis of Nicaea">Theognis of Nicaea</a> and Asterius) as pupils of Lucian and have transferred Arian views unto Lucian.<sup id="cite_ref-ThRE_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ThRE-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The first writer to clearly attest such a discipleship for a number of Arian sympathisers—but not for Arius and his closer associates—was the <a href="/wiki/Anomoeanism" title="Anomoeanism">Anomoean</a> church historian <a href="/wiki/Philostorgius" title="Philostorgius">Philostorgius</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ThRE_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ThRE-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Others have interpreted the word as indicating not a theological link but the special veneration accorded to Lucian by Eusebius, who by that time headed the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Nicomedia" class="mw-redirect" title="Church of Nicomedia">church of Nicomedia</a>, the place of Lucian's martyrdom. Lucian's veneration increased during the latter half of Constantine's reign, in particular due to the patronage of the Empress <a href="/wiki/Helena_(Empress)" class="mw-redirect" title="Helena (Empress)">Helena</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ThRE_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ThRE-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Opponents of Arianism, such as <a href="/wiki/Pope_Alexander_I_of_Alexandria" title="Pope Alexander I of Alexandria">Alexander of Alexandria</a>, countered this veneration by noting Lucian's schismatic past. <a href="/w/index.php?title=Marcus_Victorinus&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Marcus Victorinus (page does not exist)">Marcus Victorinus</a> identified the Eusebian party with Lucian. Epiphanius associates Lucian with heretical views about Christ's human soul held by Arians (but also by others) and relates that the Arians venerated Lucian as their martyr and that Lucian lived together with Eusebius at Nicomedia.<sup id="cite_ref-ThRE_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ThRE-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Associated with Lucian's name is also the <i>Creed of the Dedication</i> passed at the <a href="/wiki/Synods_of_Antioch" title="Synods of Antioch">Council of Antioch</a> in 341. This association is unknown to <a href="/wiki/Athanasius_of_Alexandria" title="Athanasius of Alexandria">Athanasius of Alexandria</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hilarius_of_Poitiers" class="mw-redirect" title="Hilarius of Poitiers">Hilarius of Poitiers</a>, but known and accepted by later writers. It was most likely brought up by the <a href="/wiki/Arian_controversy#Homoiousian" title="Arian controversy">Homoiousian party</a>. In their opposition against the <a href="/wiki/Arian_controversy#Homoian" title="Arian controversy">Homoian party</a> supported by Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantius_II" title="Constantius II">Constantius II</a>, the Homoiousians claimed the legacy of Lucian and adopted the definition of 341 as their creed.<sup id="cite_ref-ThRE_5-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ThRE-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Other attempts to reconstruct Lucian's theology have started out with <a href="/wiki/Paul_of_Samosata" title="Paul of Samosata">Paul of Samosata</a>, whose rejection of the <a href="/wiki/Allegory" title="Allegory">allegorizing</a> tendencies of the <a href="/wiki/Alexandrian_School" class="mw-redirect" title="Alexandrian School">Alexandrian School</a>, and especially those of <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>, was transferred to Lucian.<sup id="cite_ref-ThRE_5-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ThRE-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Because these identifications created a contradictory picture of Lucian, some scholars have proposed the existence of two Lucians, the first being a follower of Paul of Samosata, the second being Lucian the martyr, a theologian in the Origenist tradition and the teacher of Arius and Eusebius of Nicomedia. However, this proposal has now been largely rejected.<sup id="cite_ref-ThRE_5-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ThRE-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Whatever his theology had been, his status as a martyr and a saint was not impacted by concerns of orthodoxy. In the words of Philip Schaff: "The contradictory reports are easily reconciled by the assumption that Lucian was a critical scholar with some peculiar views on the Trinity and Christology which were not in harmony with the later Nicene orthodoxy, but that he wiped out all stains by his heroic confession and martyrdom".<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biblical_text">Biblical text</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lucian_of_Antioch&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Biblical text">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Lucian is credited with a critical recension of the text of the <a href="/wiki/Septuagint" title="Septuagint">Septuagint</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_text-type" title="Byzantine text-type">Greek New Testament</a>.  The resulting manuscript was popular in <a href="/wiki/Syria" title="Syria">Syria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Asia_Minor" class="mw-redirect" title="Asia Minor">Asia Minor</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a><sup id="cite_ref-Anderson_Charlesworth_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Anderson_Charlesworth-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and was later used by <a href="/wiki/Chrysostom" class="mw-redirect" title="Chrysostom">Chrysostom</a> and the later Greek fathers, and which lies at the basis of the <a href="/wiki/Textus_receptus" class="mw-redirect" title="Textus receptus">textus receptus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>  However, Lucian took it upon himself to "fix" the manuscripts he received, saying he was correcting errors that had snuck in over time.  He undertook to revise the Septuagint based on the original Hebrew.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  For this, he received criticism.  <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> mentions that copies of his work on the Greek Old Testament were known in his day as "exemplaria Lucianea" but in other places he speaks rather disparagingly of the texts of Lucian.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Jerome also wrote: "This (Testament) certainly differs in our language, and is led in the way of different streams; it is necessary to seek the single fountainhead. I pass over those books which are called by the name of Lucian and Hesychius, for which a few men wrongly claim authority, who anyway were not allowed to revise either in the Old Instrument after the <a href="/wiki/Septuagint" title="Septuagint">Seventy Translators</a>, or to pour out revisions in the New; with the Scriptures previously translated into the languages of many nations, the additions may now be shown to be false."<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> In the absence of definite information it is impossible to decide the merits of Lucian's critical labors.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>  The 6th century <a href="/wiki/Gelasian_Decree" title="Gelasian Decree">Gelasian Decree</a>, a work of the Latin Western Church rather than the Greek Eastern Church where Lucian's work was more popular, contains a list of apocryphal books "to be avoided by Catholics" that includes "the Gospels which Lucianus forged."<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>  Modern scholars mark Lucianic rescensions with an L, to indicate that when variant readings arise, they may have been from Lucian's adjustments.
</p><p>Lucian integrated a number of important minuscule manuscripts of <a href="/wiki/3_Maccabees" title="3 Maccabees">3 Maccabees</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Anderson_Charlesworth_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Anderson_Charlesworth-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
