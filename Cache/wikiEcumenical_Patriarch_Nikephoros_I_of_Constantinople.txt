<p><b>Nikephoros I</b> or <b>Nicephorus I</b> (c. 758 &#8211; 5 April 828) was a <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine</a> writer and <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_of_Constantinople" title="Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople">patriarch of Constantinople</a> from 12 April 806 to 13 March 815.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikephoros_I_of_Constantinople&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was born in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> as the son of Theodore and Eudokia, of a strictly Orthodox family, which had suffered from the earlier <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm" title="Byzantine Iconoclasm">Iconoclasm</a>. His father Theodore, one of the secretaries of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantine_V" title="Constantine V">Constantine V</a>, had been scourged and banished to <a href="/wiki/Nicaea" title="Nicaea">Nicaea</a> for his zealous support of <a href="/wiki/Iconodule" class="mw-redirect" title="Iconodule">Iconodules</a>, and the son inherited the religious convictions of the father. Nevertheless, he entered the service of the Empire, became cabinet secretary (<i><a href="/wiki/Asekretis" title="Asekretis">asekretis</a></i>), and under <a href="/wiki/Irene_of_Athens" title="Irene of Athens">Irene</a> took part in the <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Nicaea" title="Second Council of Nicaea">synod of 787</a> as imperial commissioner. He then withdrew to one of the <a href="/wiki/Cloister" title="Cloister">cloisters</a> that he had founded on the eastern shore of the <a href="/wiki/Bosporus" title="Bosporus">Bosporus</a>, until he was appointed director of the largest home for the destitute in Constantinople c. 802.
</p><p>After the death of the Patriarch <a href="/wiki/Tarasios_of_Constantinople" title="Tarasios of Constantinople">Tarasios of Constantinople</a>, although still a <a href="/wiki/Laity" title="Laity">layman</a>, he was chosen patriarch by the wish of the emperor (<a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Easter</a>, 12 April 806). The uncanonical choice met with opposition from the strictly clerical party of the <a href="/wiki/Stoudios" class="mw-redirect" title="Stoudios">Stoudites</a>, and this opposition intensified into an open break when Nikephoros, in other respects a very rigid moralist, showed himself compliant to the will of the emperor by reinstating the excommunicated priest Joseph.
</p><p>After vain theological disputes, in December 814, there followed personal insults. Nikephoros at first replied to his removal from his office by excommunication, but was at last obliged to yield to force, and was taken to one of the cloisters he had founded, <i>Tou Agathou</i>, and later to that called <i>Tou Hagiou Theodorou</i>. From there he carried on a literary <a href="/wiki/Polemic" title="Polemic">polemic</a> for the cause of the <a href="/wiki/Iconodule" class="mw-redirect" title="Iconodule">iconodules</a> against the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Constantinople_(815)" title="Council of Constantinople (815)">synod of 815</a>. On the occasion of the change of emperors, in 820, he was put forward as a candidate for the patriarchate and at least obtained the promise of toleration.
</p><p>He died at the monastery of Saint Theodore (<i>Hagiou Theodorou</i>), revered as a <a href="/wiki/Confessor" title="Confessor">confessor</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His remains were solemnly brought back to Constantinople by <a href="/wiki/Methodios_I_of_Constantinople" title="Methodios I of Constantinople">Methodios I of Constantinople</a> on 13 March 847 and interred in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Holy_Apostles" title="Church of the Holy Apostles">Church of the Holy Apostles</a>, where they were annually the object of imperial devotion. His feast is celebrated on this day both in the Greek and Roman Churches; the Greeks also observe 2 June as the day of his death.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikephoros_I_of_Constantinople&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Compared with <a href="/wiki/Theodore_the_Studite" title="Theodore the Studite">Theodore of Stoudios</a>, Nikephoros appears as a friend of conciliation, learned in <a href="/wiki/Patristics" title="Patristics">patristics</a>, more inclined to take the defensive than the offensive, and possessed of a comparatively chaste, simple style. He was mild in his ecclesiastical and monastical rules and non-partisan in his historical treatment of the period from 602 to 769 (<i>Historia syntomos, breviarium</i>). He used the chronicle of <a href="/wiki/Trajan_the_Patrician" title="Trajan the Patrician">Trajan the Patrician</a> but deliberately chose not to name the source so as to connect himself to the historical tradition of Theophylact Simocatta.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Short History</i> is thematically focused around the matter of the offices of emperor and patriarch.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Nikephoros attempted to salvage the reputation of the patriarchate by criticizing iconoclast patriarchs for submitting to the emperor, not for being iconoclasts.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Herakleios was the ideal emperor in Nikephoros’ scheme because of how he worked alongside patriarch Sergios, but also how Sergios helped to defend Constantinople from the Avars in 626 as well as the patriarch’s ability to discipline the emperor for his marriage to his niece Martina. Herakleios failure to heed the Egyptian patriarch’s advice is what ultimately brought about the Arab conquest of Egypt.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His tables of universal history, <i>Chronography</i> or <i>Chronographikon Syntomon</i>, in passages extended and continued, were in great favor with the Byzantines, and were also circulated outside the Empire in the Latin version of <a href="/wiki/Anastasius_Bibliothecarius" title="Anastasius Bibliothecarius">Anastasius Bibliothecarius</a>, and also in Slavonic translation. The <i>Chronography</i> offered a universal history from the time of <a href="/wiki/Adam_and_Eve" title="Adam and Eve">Adam and Eve</a> to his own time. To it he appended a canon catalog (which does not include the <i><a href="/wiki/Book_of_Revelation" title="Book of Revelation">Revelation</a></i> of <a href="/wiki/John_of_Patmos" title="John of Patmos">John</a>). The catalog of the accepted books of the Old and New Testaments is followed by the <i><a href="/wiki/Antilegomena" title="Antilegomena">antilegomena</a></i> (including <i>Revelation</i>) and the <a href="/wiki/Apocrypha" title="Apocrypha">apocrypha</a>. Next to each book is the count of its lines, his <a href="/wiki/Stichometry_of_Nicephorus" title="Stichometry of Nicephorus">stichometry</a>, to which we can compare our accepted texts and judge how much has been added or omitted. This is especially useful for apocrypha for which only fragmentary texts have survived.
</p><p>The principal works of Nikephorus are three writings referring to <a href="/wiki/Iconoclasm" title="Iconoclasm">iconoclasm</a>:
</p>
<ul><li><i>Apologeticus minor</i>, probably composed before 814, an explanatory work for laymen concerning the tradition and the first phase of the iconoclastic movement;</li>
<li><i>Apologeticus major</i> with the three <i>Antirrhetici</i> against <a href="/wiki/Mamonas" title="Mamonas">Mamonas</a>-Constantine Kopronymos, a complete <a href="/wiki/Dogmatics" class="mw-redirect" title="Dogmatics">dogmatics</a> of the belief in images, with an exhaustive discussion and refutation of all objections made in opposing writings, as well as those drawn from the works of the <a href="/wiki/Church_Fathers" title="Church Fathers">Fathers</a>;</li>
<li>The third of these larger works is a refutation of the iconoclastic synod of 815 (ed. Serruys, Paris, 1904).</li></ul>
<p>Nikephoros follows in the path of <a href="/wiki/John_of_Damascus" title="John of Damascus">John of Damascus</a>. His merit is the thoroughness with which he traced the literary and traditional proofs, and his detailed refutations are serviceable for the knowledge they afford of important texts adduced by his opponents and in part drawn from the older church literature.
</p>
