<p><b>Theophanes the Confessor</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Θεοφάνης Ὁμολογητής</span>; c. 758/760 – 12 March 817/818) was a member of the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine</a> aristocracy who became a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> and <a href="/wiki/Chronicle" title="Chronicle">chronicler</a>. He served in the court of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Leo_IV_the_Khazar" title="Leo IV the Khazar">Leo IV the Khazar</a> before taking up the religious life. Theophanes attended the <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Nicaea" title="Second Council of Nicaea">Second Council of Nicaea</a> in 787 and resisted the iconoclasm of <a href="/wiki/Leo_V_the_Armenian" title="Leo V the Armenian">Leo V the Armenian</a>, for which he was imprisoned. He died shortly after his release.
</p><p>Theophanes the Confessor, <a href="/wiki/Veneration" title="Veneration">venerated</a> on 12 March in both the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic</a> churches, should not be confused with <a href="/wiki/Theophanes_the_Branded" class="mw-redirect" title="Theophanes the Branded">Theophanes of Nicaea</a>, whose <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast</a> is commemorated on 11 October.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theophanes_the_Confessor&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Theophanes was born in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> of wealthy and noble <a href="/wiki/Iconodule" class="mw-redirect" title="Iconodule">iconodule</a> parents: Isaac, governor of the <a href="/wiki/Aegean_Islands" title="Aegean Islands">islands</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Aegean_Sea" title="Aegean Sea">Aegean Sea</a>, and Theodora, of whose family nothing is known.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMershman1912_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMershman1912-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>  His father died when Theophanes was three years old, and the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Emperors" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine Emperors">Byzantine Emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Constantine_V" title="Constantine V">Constantine V</a> (740–775) subsequently saw to the boy's education and upbringing at the imperial court. Theophanes would hold several offices under Leo IV the Khazar.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He was married at the age of eighteen, but convinced his wife to lead a life of virginity. In 779, after the death of his father-in-law, they separated with mutual consent to embrace the religious life. She chose a convent on an island near Constantinople, while he entered the Polychronius Monastery, located in the district of Sigiane (Sigriano), near <a href="/wiki/Cyzicus" title="Cyzicus">Cyzicus</a> on the Asian side of the <a href="/wiki/Sea_of_Marmara" title="Sea of Marmara">Sea of Marmara</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMershman1912_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMershman1912-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Later, he built a monastery on his own lands on the island of Calonymus (now <a href="/wiki/Kalymnos" title="Kalymnos">Calomio</a>),<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> where he acquired a high degree of skill in transcribing manuscripts.
</p><p>After six years he returned to Sigriano, where he founded an abbey known by the name "of the big settlement" and governed it as <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a>. In this position of leadership, he was present at the <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Nicaea" title="Second Council of Nicaea">Second Council of Nicaea</a> in 787, and signed its decrees in defense of the <a href="/wiki/Veneration" title="Veneration">veneration</a> of <a href="/wiki/Icon" title="Icon">icons</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMershman1912_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMershman1912-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Emperor <a href="/wiki/Leo_V_the_Armenian" title="Leo V the Armenian">Leo V the Armenian</a> (813–820) resumed his <a href="/wiki/Iconoclasm" title="Iconoclasm">iconoclastic</a> warfare, he ordered Theophanes brought to Constantinople. The Emperor tried in vain to induce him to condemn the same veneration of icons that had been sanctioned by the council. Theophanes was cast into prison and for two years suffered cruel treatment. After his release, he was banished to <a href="/wiki/Samothrace" title="Samothrace">Samothrace</a> in 817, where overwhelmed with afflictions, he lived only seventeen days. He is credited with many miracles that occurred after his death,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMershman1912_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMershman1912-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> which most likely took place on 12 March, the day he is commemorated in the <i><a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMershman1912_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMershman1912-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Chronicle"><i>Chronicle</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theophanes_the_Confessor&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Chronicle">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>At the urgent request of his friend <a href="/wiki/George_Syncellus" title="George Syncellus">George Syncellus</a>, Theophanes undertook the continuation of Syncellus' <i>Chronicle</i> (<span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Χρονογραφία</span></span>, Chronographia), during the years 810 to 815.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The language used occupies a place midway between the stiff ecclesiastical and the vernacular Greek.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>He made use of three main sources: first, material already prepared by Syncellus; second, he probably made the use of a set of extracts made by <a href="/wiki/Theodore_Lector" class="mw-redirect" title="Theodore Lector">Theodore Lector</a> from the works of <a href="/wiki/Socrates_Scholasticus" class="mw-redirect" title="Socrates Scholasticus">Socrates Scholasticus</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sozomenus" class="mw-redirect" title="Sozomenus">Sozomenus</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Theodoret" title="Theodoret">Theodoret</a>; and third, the city chronicle of Constantinople.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMershman1912_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMershman1912-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Cyril Mango has argued that Theophanes contributed but little to the chronicle that bears his name, and that the vast bulk of its contents are the work of Syncellus; on this model, Theophanes' main contribution was to cast Syncellus' rough materials together in a unified form.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2016)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Theophanes' part of the chronicle covered events from the accession of <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a> in 284 (which is the point where the chronicle of <a href="/wiki/George_Syncellus" title="George Syncellus">George Syncellus</a> ends) to the downfall of <a href="/wiki/Michael_I_Rhangabes" class="mw-redirect" title="Michael I Rhangabes">Michael I Rhangabes</a> in 813. This part of the chronicle is valuable for having preserved the accounts of lost authorities on Byzantine history for the seventh and eighth centuries that would be otherwise have been lost.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The work consists of two parts, wherein the first provides a chronological history arranged per annum, and the second contains chronological tables that are regrettably full of inaccuracies. It seems that Theophanes had only prepared the tables, leaving vacant spaces for the proper dates, but that these had been filled out by someone else (<a href="/wiki/Hugo_von_Hurter" class="mw-redirect" title="Hugo von Hurter">Hugo von Hurter</a>, <i>Nomenclator literarius recentioris</i> I, Innsbruck, 1903, 735). In the chronological first part, in addition to reckoning by the years of the world and the Christian era, Theophanes introduces in tabular form the regnal years of the Roman emperors, of the Persian kings and Arab caliphs, and of the five ecumenical patriarchs, a complex system which sometimes leads to considerable confusion.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The first part, though lacking in critical insight and chronological accuracy, greatly surpasses the majority of Byzantine chronicles.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Theophanes's Chronicle is particularly valuable beginning with the reign of <a href="/wiki/Justin_II" title="Justin II">Justin II</a> (565), as in his work, he then drew upon sources that have not survived his times<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Theophanes' <i>Chronicle</i> was much used by succeeding chroniclers, and in 873–875 a <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> compilation  was made<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  by the <a href="/wiki/Pope" title="Pope">papal</a> librarian <a href="/wiki/Anastasius_Bibliothecarius" title="Anastasius Bibliothecarius">Anastasius</a> from the chronicles of <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_Nicephorus_I_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Patriarch Nicephorus I of Constantinople">Nicephorus</a>, George Syncellus, and Theophanes for the use of a deacon named Johannes in the second half of the ninth century and thus was known to Western Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMershman1912_1-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMershman1912-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There also survives a further continuation, in six books, of the <i>Chronicle</i> down to the year 961 written by a number of mostly anonymous writers (called <a href="/wiki/Theophanes_Continuatus" title="Theophanes Continuatus">Theophanes Continuatus</a> or <i>Scriptores post Theophanem</i>), who undertook the work at the instructions of <a href="/wiki/Constantine_Porphyrogenitus" class="mw-redirect" title="Constantine Porphyrogenitus">Constantine Porphyrogenitus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMershman1912_1-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMershman1912-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Theophanes was the first to claim that the Prophet of the Islamic religion <a href="/wiki/Muhammad" title="Muhammad">Muhammad</a> had <a href="/wiki/Epilepsy" title="Epilepsy">epilepsy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
