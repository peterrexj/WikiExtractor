<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1066479718">.mw-parser-output .infobox-subbox{padding:0;border:none;margin:-3px;width:auto;min-width:100%;font-size:100%;clear:none;float:none;background-color:transparent}.mw-parser-output .infobox-3cols-child{margin:auto}.mw-parser-output .infobox .navbar{font-size:100%}body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-header,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-subheader,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-above,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-title,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-image,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-full-data,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-below{text-align:center}</style><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div class="honorific-prefix" style="display:inline;font-size: 77%; <p><b>Hegesippus</b> (Ἅγιος Ἡγήσιππος; c. 110 – c.180 AD<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>), also known as <b>Hegesippus the Nazarene</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> was a <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christian</a> writer of the early Church who may, in spite of his Greek name, have been a Jewish convert<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and certainly wrote against heresies of the <a href="/wiki/Gnosticism" title="Gnosticism">Gnostics</a> and of <a href="/wiki/Marcion" class="mw-redirect" title="Marcion">Marcion</a>. The dates that Hegesippus flourished are insecurely fixed by the statement of <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea" class="mw-redirect" title="Eusebius of Caesarea">Eusebius</a> that the death and apotheosis of <a href="/wiki/Antinous" title="Antinous">Antinous</a> (130) occurred in Hegesippus' lifetime,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and that he came to Rome under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Anicetus" title="Pope Anicetus">Pope Anicetus</a> (Bishop of Rome c. 157–168) and wrote in the time of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Eleuterus" class="mw-redirect" title="Pope Eleuterus">Pope Eleuterus</a> (pontificate c. 174–189).
</p><p>Hegesippus' works are now entirely lost, save eight passages concerning Church history quoted by Eusebius,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> who tells us that he wrote <i>Hypomnemata</i> (Ὑπομνήματα; "Memoirs" or "Memoranda"<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>) in five books, in the simplest style concerning the tradition of the Apostolic preaching. Through Eusebius, Hegesippus was also known to <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> who is responsible for the idea that Hegesippus "wrote a history of all ecclesiastical events from the passion of our Lord down to his own period... in five volumes", which has established the <i>Hypomnemata</i> as a Church history.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Hegesippus appealed principally to tradition as embodied in the teaching which had been handed down through the succession of bishops, thus providing for Eusebius information about the earliest bishops that otherwise would have been lost. 
</p>

<p>Eusebius says that Hegesippus was a convert from Judaism, learned in the <a href="/wiki/Semitic_languages" title="Semitic languages">Semitic languages</a> and conversant with the oral tradition and customs of the Jews, for he quoted from the <a href="/wiki/Hebrew_language" title="Hebrew language">Hebrew</a>, was acquainted with the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_the_Hebrews" title="Gospel of the Hebrews">Gospel of the Hebrews</a><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> and with a Syriac Gospel, and he also cited unwritten traditions of the <a href="/wiki/Jew" class="mw-redirect" title="Jew">Jews</a>. Eusebius' own shaky command of Hebrew and Aramaic,<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> and his lack of personal knowledge of customs of the Jews, were insufficiently founded to judge Hegesippus as a dependable source.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> He seems to have lived in some part of the East, for, in the time of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Anicetus" title="Pope Anicetus">Pope Anicetus</a> (A.D. 155–166) he travelled through <a href="/wiki/Corinth" title="Corinth">Corinth</a> to reach <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, collecting on the spot the teachings of the various churches which he visited, and ascertaining their uniformity with Rome, according to this excerpt: 
</p>
<dl><dd>"And the Church of the Corinthians remained in the true word until Primus was bishop in Corinth; I made their acquaintance in my journey to Rome, and remained with the Corinthians many days, in which we were refreshed with the true word. And when I was in Rome, I made a succession up to Anicetus, whose deacon was <a href="/wiki/Pope_Eleuterus" class="mw-redirect" title="Pope Eleuterus">Eleuterus</a>. And in each succession and in each city all is according to the ordinances of the law and the Prophets and the Lord"<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup></dd></dl>
<p>With great ingenuity <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Barber_Lightfoot" class="mw-redirect" title="Joseph Barber Lightfoot">J.B. Lightfoot</a>, in <i><a href="/wiki/Clement_of_Rome" title="Clement of Rome">Clement of Rome</a></i> (London, 1890), found traces of a list of popes in <a href="/wiki/Epiphanius_of_Cyprus" class="mw-redirect" title="Epiphanius of Cyprus">Epiphanius of Cyprus</a>, (<i>Haer.</i>, xxvii, 6) that may also derive from Hegesippus, where that fourth-century writer carelessly says: "<a href="/wiki/Marcellina_(gnostic)" class="mw-redirect" title="Marcellina (gnostic)">Marcellina</a> came to us lately and destroyed many, in the days of Anicetus, Bishop of Rome", and then refers to "the above catalogue", though he has given none. He is clearly quoting a writer who was at Rome in the time of Anicetus and made a list of popes<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> A list which has some curious agreements with Epiphanius in that it extends only to Anicetus, is found in the poem of <a href="/wiki/Pseudo-Tertullian" title="Pseudo-Tertullian">Pseudo-Tertullian</a> against <a href="/wiki/Marcion" class="mw-redirect" title="Marcion">Marcion</a>; apparently Epiphanius has mistaken Marcion for "Marcellina". The same list is at the base of the earlier part of the <a href="/wiki/Liberian_Catalogue" title="Liberian Catalogue">Liberian Catalogue</a>, doubtless taken from <a href="/wiki/Hippolytus_(writer)" class="mw-redirect" title="Hippolytus (writer)">Hippolytus</a>. Correspondences among the lists of  <a href="/wiki/Irenaeus" title="Irenaeus">Irenaeus</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sextus_Julius_Africanus" title="Sextus Julius Africanus">Africanus</a>, and Eusebius cannot be assumed to have come from the lost list of Hegesippus, as only Eusebius mentions his name. 
</p><p>Eusebius quotes from Hegesippus fifth and last book<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> a long account of the death of <a href="/wiki/James_the_Just" class="mw-redirect" title="James the Just">James the Just</a>, "the brother of the Lord", who was given the obscure Greek epithet <i>Oblias</i>, which is supposed to be a Greek transliteration of a Semitic word.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Dr. Robert Eisenman connects "Oblias" with "Protector of the people", as were other 'Zaddikim'.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> He also transcribes from Hegesippus the story of the election of his successor <a href="/wiki/Simeon_of_Jerusalem" title="Simeon of Jerusalem">Simeon</a>, and the summoning of the descendants of <a href="/wiki/Jude_the_Apostle" title="Jude the Apostle">Jude the Apostle</a> to Rome by the Emperor <a href="/wiki/Domitian" title="Domitian">Domitian</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> A list of heresies against which Hegesippus wrote is also cited. Dr. Lawlor has argued that all these passages cited by Eusebius were connected in the original, and were in the fifth book of Hegesippus.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> He has also argued the likelihood that Eusebius got from Hegesippus the statement that <a href="/wiki/John_the_Evangelist" title="John the Evangelist">John the Evangelist</a> was exiled to <a href="/wiki/Patmos" title="Patmos">Patmos</a> by Domitian.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Hegesippus mentioned the letter of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Pope Clement I">Pope Clement I</a> to the Corinthians, apparently in connection with the persecution of Domitian. It is very likely that the dating of heretics according to papal reigns in Irenaeus and Epiphanius—e.g., that <a href="/wiki/Marcion" class="mw-redirect" title="Marcion">Marcion</a>'s disciple Cerdon and <a href="/wiki/Valentinius" class="mw-redirect" title="Valentinius">Valentinus</a> came to Rome under Anicetus—was derived from Hegesippus, and the same may be true of the assertion that Hermas, author of <i><a href="/wiki/The_Shepherd_of_Hermas" title="The Shepherd of Hermas">The Shepherd of Hermas</a></i>, was the brother of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_I" title="Pope Pius I">Pope Pius I</a> (as the <a href="/wiki/Liberian_Catalogue" title="Liberian Catalogue">Liberian Catalogue</a>, the poem against Marcion, and the <a href="/wiki/Muratorian_fragment" title="Muratorian fragment">Muratorian fragment</a> all state).
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Theodor_Zahn" title="Theodor Zahn">Theodor Zahn</a><sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> has shown that the work of Hegesippus may still have been extant in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries in three Eastern libraries, saying: "We must lament the loss of other portions of the <i>Memoirs</i> which were known to exist in the seventeenth century."<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
