<p><b>Nil Sorsky</b> (<a href="/wiki/Russian_language" title="Russian language">Russian</a>: <span lang="ru">Нил Сорский</span>, also <b>Nilus of Sora</b> and <b>Nil Sorski</b>; birth name: <b>Nikolai Maikov</b> (<a href="/wiki/Russian_language" title="Russian language">Russian</a>: <span lang="ru">Николай Майков</span>) (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1433</span> – 1508) became a <a href="/wiki/Leader" class="mw-redirect" title="Leader">leader</a> of a tendency in the medieval <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodox Church</a> known as the <a href="/wiki/Non-possessors" title="Non-possessors">Non-possessors</a> (<i>nestyazhateli</i>) which opposed ecclesiastic <a href="/wiki/Landownership" class="mw-redirect" title="Landownership">landownership</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>  The <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodox Church</a> venerates Nil Sorsky as a <a href="/wiki/Glorification" title="Glorification">saint</a>, marking his <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> on the anniversary of his <a href="/wiki/Death" title="Death">repose</a> on 7 May.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nilus_of_Sora&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nilus of Sora, a great ascetic of the Russian Church, was descended from the <a href="/wiki/Maikov" class="mw-redirect" title="Maikov">Maikov</a> nobility.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
Before becoming a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a>, Nil Sorsky worked as a <a href="/wiki/Scribe" title="Scribe">scribe</a> and was engaged in book copying. He also journeyed to <a href="/wiki/Palestine_(region)" title="Palestine (region)">Palestine</a> and Greece early in his life and was acquainted there with the <a href="/wiki/Hesychasm" title="Hesychasm">Hesychast</a> movement.<sup id="cite_ref-:12_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:12-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Later in his life, he took monastic vows at the <a href="/wiki/Kirillo-Belozersky_Monastery" title="Kirillo-Belozersky Monastery">Kirillo-Belozersky Monastery</a>, which had been known for its hostile stance towards monastic landownership. The founder of the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a>&#160;– Saint <a href="/wiki/Kirill_of_Beloozero" class="mw-redirect" title="Kirill of Beloozero">Kirill of Beloozero</a>&#160;– was himself known for rejecting villages that had been offered to him by devout nobles.
</p><p>Kirill's followers adopted his ways and would later become known as the <i><a href="/wiki/Starets" title="Starets">startsy</a> from out the <a href="/wiki/Volga" title="Volga">Volga</a></i> with Nil Sorsky as their leader. Soon, he went on a journey to the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Land" title="Holy Land">Holy Land</a> and visited <a href="/wiki/Palestine_(region)" title="Palestine (region)">Palestine</a>, <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Mount_Athos" title="Mount Athos">Mount Athos</a>, acquainting himself with a mystical doctrine of <a href="/wiki/Hesychasm" title="Hesychasm">Hesychasm</a> and reading <a href="/wiki/Patristics" title="Patristics">patristic literature</a>. Upon his return to Russia (between 1473 and 1489), Nil Sorsky founded a <a href="/wiki/Cloister" title="Cloister">cloister</a> on the <a href="/w/index.php?title=Sora_River_(Sheksna)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Sora River (Sheksna) (page does not exist)">Sora River</a> (hence, Nil Sorsky, or Nil of Sora) not far from the Kirillo-Belozersky Monastery, where he would settle down with his followers.  He wrote extensively.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> This travelling to the holy places made a great impression upon him and changed all his individuality. Solitude, isolation, oblivion of the world became the main features of his character.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Novgorod_heresy_affair">The Novgorod heresy affair</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nilus_of_Sora&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: The Novgorod heresy affair">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nil Sorsky was involved in the <a href="/wiki/Novgorod" class="mw-redirect" title="Novgorod">Novgorod</a> <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heresy</a> affair (see <i><a href="/wiki/Sect_of_Skhariya_the_Jew" title="Sect of Skhariya the Jew">Sect of Skhariya the Jew</a></i> for details), which had stirred a lot of minds in the Tsardom of Russia at that time.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> It appears that Sorsky and his closest associate <a href="/wiki/Paisiy_Yaroslavov" class="mw-redirect" title="Paisiy Yaroslavov">Paisiy Yaroslavov</a> were much more tolerant towards the heretics than most of the Russian <a href="/wiki/Clergy" title="Clergy">clergy</a>, led by <a href="/wiki/Gennady_(Archbishop_of_Novgorod)" class="mw-redirect" title="Gennady (Archbishop of Novgorod)">Gennady (Archbishop of Novgorod)</a> and <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Volotsky" title="Joseph Volotsky">Joseph Volotsky</a>.
</p><p>In 1489, Archbishop Gennady embarked on the path of fighting the heretics and asked Archbishop of <a href="/wiki/Rostov" title="Rostov">Rostov</a> to consult with the elders Nil Sorsky and Paisiy Yaroslavov (who had been living in his <a href="/wiki/Eparchy" title="Eparchy">eparchy</a>) and seek their assistance in this matter. Historical accounts of that period do not shed any light on the outcome of these "negotiations", but from that time on there seems to have been no interaction between Sorsky and Yaroslavov on one side and Gennady and Joseph Volotsky on the other.
</p><p>The two elders, however, did not treat heresy with indifference. They were both present at the Synod of 1490, which dealt with heresy, and exerted their influence upon its final decision. Initially, the clergy unanimously spoke in support of burning all the heretics at the stake. At the conclusion of the synod, however, only a few priests were condemned and then <a href="/wiki/Defrock" class="mw-redirect" title="Defrock">defrocked</a> without being executed.  Later in the decade, however, several heretics were burned in both Novgorod and Moscow.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Teaching_and_influence">Teaching and influence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nilus_of_Sora&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Teaching and influence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nil Sorsky was opposed to formality in Orthodoxy faith which were widely spread in the midst of 16th century Russia. It was so deeply crystallized in church life of that period that such persons as <a href="/wiki/Maximus_the_Greek" title="Maximus the Greek">Maximus the Greek</a> and the elder Artemiy, who had tried to show the deep basics of Orthodoxy, were considered as Heretics. His wish was to disclose the deep understanding of Orthodoxy devoting more attention to spiritual life of a soul rather than to religious rituals. He was not a bookworm but a speculator-philosopher or speculator-theorist and an uncommon spiritual freedom marked his personality. The whole life of a person - the path of incessant self-perfection and self-improvement - is based on the Holy Gospel. And the Elder showed a lot of psychological understanding of the spiritual struggle and outlined clear ways of "graceful struggle". He deeply believed that moral, inner, spiritual perfection was the ideal foundation of the active, productive life of the believer.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In his teachings, he developed mystical and <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetical</a> ideas along the lines of <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Sinai" title="Gregory of Sinai">Gregory Sinaite</a>'s hesychasm, asking the believers to concentrate on their inner world and personal emotional experiences of faith as means for achieving unity with <a href="/wiki/God" title="God">God</a>. Nil Sorsky demanded that monks participate in productive labour and spoke in support of monastic reforms on a basis of a secluded and modest lifestyle.
</p><p>Nil Sorsky dedicated his efforts towards fighting against monastic landownership rights at the Synod of 1503 in <a href="/wiki/Moscow" title="Moscow">Moscow</a>. There, he raised a question about monastic estates, which comprised about one third of the territory of the whole Russian state at that time and which, in his view, had been responsible for demoralization of the Russian monastic communities. Nil Sorsky was supported by the elders of the Kirillo-Belozersky Monastery and his disciple <a href="/wiki/Vassian_Patrikeyev" title="Vassian Patrikeyev">Vassian Patrikeyev</a>. Although he spoke in favour of <a href="/wiki/Ivan_III_of_Russia" class="mw-redirect" title="Ivan III of Russia">Ivan III</a>’s policy of <a href="/wiki/Secularization" title="Secularization">secularization</a> of monastic lands,<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  Sorsky did not live long enough to see the end of this struggle. Patrikeyev and Artemius of <a href="/wiki/Troitse-Sergiyeva_Lavra" class="mw-redirect" title="Troitse-Sergiyeva Lavra">the Trinity</a> were Nil Sorsky's successors.
</p>
