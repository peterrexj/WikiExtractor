<p>The <b>Martyrs of Compiègne</b> were the 16 members of the <a href="/wiki/Carmelites" title="Carmelites">Carmel</a> of <a href="/wiki/Compi%C3%A8gne" title="Compiègne">Compiègne, France</a>: 11 <a href="/wiki/Discalced_Carmelites" title="Discalced Carmelites">Discalced Carmelite</a> <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nuns</a>, three <a href="/wiki/Lay_sisters" class="mw-redirect" title="Lay sisters">lay sisters</a>, and two externs (or <a href="/wiki/Third_order" title="Third order">tertiaries</a>). They were executed by the <a href="/wiki/Guillotine" title="Guillotine">guillotine</a> towards the end of the <a href="/wiki/Reign_of_Terror" title="Reign of Terror">Reign of Terror</a>, at what is now the <a href="/wiki/Place_de_la_Nation" title="Place de la Nation">Place de la Nation</a> in <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a> on 17 July 1794, and are venerated as <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyrs</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>. Ten days after their execution, <a href="/wiki/Maximilien_Robespierre" title="Maximilien Robespierre">Maximilien Robespierre</a> himself was executed, ending the Reign of Terror.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-opera_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-opera-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Their story has inspired a novella, a motion picture, a television movie, and an <a href="/wiki/Opera" title="Opera">opera</a>, <i><a href="/wiki/Dialogues_of_the_Carmelites" title="Dialogues of the Carmelites">Dialogues of the Carmelites</a>,</i> written by French composer <a href="/wiki/Francis_Poulenc" title="Francis Poulenc">Francis Poulenc</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martyrs_of_Compi%C3%A8gne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Conciergerie_June_2010.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Conciergerie_June_2010.jpg/220px-Conciergerie_June_2010.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="356" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Conciergerie_June_2010.jpg/330px-Conciergerie_June_2010.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Conciergerie_June_2010.jpg/440px-Conciergerie_June_2010.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1680" data-file-height="2720" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Conciergerie_June_2010.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>The <a href="/wiki/Conciergerie" title="Conciergerie">Conciergerie</a>, the prison the sisters were held while awaiting trial</div></div></div>
<p>According to writer William Bush, the number of Christian martyrs greatly expanded in the early years of the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>. Thousands of Christians were killed by the <a href="/wiki/Guillotine" title="Guillotine">guillotine</a>, as well as by mass deportations, drownings, imprisonments, shootings, mob violence, and "sheer butchery".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-1_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-1-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> In 1790, the French Revolutionary government passed the <a href="/wiki/Civil_Constitution_of_the_Clergy" title="Civil Constitution of the Clergy">Civil Constitution of the Clergy</a>, which outlawed religious life.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The community of <a href="/wiki/Carmelites" title="Carmelites">Carmelite</a> sisters at <a href="/wiki/Compi%C3%A8gne" title="Compiègne">Compiègne</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Communes_of_France" title="Communes of France">commune</a> in northern France, 72&#160;km north of <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a>, was founded in 1641, a daughter house of the monastery in <a href="/wiki/Amiens" title="Amiens">Amiens</a>. The community grew rapidly, and "was renowned for its fervor and fidelity".<sup id="cite_ref-lafayette_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lafayette-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> It was supported by the French court from its beginnings, until interrupted by the French Revolution, which was hostile towards religion and the Catholic Church.<sup id="cite_ref-lafayette_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lafayette-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Shortly after <a href="/wiki/Bastille_Day" title="Bastille Day">Bastille Day</a>, on 4 August 1790, government officials, with armed guards, interviewed each sister at their convent in Compiègne and forced them to choose between breaking their vows or risking further punishment. They all refused to abandon their lives of obedience, chastity, and poverty.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-bush-83_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-83-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> They were allowed to stay at the convent, becoming wards of the state, which entitled them to receive government pensions. The revolutionary government, at the end of 1791, required all clergy to swear a civic oath supporting the Civil Constitution or risk losing their pensions and becoming destitute. At Easter in 1792, the government plundered churches and interrupted services; it was the last Easter the sisters celebrated at Compiègne. Mother Teresa of St. Augustine, the convent's <a href="/wiki/Prior" title="Prior">prioress</a>, suggested to the community that they commit themselves to execution, and offer themselves as a <a href="/wiki/Redemptive_suffering" title="Redemptive suffering">sacrifice</a> for France and for the French Church.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> She almost missed participating in the sacrifice she proposed because she had to return to her family's home in Paris to care for her elderly, widowed mother. She returned to Compiègne four days before their execution and along with the rest of the sisters, was arrested.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In August 1792, the government ordered all women's monasteries closed; the seizure and removal of the Compiègne convent's furnishings occurred on 12 September, and the sisters were forced to leave the convent and re-enter the world on 14 September, the end of their cloistered community. Mother Teresa made arrangements for the 20 sisters living in the convent at the time to hide in the city in four separate apartments and find civilian clothes for them to wear, since the wearing of <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">habits</a> and religious apparel had been outlawed.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-89_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-89-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-michaeljwalsh-400_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-michaeljwalsh-400-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> They were dependent on the charity of friends, and "courageously continued to practice community prayer",<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> despite the government's orders.
</p><p>In 1794, after the <a href="/wiki/Reign_of_Terror" title="Reign of Terror">Terror</a> began, the government searched the sisters' apartments for two days; they found letters revealing their "crimes" against the Revolution, which included hostility to the Revolution, strong sympathies to the monarchy, and evidence that they continued to live as a community of consecrated Christian women.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-165_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-165-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> They also found two letters written by "the unfortunate"<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Mulot de la Ménardière to his cousin, Sister Euphrasia of the Immaculate Conception, containing unfavorable criticisms of the Revolution. Mulot was accused of helping them and of being a <a href="/wiki/Refractory_clergy" title="Refractory clergy">non-juring priest</a>, even though he was married, and was arrested and imprisoned with the sisters.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> On 22 June, the sisters and Mulot were arrested and locked up in the former convent of the Visitation, an improvised jail for political prisoners in Compiègne. On 10 July 1794, they were transferred to the <a href="/wiki/Conciergerie" title="Conciergerie">Conciergerie Prison</a> in Paris to await trial.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-michaeljwalsh-400_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-michaeljwalsh-400-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The sisters recanted their civic oath while in prison.<sup id="cite_ref-michaeljwalsh-400_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-michaeljwalsh-400-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During their trial on 17 July 1794, in which they received no legal counsel,<sup id="cite_ref-michaeljwalsh-400_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-michaeljwalsh-400-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Sister Mary-Henrietta tried to force the prosecutor to define the word "fanatic", one of the charges against them. She pretended she did not know what the word meant, thus getting him to admit their fanaticism was due to their religion, which made them "criminals and annihilators of public freedom".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-63_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-63-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Mother Teresa claimed full responsibility for the charges of being counter-revolutionaries and religious fanatics, and defended and insisted on the others' innocence.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> All 16 sisters, along with Mulot, were sentenced to death.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> At one point, while waiting for the transportation from the Conciergerie to the site of their executions, one of the nuns, Sister St. Louis, after consulting with Mother Teresa, bartered a fur wrap she owned for a cup of chocolate for the sisters to drink from to give them strength after not being able to eat anything all day.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> There were 26 nights between their arrest and execution.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Execution">Execution</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martyrs_of_Compi%C3%A8gne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Execution">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Plaque_Carm%C3%A9lites_de_Compi%C3%A8gne,_Cimeti%C3%A8re_de_Picpus,_Paris_12.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/Plaque_Carm%C3%A9lites_de_Compi%C3%A8gne%2C_Cimeti%C3%A8re_de_Picpus%2C_Paris_12.jpg/220px-Plaque_Carm%C3%A9lites_de_Compi%C3%A8gne%2C_Cimeti%C3%A8re_de_Picpus%2C_Paris_12.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="183" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/Plaque_Carm%C3%A9lites_de_Compi%C3%A8gne%2C_Cimeti%C3%A8re_de_Picpus%2C_Paris_12.jpg/330px-Plaque_Carm%C3%A9lites_de_Compi%C3%A8gne%2C_Cimeti%C3%A8re_de_Picpus%2C_Paris_12.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/Plaque_Carm%C3%A9lites_de_Compi%C3%A8gne%2C_Cimeti%C3%A8re_de_Picpus%2C_Paris_12.jpg/440px-Plaque_Carm%C3%A9lites_de_Compi%C3%A8gne%2C_Cimeti%C3%A8re_de_Picpus%2C_Paris_12.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2655" data-file-height="2205" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Plaque_Carm%C3%A9lites_de_Compi%C3%A8gne,_Cimeti%C3%A8re_de_Picpus,_Paris_12.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Plaque at <a href="/wiki/Picpus_Cemetery" title="Picpus Cemetery">Picpus Cemetery</a> dedicated to the Martyrs of Compiègne</div></div></div>
<p>On the night of 17 July 1794, the sisters were transported through the streets of Paris in an open cart, a journey that took two hours. During that time, they sang "hymns of praise,"<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> including the <i><a href="/wiki/Miserere_(Allegri)" title="Miserere (Allegri)">Miserere</a></i>, the <i><a href="/wiki/Salve_Regina" title="Salve Regina">Salve Regina</a></i>, the evening <a href="/wiki/Vespers" title="Vespers">vespers</a>, and the <a href="/wiki/Compline" title="Compline">Compline</a>. Other sources state that they sang a combination of the <a href="/wiki/Office_of_the_Dead" title="Office of the Dead">Office of the Dead</a>, the vespers, the Compline, and other shorter texts.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Onlookers berated them, yelling insults and throwing things at them. While waiting to be executed, a sympathetic woman from the crowd offered the sisters water, but Sister Mary-Henrietta stopped one sister from accepting, insisting that it would break their unity and promising that they would drink when they were in heaven.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-a">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-bush-63_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-63-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> A crowd gathered, as usual, at the Place du Trône Renversé (now called <a href="/wiki/Place_de_la_Nation" title="Place de la Nation">Place de la Nation</a>),<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-a">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> the site of the executions, to watch, but the sisters showed no fear and forgave their guards. The final song the sisters sang was <a href="/wiki/Psalm_117" title="Psalm 117">Psalm 116</a>,  <i>Laudate Dominum</i>.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-a">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> Sister Constance, a novice, the youngest of the group and the first to die, "spontaneously"<sup id="cite_ref-bush-25_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-25-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> began the chant, but it was cut short by the guillotine blade. Each sister joined her and was silenced in the same way.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-25_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-25-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The crowd became quiet<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-a">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> as each sister approached Mother Teresa, kissed the statue of the <a href="/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus" title="Mary, mother of Jesus">Virgin Mary</a> she held in her hands, and asked her for permission to die. After watching each sister die, she was the last one to place her head under the guillotine.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> Each sister knelt and chanted the "Veni Creator Spiritus" before her execution, "as at a profession",<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-a">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> then renewed their baptismal and religious vows. <a href="/wiki/Charlotte_de_la_R%C3%A9surrection" title="Charlotte de la Résurrection">Sister Charlotte</a>, who at 78 years of age was the oldest sister, walked with a crutch and was unable to stand up and get out of the cart because her hands were tied and the other sisters were unable to help her. Eventually a guard gathered her up in his arms and threw her on the street; she lay face down on the pavement stones, with no signs of life as the crowd protested the guard's treatment of her. She stirred, lifted up her blood-smeared face, and warmly thanked the guard for not killing her, "thereby depriving her of her share in her community's glorious witness for Jesus Christ".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-77_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-77-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Sister Mary-Henrietta stood by her prioress until it was her turn to die, helping the 14 other sisters climb the scaffold steps before climbing them herself, and was the second-to-last to die.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-63_14-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-63-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Mother Teresa died last.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-a">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There are no surviving relics of the Martyrs of Compiègne because their heads and bodies were buried, along with 128 other victims executed that day, in a deep, 30-feet square sand-pit in the <a href="/wiki/Picpus_Cemetery" title="Picpus Cemetery">Picpus Cemetery</a>.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-a">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> Their burial site, located in the back of the cemetery, is marked with two large, gravel-covered quadrangles. The heads and torsos of the 1,306 people who were guillotined at the Place de la Nation between 13 June and 27 July 1794 are buried there. Their names, including the 16 Martyrs of Compiègne as well as Mulot de la Ménardière, are inscribed on marble plaques covering the walls of a nearby church, where prayer is offered continuously.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-79_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-79-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> 24 other victims died with the sisters the day they were killed.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martyrs_of_Compi%C3%A8gne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Ten days after the Martyrs of Compiègne were executed, <a href="/wiki/Maximilien_Robespierre" title="Maximilien Robespierre">Maximilien Robespierre</a> was executed himself, ending the Reign of Terror.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-opera_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-opera-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> French Catholics of the time believed that the public executions of the nuns "helped bring about the end to the horrors of the revolution"<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> and hastened the end of the Reign of Terror.<sup id="cite_ref-harrowingstory_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-harrowingstory-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Three of the sisters were away from the community at the time of the arrests and so were not executed along with the others. One of these, Marie de l'Incarnation (Françoise Geneviève Philippe), later wrote an account of the execution, <i>History of the Carmelite nuns of Compiègne,</i> which was published in 1836.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> The story of the Martyrs of Compiègne has inspired a <a href="/wiki/Novella" title="Novella">novella</a>, an unproduced film, a play, and an <a href="/wiki/Opera" title="Opera">opera</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-opera_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-opera-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> In 1931, German writer <a href="/wiki/Gertrud_von_Le_Fort" title="Gertrud von Le Fort">Gertrude von le Fort</a>, a student of <a href="/wiki/Ernst_Troeltsch" title="Ernst Troeltsch">Ernst Troeltsch</a> and a convert to Catholicism, drew on the <i>History</i> to write a <a href="/wiki/Novella" title="Novella">novella</a>, <i>The Song at the Scaffold</i>, told from the viewpoint of the fictional character Blanche de la Force, "a young aristocrat haunted by fear, who seeks peace in the convent".<sup id="cite_ref-harrowingstory_23-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-harrowingstory-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>French <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican</a> <a href="/wiki/Raymond_L%C3%A9opold_Bruckberger" title="Raymond Léopold Bruckberger">Raymond-Leopold Bruckberger</a> and <a href="/wiki/Cinematographer" title="Cinematographer">cinematographer</a> <a href="/wiki/Philippe_Agostini" title="Philippe Agostini">Phillippe Agostini</a> developed a film based on the novella, and in 1947, they persuaded <a href="/wiki/Georges_Bernanos" title="Georges Bernanos">Georges Bernanos</a> to write the dialogue. The film was never produced, but the text written by Bernanos was staged as a play that premiered in 1951 in Zurich and ran for 300 performances the following year.<sup id="cite_ref-harrowingstory_23-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-harrowingstory-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Bernanos’ text, due to Bruckberger’s efforts, was used as the basis for the French film <i><a href="/wiki/Dialogue_with_the_Carmelites" title="Dialogue with the Carmelites">Le Dialogue des Carmélites</a></i>, which was written and directed by Agostini and released in 1960.<sup id="cite_ref-madbeppo_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-madbeppo-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> James Travers and Willems Henri stated that the film, even with its cast and production values, "stood the test of time and deserves to be more widely known".<sup id="cite_ref-frenchfilmsreview_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-frenchfilmsreview-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> Travers and Henri also said that the film "more than does justice to Georges Bernanos’ play and provides a thoughtful and emotionally involving reflection on the power and limits of faith".<sup id="cite_ref-frenchfilmsreview_26-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-frenchfilmsreview-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> The cast was composed of well-known French actors: <a href="/wiki/Pierre_Brasseur" title="Pierre Brasseur">Pierre Brasseur</a>, <a href="/wiki/Jeanne_Moreau" title="Jeanne Moreau">Jeanne Moreau</a>, <a href="/wiki/Madeleine_Renaud" title="Madeleine Renaud">Madeline Renaud</a>, <a href="/wiki/Alida_Valli" title="Alida Valli">Alida Valli</a>, <a href="/wiki/Georges_Wilson" title="Georges Wilson">Georges Wilson</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Jean-Louis_Barrault" title="Jean-Louis Barrault">Jean-Louis Barrault</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-frenchfilmsreview_26-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-frenchfilmsreview-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> In 1984, a version, directed by <a href="/wiki/Pierre_Cardinal" title="Pierre Cardinal">Pierre Cardinal</a>, based upon Bernanos’ dialogue, was produced for French television. The television version included more of Bernanos’ dialogue than the 1960 film, and Anne Caudry, who was his granddaughter, was featured in it.<sup id="cite_ref-madbeppo_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-madbeppo-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>French composer <a href="/wiki/Francis_Poulenc" title="Francis Poulenc">Francis Poulenc</a> was commissioned to write a <a href="/wiki/Ballet" title="Ballet">ballet</a> based on Bernanos' dialogue for <a href="/wiki/La_Scala" title="La Scala">La Scala</a> and <a href="/wiki/Casa_Ricordi" title="Casa Ricordi">Casa Ricordi</a>, but he wrote an opera instead, titled <i><a href="/wiki/Dialogues_of_the_Carmelites" title="Dialogues of the Carmelites">Dialogues of the Carmelites</a>.</i> As of 2019, the <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_Opera" title="Metropolitan Opera">Metropolitan Opera</a> had performed the opera 59 times, first in English, then in its original French, since its premiere in 1977, to sold-out audiences.<sup id="cite_ref-opera_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-opera-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Martyrs of Compiègne were beatified on May 27, 1906. They were the first martyrs of the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a> to be so recognized by the <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Holy See</a>. Their feast day is 17 July.<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-b">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On February 22, 2022, the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Beauvais" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of Beauvais">Roman Catholic Diocese of Beauvais</a> announced that <a href="/wiki/Pope_Francis" title="Pope Francis">Pope Francis</a> had accepted the procedure of <a href="/wiki/Equivalent_canonization" title="Equivalent canonization">equipollent canonization</a> for the Martyrs of Compiègne, by which they could be <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> as saints without recognition of a miracle attributed to their intercession.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="List_of_the_martyrs">List of the martyrs</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martyrs_of_Compi%C3%A8gne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: List of the martyrs">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<p>The Martyrs of Compiègne consisted of 11 nuns, three <a href="/wiki/Lay_sisters" class="mw-redirect" title="Lay sisters">lay sisters</a>, and two externs (or <a href="/wiki/Third_order" title="Third order">tertiaries</a>).<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-a">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><b>Choir Nuns</b>
</p>
<ul><li>Mother Teresa of St. Augustine, <a href="/wiki/Prior" title="Prior">prioress</a> (Madeleine-Claudine Ledoine). Born in Paris, 22 September 1752. <a href="/wiki/Vow" title="Vow">Professed</a> May 1775. She was the only child of an employee of the <a href="/wiki/Paris_Observatory" title="Paris Observatory">Paris Observatory</a>. According to Bush, "she received every educational advantage available to young ladies of the time".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-31_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-31-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Her artistic and poetic gifts were cultivated; some of her work has been preserved at the Carmels of Compiègne and <a href="/wiki/Sens" title="Sens">Sens</a>. Her dowry to enter the convent was paid by <a href="/wiki/Marie_Antoinette" title="Marie Antoinette">Marie Antoinette</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-31_29-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-31-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Mother St. Louis, sub-prioress (Marie-Anne [or Antoinette] Brideau). Born in <a href="/wiki/Belfort" title="Belfort">Belfort</a>, 7 December 1752. Professed Sept, 1771. Her father was a professional soldier, probably stationed at Compiègne at some point in his career.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Mother Henriette of Jesus, ex-prioress for two terms, elected by the community in 1779 and 1782; novice mistress (Marie-Françoise Gabrielle de Croissy). Born in Paris, 18 June 1745. Professed February 1764, prioress from 1779 to 1785. Mother Henriette was the great-niece of <a href="/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Colbert" title="Jean-Baptiste Colbert">Jean-Baptiste Colbert</a>, <a href="/wiki/Louis_XIV" title="Louis XIV">King Louis XIV's</a> minister. She had already spent half her life as a Carmelite at the time of her execution, coming to Compiègne when she was 16. She was refused entrance at first by the prioress at the time because of her youth. She was sent home in Amiens for another year, and finally made her profession in 1764.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> According to Mother Teresa, Henriette "won all hearts by her natural gentleness and affection, as might a real mother".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-56_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-56-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> Like Mother Teresa, Henriette wrote verses and was a talented artist; some of her works have also been preserved at the Carmels of Compiègne and Sens.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-56_32-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-56-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Sister Mary of Jesus Crucified (Marie-Anne Piedcourt). Born 1715, professed 1737. According to writer John B. Wainewright, while mounting the scaffold she said, "I forgive you as heartily as I wish God to forgive me."<sup class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-listref-a">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Sister Charlotte of the Resurrection, ex-sub-prioress (1764 and 1778) and <a href="/wiki/Sacristan" title="Sacristan">sacristan</a> (Anne-Marie-Madeleine Thouret). Born in <a href="/wiki/Mouy" title="Mouy">Mouy</a>, 16 September 1715. Professed August 1740. Sister Charlotte was the oldest sister of the group of martyrs. She "possessed a very lively mind" and was "naturally inclined towards gaiety".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-74_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-74-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Her father died early in her life; her mother remarried, but Sister Charlotte resented her stepfather. She entered the religious life after witnessing a tragedy at one of the balls she attended as a young girl. She nursed other sickly nuns, despite the toll it took on her own body. She was miraculously healed after toxic exposure to paint lead left her seriously cognitively impaired for two years.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-76_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-76-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Sister Euphrasia of the Immaculate Conception (Marie-Claude Cyprienne). Born in 1736 in <a href="/wiki/Bourth" title="Bourth">Bourth</a>. Professed in 1757; entered Compiègne in 1756, at the age of 20. She was witty, humorous, and "possessed an undeniable exterior charm".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-58_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-58-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> Sister Euphrasia wrote priests and others in the religious life for spiritual direction and "left a voluminous correspondence"<sup id="cite_ref-bush-60_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-60-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> during her 30 years in the community. Her letters reveal "a strong personality plagued by a certain restlessness, something always potentially problematic in a cloistered community".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-60_36-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-60-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Sister Teresa of the Sacred Heart of Mary (Marie-Antoniette Hanisset). Born in <a href="/wiki/Reims" title="Reims">Rheims</a> in 1740 or 1742. Professed in 1764. She was the daughter of a <a href="/wiki/Saddle" title="Saddle">saddle</a> maker. She served as the carmel's interior turn sister, receiving goods for the community from the outside world.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Sister Julie Louise of Jesus, <a href="/wiki/Widow" title="Widow">widow</a> (Rose-Chrétien de la Neuville). Born in Loreau (or <a href="/wiki/%C3%89vreux" title="Évreux">Évreux</a>), in 1741. Professed probably in 1777. She had married a cousin despite her calling to the religious life. After her husband died prematurely, she became so depressed and disconsolate, she went into deep mourning, to the point that her family feared for her sanity.  She received help from a cleric associated with her family and recovered with a new sense of her calling. She wrote five stanzas of verse for her and her sisters to recite as they prepared for their deaths.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Sister Teresa of St. Ignatius (Marie-Gabrielle Trézel) Born in Compiègne, 4 April 1743. Professed in 1771. She was a native of Compiègne. She was called "a mystic with a sense of the Absolute".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-79_21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-79-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Sister Mary-Henrietta of Providence (Anne Petras). Born in <a href="/wiki/Cajarc" title="Cajarc">Cajarc</a>, 17 June 1760. Professed in October 1786. Sister Mary-Henrietta, before joining the Carmelite order, was a member of the <a href="/wiki/Sisters_of_Charity_of_Nevers" title="Sisters of Charity of Nevers">Sisters of Charity of Nevers</a>. She was afraid "her natural beauty might prove a danger in a congregation where she was constantly exposed to the outside world",<sup id="cite_ref-bush-62_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-62-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> so she sought a more cloistered life. She came from a large, pious family; five of her sisters were also nuns in the Nevers order, and two of her brothers were priests.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-62_39-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-62-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Sister Constance of St. Denis, <a href="/wiki/Novice" title="Novice">novice</a> (Marie-Geneviève Meunier). Born in <a href="/wiki/Saint-Denis,_Seine-Saint-Denis" title="Saint-Denis, Seine-Saint-Denis">Saint-Denis</a>, 28 May 1765 or 1766. Sister Constance was the youngest member of the community. She was barred from making her final vows as a nun due to the revolutionary laws outlawing it, so she professed them to Mother Teresa before going to her death.<sup id="cite_ref-bush-25_19-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-25-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> When it became obvious to her family that she would not be able to legally profess her vows, they sent her brother to force her to return home. She refused, so he brought in the police, but they were convinced that she was in Compiègne by her own choice and did not force her to leave with her brother.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<p><b>Lay Sisters</b>
</p>
<ul><li>Sister St. Martha, <a href="/wiki/Lay_sister" class="mw-redirect" title="Lay sister">lay sister</a> (Marie Dufour). Born in <a href="/wiki/Beaune" title="Beaune">Beaune</a>, 1 October or 2, 1742. Entered the community in 1772.</li>
<li>Sister Mary of the Holy Spirit, lay sister (Angélique Roussel). Born in Fresnes, 4 August 1742. Professed in 1769.</li>
<li>Sister St. Francis Xavier (Julie Vérolot), lay sister. Born in <a href="/wiki/Laignes" title="Laignes">Laignes</a> or <a href="/wiki/Ligni%C3%A8res-en-Vimeu" title="Lignières-en-Vimeu">Lignières</a>, 11 January 1764. Professed in 1789. She was illiterate, but she "distinguished herself as much by her youthful zeal and good humor as by her terse expressions of love for Jesus Christ".<sup id="cite_ref-bush-66_41-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bush-66-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> In the convent, her main task was to care for the older nuns.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<p><b>Externs</b>
</p>
<ul><li>Catherine Soiron, born in 1742.</li>
<li>Thérèse Soiron, born in 1748. Both had been <a href="/wiki/Third_order" title="Third order">tertiaries</a> in service of the community since 1772.</li></ul>
