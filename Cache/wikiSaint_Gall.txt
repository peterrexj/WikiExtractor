<p><b>Gall</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Gallus</i>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 550 – <span title="circa">c.</span> 646) according to hagiographic tradition was a disciple and one of the traditional twelve companions of <a href="/wiki/Columbanus" title="Columbanus">Columbanus</a> on his <a href="/wiki/Hiberno-Scottish_mission" title="Hiberno-Scottish mission">mission from Ireland</a> to the continent. However, he may have originally come from the border region between <a href="/wiki/Lorraine" title="Lorraine">Lorraine</a> and <a href="/wiki/Alamannia" title="Alamannia">Alemannia</a> and only met <a href="/wiki/Columbanus" title="Columbanus">Columbanus</a> at the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Luxeuil_Abbey" title="Luxeuil Abbey">Luxeuil</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Vosges" title="Vosges">Vosges</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Gall is known as a representative of the Irish monastic tradition.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Gall" title="Abbey of Saint Gall">Abbey of Saint Gall</a> in the city of <a href="/wiki/St._Gallen" title="St. Gallen">Saint Gallen</a>, Switzerland was built upon his original hermitage.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Deicolus" title="Deicolus">Deicolus</a> was the elder brother of Gall.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Gall&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Columban_und_Gallus_auf_dem_Bodensee.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cf/Columban_und_Gallus_auf_dem_Bodensee.jpg/220px-Columban_und_Gallus_auf_dem_Bodensee.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="306" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/cf/Columban_und_Gallus_auf_dem_Bodensee.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="269" data-file-height="374" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Columbanus" title="Columbanus">Columbanus</a> and Saint Gall on Lake Constance (<i>Bodensee</i>), from a 15th-century manuscript</figcaption></figure>
<p>The fragmentary oldest <i>Life</i> was recast in the 9th century by two monks of <a href="/wiki/Reichenau_Island#History" title="Reichenau Island">Reichenau</a>, enlarged in 816–824 by <a href="/wiki/Wettinus" class="mw-redirect" title="Wettinus">Wettinus</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and about 833–884 by <a href="/wiki/Walafrid_Strabo" title="Walafrid Strabo">Walafrid Strabo</a>, who also revised a book of the miracles of the saint. Other works ascribed to Walafrid tell of Saint Gall in prose and verse.
</p><p>Gall's origin is a matter of dispute. According to his 9th-century biographers in Reichenau, he was from Ireland and entered Europe as a companion of <a href="/wiki/Columbanus" title="Columbanus">Columbanus</a>. The Irish origin of the historical Gallus was called into question by Hilty (2001), who proposed it as more likely that he was from the <a href="/wiki/Vosges" title="Vosges">Vosges</a> or <a href="/wiki/Alsace" title="Alsace">Alsace</a> region. Schär (2010) proposed that Gall may have been of Irish descent but born and raised in the Alsace.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to the 9th-century hagiographies, Gall as a young man went to study under <a href="/wiki/Comgall" title="Comgall">Comgall</a> of <a href="/wiki/Bangor_Abbey" title="Bangor Abbey">Bangor Abbey</a>. The monastery at Bangor had become renowned throughout Europe as a great centre of Christian learning. Studying in Bangor at the same time as Gall was Columbanus, who with twelve companions, set out about the year 589.<sup id="cite_ref-bangor_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bangor-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gall and his companions established themselves with Columbanus at first at <a href="/wiki/Luxeuil_Abbey" title="Luxeuil Abbey">Luxeuil</a> in <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a>. In 610, Columbanus was exiled by leaders opposed to Christianity and fled with Gall to <a href="/wiki/Alemannia" class="mw-redirect" title="Alemannia">Alemannia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-chi_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chi-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> He accompanied Columbanus on his voyage up the <a href="/wiki/Rhine" title="Rhine">Rhine River</a> to <a href="/wiki/Bregenz" title="Bregenz">Bregenz</a> but when in 612 Columbanus travelled on to Italy from Bregenz, Gall had to remain behind due to illness and was nursed at <a href="/wiki/Arbon,_Switzerland" class="mw-redirect" title="Arbon, Switzerland">Arbon</a>. He remained in Alemannia, where, with several companions, he led the life of a hermit in the forests southwest of <a href="/wiki/Lake_Constance" title="Lake Constance">Lake Constance</a>, near the source of the river Steinach.<sup id="cite_ref-poncelot_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-poncelot-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Cells were soon added for twelve monks whom Gall carefully instructed.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Gall was soon known in Switzerland as a powerful preacher.
</p><p>When the See of Constance became vacant, the clergy who assembled to elect a new bishop were unanimously in favour of Gall. He, however, refused, pleading that the election of a stranger would be contrary to church law. Some time later, in the year 625, on the death of Eustasius, abbott of Luxeuil, a monastery founded by Columbanus, members of that community were sent by the monks to request Gall to undertake the government of the monastery. He refused to quit his life of solitude, and undertake any office of rank which might involve him in the cares of the world. He was then an old man.<sup id="cite_ref-bangor_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bangor-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He died at the age of ninety-five around 646–650 in Arbon.<sup id="cite_ref-chi_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chi-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legends">Legends</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Gall&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legends">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>From as early as the 9th century a series of fantastically embroidered <i>Lives</i> of Saint Gall were circulated. Prominent was the story in which Gall delivered Fridiburga from the <a href="/wiki/Demon" title="Demon">demon</a> by which she was possessed.  Fridiburga was the betrothed of <a href="/wiki/Sigebert_II" title="Sigebert II">Sigebert II</a>, King of the <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Franks</a>, who had granted an estate at Arbon (which belonged to the royal treasury) to Gall so that he might found a monastery there.<sup id="cite_ref-poncelot_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-poncelot-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Another popular story has it that as Gall was travelling in the woods of what is now Switzerland he was sitting one evening warming his hands at a fire. A bear emerged from the woods and charged. The holy man rebuked the bear, so awed by his presence it stopped its attack and slunk off to the trees. There it gathered firewood before returning to share the heat of the fire with Gall. The legend says that for the rest of his days Gall was followed around by his companion the bear.<sup id="cite_ref-bear_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bear-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Gall&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>His feast is celebrated on 16 October.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<p>Images of Saint Gall typically represent him standing with a bear.<sup id="cite_ref-chi_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chi-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Gall&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>When Columbanus, Gall and their companions left Ireland for mainland Europe, they took with them learning and the written word.
Their effect on the historical record was significant as the books were painstakingly reproduced on vellum by monks across Europe. Many of the Irish texts <a href="/wiki/Lost_works" class="mw-redirect" title="Lost works">destroyed</a> in Ireland during <a href="/wiki/History_of_Ireland_(800%E2%80%931169)" title="History of Ireland (800–1169)">Viking raids</a> were preserved in Abbeys across the channel.<sup id="cite_ref-bear_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bear-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Abbey_of_St._Gall">Abbey of St. Gall</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Gall&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Abbey of St. Gall">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>For several decades after his death, Gall's hermit cell remained; his disciples remained together in the cell he had built and followed the rule of St. Columban, combining prayer with work of the hands and reading with teaching.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> In 719, <a href="/wiki/Saint_Othmar" title="Saint Othmar">St. Otmar</a>, the brotherhood's first abbot, extended Gall's cell into the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_St._Gall" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of St. Gall">Abbey of St. Gall</a>, which became the nucleus of the <a href="/wiki/Canton_of_St._Gallen" title="Canton of St. Gallen">Canton of St. Gallen</a> in eastern <a href="/wiki/Switzerland" title="Switzerland">Switzerland</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The abbey followed the rule of <a href="/wiki/Benedict_of_Nursia" title="Benedict of Nursia">St. Benedict of Nursia</a> beginning in 747.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> As many as 53 monks joined the order under St. Otmar and the community grew to acquire land in <a href="/wiki/Thurgau" title="Thurgau">Thurgau</a>, the region of Zurich and <a href="/wiki/Alamannia" title="Alamannia">Alemannia</a>, up to the <a href="/wiki/Neckar" title="Neckar">River Neckar</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> In the second half of the 8th century, the community continued to grow but became legally dependent on the Bishop of <a href="/wiki/Konstanz" title="Konstanz">Constance</a>. After an extended conflict with the see of Constance, the Abbey of St. Gallen regained its independence in the 9th century when Emperor <a href="/wiki/Louis_the_Pious" title="Louis the Pious">Louis the Pious</a> made it a royal monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The Abbey's monastery and especially its celebrated <a href="/wiki/Scriptorium" title="Scriptorium">scriptorium</a> (evidenced from 760 onwards) played an illustrious part in Catholic and intellectual history until it was secularised in 1798.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-poncelot_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-poncelot-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> It is very likely that Gall kept a small library of books for himself and his disciples for their liturgical worship. Following his death and the establishment of his tomb, the brotherhood of priests gathered there likely added to this small collection of books. These books would become the basis for the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_library_of_Saint_Gall" title="Abbey library of Saint Gall">Abbey Library of Saint Gall</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_popular_culture">In popular culture</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Gall&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: In popular culture">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><i>St Gall</i> is the name of a wheel shaped hard cheese made from the milk of Friesian cows, which won a Gold Medal at the World Cheese Awards held in Dublin 2008.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Robertson_Davies" title="Robertson Davies">Robertson Davies</a>, in his book, <i><a href="/wiki/The_Manticore" title="The Manticore">The Manticore</a></i>, interprets the legend in Jungian psychological terms. In the final scene of the novel where David Staunton is celebrating Christmas with Lizelloti Fitziputli, Magnus Eisengrim, and Dunstan Ramsay he is given a gingerbread bear. Ramsay explains that Gall made a pact of peace with a bear who was terrorizing the citizens of the nearby village. They would feed him gingerbread and he would refrain from eating them. The parable is presented as a Jungian exhortation to make peace with one's dark side. This Jungian interpretation is however incompatible with Catholic Orthodoxy which Gall promoted.
</p>
