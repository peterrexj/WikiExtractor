<p><b>Saint Alexius</b> <b>of Rome</b> or <b>Alexius of Edessa</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Ἀλέξιος</span>, <i>Alexios</i>), also <b>Alexis</b>, was a fourth-century <a href="/wiki/Greeks" title="Greeks">Greek</a> <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> who lived in anonymity and is known for his dedication to Christ. Two versions of his life exist, one in <a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a> and the other in Greek.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Syriac_version">Syriac version</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alexius_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Syriac version">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to Syriac tradition, St. Alexius was an Eastern <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> whose veneration was later transplanted to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>.  The relocation of the veneration to Rome was facilitated by the belief that the saint was a native of Rome and had died there.  This Roman connection stemmed from an earlier <a href="/wiki/Syriac_Christians" class="mw-redirect" title="Syriac Christians">Syriac</a> legend, which recounted that, during the episcopate of Bishop <a href="/wiki/Rabbula" title="Rabbula">Rabbula</a> (412–435), a "Man of God", who lived in <a href="/wiki/Edessa,_Mesopotamia" class="mw-redirect" title="Edessa, Mesopotamia">Edessa, Mesopotamia</a> as a beggar and shared the alms he received with other poor people, was found to be a native of Rome after his death.<sup id="cite_ref-newadvent.org_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-newadvent.org-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Greek_version">Greek version</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alexius_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Greek version">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Garcia_Fernandes_-_Casamento_de_Santo_Aleixo,_1541.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Garcia_Fernandes_-_Casamento_de_Santo_Aleixo%2C_1541.jpg/220px-Garcia_Fernandes_-_Casamento_de_Santo_Aleixo%2C_1541.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="295" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/31/Garcia_Fernandes_-_Casamento_de_Santo_Aleixo%2C_1541.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="224" data-file-height="300" /></a><figcaption><i>The Wedding of Saint Alexius</i>; <a href="/wiki/Garcia_Fernandes" title="Garcia Fernandes">Garcia Fernandes</a>, 1541.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The Greek version of his legend made Alexius the only son of Euphemianus, a wealthy Christian Roman of the senatorial class. Alexius fled his arranged marriage to follow his holy vocation. Disguised as a beggar, he lived near <a href="/wiki/Edessa,_Mesopotamia" class="mw-redirect" title="Edessa, Mesopotamia">Edessa</a> in <a href="/wiki/Syria" title="Syria">Syria</a>, accepting alms even from his own household slaves, who had been sent to look for him; they did not recognize him<sup id="cite_ref-magnificat_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-magnificat-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> until a miraculous icon of the <a href="/wiki/Mary,_the_mother_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Mary, the mother of Jesus">Blessed Virgin Mary</a> (later this image was called <a href="/wiki/Madonna_di_sant%27Alessio" title="Madonna di sant&#39;Alessio">Madonna of St Alexius</a>) singled him out as a <i>"<a href="/wiki/Man_of_God" title="Man of God">Man of God</a>"</i> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Ἄνθρωπος τοῦ Θεοῦ</span>).
</p><p>Fleeing the resultant notoriety, he returned to Rome, so changed that his parents did not recognize him, but as good Christians took him in and sheltered him for seventeen years, which he spent in a dark cubbyhole beneath the stairs, praying and teaching catechism to children.<sup id="cite_ref-magnificat_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-magnificat-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> After his death, his family found a note on his body which told them who he was and how he had lived his life of penance from the day of his wedding, for the love of God.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHolweck192450_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHolweck192450-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="French_version">French version</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alexius_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: French version">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The life of St Alexius is recounted in a French poem, <span title="French-language text"><i lang="fr">la Vie de saint Alexis</i></span>, believed to date from the early or mid-11th century (although the earliest manuscript was written in the 12th century).<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> This is regarded as one of the earliest works of French literature, and also one of the first poems in any romance language<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> to be written in the 10 or 11-syllable iambic line which later became the <a href="/wiki/Iambic_pentameter" title="Iambic pentameter">iambic pentameter</a>:
</p>
<dl><dd><span title="French-language text"><i lang="fr">A un des porz ki plus est pres de Rome,</i></span></dd>
<dd><span title="French-language text"><i lang="fr">iloec arivet la nef a cel saint home.</i></span></dd>
<dd>"At one of the ports which is most near to Rome,</dd>
<dd>there arrived the ship of this holy man"</dd></dl>
<p>The poem consists of 125 five-line stanzas. The story follows the Greek version recounted above.
</p>

<p>Alexius seems to have been completely unknown in the West prior to the end of the tenth century. Only from the end of the 10th century did his name begin to appear in any <a href="/wiki/Liturgical_book" title="Liturgical book">liturgical books</a> there.
</p><p>Since before the 8th century, there was on the <a href="/wiki/Aventine_Hill" title="Aventine Hill">Aventine Hill</a> in Rome a church that was dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Boniface_of_Tarsus" title="Boniface of Tarsus">St Boniface</a>. In 972, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_VII" title="Pope Benedict VII">Pope Benedict VII</a> transferred this almost abandoned church to the exiled Greek metropolitan, Sergius of Damascus. Sergius erected beside the church a monastery for Greek and Latin monks, soon made famous for the austere life of its inmates. St Alexius was added to the name of St Boniface as the titular saint of the church and monastery known as <a href="/wiki/Santi_Bonifacio_e_Alessio" class="mw-redirect" title="Santi Bonifacio e Alessio">Santi Bonifacio e Alessio</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-newadvent.org_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-newadvent.org-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It was evidently Sergius and his monks who brought to Rome the veneration of Saint Alexius. The Eastern saint, according to his legend a native of Rome, was soon very popular with the people of Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-newadvent.org_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-newadvent.org-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> This church, being associated with the legend, was considered to be built on the site of the home that Alexius returned to from Edessa.
</p><p>St Alexius is mentioned in the <i><a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a></i> under 17 July in the following terms: "At Rome, in a church on the Aventine Hill, a man of God is celebrated under the name of Alexius, who, as reported by tradition, abandoned his wealthy home, for the sake of becoming poor and to beg for alms unrecognized."<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>While the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> continues to recognize St Alexius as a saint, his feast was removed from the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> in 1969. The reason given was the legendary character of the written life of the saint.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Johann_Peter_Kirsch" title="Johann Peter Kirsch">Johann Peter Kirsch</a> remarked: "Perhaps the only basis for the story is the fact that a certain pious ascetic at Edessa lived the life of a beggar and was later venerated as a saint."<sup id="cite_ref-newadvent.org_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-newadvent.org-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Tridentine_Calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Tridentine Calendar">Tridentine Calendar</a> gave his feast day the rank of "Simple" but, by 1862, it had become a "Semidouble" and, in Rome itself, a "Double".<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> It was reduced again to the rank of "Simple" in 1955<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> and in 1960 became a "<a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(liturgy)" title="Commemoration (liturgy)">Commemoration</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> According to the rules in the present-day <a href="/wiki/Roman_Missal" title="Roman Missal">Roman Missal</a>, the saint may now be celebrated everywhere on his feast day with a "<a href="/wiki/Memorial_(liturgy)" title="Memorial (liturgy)">Memorial</a>", unless in some locality an obligatory celebration is assigned to that day.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> venerates St Alexius on 17 March.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Five <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Emperors" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine Emperors">Byzantine Emperors</a>, four <a href="/wiki/Empire_of_Trebizond" title="Empire of Trebizond">Emperors of Trebizond</a> and numerous other eastern European and Russian personalities have borne his name (see <a href="/wiki/Alexius" title="Alexius">Alexius</a>). There are numerous churches bearing his name in Greece, Russia, and in other Orthodox countries. Saint Alexius is well known to the region of the north <a href="/wiki/Peloponnese" title="Peloponnese">Peloponnese</a> due to his skull being kept in the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Agia_Lavra" title="Agia Lavra">Agia Lavra</a>. Churches dedicated to St. Alexius are also found in <a href="/wiki/Aigio" title="Aigio">Aigio</a> and <a href="/wiki/Patras" title="Patras">Patras</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Relics">Relics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Alexius_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Relics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Relics of Saint Alexius are found in some churches and monasteries in Greece, including the <a href="/wiki/Esphigmenou" title="Esphigmenou">Esphigmenou</a> monastery of <a href="/wiki/Mount_Athos" title="Mount Athos">Mount Athos</a> and the Dormition of Theotokos Monastery, <a href="/wiki/Boeotia" title="Boeotia">Boeotia</a>. In Russia, relics of St Alexius are kept in the <a href="/wiki/Alexander_Nevsky_Lavra" title="Alexander Nevsky Lavra">Alexander Nevsky Lavra</a> in Saint Petersburg. In Cyprus, relics are kept in the <a href="/wiki/Kykkos_Monastery" title="Kykkos Monastery">Kykkos Monastery</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The most precious relic is a large part of the honorable skull of the Saint, which is kept in the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Agia_Lavra" title="Agia Lavra">Agia Lavra</a> near <a href="/wiki/Kalavrita" class="mw-redirect" title="Kalavrita">Kalavrita</a>, <a href="/wiki/Greece" title="Greece">Greece</a>. According to the Ktetorikon (monastic foundation codex) of the monastery, the honorable skull was donated to the monastery by the Byzantine emperor <a href="/wiki/Manuel_II_Palaiologos" title="Manuel II Palaiologos">Manuel II Palaiologos</a> in 1398 (this is also the inscription on the reliquary).<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<li>St Alexius is also the <a href="/wiki/Patron_Saint" class="mw-redirect" title="Patron Saint">Patron Saint</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Religious_institute" title="Religious institute">religious institute</a> known as the <a href="/wiki/Alexians" title="Alexians">Alexians</a> and of the Greek town of <a href="/wiki/Kalavryta" title="Kalavryta">Kalavryta</a>.</li>
<li>The tale of St Alexius has parallels with that of <a href="/wiki/Parable_of_the_Prodigal_Son" title="Parable of the Prodigal Son">The Prodigal Son</a>, as told in the biblical <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Luke" class="mw-redirect" title="Book of Luke">Book of Luke</a>. As it appears in <a href="/wiki/Golden_Legend" title="Golden Legend"><i>Legenda aurea</i></a><sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> (later retold in the <i><a href="/wiki/Gesta_Romanorum" title="Gesta Romanorum">Gesta Romanorum</a></i><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>).</li></ul>
