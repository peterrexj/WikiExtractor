<p><b>Kassia</b>, <b>Cassia</b> or <b>Kassiani</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Κασσιανή</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek" title="Romanization of Greek">romanized</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Greek-language romanization"><i lang="el-Latn">Kassianí</i></span>, <small>pronounced&#160;</small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="el-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Greek" title="Help:IPA/Greek">[kasia'ni]</a></span>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;810</span> – before 865) was a <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine</a>-Greek <a href="/wiki/Composer" title="Composer">composer</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hymnographer" class="mw-redirect" title="Hymnographer">hymnographer</a> and poet.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> She holds a unique place in <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_music" title="Byzantine music">Byzantine music</a> as the only known woman whose music appears in the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_liturgy" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine liturgy">Byzantine liturgy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020141_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020141-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Approximately fifty of her hymns are extant, most of which are <a href="/wiki/Sticheron" title="Sticheron">stichera</a>, though at least 26 have uncertain attribution.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The authenticity issues are due to many hymns being anonymous, and others ascribed to different authors in different manuscripts. She was an <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> of a <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a> in the west of <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>.
</p><p>Additionally, many <a href="/wiki/Epigram" title="Epigram">epigrams</a> and <a href="/wiki/Gnomic_poetry" title="Gnomic poetry">gnomic verses</a> are attributed to her,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> at least 261.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020149_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020149-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Kassia is notable as one of at least two women in the middle Byzantine period known to have written in their own names, the other being <a href="/wiki/Anna_Comnena" class="mw-redirect" title="Anna Comnena">Anna Comnena</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Byzantine_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Byzantine-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Like her predecessors <a href="/wiki/Romanos_the_Melodist" title="Romanos the Melodist">Romanos the Melodist</a> and <a href="/wiki/Andrew_of_Crete" title="Andrew of Crete">Andrew of Crete</a>, the earliest surviving manuscripts of her works are dated centuries after her lifetime.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas202024–25_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas202024–25-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Name">Name</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Name">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Her name is a feminine Greek form of the Latin name Cassius. It is variously spelled Κασσιανή (contemporary pronunciation <small></small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="el-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Greek" title="Help:IPA/Greek">[kaˈsçani]</a></span>), Κασ(σ)ία (<i>Kas[s]ia</i>), Εικασία (<i>Eikasia</i>), Ικασία (<i>Ikasia</i>), <i>Kassiani</i>, <i>Cas[s]ia</i>, <i>Cassiane</i>, <i>Kassiana</i>.  Modern English-language references to her as a composer generally use the name "Kassia," while references to her religious life tend to use Kassia or Kassiani.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos-Banker198465_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos-Banker198465-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:262px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Emperor_Theophilos_chooses_his_wife.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Emperor_Theophilos_chooses_his_wife.jpg/260px-Emperor_Theophilos_chooses_his_wife.jpg" decoding="async" width="260" height="188" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Emperor_Theophilos_chooses_his_wife.jpg/390px-Emperor_Theophilos_chooses_his_wife.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Emperor_Theophilos_chooses_his_wife.jpg/520px-Emperor_Theophilos_chooses_his_wife.jpg 2x" data-file-width="752" data-file-height="544" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Emperor_Theophilos_chooses_his_wife.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>19th century representation by <a href="/wiki/Valentine_Cameron_Prinsep" title="Valentine Cameron Prinsep">Valentine Cameron Prinsep</a> of <a href="/wiki/Theophilos_(emperor)" title="Theophilos (emperor)">Theophilos</a>' choice</div></div></div>
<p>Kassia was born between 805 and 810 in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> into a wealthy family<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> and grew to be exceptionally beautiful and intelligent. Three Byzantine chroniclers, <a href="/wiki/Symeon_Metaphrastes" class="mw-redirect" title="Symeon Metaphrastes">Pseudo-Symeon the Logothete</a>, <a href="/wiki/George_the_Monk" class="mw-redirect" title="George the Monk">George the Monk</a> (a.k.a. George the Sinner) and <a href="/wiki/Leo_the_Mathematician" title="Leo the Mathematician">Leo the Grammarian</a>, claim that she was a participant in the "<a href="/wiki/Bride_show" class="mw-redirect" title="Bride show">bride show</a>" (the means by which Byzantine princes/emperors sometimes chose a bride, by giving a golden apple to his choice) organized for the young bachelor <a href="/wiki/Theophilos_(emperor)" title="Theophilos (emperor)">Theophilos</a> by his stepmother, the Empress Dowager <a href="/wiki/Euphrosyne_(9th_century)" title="Euphrosyne (9th century)">Euphrosyne</a>. Smitten by Kassia's beauty, the young emperor approached her and said: "Through a woman [came forth] the baser [things]," referring to the sin and suffering coming as a result of <a href="/wiki/Eve" title="Eve">Eve</a>'s transgression.  Kassia promptly responded, "And through a woman [came forth] the better [things]," referring to the hope of salvation resulting from the <a href="/wiki/Incarnation_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Incarnation of Christ">Incarnation of Christ</a> through the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a>.   According to tradition, the <a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/verbatim" class="extiw" title="wikt:verbatim">verbatim</a> dialogue was:
</p>
<blockquote><p>"-<span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Ἐκ γυναικὸς τὰ χείρω</span></span>." <small><a href="/wiki/Medieval_Greek" title="Medieval Greek">Medieval Greek</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="grc-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Greek" title="Help:IPA/Greek">[ek ʝyne'kos ta 'çiro]</a></span><br />"-<span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Kαὶ ἐκ γυναικὸς τὰ κρείττω</span></span>." <small><a href="/wiki/Medieval_Greek" title="Medieval Greek">Medieval Greek</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="grc-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Greek" title="Help:IPA/Greek">[c(e) ek ʝyneˈkos ta ˈkrito]</a></span></p></blockquote>
<p>Unsatisfied with her response, Theophilos rejected her and chose <a href="/wiki/Theodora_(9th_century)" class="mw-redirect" title="Theodora (9th century)">Theodora</a> as his wife.
</p><p>When next we hear of Kassia in 843 she had founded a <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a> in the west of <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>, near the Constantinian Walls, and became its first <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Although many scholars<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="margin-left:0.1em; white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Avoid_weasel_words" class="mw-redirect" title="Wikipedia:Avoid weasel words"><span title="Who says this? (September 2020)">weasel&#160;words</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> attribute this to bitterness at having failed to marry Theophilos and to become Empress, a letter from <a href="/wiki/Theodore_the_Studite" title="Theodore the Studite">Theodore the Studite</a> indicates that she had other motivations for wanting a monastic life. It had a close relationship with the nearby monastery of <a href="/wiki/Stoudios" class="mw-redirect" title="Stoudios">Stoudios</a>, which was to play a central role in re-editing the Byzantine liturgical books in the 9th and 10th centuries, thus ensuring the survival of her work (Kurt Sherry, p.&#160;56). However, since the monastic life was a common vocation in her day, religious zeal is as likely a motive as either depression or aspiration for artistic renown.<sup id="cite_ref-lsm_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lsm-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Emperor Theophilos was a fierce <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm" title="Byzantine Iconoclasm">iconoclast</a>, and any residual feelings he may have had for Kassia did not preserve her from the imperial policy of <a href="/wiki/Religious_persecution" title="Religious persecution">persecution</a> for her defence of the <a href="/wiki/Veneration" title="Veneration">veneration</a> of icons. Among other things, she was subjected to <a href="/wiki/Scourging" class="mw-redirect" title="Scourging">scourging</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Whip" title="Whip">lash</a>. In spite of this, she remained outspoken in defence of the Orthodox Faith, at one point saying, "I hate silence when it is time to speak."<sup id="cite_ref-lsm_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lsm-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After the death of Theophilos in 842 his young son <a href="/wiki/Michael_III" title="Michael III">Michael III</a> became Eastern Roman Emperor, with Empress Theodora acting as <a href="/wiki/Regent" title="Regent">Regent</a>. Together they ended the second iconoclastic period (814-842); peace was restored to the empire.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Kassia traveled to Italy briefly, but eventually settled on the Greek Island of <a href="/wiki/Kasos" title="Kasos">Kasos</a> where she died sometime between 867 and 890. In the city of Panaghia, there is a church where Kassia's tomb/reliquary may be found <sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Overview">Overview</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Overview">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Kassiani wrote many hymns which are still used in the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Rite" title="Byzantine Rite">Byzantine</a> <a href="/wiki/Christian_liturgy" title="Christian liturgy">liturgy</a> to this day. She became known to the great <a href="/wiki/Theodore_the_Studite" title="Theodore the Studite">Theodore the Studite</a>, while she was still a young girl, and he was impressed by her learning and literary style.<sup id="cite_ref-lsm_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lsm-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> She not only wrote spiritual poetry, but composed music to accompany it. She is regarded as an "exceptional and rare phenomenon" among composers of her day<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> At least twenty-three genuine hymns are ascribed to her.<sup id="cite_ref-lsm_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lsm-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Her <i>oeuvre</i> as a whole demonstrates influence from classical <a href="/wiki/Greek_literature" title="Greek literature">Greek literature</a>, which she studied early in life.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Sacred_music">Sacred music</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Sacred music">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Hymn_of_Kassia">Hymn of Kassia</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Hymn of Kassia">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The most famous of her compositions is the eponymous <b>Hymn of Kassia</b> (also known as the <i><a href="/wiki/Troparion" title="Troparion">Troparion</a> of Kassiani</i>), which is chanted each year at <a href="/wiki/Orthros" title="Orthros">matins</a> on <a href="/wiki/Holy_Wednesday" title="Holy Wednesday">Holy Wednesday</a> (which in usual parish practice is sung Tuesday evening) at the end of the <a href="/wiki/Aposticha" title="Aposticha">aposticha</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Tradition says that in his later years the Emperor Theophilus, still in love with her, wished to see her one more time before he died, so he rode to the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> where she resided. Kassia was alone in her cell, writing her <i>Hymn</i> when she realized that the commotion she heard was because the imperial retinue had arrived.  She was still in love with him but was now devoted to God and hid away because she did not want to let her old passion overcome her <a href="/wiki/Religious_vows#In_the_Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Religious vows">monastic vow</a>.  She left the unfinished hymn on the table. Theophilus found her cell and entered it alone. He looked for her but she was not there; she was hiding in a closet, watching him. Theophilus, overcome with sadness, cried and regretted that moment of pride when he rejected such a beautiful and intellectual woman; then he noticed the papers on the table and read them.  When he had finished reading, he sat and added one line to the hymn; then he left. The line attributed to the Emperor is the line "those feet whose sound Eve heard at dusk in Paradise and hid herself for fear." Legend says that as he was leaving he noticed Kassia in the closet but did not speak to her, out of respect for her wished privacy. Kassia emerged when the emperor was gone, read what he had written and finished the hymn.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>The music for the hymn is slow, sorrowful and plaintive, lasting about ten to twenty minutes, depending on tempo and style of execution.  It requires a very wide vocal range, and is considered one of the most demanding pieces — if not <i>the</i> most demanding one — of solo Byzantine chant, and cantors take great pride in delivering it well.  It is also sung by choirs in unison, often underpinned by Byzantine vocal bass drone.  The faithful make a point of going to church specifically "to listen to Kassiani" that evening. In many places in Greece, the Bridegroom Matins service of Great Tuesday is popular with <a href="/wiki/Sex_workers" class="mw-redirect" title="Sex workers">sex workers</a>, who may not often be seen in church at other times of the year. They come in great numbers, in order to hear the Hymn of Kassiani, as the hymn is associated with the <i>woman fallen in many sins</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Secular_writings">Secular writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Secular writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>261 works of secular literature are attributed to Kassia, consisting of <a href="/wiki/Epigram" title="Epigram">epigrams</a> and <a href="/wiki/Gnomic_poetry" title="Gnomic poetry">gnomic verses</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Never set to music, they were written during the reigns of Theophilos and Michael III.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Akin to the rest of her <i>oeuvre</i>, her writings demonstrate influence from classical <a href="/wiki/Greek_literature" title="Greek literature">Greek literature</a>, which she studied early in life.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos2001-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> These literary genres were chiefly dominated by men, so her contributions would have been unusual; Byzantinist Andrew Mellas noted that "perhaps this was an advantage for Kassia and accounts for her originality".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The genre of epigrams had existed from <a href="/wiki/Classical_antiquity" title="Classical antiquity">classical antiquity</a> and into the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_empire" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine empire">Byzantine empire</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> being defined as somewhere in between the scope of <a href="/wiki/Lyric_poetry" title="Lyric poetry">lyric</a> and <a href="/wiki/Epic_poetry" title="Epic poetry">epic poetry</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962_13-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Their exact organization was unformalized, ranging from the use of <a href="/wiki/Couplets" class="mw-redirect" title="Couplets">couplets</a> to <a href="/wiki/Iambic_pentameter" title="Iambic pentameter">iambic pentameter</a>, among others.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962_13-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The works are generally rooted in the long tradition,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962_13-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> though they include her own unique innovations.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKazhdan2005_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKazhdan2005-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> In this genre, she is credited with first synthesizing secular and sacred values.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> While the works are secular in character, they promoted ethical teachings for the lives of Christians.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Byzantinist <a href="/w/index.php?title=Marc_Lauxtermann&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Marc Lauxtermann (page does not exist)">Marc Lauxtermann</a><span class="noprint" style="font-size:85%; font-style: normal;">&#160;&#91;<a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Lauxtermann" class="extiw" title="de:Marc Lauxtermann">de</a>&#93;</span> described this approach as "An osmosis of ancient wisdom and monastic truth".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Kassia abandoned typical themes found in Greek <a href="/wiki/Prosody_(Greek)" title="Prosody (Greek)">prosody</a>, preferring to discuss more individual and personal issues.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKazhdan2005_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKazhdan2005-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> In addition to ethnical ideals, the themes Kassia covers include ethnical weaknesses (her examples include 'foolishness' and 'friendship');<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKazhdan2005_14-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKazhdan2005-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> social structures;<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020143_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020143-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> femininity (particularly 'beauty');<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKazhdan2005_14-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKazhdan2005-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> and the defense of women's rights.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrashier201213_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrashier201213-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> This approach would have made the genre more relevant and accessible to the Byzantine people,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020142-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> though the extent of her epigrams' circulation remains unclear.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020143_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020143-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Her epigrams are particularly reminiscent of similar works by her colleague Theodore the Studite, and are characterized by a concise and straight forward presentation,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962_13-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> often including witty or humorous elements.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrashier201213_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrashier201213-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> According to Mellas, it is probable that they were "a form <a href="/wiki/Catechism" title="Catechism">Catechism</a> for her nuns and perhaps any friends of the monastery."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas2020143_15-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas2020143-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Kassia's gnomic verses were much shorter, usually a single-verse teachings. They often include moral adages and like the epigrams, were succinct.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962_13-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouliatos19962-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Portrayals">Portrayals</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Portrayals">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Many fictitious stories were created around her relationship with Emperor Theofilos. Contemporary historians such as Glykas, Ptochoprodromos, Kodinos, Zonaras, and Georgios Amartolos described her exile in Italy during the Iconoclasy wars and later settled and died in Kasos sometime between 867 and 890.
</p><p>She is portrayed by <a href="/wiki/Karima_Adebibe" title="Karima Adebibe">Karima McAdams</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Vikings_(season_5)" title="Vikings (season 5)">5th season</a> of the television series <i><a href="/wiki/Vikings_(2013_TV_series)" class="mw-redirect" title="Vikings (2013 TV series)">Vikings</a></i>.
</p><p>In August 2019, English singer-songwriter <a href="/wiki/Frank_Turner" title="Frank Turner">Frank Turner</a> included a song told from her point of view on his album <a href="/wiki/No_Man%27s_Land_(Frank_Turner_album)" title="No Man&#39;s Land (Frank Turner album)"><i>No Man's Land</i></a>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_works">Other works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Other works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Among the other hymns she composed are the following:
</p>
<ul><li>The <a href="/wiki/Doxastichon" class="mw-redirect" title="Doxastichon">Doxastichon</a> chanted at the <a href="/wiki/Vespers" title="Vespers">Vesperal</a> <a href="/wiki/Divine_Liturgy" title="Divine Liturgy">Divine Liturgy</a> on <a href="/wiki/Christmas_Eve" title="Christmas Eve">Christmas Eve</a></li>
<li>Numerous hymns in honor of <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saints</a> found in the <a href="/wiki/Menaion" title="Menaion">Menaion</a> (fixed cycle of the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_liturgical_calendar" title="Eastern Orthodox liturgical calendar">Eastern Orthodox liturgical calendar</a>), such as <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">Feast</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Nativity_of_the_Forerunner" class="mw-redirect" title="Nativity of the Forerunner">Nativity of the Forerunner</a>, 24 June.</li>
<li>Among her hymns in the <a href="/wiki/Triodion" title="Triodion">Triodion</a> (liturgical book used during <a href="/wiki/Great_Lent" title="Great Lent">Great Lent</a>) are the <a href="/wiki/Irmos" title="Irmos">irmoi</a> for the <a href="/wiki/Matins" title="Matins">Matins</a> Canon of <a href="/wiki/Great_Thursday" class="mw-redirect" title="Great Thursday">Great Thursday</a>.</li>
<li>Her longest composition is a <a href="/wiki/Canon_(hymnography)" title="Canon (hymnography)">Canon</a> for the <a href="/wiki/Christian_burial" title="Christian burial">Departed</a>, consisting of 32 <a href="/wiki/Strophe" title="Strophe">strophes</a>, to be chanted at a <a href="/wiki/Memorial_service_(Orthodox)" class="mw-redirect" title="Memorial service (Orthodox)">Parastas</a> (memorial services).</li></ul>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Religious_commemoration">Religious commemoration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kassia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Religious commemoration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> of Saint Kassiani is celebrated by the Orthodox Church on <a href="/wiki/7_September" class="mw-redirect" title="7 September">7 September</a>.
</p><p>She is often depicted on the <a href="/wiki/Icon" title="Icon">icon</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Sunday_of_Orthodoxy" class="mw-redirect" title="Sunday of Orthodoxy">Sunday of Orthodoxy</a> (the First Sunday of Great Lent), because of her strong defence of the <a href="/wiki/Iconodule" class="mw-redirect" title="Iconodule">veneration of icons</a>.
</p><p>In 2022, Kassia was officially added to the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church liturgical calendar</a> with a feast day on 7 September.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
