<p><b>Dominic Barberi</b>, CP (22 June 1792 – 27 August 1849) was an <a href="/wiki/Italians" title="Italians">Italian</a> theologian and <a href="/wiki/Passionist" class="mw-redirect" title="Passionist">Passionist</a> priest who was prominent in spreading <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_England_and_Wales" title="Catholic Church in England and Wales">Catholicism in England</a>. He contributed to the conversion of <a href="/wiki/John_Henry_Newman" title="John Henry Newman">John Henry Newman</a>. In 1963, he was beatified by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI" title="Pope Paul VI">Pope Paul VI</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Birth_and_early_life">Birth and early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dominic_Barberi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Birth and early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Barberi was born <b>Domenico Giovanni Barberi</b> near <a href="/wiki/Viterbo" title="Viterbo">Viterbo</a> to a poor family of Italian farmers in 1792. His parents died in his early childhood and he was raised by his maternal uncle, Bartolomeo Pacelli. As a boy he was employed to take care of sheep. An elderly <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Friars_Minor_Capuchin" title="Order of Friars Minor Capuchin">Capuchin</a> priest taught him to read. Although he read all the books he could obtain, he had no regular education.
</p><p>When Napoleon suppressed the religious communities in the Papal States, Barberi became acquainted with several Passionists living in exile near his town. Barberi befriended these Passionists and served daily <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">Mass</a> with them.
</p><p>When Barberi was one of the few men in his locality not chosen for military conscription, he felt it was a sign from God that he should enter a religious community. Barberi believed that he was called to preach the Gospel in far-off lands. He would later affirm that he had received a specific call to preach to the people of England. <a href="/wiki/Paul_of_the_Cross" title="Paul of the Cross">Paul of the Cross</a>, founder of the Passionist Congregation, also had great enthusiasm for converting England.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Passionists">Passionists</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dominic_Barberi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Passionists">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Barberi was received into the <a href="/wiki/Congregation_of_the_Passion" class="mw-redirect" title="Congregation of the Passion">Congregation of the Passion</a> in 1814 after the re-establishment of the religious orders in the <a href="/wiki/Papal_States" title="Papal States">Papal States</a>. Initially Barberi served as a lay brother, but once his extraordinary gifts were revealed his status was changed to that of a clerical novice, in an extraordinary break with custom.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson1967123_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson1967123-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> During his studies Barberi's brilliance was an example to his fellow students, although he would often take steps to play down his intelligence. He was ordained a priest on 1 March 1818.
</p><p>After completing the regular course of studies, Barberi taught philosophy and theology to the students of the congregation as lector for a period of ten years, first in Sant'Angelo and then in Rome. He now lived at the monastery of Saints John and Paul on the Celian Hill. During this period he produced many theological and philosophical works. In the summer of 1830 he was asked to aid an English convert to Catholicism, Sir <a href="/wiki/Sir_Harry_Trelawny,_7th_Baronet" title="Sir Harry Trelawny, 7th Baronet">Henry Trelawney</a>, with regard to the <a href="/wiki/Rubric" title="Rubric">rubrics</a> of the Mass. Through this meeting Barberi made the acquaintance of <a href="/wiki/Ignatius_Spencer" title="Ignatius Spencer">Ignatius Spencer</a> and influential English Catholics such as <a href="/wiki/Ambrose_Lisle_March_Phillipps_De_Lisle" title="Ambrose Lisle March Phillipps De Lisle">Ambrose Phillips</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson1967123_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson1967123-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> This was to be the first step in a journey which would eventually bring Barberi to England. Through his continued correspondence with these persons, Barberi's hopes for England's conversion were kept alive.
</p><p>Barberi at that time held in Italy the offices of rector, provincial consultor, and provincial, and fulfilled the duties of these positions with ability. At the same time he constantly gave missions and retreats, always mindful of his hopes to travel and preach in England. In 1830, when he was thirty-eight, Barberi was chosen to establish a new monastery at <a href="/wiki/Lucca" title="Lucca">Lucca</a>. In 1839 the Passionist General Chapter met and discussed the possibility of making a foundation in England. Finally in January 1840, negotiations were completed with regard to a Passionist foundation at Ere, near Tournai, in <a href="/wiki/Belgium" title="Belgium">Belgium</a>, the superiors, mindful of Barberi's singular vocation to England, in spite of his age and ill health, sent Barberi to be superior of the Belgian mission.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Foundations_in_Belgium_and_England">Foundations in Belgium and England</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dominic_Barberi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Foundations in Belgium and England">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The first Passionist Retreat in Belgium was founded at Ere in June 1840.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson1967210_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson1967210-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> On arrival in Belgium the local bishop was so unimpressed with Barberi's plebeian appearance that he was subjected to intense examination in moral theology before being allowed to hear confessions. Life in Belgium posed plenty of problems for the Passionists; one of the Brothers had fallen ill, the community was in abject poverty, and Barberi had few words of French. Barberi's spirit rose to the occasion and soon the community was flourishing and even Barberi enjoyed good health.
</p><p>In September Barberi received a letter from Bishop <a href="/wiki/Nicholas_Wiseman" title="Nicholas Wiseman">Wiseman</a>, the head of the English mission, inviting Barberi to start a Passionist foundation in England at Aston Hall. Barberi, with the permission of the Passionist General, visited the site in November 1840, though <a href="/wiki/Ignatius_Spencer" title="Ignatius Spencer">Ignatius Spencer</a> warned Barberi that the situation in England would mean this would not be a favourable time to make such a foundation. Barberi set out for England once more in October 1841, where he was greeted with stares and suspicion, not only as a Catholic priest, but for the strange garb of the Passionist habit. J. Brodrick SJ, in his work on the "Second Spring" of Catholicism in England, says of Barberi's arrival, "The second spring did not begin when <a href="/wiki/John_Henry_Newman" title="John Henry Newman">Newman</a> was converted nor when the hierarchy was restored. It began on a bleak October day of 1841, when a little Italian priest in comical attire shuffled down a ship's gangway at <a href="/wiki/Folkestone" title="Folkestone">Folkestone</a>."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson1967233_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson1967233-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After many months of waiting at <a href="/wiki/Oscott_College" class="mw-redirect" title="Oscott College">Oscott College</a>, Barberi finally secured possession of Aston Hall in <a href="/wiki/Staffordshire" title="Staffordshire">Staffordshire</a> in February 1842, after 28 years of effort, thereby establishing the Passionists in <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a>. The initial reception of Barberi and his fellow Passionists was less than welcoming. Local Catholics feared the arrival of these newcomers would cause renewed persecutions. Barberi was also met with ridicule: his attempts to read prayers in English were met with the laughter of his congregation. However, the community increased in numbers. As the people of Aston grew to know Barberi, they became enamoured of him and Barberi soon began to receive a steady stream of converts.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson196310_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson196310-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> A centre was also set up in neighbouring Stone, where Barberi would also say Mass and preach to the local populace.
</p><p>Opposition to Barberi was also present in Stone, where on his journeys to the Mass centre, local youths would throw stones at him, though two youths took to the decision to become Catholics when they were greatly edified to see Barberi kiss each stone that hit him and place it in his pocket. During many of these frequent attacks, Barberi barely escaped death. Local Protestant ministers often held anti-Catholic lectures and sermons to ward the people away from Barberi and the Catholics. Wilson records how one of these ministers followed Barberi along a street shouting out various arguments against <a href="/wiki/Transubstantiation" title="Transubstantiation">transubstantiation</a>, Barberi was silent, but as the man was about to turn off, Barberi retorted: "Jesus Christ said over the consecrated elements, "This is my body." You say, "No. It is not his body!" Who then am I to believe? I prefer to believe Jesus Christ."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson1967256._6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson1967256.-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Converts increased at Stone, so much so that a new church had to be built. It was at Aston, however, that on 10 June 1844 the first <a href="/wiki/Corpus_Christi_(feast)" class="mw-redirect" title="Corpus Christi (feast)">Corpus Christi</a> procession was held in the British Isles, an event which attracted thousands of Catholics and Protestants alike.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson1967259_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson1967259-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Barberi then began to visit other parishes and religious communities in order to preach, such "missions", as they were called, causing Barberi's reputation to become widely known. They frequently took place in the industrial cities of northern England, such as <a href="/wiki/Manchester" title="Manchester">Manchester</a>, <a href="/wiki/Liverpool" title="Liverpool">Liverpool</a> and <a href="/wiki/Birmingham" title="Birmingham">Birmingham</a>.
</p>
<h2><span id="Newman.27s_conversion"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Newman's_conversion">Newman's conversion</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dominic_Barberi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Newman&#039;s conversion">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Newmans_College_Littlemore_11_Feb_2007.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f3/Newmans_College_Littlemore_11_Feb_2007.JPG/180px-Newmans_College_Littlemore_11_Feb_2007.JPG" decoding="async" width="180" height="135" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f3/Newmans_College_Littlemore_11_Feb_2007.JPG/270px-Newmans_College_Littlemore_11_Feb_2007.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f3/Newmans_College_Littlemore_11_Feb_2007.JPG/360px-Newmans_College_Littlemore_11_Feb_2007.JPG 2x" data-file-width="2048" data-file-height="1536" /></a><figcaption>The College, Littlemore where Barberi received Newman into the Church</figcaption></figure>
<p>While in Italy and later in Belgium, Barberi had always kept up a keen interest in the <a href="/wiki/Oxford_Movement" title="Oxford Movement">Oxford Movement</a>. In 1841 a letter by <a href="/wiki/John_Dobree_Dalgairns" title="John Dobree Dalgairns">John Dobree Dalgairns</a> appeared in <i><a href="/wiki/L%27Univers" title="L&#39;Univers">L'Univers</a></i> explaining the position of the <a href="/wiki/Anglican" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican">Anglican</a> <a href="/wiki/High_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="High Church">High Church</a> party. Barberi decided to respond to this letter in the mistaken belief that it represented the views of the entire faculty of <a href="/wiki/Oxford_University" class="mw-redirect" title="Oxford University">Oxford University</a>. (Dalgairns was an undergraduate when he wrote the letter.) Barberi's "Letter to University Professors at Oxford"<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> describes his long hopes for the conversion of England and his belief that the men of Oxford would be instrumental in such a conversion. The letter, through the help of <a href="/wiki/Ignatius_Spencer" title="Ignatius Spencer">Ignatius Spencer</a>, eventually ended up in the hands of Dalgairns, who was residing with <a href="/wiki/John_Henry_Newman" title="John Henry Newman">John Henry Newman</a> at <a href="/wiki/Littlemore" title="Littlemore">Littlemore</a>. Barberi repudiated the Anglican claim that the <a href="/wiki/39_Articles" class="mw-redirect" title="39 Articles">39 Articles</a> could be interpreted in a Catholic light. In their continued correspondence Dalgairns and Barberi debated the Catholic position and Dalgairns requested copies of the Passionist Rule and Dominic's 'The Lament of England'. Eventually Dalgairns was received into the Catholic Church by Barberi at Aston in September 1845.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson1967301_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson1967301-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In October of that same year Barberi visited Littlemore, where Newman made his confession to him.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson196311_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson196311-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Newman relates in his "Apologia" how Barberi arrived soaked from the rain and as was drying himself by the fire when Newman knelt and asked to be received into the Catholic Church.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> This event is marked by a sculpture in the Catholic Church of Blessed Dominic Barberi at Littlemore. Two of Newman's companions at Littlemore were also received, and Barberi celebrated Mass for them the following morning.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Further_work_and_death">Further work and death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dominic_Barberi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Further work and death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Blesseddominic.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/65/Blesseddominic.JPG/180px-Blesseddominic.JPG" decoding="async" width="180" height="485" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/65/Blesseddominic.JPG 1.5x" data-file-width="213" data-file-height="574" /></a><figcaption>Window from Blessed Dominic's Shrine</figcaption></figure>
<p>The community at Aston had gained 15 religious members. In 1846 a new foundation was made at <a href="/wiki/Woodchester" title="Woodchester">Woodchester</a> in <a href="/wiki/Gloucestershire" title="Gloucestershire">Gloucestershire</a> and in 1848 the Passionists arrived in London. In the last years of his life, Barberi engaged in negotiations for the foundation of St Anne's Retreat, Sutton, where today he lies buried. In 1847 <a href="/wiki/Ignatius_Spencer" title="Ignatius Spencer">George Spencer</a>, Barberi's longstanding friend, was received into the Congregation of the Passion.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson1967339_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson1967339-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Throughout this time, Barberi fulfilled his duties in preaching missions and heading the English and Belgian foundations.
</p><p>One story told of Barberi during this time exemplifies a sense of humour. While he was visiting a convent of nuns who were instructing many converts, some of them male, Barberi was informed that some of the sisters were worried about teaching men.  Barberi retorted, "Have no fear, Sisters. You are all too old and too ugly."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilson1967348_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson1967348-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The Sisters appreciated Barberi's humour so much that they recorded the incident in their archives.
</p><p>Such work inevitably took its toll upon Barberi's health and from 1847 he insisted that his life had nearly run its course. He had preached numerous retreats, both alone and with Spencer, both in England and Ireland. 
On 27 August 1849, Barberi was travelling from <a href="/wiki/Paddington" title="Paddington">Paddington, London</a> to <a href="/wiki/Woodchester" title="Woodchester">Woodchester</a>, when at <a href="/wiki/Pangbourne" title="Pangbourne">Pangbourne</a> he suffered a heart attack. Because in London "the <a href="/wiki/Cholera" title="Cholera">cholera</a> was then prevalent", there were no hotels that would take him in, and so it was decided to bring him back at <a href="/wiki/Reading,_Berkshire" title="Reading, Berkshire">Reading</a><sup id="cite_ref-Apostle_of_England,_book_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Apostle_of_England,_book-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> (in the <i>Railway Tavern</i>, later the Duke of Edinburgh Hotel, now demolished). The up-train arrived more than hour later, while "he remained in great agony but in peace and with 'Thy will be done' ever on his lips".<sup id="cite_ref-Apostle_of_England,_book_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Apostle_of_England,_book-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-compare_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-compare-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Barberi is buried in St. Anne and Blessed Dominic Church, Sutton, Merseyside, which is also the shrine of <a href="/wiki/Elizabeth_Prout" title="Elizabeth Prout">Elizabeth Prout</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ignatius_Spencer" title="Ignatius Spencer">Ignatius Spencer</a>. During the Mass celebrated at the "Blessed Dominic shrine Church of St Anne and Blessed Dominic" two months before the 50th anniversary of the beatification, Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Bernard_Longley" title="Bernard Longley">Bernard Longley</a> explained why he believed "Blessed Dominic" was an <a href="/wiki/Ius_patronatus" class="mw-redirect" title="Ius patronatus">ideal patron</a> for the Year of Faith, which ran until 24 November.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholicherald.co.uk,_27_Ago_2013_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholicherald.co.uk,_27_Ago_2013-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Barberi was also been claimed patron of the Year of Faith for the Archdiocese of Birmingham.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholicherald.co.uk,_27_Ago_2013_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholicherald.co.uk,_27_Ago_2013-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Beatification_and_legacy">Beatification and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dominic_Barberi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Beatification and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Barberi was beatified by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI" title="Pope Paul VI">Pope Paul VI</a> in 1963, during the <a href="/wiki/Second_Vatican_Council" title="Second Vatican Council">Second Vatican Council</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-pofc_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pofc-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Barberi is best remembered for his part in Newman's conversion, but is also commemorated for his work in the efforts to return England to the Catholic faith in the 19th century. In his years in England Barberi established three churches and several chapels, preached innumerable missions and received hundreds of converts, not only Newman, but others such as Spencer and Dalgairns.<sup id="cite_ref-pofc_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pofc-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A relic of Barberi was given to Monsignor <a href="/wiki/Keith_Newton_(prelate)" title="Keith Newton (prelate)">Keith Newton</a>, Ordinary of the <a href="/wiki/Personal_Ordinariate_of_Our_Lady_of_Walsingham" title="Personal Ordinariate of Our Lady of Walsingham">Personal Ordinariate of Our Lady of Walsingham</a>, for the ordinariate to be kept at Our Lady of the Assumption Church, Warwick Street.<sup id="cite_ref-Ordinariate_given_relic_of_Bl._Dominic_Barberi_-_02_March_2013_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ordinariate_given_relic_of_Bl._Dominic_Barberi_-_02_March_2013-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Literary_works">Literary works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Dominic_Barberi&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Literary works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Blesseddom2.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Blesseddom2.jpg/160px-Blesseddom2.jpg" decoding="async" width="160" height="120" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Blesseddom2.jpg/240px-Blesseddom2.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Blesseddom2.jpg/320px-Blesseddom2.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2560" data-file-height="1920" /></a><figcaption>The shrine of Blessed Dominic Barberi</figcaption></figure>
<p>Among Barberi's works are courses of philosophy and moral theology; a volume on the Passion of Our Lord; a work for nuns on the Sorrows of the Blessed Virgin, "Divina Paraninfa"; a refutation of <a href="/wiki/Hugues_Felicit%C3%A9_Robert_de_Lamennais" class="mw-redirect" title="Hugues Felicité Robert de Lamennais">de Lamennais</a>; three series of sermons; and various controversial and ascetic works. One of Barberi's most famed works was his 'Lamentation of England',<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> in which he used the words of the Prophet <a href="/wiki/Jeremiah" title="Jeremiah">Jeremiah</a> to express the lamentations of English Catholics.
</p>
