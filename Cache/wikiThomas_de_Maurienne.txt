<p><b>Farfa Abbey</b> (<a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Abbazia di Farfa</i>) is a <a href="/wiki/Territorial_abbey" title="Territorial abbey">territorial abbey</a> in northern <a href="/wiki/Lazio" title="Lazio">Lazio</a>, <a href="/wiki/Central_Italy" title="Central Italy">Central Italy</a>. In the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>, it was one of the richest and most famous <a href="/wiki/Abbey" title="Abbey">abbeys</a> in Italy. It belongs to the <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">Benedictine Order</a> and is located about 60&#160;km (37&#160;mi) from <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, in the commune of <a href="/wiki/Fara_Sabina" class="mw-redirect" title="Fara Sabina">Fara Sabina</a>, of which it is also a hamlet (<i>It. <a href="/wiki/Frazione" title="Frazione">frazione</a></i>).<sup id="cite_ref-itind_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-itind-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 2016 it was added to the "tentative" list to be a UNESCO <a href="/wiki/World_Heritage_Site" title="World Heritage Site">World Heritage Site</a>, as part of a group of eight Italian medieval Benedictine monasteries, representing "The cultural landscape of the Benedictine settlements in medieval Italy".<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<p>A legend in the 12th-century <i>Chronicon Farfense</i> (Chronicle of Farfa)<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> dates the founding of a monastery at Farfa to the time of the Emperors <a href="/wiki/Julian_(emperor)" title="Julian (emperor)">Julian</a>, or <a href="/wiki/Gratian" title="Gratian">Gratian</a>, and attributes the founding to Laurence of Syria, who had come to Rome with his sister, Susannah, together with other monks, and had been made <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Spoleto" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Spoleto">Bishop of Spoleto</a>. According to the tradition, after being named bishop, he became enamoured of the monastic life, and chose a forested hill near the Farfa stream, a tributary of the <a href="/wiki/Tiber" title="Tiber">Tiber</a>, to build a church and a monastery. Archaeological discoveries in 1888<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> find strong evidence that the first monastic establishment was built on the ruins of a pagan temple. This first monastery was devastated by the <a href="/wiki/Vandals" title="Vandals">Vandals</a> in the fifth century. Only a handful of sixth-century finds document the early presence of the monastic community.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the seventh century, a <a href="/wiki/Hiberno-Scottish_mission" title="Hiberno-Scottish mission">wave of Irish monasticism</a> spread over Italy. The foundation the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Columbanus" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of Saint Columbanus">Abbey of Saint Columbanus</a> in <a href="/wiki/Bobbio" title="Bobbio">Bobbio</a>. and of Farfa by monks from <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a>, about 681, heralded a revival of the great Benedictine tradition in Italy. The <i>Constructio Monasterii Farfensis</i>, which dates probably from 857, relates at length the story of its principal founder <a href="/wiki/Thomas_of_Maurienne" title="Thomas of Maurienne">Thomas of Maurienne</a>;<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> he had made a pilgrimage to Jerusalem and spent three years there. While in prayer before the Holy Sepulchre, the Virgin Mary in a vision warned him to return to Italy, and restore Farfa; and the <a href="/wiki/Duchy_of_Spoleto" title="Duchy of Spoleto">Duke of Spoleto</a>. <a href="/wiki/Faroald_II_of_Spoleto" title="Faroald II of Spoleto">Faroald II</a>, who had also had a vision, was commanded to aid in this work. At a very early date we find traces of this legend in connexion with the foundation by three nobles from <a href="/wiki/Benevento" title="Benevento">Benevento</a> of the monastery of St Vincent on the <a href="/wiki/Volturno" title="Volturno">Volturno</a>, over which Farfa claimed jurisdiction. Thomas died in 720; and for more than a century <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frankish</a> abbots ruled at Farfa.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Abbazia_di_Farfa_-_cortile_(2)_-_gennaio_2011.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/36/Abbazia_di_Farfa_-_cortile_%282%29_-_gennaio_2011.jpg/170px-Abbazia_di_Farfa_-_cortile_%282%29_-_gennaio_2011.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="227" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/36/Abbazia_di_Farfa_-_cortile_%282%29_-_gennaio_2011.jpg/255px-Abbazia_di_Farfa_-_cortile_%282%29_-_gennaio_2011.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/36/Abbazia_di_Farfa_-_cortile_%282%29_-_gennaio_2011.jpg/340px-Abbazia_di_Farfa_-_cortile_%282%29_-_gennaio_2011.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3000" data-file-height="4000" /></a><figcaption>The abbey's court</figcaption></figure>
<p>The Lombard chiefs, and later the <a href="/wiki/Carolingians" class="mw-redirect" title="Carolingians">Carolingians</a>, succeeded in withdrawing Farfa from obedience to the Bishops of <a href="/wiki/Rieti" title="Rieti">Rieti</a>, and in securing many immunities and privileges for the monastery. If we may credit the <i>Chronicon Farfense</i>, with the exception of the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Nonatola" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of Nonatola">Abbey of Nonatola</a>, Farfa was at this period the most important monastery in Italy both from the point of view of worldly riches and ecclesiastical dignity. In 898, the abbey was sacked by <a href="/wiki/Saracens" class="mw-redirect" title="Saracens">Saracens</a> who then burned it.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Between 930 and 936, Farfa was rebuilt by Abbot <a href="/w/index.php?title=Ratfredus&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Ratfredus (page does not exist)">Ratfredus</a>, who was afterwards poisoned by two wicked monks, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Campo_of_Farfa&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Campo of Farfa (page does not exist)">Campo</a> and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Hildebrand_of_Farfa&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Hildebrand of Farfa (page does not exist)">Hildebrand</a>, who divided the wealth of the abbey between them, and ruled over it until <a href="/wiki/Alberic_I_of_Spoleto" title="Alberic I of Spoleto">Alberic I of Spoleto</a>, Prince of the Romans, called in <a href="/wiki/Odo_of_Cluny" title="Odo of Cluny">Odo of Cluny</a> to reform Farfa and other monasteries in the <a href="/wiki/Duchy_of_Rome" title="Duchy of Rome">Duchy of Rome</a>. Campo was exiled, and a holy monk with the Merovingian name of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Dagobert_of_Farfa&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Dagobert of Farfa (page does not exist)">Dagibert</a> took his place. At the end of five years, he also died by poison — and the moral condition of Farfa was once more deplorable. The monks robbed the altars of their ornaments, and led lives of unbridled vice.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:ItalyAbbaziaFarfaTorre.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/ItalyAbbaziaFarfaTorre.jpg/220px-ItalyAbbaziaFarfaTorre.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="313" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/ItalyAbbaziaFarfaTorre.jpg/330px-ItalyAbbaziaFarfaTorre.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/ItalyAbbaziaFarfaTorre.jpg/440px-ItalyAbbaziaFarfaTorre.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1260" data-file-height="1795" /></a><figcaption>Abbey's tower.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Owing to the protection of the Emperor <a href="/wiki/Otho_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Otho I, Holy Roman Emperor">Otho</a>, the abbot <a href="/w/index.php?title=John_III_of_Farfa&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="John III of Farfa (page does not exist)">John III</a>, who had been consecrated circa 967 by the pope, succeeded in re-establishing a semblance of order. But the great reformer of Farfa was <a href="/wiki/Hugh_of_Farfa" title="Hugh of Farfa">Hugues</a> (998-1010). His nomination as abbot was not secured without simony — but the success of his government palliates the vice of his election. At this instance, abbots <a href="/wiki/Odilo_of_Cluny" title="Odilo of Cluny">Odilo of Cluny</a> and <a href="/wiki/William_of_Dijon" class="mw-redirect" title="William of Dijon">William of Dijon</a>, visited Farfa, and re-established there the love of piety and of study.
</p><p>The <i>Consuetudines Farfenses</i> drawn up about 1010 under the supervision of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Guy_of_Farfa&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Guy of Farfa (page does not exist)">Guido</a>, successor to Hugues of Farfa, bear witness to the care with which Hugues organized the monastic life at Farfa. Under the title <i>Destructio Monasterii</i>, Hugues himself wrote a history of the sad period previous to his rule; and again under the title <i>Diminutio Monasterii</i>, and <i>Querimonium</i>, he related the temporal difficulties that encompassed Farfa owing to the ambition of petty Roman lords. These works are very important for the historian of the period.
</p>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Hendrick_van_den_Broeck_-_The_Last_Judgement.jpeg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/Hendrick_van_den_Broeck_-_The_Last_Judgement.jpeg/270px-Hendrick_van_den_Broeck_-_The_Last_Judgement.jpeg" decoding="async" width="270" height="190" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/Hendrick_van_den_Broeck_-_The_Last_Judgement.jpeg/405px-Hendrick_van_den_Broeck_-_The_Last_Judgement.jpeg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/Hendrick_van_den_Broeck_-_The_Last_Judgement.jpeg/540px-Hendrick_van_den_Broeck_-_The_Last_Judgement.jpeg 2x" data-file-width="1119" data-file-height="786" /></a><figcaption><i>The Last Judgement</i> by <a href="/wiki/Hendrick_van_den_Broeck" title="Hendrick van den Broeck">Hendrick van den Broeck</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>One of Hugue's successors, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Berard_I&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Berard I (page does not exist)">Berard I</a>, abbot from 1049 to 1089, made the abbey a great seat of intellectual activity. The monk <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Catino" title="Gregory of Catino">Gregory of Catino</a> (b. 1060) arranged the archives. To substantiate Farfa's claims and the rights of its monks, he edited the <i>Regesto di Farfa</i>, or <i>Liber Gemniagraphus sive Cleronomialis ecclesiæ Farfensis</i> composed of 1324 documents, all very important for the history of Italian society in the 11th century.
</p><p>In 1103, Gregory wrote the <i>Largitorium</i>, or <i>Liber Notarius sive emphiteuticus</i>, a lengthy list of all the concessions, or grants, made by the monastery to its tenants. Having collected all this detailed information, he set to work on a history of the monastery, the <i>Chronicon Farfense</i>; and when he was 70 years old, in order to facilitate reference to his earlier works, he compiled a sort of index which he styled "Liber Floriger Chartarum cenobii Farfensis". Gregory was a man of real learning, remarkable in that, as early as the eleventh century, he wrote history with accuracy of view-point, and a great wealth of information.
</p><p>The monks of Farfa owned 683 churches or convents; two towns, Centumcellæ (<a href="/wiki/Civitavecchia" title="Civitavecchia">Civitavecchia</a>) and <a href="/wiki/Alatri" title="Alatri">Alatri</a>; 132 castles; 16 strongholds; 7 <a href="/wiki/Seaport" class="mw-redirect" title="Seaport">seaports</a>; 8 <a href="/wiki/Salt" title="Salt">salt</a> mines; 14 villages; 82 mills; 315 hamlets. All this wealth was a hindrance to the religious life once more. Between 1119 and 1125, Farfa was troubled by the rivalries between Abbot Guido, and the monk Berard who aimed at being abbot. During the <a href="/wiki/Investiture_Controversy" title="Investiture Controversy">Investiture conflict</a>, Farfa was, more or less, on the side of the <a href="/wiki/Ghibellines" class="mw-redirect" title="Ghibellines">Ghibellines</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The monks issued an <i>Orthodoxa defensio imperialis</i> in support of the Ghibelline party. The collection of canonical texts contained in the <i>Regesto</i> seems to omit purposely any mention of the canonical texts of the reforming popes of the eleventh century.s But when, in 1262, the victory of the popes over the <a href="/wiki/Conradin" title="Conradin">last</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Hohenstaufen" title="Hohenstaufen">Hohenstaufen</a> put an end to the Germanic rule in Italy, Farfa sought the protection of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_IV" title="Pope Urban IV">Urban IV</a>. At the end of the 14th century the Abbey of Farfa became a <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_(Catholicism)" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinal (Catholicism)">cardinalatial in commendam</a>, and since 1842 the <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_Bishop_of_Sabina" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinal Bishop of Sabina">Cardinal Bishop of Sabina</a>, a suburbicarian bishop, bears also the title of Abbot of Farfa.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Orsini_family" title="Orsini family">Orsini family</a> stayed to the early 16th century, consecrating the cathedral in 1494. They were succeeded by the <a href="/wiki/Della_Rovere" title="Della Rovere">Della Rovere</a>, but the Orsini came back and stayed until 1542.  Later the monastery was taken over by the <a href="/wiki/House_of_Farnese" title="House of Farnese">Farnese</a> family. <a href="/wiki/Ranuccio_Farnese_(cardinal)" title="Ranuccio Farnese (cardinal)">Ranuccio Farnese (cardinal)</a> was its abbot commendatory when in 1561 he commissioned the Flemish painter <a href="/wiki/Hendrick_van_den_Broeck" title="Hendrick van den Broeck">Hendrick van den Broeck</a> to create a large painting of the <i>Last Judgememt</i> for the Abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Under the management of Ranuccio's brother <a href="/wiki/Alessandro_Farnese_(cardinal)" title="Alessandro Farnese (cardinal)">Cardinal Alessandro Farnese</a>, the monastery joined the <a href="/wiki/Cassinese_Congregation" class="mw-redirect" title="Cassinese Congregation">Cassinese Congregation</a> (1567). During the next two centuries in spite of some restorations and new constructions Farfa lost all importance. The monastery was suppressed in 1798 followed another in 1861 by the new Kingdom of Italy. 
</p><p>Part of the possessions were sold to private citizens. Felice Giacomo Vitale was the previous owner who sold Farfa Abbey to Count Volpi. (Vitale was a notable lawyer in Rome Turn of the 20th century). He was the same owner of villino Vitale in Via dei Gracchi, Rome.) The heirs of the last owner, Count Volpi, donated part of the monastery owned by them and some land around it to the monks. In 1920, a group of monks sent by <a href="/wiki/Alfredo_Ildefonso_Schuster" title="Alfredo Ildefonso Schuster">Alfredo Ildefonso Schuster</a>, then Abbot of the Abbey of St. Paul Outside the Walls in Rome (attached to the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint_Paul_Outside_the_Walls" title="Basilica of Saint Paul Outside the Walls">Basilica of Saint Paul Outside the Walls</a>), gave new life to Farfa Abbey in establishing a monastic community, Farfa was declared a national monument but only much later were steps taken to reconstruct and repair the buildings.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Cathedral">Cathedral</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Farfa_Abbey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Cathedral">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<p>The cathedral has a huge <a href="/wiki/Romanesque_architecture" title="Romanesque architecture">Romanesque</a> gate, with magnificent floral friezes. The interior has three naves, and the middle one is surmounted with a lunette representing the Virgin and the Child. The Renaissance hall has several chapels: the most venerated image of Farfa is housed in the Crucifix Chapel. The interior wall of the façade has a large canvas depicting the <i>Last Judgment</i> (1571) by the Flemish painter <a href="/wiki/Hendrick_van_den_Broeck" title="Hendrick van den Broeck">Hendrick van den Broeck</a>.
</p><p>Ruins of the ancient 9th century church are included in the church and the monastery. In 1961, in the apse, a precious Roman sarcophagus from the 1st or 2nd century AD was discovered. The large cloister is from the 16th century. The <a href="/wiki/Bell_tower" title="Bell tower">bell tower</a> belongs to the original Carolingian buildings. Inside, at the lower end, Abbot <a href="/wiki/Sichard" title="Sichard">Sichard</a> had an oratory built.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:ItalyAbbaziaFarfaBorgo.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/97/ItalyAbbaziaFarfaBorgo.jpg/220px-ItalyAbbaziaFarfaBorgo.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="174" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/97/ItalyAbbaziaFarfaBorgo.jpg/330px-ItalyAbbaziaFarfaBorgo.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/97/ItalyAbbaziaFarfaBorgo.jpg/440px-ItalyAbbaziaFarfaBorgo.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1809" data-file-height="1434" /></a><figcaption>Some houses of the village</figcaption></figure>
<p>The little medieval village of Farfa lies around the abbey and has a population of 42.<sup id="cite_ref-itind_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-itind-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Transport">Transport</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Farfa_Abbey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Transport">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nearest railway station, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Fara_Sabina-Montelibretti_railway_station&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Fara Sabina-Montelibretti railway station (page does not exist)">Fara Sabina-Montelibretti</a>, is located at <a href="/wiki/Passo_Corese" title="Passo Corese">Passo Corese</a> and is 10&#160;km (6.2&#160;mi) from the abbey. The station is part of the <a href="/wiki/FL_lines" title="FL lines">Roman suburban railway</a> line <a href="/wiki/FL1_(Lazio_regional_railways)" title="FL1 (Lazio regional railways)">FL1 Orte–Fiumicino</a>.
</p>
