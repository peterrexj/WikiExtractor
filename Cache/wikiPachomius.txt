<p><b>Pachomius</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="&#39;p&#39; in &#39;pie&#39;">p</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;k&#39; in &#39;kind&#39;">k</span><span title="/oʊ/: &#39;o&#39; in &#39;code&#39;">oʊ</span><span title="&#39;m&#39; in &#39;my&#39;">m</span><span title="/i/: &#39;y&#39; in &#39;happy&#39;">i</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;s&#39; in &#39;sigh&#39;">s</span></span>/</a></span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Παχώμιος</span> <i>Pakhomios</i>; <a href="/wiki/Coptic_language" title="Coptic language">Coptic</a>: <span lang="cop">Ⲡⲁϧⲱⲙ</span>; c. 292 – 9 May 348 AD), also known as <b>Saint Pachomius the Great</b>, is generally recognized as the founder of Christian <a href="/wiki/Cenobitic" class="mw-redirect" title="Cenobitic">cenobitic</a> monasticism.<sup id="cite_ref-GabraTakla2010_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-GabraTakla2010-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Copts" title="Copts">Coptic</a> churches celebrate his feast day on 9 May, and <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Orthodox">Eastern Orthodox</a> and <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> churches mark his feast on 15 May<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> or 28 May.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> In the <a href="/wiki/Lutheran_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Lutheran Church">Lutheran Church</a>, he is remembered as a renewer of the church, along with his contemporary (and fellow desert saint), <a href="/wiki/Anthony_of_Egypt" class="mw-redirect" title="Anthony of Egypt">Anthony of Egypt</a> on 17 January.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pachomius_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Pachomius was born in 292 in the <a href="/wiki/Thebaid" title="Thebaid">Thebaid</a> (near modern-day <a href="/wiki/Luxor" title="Luxor">Luxor</a>, <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a>) to pagan parents.<sup id="cite_ref-holy_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-holy-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> According to his <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a>, at age 21, Pachomius was swept up against his will in a Roman army recruitment drive, a common occurrence during this period of turmoil and civil war. With several other youths, he was put onto a ship that floated down the <a href="/wiki/Nile" title="Nile">Nile</a> and arrived at <a href="/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt" title="Thebes, Egypt">Thebes</a> in the evening.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Here he first encountered local Christians, who customarily brought food and comfort daily to the conscripted troops. This made a lasting impression, and Pachomius vowed to investigate Christianity further when he got out. He was able to leave the army without ever having to fight, was converted and baptized (314).
</p><p>Pachomius then came into contact with several well known ascetics and decided to pursue that path under the guidance of the hermit named Palaemon (317). One of his devotions, popular at the time, was praying with his arms stretched out in the form of a cross.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> After studying seven years with Palaemon, Pachomius set out to lead the life of a <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a> near <a href="/wiki/Anthony_the_Great" title="Anthony the Great">St. Anthony of Egypt</a>, whose practices he imitated until Pachomius heard a voice in <a href="/wiki/Tabenna" title="Tabenna">Tabennisi</a> that told him to build a dwelling for the hermits to come to.<sup id="cite_ref-bacchus_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bacchus-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> An earlier ascetic named <a href="/wiki/Macarius_of_Alexandria" title="Macarius of Alexandria">Macarius</a> had created a number of proto-monasteries called <a href="/wiki/Lavra" title="Lavra">lavra</a>, or cells, where holy men who were physically or mentally unable to achieve the rigors of Anthony's solitary life would live in a community setting.
</p><p>Pachomius established his first monastery between 318 and 323 at <a href="/wiki/Tabennisi" class="mw-redirect" title="Tabennisi">Tabennisi</a>, Egypt.<sup id="cite_ref-huddleston_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-huddleston-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> His elder brother John joined him, and soon more than 100 monks lived nearby. Pachomius set about organizing these cells into a formal organization. Until then, Christian <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a> had been solitary or <i>eremitic</i> with male or female monastics living in individual huts or caves and meeting only for occasional worship services. Pachomius created the community or <i><a href="/wiki/Cenobitic" class="mw-redirect" title="Cenobitic">cenobitic</a></i> organization, in which male or female monastics lived together and held their property in common under the leadership of an <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> or <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a>. Pachomius realized that some men, acquainted only with the eremitical life, might speedily become disgusted if the distracting cares of the cenobitical life were thrust too abruptly upon them. He therefore allowed them to devote their whole time to spiritual exercises, undertaking all the community's administrative tasks himself. The community hailed Pachomius as "Abba" ("father" in Aramaic), from which "Abbot" derives.
The monastery at <a href="/wiki/Tabenna" title="Tabenna">Tabennisi</a>, though enlarged several times, soon became too small and a second was founded at <a href="/wiki/Pbow" title="Pbow">Pbow</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-bacchus_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bacchus-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> This monastery at <a href="/wiki/Pbow" title="Pbow">Pbow</a> would go on to become the center for monasteries springing up along the Nile in Upper Egypt.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Both of these are believed to have initially been abandoned villages, which were then repurposed for Pachomius’ vision of his <i>Koinonia</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> After 336, Pachomius spent most of his time at <a href="/wiki/Pbow" title="Pbow">Pbow</a>. Though Pachomius sometimes acted as <a href="/wiki/Reader_(liturgy)" title="Reader (liturgy)">lector</a> for nearby shepherds, neither he nor any of his monks became priests. <a href="/wiki/St._Athanasius" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Athanasius">St. Athanasius</a> visited and wished to ordain him in 333, but Pachomius fled from him. Athanasius' visit was probably a result of Pachomius' zealous defence of orthodoxy against <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea" title="Basil of Caesarea">Basil of Caesarea</a> visited, then took many of Pachomius' ideas, which he adapted and implemented in Caesarea. <a href="/w/index.php?title=Rule_of_St._Pachomius&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Rule of St. Pachomius (page does not exist)">This ascetic rule</a>, or Ascetica, is still used today by the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, comparable to that of the <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_St._Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="Rule of St. Benedict">Rule of St. Benedict</a> in the West.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Rule_of_St._Pachomius">Rule of St. Pachomius</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pachomius_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Rule of St. Pachomius">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Pachomius was the first to set down a written rule.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="margin-left:0.1em; white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify" title="Wikipedia:Please clarify"><span title="The text near this tag may need clarification or removal of jargon. (February 2023)">clarification needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> The first rule was composed of prayers generally known and in general use, such as the Lord's Prayer. The monks were to pray them every day. As the community developed, the rules were elaborated with precepts taken from the Bible. He drew up a rule which made things easier for the less proficient, but did not check the most extreme asceticism in the more proficient.<sup id="cite_ref-bacchus_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bacchus-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The Rule sought to balance prayer with work, the communal life with solitude. The day was organised around the liturgy, with time for manual work and devotional reading.
</p><p>Fasts and work were apportioned according to the individual's strength. Each monk received the same food and clothing.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Common meals were provided, but those who wished to absent themselves from them were encouraged to do so, and bread, salt, and water were placed in their cells. In the Pachomian monasteries it was left very much to the individual taste of each monk to fix the order of life for himself. Thus the hours for meals and the extent of his fasting were settled by him alone, he might eat with the others in common or have bread and salt provided in his own cell every day or every second day.<sup id="cite_ref-huddleston_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-huddleston-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His rule was translated into Latin by <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>. <a href="/wiki/Honoratus" title="Honoratus">Honoratus</a> of <a href="/wiki/L%C3%A9rins_Abbey" title="Lérins Abbey">Lérins</a> followed the Rule of St. Pachomius. <a href="/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea" title="Basil of Caesarea">Basil the Great</a> and <a href="/wiki/Benedict_of_Nursia" title="Benedict of Nursia">Benedict of Nursia</a> adapted and incorporated parts of it in their rules.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_legacy">Death and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pachomius_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Death and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Bucharest_-_Biserica_Sf._Anton_06.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Bucharest_-_Biserica_Sf._Anton_06.jpg/220px-Bucharest_-_Biserica_Sf._Anton_06.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="331" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Bucharest_-_Biserica_Sf._Anton_06.jpg/330px-Bucharest_-_Biserica_Sf._Anton_06.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Bucharest_-_Biserica_Sf._Anton_06.jpg/440px-Bucharest_-_Biserica_Sf._Anton_06.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2848" data-file-height="4288" /></a><figcaption>Painting of Pachomius the Great in the <a href="/wiki/Curtea_Veche" title="Curtea Veche">Curtea Veche</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bucharest" title="Bucharest">Bucharest</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Pachomius continued as abbot to the cenobites for some thirty years. During an epidemic (probably <a href="/wiki/Bubonic_plague" title="Bubonic plague">plague</a>), Pachomius called the monks, strengthened their faith, and failed to appoint his successor. Pachomius then died on <a href="/wiki/Pashons_14_(Coptic_Orthodox_liturgics)" class="mw-redirect" title="Pashons 14 (Coptic Orthodox liturgics)">14 Pashons</a>, 64 <a href="/wiki/Era_of_Martyrs" class="mw-redirect" title="Era of Martyrs">AM</a> (9 May 348 AD).
</p><p>By the time Pachomius died, eight monasteries and several hundred monks followed his guidance.<sup id="cite_ref-huddleston_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-huddleston-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Within a generation, cenobic practices spread from Egypt to Palestine and the Judean Desert, Syria, North Africa and eventually Western Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The number of monks, rather than the number of monasteries, may have reached 7000.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His reputation as a holy man has endured. As mentioned above, several liturgical calendars commemorate Pachomius. Among many miracles attributed to Pachomius, that though he had never learned the Greek or Latin tongues, he sometimes miraculously spoke them.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Pachomius is also credited with being the first Christian to use and recommend use of a <a href="/wiki/Prayer_rope" title="Prayer rope">prayer rope</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Name">Name</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pachomius_the_Great&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Name">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The name of the saint is of <a href="/wiki/Coptic_language" title="Coptic language">Coptic</a> origin:<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> ⲡⲁϧⲱⲙ <i>pakhōm</i> from <a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E2%B2%81%CF%A7%E2%B2%B1%E2%B2%99" class="extiw" title="wikt:ⲁϧⲱⲙ">ⲁϧⲱⲙ</a> <i>akhōm</i> "eagle or falcon" (ⲡ <i>p</i>- at the beginning is the Coptic definite article), from Middle Egyptian <a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%EA%9C%A5%E1%BA%96m" class="extiw" title="wikt:ꜥẖm">ꜥẖm</a> "falcon", originally "divine image". Into Greek it was adopted as Παχούμιος and Παχώμιος. By Greek folk etymology it was sometimes interpreted as "broad-shouldered" from <a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CF%80%CE%B1%CF%87%CF%8D%CF%82" class="extiw" title="wikt:παχύς">παχύς</a> "thick, large" and <a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BD%A6%CE%BC%CE%BF%CF%82" class="extiw" title="wikt:ὦμος">ὦμος</a> "shoulder".
</p>
