<p><b>Robert Southwell</b> (c. 1561 – 21 February 1595), also <b>Saint Robert Southwell</b>, was an <a href="/wiki/English_people" title="English people">English</a> <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_priest" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic priest">Roman Catholic priest</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Jesuit_Order" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit Order">Jesuit Order</a>. He was also a poet, <a href="/wiki/Hymnodist" class="mw-redirect" title="Hymnodist">hymnodist</a>, and clandestine missionary in <a href="/wiki/Elizabethan_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Elizabethan England">Elizabethan England</a>.
</p><p>After being arrested and imprisoned in 1592, and intermittently tortured and questioned by <a href="/wiki/Richard_Topcliffe" title="Richard Topcliffe">Richard Topcliffe</a>, Southwell was eventually tried and convicted of <a href="/wiki/High_treason" class="mw-redirect" title="High treason">high treason</a> for his links to the <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Holy See</a>. On 21 February 1595, Father Southwell was <a href="/wiki/Hanged" class="mw-redirect" title="Hanged">hanged</a> at <a href="/wiki/Tyburn,_London" class="mw-redirect" title="Tyburn, London">Tyburn</a>. In 1970, he was <a href="/wiki/Canonised" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonised">canonised</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI" title="Pope Paul VI">Pope Paul VI</a> as one of the <a href="/wiki/Forty_Martyrs_of_England_and_Wales" title="Forty Martyrs of England and Wales">Forty Martyrs of England and Wales</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_in_England">Early life in England</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Robert_Southwell_(priest)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life in England">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was born at <a href="/wiki/Horsham_St_Faith" title="Horsham St Faith">Horsham St Faith</a>, Norfolk, England. Southwell, the youngest of eight children, was brought up in a family of the Norfolk gentry. Despite their Catholic sympathies, the Southwells had profited considerably from King <a href="/wiki/Henry_VIII" title="Henry VIII">Henry VIII</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Suppression_of_the_Monasteries" class="mw-redirect" title="Suppression of the Monasteries">Suppression of the Monasteries</a>. Robert was third son of Richard Southwell of <a href="/wiki/Horsham_St._Faith%27s" class="mw-redirect" title="Horsham St. Faith&#39;s">Horsham St. Faith's</a>, Norfolk, by his first wife, Bridget, daughter of Sir Roger Copley of Roughway, Sussex. The hymnodist's maternal grandmother was Elizabeth, daughter of <a href="/wiki/Sir_William_Shelley" title="Sir William Shelley">Sir William Shelley</a>; <a href="/wiki/Richard_Southwell_(courtier)" title="Richard Southwell (courtier)">Sir Richard Southwell</a> was his paternal grandfather, but his father was born out of wedlock.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Enters_the_Society_of_Jesus">Enters the Society of Jesus</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Robert_Southwell_(priest)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Enters the Society of Jesus">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1576, he was sent to the <a href="/wiki/English_College,_Douai" title="English College, Douai">English college</a> at <a href="/wiki/Douai" title="Douai">Douai</a>, boarding there but studying at the Jesuit College of Anchin, a French college associated, like the English College, with the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Douai" title="University of Douai">university of Douai</a>. He studied briefly under <a href="/wiki/Leonard_Lessius" class="mw-redirect" title="Leonard Lessius">Leonard Lessius</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> At the end of the summer, however, his education was interrupted by the movement of French and Spanish forces. For greater safety Southwell was sent to Paris and studied at the <a href="/wiki/Lyc%C3%A9e_Louis-le-Grand" title="Lycée Louis-le-Grand">College de Clermont</a> under the tutelage of the Jesuit <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Darbyshire" title="Thomas Darbyshire">Thomas Darbyshire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Oxford_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Oxford-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He returned to Douai on 15 June 1577.  A year later he set off on foot to Rome with the intention of joining the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">Society of Jesus</a>. A two-year <a href="/wiki/Novitiate" title="Novitiate">novitiate</a> at <a href="/wiki/Tournai" title="Tournai">Tournai</a> was required before joining the Society, however, and initially he was denied entry. He appealed the decision by sending a heartfelt, emotional letter to the school.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He bemoans the situation, writing, "How can I but wast in anguish and agony that find myself disjoined from that company, severed from that Society, disunited from that body wherein lyeth all my life my love my whole hart and affection" (Archivum Romanum Societatis Iesu, Anglia 14, fol. 80, under date 1578).<sup id="cite_ref-Oxford_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Oxford-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His efforts succeeded as he was admitted to the probation house of Sant' Andrea on 17 October 1578 and in 1580 became a member of the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">Society of Jesus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Oxford_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Oxford-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Immediately after the completion of the novitiate, Southwell began studies in philosophy and theology at the Jesuit College in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>.  During this time, he worked as a secretary to the rector and writings of his are to be found among the school's documents.  Upon completion of his studies, Southwell was granted his BA in 1584, which was also the year of his ordination. He was appointed "repetitor" (tutor) in the <a href="/wiki/Venerable_English_College" class="mw-redirect" title="Venerable English College">Venerable English College</a> at Rome and after for two years became the prefect of studies there.<sup id="cite_ref-Oxford_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Oxford-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> It was in 1584 that an act was passed forbidding any English-born subject of <a href="/wiki/Elizabeth_I_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Elizabeth I of England">Queen Elizabeth</a>, who had entered into priests' orders in the Catholic Church since her accession, to remain in England longer than forty days on pain of death.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="On_the_English_mission">On the English mission</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Robert_Southwell_(priest)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: On the English mission">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1586 Southwell, at his own request, was sent to England as a Jesuit <a href="/wiki/Missionary" title="Missionary">missionary</a> with <a href="/wiki/Henry_Garnet" title="Henry Garnet">Henry Garnet</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He went from one Catholic family to another. The Jesuit <a href="/wiki/William_Weston_(Jesuit)" title="William Weston (Jesuit)">William Weston</a> had previously made his way to England; but he was arrested and sent to <a href="/wiki/Wisbech_Castle" title="Wisbech Castle">Wisbech Castle</a> in 1587.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The Garnet–Southwell Jesuit English mission is considered the third;<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> the first such mission was that of <a href="/wiki/Robert_Parsons_(Jesuit)" class="mw-redirect" title="Robert Parsons (Jesuit)">Robert Parsons</a> and <a href="/wiki/Edmund_Campion" title="Edmund Campion">Edmund Campion</a> of 1580–1581.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A spy reported to <a href="/wiki/Sir_Francis_Walsingham" class="mw-redirect" title="Sir Francis Walsingham">Sir Francis Walsingham</a> the Jesuits' landing on the east coast in July, but they arrived without molestation at the house at <a href="/wiki/Hackney_parish" class="mw-redirect" title="Hackney parish">Hackney</a> of <a href="/wiki/William_Vaux,_3rd_Baron_Vaux_of_Harrowden" title="William Vaux, 3rd Baron Vaux of Harrowden">William Vaux, 3rd Baron Vaux of Harrowden</a>. In 1588 Southwell and Garnet were joined by <a href="/wiki/John_Gerard_(Jesuit)" title="John Gerard (Jesuit)">John Gerard</a> and <a href="/wiki/Edward_Oldcorne" title="Edward Oldcorne">Edward Oldcorne</a>. Southwell was from the outset closely watched; he mixed furtively in Protestant society under the assumed name of Cotton. He studied the terms of sport, and used them in conversation. For the most part residing in London, he made occasional excursions to <a href="/wiki/Sussex" title="Sussex">Sussex</a> and the North.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1589 Southwell became domestic chaplain to <a href="/wiki/Anne_Howard,_Countess_of_Arundel" title="Anne Howard, Countess of Arundel">Anne Howard</a>, whose husband, the <a href="/wiki/Philip_Howard,_20th_Earl_of_Arundel" class="mw-redirect" title="Philip Howard, 20th Earl of Arundel">First Earl of Arundel</a>, was in prison convicted of treason.<sup id="cite_ref-FILE_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FILE-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Arundel had been confined to the <a href="/wiki/Tower_of_London" title="Tower of London">Tower of London</a> since 1585; but his execution was postponed, and he remained in prison till his death in 1595. Southwell took up his residence with the countess at <a href="/wiki/Arundel_House" title="Arundel House">Arundel House</a> in <a href="/wiki/The_Strand,_London" class="mw-redirect" title="The Strand, London">The Strand, London</a>. During 1591 he occupied most of his time in writing; although Southwell's name was not publicly associated with any of his works, his literary activity was suspected by the government.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Arrest_and_imprisonment">Arrest and imprisonment</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Robert_Southwell_(priest)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Arrest and imprisonment">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After six years of missionary labour, Southwell was arrested at Uxendon Hall, Harrow. He was in the habit of visiting the house of Richard Bellamy who lived near <a href="/wiki/Harrow,_London" title="Harrow, London">Harrow</a> and was under suspicion on account of his connection with <a href="/wiki/Jerome_Bellamy" title="Jerome Bellamy">Jerome Bellamy</a>, who had been executed for sharing in <a href="/wiki/Anthony_Babington%27s_plot" class="mw-redirect" title="Anthony Babington&#39;s plot">Anthony Babington's plot</a>.  One of the daughters, Anne Bellamy, was arrested and imprisoned in the gatehouse of <a href="/wiki/Holborn" title="Holborn">Holborn</a> for being linked to the situation. Having been interrogated and raped by <a href="/wiki/Richard_Topcliffe" title="Richard Topcliffe">Richard Topcliffe</a>, the Queen's chief priest-hunter and torturer, she revealed Southwell's movements and he was immediately arrested.<sup id="cite_ref-Oxford_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Oxford-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He was first taken to Topcliffe's own house, adjoining the Gatehouse Prison, where Topcliffe subjected him to the torture of "the manacles".  He remained silent in Topcliffe's custody for forty hours.  The queen then ordered Southwell moved to the Gatehouse, where a team of Privy Council torturers went to work on him. When they proved equally unsuccessful, he was left "hurt, starving, covered with maggots and lice, to lie in his own filth."  After about a month he was moved by order of the council to solitary confinement in the Tower of London. According to the early narratives, his father had petitioned the queen that his son, if guilty under the law, should so suffer, but if not should be treated as a gentleman, and that as his father he should be allowed to provide him with the necessities of life.  No documentary evidence of such a petition survives, but something of the kind must have happened, since his friends were able to provide him with food and clothing, and to send him the works of St. Bernard and a Bible. His superior Henry Garnet later smuggled a <a href="/wiki/Breviary" title="Breviary">breviary</a> to him. He remained in the Tower for three years, under Topcliffe's supervision.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Trial_and_execution">Trial and execution</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Robert_Southwell_(priest)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Trial and execution">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<p>In 1595 the Privy Council passed a resolution for Southwell's prosecution on the charges of treason.  He was removed from the Tower to <a href="/wiki/Newgate_Prison" title="Newgate Prison">Newgate Prison</a>, where he was put into a hole called Limbo.<sup id="cite_ref-FILE_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FILE-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A few days later, Southwell appeared before the Lord Chief Justice, <a href="/wiki/John_Popham_(Lord_Chief_Justice)" class="mw-redirect" title="John Popham (Lord Chief Justice)">John Popham</a>, at the bar of the <a href="/wiki/Court_of_King%27s_Bench_(England)" title="Court of King&#39;s Bench (England)">King's Bench</a>. Popham made a speech against Jesuits and seminary priests.  Southwell was indicted before the jury as a traitor under the statutes prohibiting the presence, within the kingdom, of priests ordained by Rome. Southwell admitted the facts but denied that he had "entertained any designs or plots against the queen or kingdom." His only purpose, he said, in returning to England had been to administer the sacraments according to the rite of the Catholic Church to such as desired them. When asked to enter a plea, he declared himself "not guilty of any treason whatsoever," objecting to a jury being made responsible for his death but allowing that he would be tried by God and country.<sup id="cite_ref-Oxford_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Oxford-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As the evidence was pressed, Southwell stated that he was the same age as "our Saviour".  He was immediately reproved by Topcliffe for insupportable pride in making the comparison, but he said in response that he considered himself "a worm of the earth". After a brief recess, the jury returned with the predictable guilty verdict. The sentence of death was pronounced – <a href="/wiki/Hanging,_drawing_and_quartering" class="mw-redirect" title="Hanging, drawing and quartering">to be hanged, drawn and quartered</a>.  He was returned through the city streets to Newgate.
</p><p>On 21 February 1595, Southwell was sent to Tyburn. Execution of sentence on a notorious highwayman had been appointed for the same time, but at a different place – perhaps to draw the crowds away – and yet many came to witness Southwell's death. Having been dragged through the streets on a sled, he stood in the cart beneath the gibbet and made the sign of the cross with his pinioned hands before reciting a Bible passage from <i>Romans 14.</i> The sheriff made to interrupt him; but he was allowed to address the people at some length, confessing that he was a Jesuit priest and praying for the salvation of Queen and country. As the cart was drawn away, he commended his soul to God with the words of the psalm <i>in manus tuas</i>. He hung in the noose for a brief time, making the sign of the cross as best he could. As the executioner made to cut him down, in preparation for <a href="/wiki/Disembowelled" class="mw-redirect" title="Disembowelled">disembowelling</a> him while still alive, <a href="/wiki/Charles_Blount,_8th_Baron_Mountjoy" title="Charles Blount, 8th Baron Mountjoy">Lord Mountjoy</a> and some other onlookers tugged at his legs to hasten his death. His lifeless body was then disembowelled and quartered.  As his severed head was displayed to the crowd, no one shouted the traditional "Traitor!".
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works_and_legacy">Works and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Robert_Southwell_(priest)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Works and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Southwell addressed his <i>Epistle of Comfort</i> to Philip, Earl of Arundel.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> This and other of his religious tracts, <i>A Short Rule of Good Life</i>, <i>Triumphs over Death</i>, and a <i>Humble Supplication to Queen Elizabeth</i>, circulated in manuscript. <i>Mary Magdalen's Funeral Tears</i> was openly published in 1591. It proved to be very popular, going through ten editions by 1636. <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Nashe" title="Thomas Nashe">Thomas Nashe</a>'s imitation of <i>Mary Magdalen's Funeral Tears</i> in <i>Christ's Tears over Jerusalem</i> proves that the works received recognition outside of Catholic circles.<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Soon after Southwell's death, <i>St Peter's Complaint with other poems</i> appeared, printed by <a href="/wiki/John_Windet" title="John Windet">John Windet</a> for <a href="/wiki/John_Wolfe_(printer)" title="John Wolfe (printer)">John Wolfe</a>, but without the author's name. A second edition, including eight more poems, appeared almost immediately.  Then on 5 April, <a href="/wiki/John_Cawood_(printer)" title="John Cawood (printer)">John Cawood</a>, the publisher of <i>Mary Magdalen's Funeral Tears,</i> who probably owned the copyright all along, entered the book in the <a href="/wiki/Stationers%27_Register" title="Stationers&#39; Register">Stationers' Register</a>, and brought out a third edition.  <i>Saint Peter's Complaint</i> proved even more popular than <i>Mary Magdalen's Funeral Tears</i>; it went into fourteen editions by 1636. Later that same year, another publisher, John Busby, having acquired a manuscript of Southwell's collection of lyric poems, brought out a little book containing a further twenty-two poems, under the title <i>Maeoniae</i>. When in 1602 Cawood added another eight poems to his book, the English publication of Southwell's works came to an end. Southwell's <i>Of the Blessed Sacrament of the Altar</i>, unpublishable in England, appeared in a broadsheet published at Douai in 1606. <i>A Foure fould Meditation of the foure last things</i>, formerly attributed to Southwell, is by <a href="/wiki/Philip_Howard,_20th_Earl_of_Arundel" class="mw-redirect" title="Philip Howard, 20th Earl of Arundel">Philip Earl of Arundel</a>. Similarly, the prose <i>A Hundred Meditations of the Love of God</i>, once thought to be Southwell's, is a translation of Fray <a href="/wiki/Diego_de_Estella" title="Diego de Estella">Diego de Estella</a>'s <i>Meditaciones devotisimas del amor de Dios</i>.
</p><p>Much of Southwell's literary legacy rests on his considerable influence on other writers. There is evidence of Shakespeare's allusions to Southwell's work, particularly in The Merchant of Venice, Romeo &amp; Juliet, Hamlet, and King Lear. Southwell's influence can be seen in the work of Donne, Herbert, Crashaw and Hopkins.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A memoir of Southwell was drawn up soon after his death. Much of the material was incorporated by <a href="/wiki/Richard_Challoner" title="Richard Challoner">Richard Challoner</a> in his <i>Memoirs of Missionary Priests </i>(1741), and the manuscript is now in the Public Record Office in Brussels. See also Alexis Possoz, <i>Vie du Pre R. Southwell</i> (1866); and a life in <a href="/wiki/Henry_Foley_(historian)" title="Henry Foley (historian)">Henry Foley</a>'s <i>Records of the English Province of the Society of Jesus: historic facts illustrative of the labours and sufferings of its members in the 16th and 17th centuries</i>, 1877 (i. 301387).  Foley's narrative includes copies of documents connected with his trial, and gives information on the original sources.<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The standard modern life, however, is Christopher Devlin's <i>The Life of Robert Southwell, Poet and Martyr</i>, London, 1956.
</p><p>As the prefatory letter to his poems "The Author to his Loving Cousin" implies, Southwell seems to have composed with musical setting in mind. One such contemporary setting survives, <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Morley" title="Thomas Morley">Thomas Morley</a>'s provision of music for stanzas from "Mary Magdalen's Complaint at Christ's Death" in his  <i>First book of ayres</i> (1600). <a href="/wiki/Elizabeth_Grymeston" class="mw-redirect" title="Elizabeth Grymeston">Elizabeth Grymeston</a>, in a book published for her son (1604), described how she sang stanzas from <i>Saint Peter's Complaint</i> as part of her daily prayer. The best known modern setting of Southwell's words is <a href="/wiki/Benjamin_Britten" title="Benjamin Britten">Benjamin Britten</a>'s use of stanzas from "New Heaven, New War" and "New Prince, New Pomp", two of the pieces in his <i>Ceremony of Carols</i> (1942).
</p><p>In the Elizabethan and Jacobean eras, Southwell and his companion and associate <a href="/wiki/Henry_Garnet" title="Henry Garnet">Henry Garnet</a> were noted for their allegiance to the <a href="/wiki/Doctrine_of_mental_reservation" class="mw-redirect" title="Doctrine of mental reservation">doctrine of mental reservation</a>, a controversial ethical concept of the period.<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Under Southwell's Latinised name, Sotvellus, and in his memory, the English Jesuit <a href="/wiki/Nathaniel_Bacon_(Jesuit)" title="Nathaniel Bacon (Jesuit)">Nathaniel Bacon</a>, Secretary of the Society of Jesus, published the updated third edition of the <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bibliotheca_Scriptorum_Societatis_Iesu&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Bibliotheca Scriptorum Societatis Iesu (page does not exist)">Bibliotheca Scriptorum Societatis Iesu</a></i> (Rome, 1676). This Jesuit bibliography containing more than 8000 authors made "Sotvel" a common reference.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Southwell was <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> in 1929 and <a href="/wiki/Canonised" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonised">canonised</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI" title="Pope Paul VI">Pope Paul VI</a> as one of the <a href="/wiki/Forty_Martyrs_of_England_and_Wales" title="Forty Martyrs of England and Wales">Forty Martyrs of England and Wales</a> on 25 October 1970.<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Southwell is also the patron saint of Southwell House, a house in the <a href="/wiki/London_Oratory_School" title="London Oratory School">London Oratory School</a> in Fulham, London.<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Critical_views">Critical views</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Robert_Southwell_(priest)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Critical views">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Complete_poems_of_Robert_Southwell,_S.J._-_for_the_first_time_fully_collected_and_collated_with_the_original_and_early_editions_and_mss._(1872)_(14587571879).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/04/The_Complete_poems_of_Robert_Southwell%2C_S.J._-_for_the_first_time_fully_collected_and_collated_with_the_original_and_early_editions_and_mss._%281872%29_%2814587571879%29.jpg/220px-thumbnail.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="333" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/04/The_Complete_poems_of_Robert_Southwell%2C_S.J._-_for_the_first_time_fully_collected_and_collated_with_the_original_and_early_editions_and_mss._%281872%29_%2814587571879%29.jpg/330px-thumbnail.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/04/The_Complete_poems_of_Robert_Southwell%2C_S.J._-_for_the_first_time_fully_collected_and_collated_with_the_original_and_early_editions_and_mss._%281872%29_%2814587571879%29.jpg/440px-thumbnail.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1692" data-file-height="2560" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Complete_poems_of_Robert_Southwell,_S.J._-_for_the_first_time_fully_collected_and_collated_with_the_original_and_early_editions_and_mss._(1872)_(14587571879).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Frontispiece of <i>Saint Peter's complaint</i></div></div></div>
<p>In the view of the critic <a href="/wiki/Helen_C._White" title="Helen C. White">Helen C. White</a>, probably no work of Southwell's is more "representative of his <a href="/wiki/Baroque" title="Baroque">Baroque</a> genius than the prose <i>Marie Magdalens Funeral Teares</i>, published late in 1591, close to the end of his career. The very choice of this subject would seem the epitome of the Baroque; for it is a commonplace that the penitent <a href="/wiki/Mary_Magdalen" class="mw-redirect" title="Mary Magdalen">Magdalen</a>, with her combination of past sensuality and current remorsefulness, was a favourite object of contemplation to the Counter-Reformation."<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Southwell's poetry is largely addressed to an English Catholic community under siege in post-Reformation Elizabethan England.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Southwell endeavored to convince remaining English Catholics that <a href="/wiki/Religious_persecution" title="Religious persecution">religious persecution</a> by the State represented an opportunity for spiritual growth. In his view, <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyr" title="Christian martyr">martyrdom</a> was one of the most sincere forms of religious devotion. Southwell's poem "Life is but Losse" is an example of this concern. Throughout the seven stanzas, Southwell describes the martyrdom of English Catholics at the time, employing biblical figures of both Testaments (<a href="/wiki/Samson" title="Samson">Samson</a> and the Apostles). The poem's title forewarns the reader of the pessimistic tone Southwell uses to describe life, as in the line "Life is but losse, where death is deemed gaine." Being next to God is the perfect way to achieve spiritual bliss: "To him I live, for him I hope to dye" is Southwell's manner of informing the reader of the reason for his existence, which does not end with death.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Southwell's writing differs from that of the <a href="/wiki/Neostoicism" title="Neostoicism">Neostoics</a> of his time and the negative <a href="/wiki/Stoic_passions" title="Stoic passions">Stoic view of the passions</a> in his belief in the creative value of <a href="/wiki/Passion_(emotion)" title="Passion (emotion)">passion</a>. Some of Southwell's contemporaries were also defenders of passion, but he was very selective when it came to where passions were directed. He was quoted as saying, "Passions I allow, and loves I approve, only I would wish that men would alter their object and better their intent." He felt that he could use his writing to stir religious feelings; and it is this pattern in his writing that has caused scholars to declare him a leading Baroque writer.
</p><p>Pierre Janelle published a study on Southwell in 1935 in which he recognized him as a pioneer Baroque figure, one of the first Baroque writers of the late 16th century and influential on numerous Baroque writers of the 17th century.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Ben_Jonson" title="Ben Jonson">Ben Jonson</a> remarked to <a href="/wiki/Drummond_of_Hawthornden" class="mw-redirect" title="Drummond of Hawthornden">Drummond of Hawthornden</a> that "so he had written that piece of [Southwell's], 'The Burning Babe', he would have been content to destroy many of his."<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> In fact, there is a strong case to be made for Southwell's influence on his contemporaries and successors, among them Drayton, Lodge, Nashe, Herbert, Crashaw, and especially <a href="/wiki/Shakespeare" class="mw-redirect" title="Shakespeare">Shakespeare</a>, who seems to have known his work, both poetry and prose, extremely well.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>More recently, the posthumously published 1873 first edition of Southwell's <a href="/wiki/Literary_translation" class="mw-redirect" title="Literary translation">literary translation</a> into <a href="/wiki/Elizabethan_English" class="mw-redirect" title="Elizabethan English">Elizabethan English</a> of Fray <a href="/wiki/Diego_de_Estella" title="Diego de Estella">Diego de Estella</a>'s <i>Meditaciónes devotíssimas del amor de Dios</i> ("A Hundred Meditations on the Love of God") helped inspire Fr. <a href="/wiki/Gerard_Manley_Hopkins" title="Gerard Manley Hopkins">Gerard Manley Hopkins</a> to write the poem <i><a href="/wiki/The_Windhover" title="The Windhover">The Windhover</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Quotations">Quotations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Robert_Southwell_(priest)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Quotations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<ul><li>"The <a href="/wiki/Chief_Justice" class="mw-redirect" title="Chief Justice">Chief Justice</a> asked how old he was, seeming to scorn his youth. He answered that he was near about the age of our Saviour, Who lived upon the earth thirty-three years; and he himself was as he thought near about thirty-four years. Hereat Topcliffe seemed to make great acclamation, saying that he compared himself to Christ. Father Southwell answered, 'No he was a humble worm created by Christ.' 'Yes,' said Topcliffe, 'you are Christ's fellow.'"—Father Henry Garnet, "Account of the Trial of Robert Southwell," quoted in Caraman's <i>The Other Face</i>, page 230.</li>
<li><b>Southwell</b>: I am decayed in memory with long and close imprisonment, and I have been tortured ten times. I had rather have endured ten executions. I speak not this for myself, but for others; that they may not be handled so inhumanely, to drive men to desperation, if it were possible.<br /><b>Topcliffe</b>: If he were <a href="/wiki/Rack_(torture_device)" class="mw-redirect" title="Rack (torture device)">racked</a>, let me die for it.<br /><b>Southwell</b>: No; but it was as evil a torture, or late device.<br /><b>Topcliffe</b>: I did but set him against a wall.<br /><b>Southwell</b>: Thou art a bad man.<br /><b>Topcliffe</b>: I would blow you all to dust if I could.<br /><b>Southwell</b>: What, all?<br /><b>Topcliffe</b>: Ay, all.<br /><b>Southwell</b>: What, <a href="/wiki/Soul" title="Soul">soul</a> and body too? <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.pbs.org/shakespeare/players/player40.html">At his Trial</a> <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://web.archive.org/web/20160303171448/http://www.pbs.org/shakespeare/players/player40.html">Archived</a> 3 March 2016 at the <a href="/wiki/Wayback_Machine" title="Wayback Machine">Wayback Machine</a></li>
<li>"Not where I breathe, but where I love, I live" on the outside of The DeNaples Center at the Jesuit <a href="/wiki/University_of_Scranton" title="University of Scranton">University of Scranton</a>. Longer version: "Not where I breathe, but where I love, I live; / Not where I love, but where I am, I die."</li>
<li>"Hoist up saile while gale doth last, Tide and wind stay no man's pleasure."—from "St. Peter's Complaint. 1595"<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>"May never was the month of love, For May is full of flowers; But rather April, wet by kind, For love is full of showers."—from "Love's Servile Lot"<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>"My mind to me an empire is, While grace affordeth health."—from "Look Home"<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>"O dying souls, behold your living spring; O dazzled eyes, behold your sun of grace; Dull ears, attend what word this Word doth bring; Up, heavy hearts, with joy your joy embrace. From death, from dark, from deafness, from despair: This life, this light, this Word, this joy repairs."—from "The Nativity of Christ"<sup id="cite_ref-luminarium_5-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-luminarium-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>"A poet, a lover and a liar are by many reckoned but three words with one signification." – from "The author to his loving cousin," published with "St. Peter's Complaint." 1595.</li></ul>
