<p><b>Mark of Ephesus</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek:</a> Μάρκος ό Εφέσιος, born <b>Manuel Eugenikos</b>) was a <a href="/wiki/Hesychast" class="mw-redirect" title="Hesychast">hesychast</a> theologian of the late <a href="/wiki/Palaiologan_period" class="mw-redirect" title="Palaiologan period">Palaiologan period</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine Empire</a> who became famous for his rejection of the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Florence" title="Council of Florence">Council of Ferrara-Florence</a> (1438–1439). As a monk in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>, Mark was a prolific <a href="/wiki/Hymnographer" class="mw-redirect" title="Hymnographer">hymnographer</a><sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> and a follower of <a href="/wiki/Gregory_Palamas" title="Gregory Palamas">Gregory Palamas</a>' theological views. As a theologian and a scholar, he was instrumental in the preparations for the Council of Ferrara-Florence, and as <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_of_Ephesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Metropolitan of Ephesus">Metropolitan of Ephesus</a> and delegate for the <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_of_Alexandria" title="Patriarch of Alexandria">Patriarch of Alexandria</a>, he was one of the most important voices at the synod. After renouncing the council as a lost cause, Mark became the leader of the Orthodox opposition to the Union of Florence, thus sealing his reputation as a defender of <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy" title="Eastern Orthodoxy">Eastern Orthodoxy</a> and pillar of the Eastern Orthodox Church.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Mark was born Manuel in 1392 in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> to George, <i><a href="/wiki/Sakellarios" title="Sakellarios">sakellarios</a></i> of <a href="/wiki/Hagia_Sophia" title="Hagia Sophia">Hagia Sophia</a>, an Orthodox <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a>, and Maria, the daughter of a devout doctor named Luke. Mark learned how to read and write from his father, who died while Mark and his younger brother <a href="/wiki/John_Eugenikos" title="John Eugenikos">John Eugenikos</a> were still children. Maria had Mark continue his education under <a href="/wiki/John_Chortasmenos" title="John Chortasmenos">John Chortasmenos</a>, who later became Metropolitan <a href="/wiki/Ignatius_of_Selymbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Ignatius of Selymbria">Ignatius of Selymbria</a>, and a <a href="/wiki/Mathematician" title="Mathematician">mathematician</a> and <a href="/wiki/Philosopher" class="mw-redirect" title="Philosopher">philosopher</a> by the name of <a href="/wiki/Gemistus_Pletho" class="mw-redirect" title="Gemistus Pletho">Gemistus Pletho</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Activity_at_the_Council_of_Florence_and_aftermath">Activity at the Council of Florence and aftermath</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Activity at the Council of Florence and aftermath">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Mark was the only Eastern bishop who refused to sign the agreement with the Roman Catholic Church on a compromise formula, "ex filio", for the <a href="/wiki/Filioque" title="Filioque">Filioque</a> clause disagreement during the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Ferrara" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Ferrara">Council of Ferrara</a>. He held that Rome continued in both <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heresy</a> and <a href="/wiki/Schism_(religion)" class="mw-redirect" title="Schism (religion)">schism</a>. Likewise the <a href="/wiki/Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic">Catholic</a> church considered him to be a heretic. He also rejected the doctrine of <a href="/wiki/Purgatory" title="Purgatory">Purgatory</a>, in that he objected to the existence of a purgatorial fire that "purified" the souls of the faithful.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He died peacefully at the age of 52 on June 23, 1444, after an excruciating two-week battle with intestinal illness. On his death bed, Mark implored Georgios Scholarios, his former pupil, who later became Patriarch <a href="/wiki/Gennadius_Scholarius" title="Gennadius Scholarius">Gennadius of Constantinople</a>, to be careful of involvement with Western Christendom and to defend Orthodoxy. According to his brother John, his last words were "Jesus Christ, Son of the Living God, into Thy hands I commit my spirit." Mark was buried in the Mangana Monastery in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Posthumous_miracle_and_canonization">Posthumous miracle and canonization</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Posthumous miracle and canonization">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Eugenikos family celebrated each anniversary of Mark's death with a <a href="/wiki/Eulogy" title="Eulogy">eulogy</a> consisting of a <a href="/wiki/Church_service" title="Church service">service</a> (akolouthia) and <a href="/wiki/Synaxarion" class="mw-redirect" title="Synaxarion">synaxarion</a> of a short life of Mark. Thanks in large part to Patriarch Gennadius Scholarius, veneration of Mark spread among the church. In 1734 Patriarch <a href="/w/index.php?title=Ecumenical_Patriarch_Serapheim_I_of_Constantinople&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Ecumenical Patriarch Serapheim I of Constantinople (page does not exist)">Seraphim of Constantinople</a> presided over the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Synod" title="Holy Synod">Holy Synod</a> of the Church of Constantinople and solemnly <a href="/wiki/Glorification" title="Glorification">glorified</a> (canonized) Mark and added six services to the two older ones.
</p><p>There is an account of a posthumous <a href="/wiki/Miracle" title="Miracle">miracle</a> performed by Mark of Ephesus. Doctors gave up on trying to save the life of the terminally ill sister of Demetrios Zourbaios, after their efforts had worsened her condition. After <a href="/wiki/Coma" title="Coma">losing consciousness</a> for three days she suddenly woke up, to the delight of her brother, who asked her why she woke up drenched in water. She related that a bishop escorted her to a fountain and washed her and told her, "Return now; you no longer have any illness." She asked him who he was and he informed her, "I am the Metropolitan of Ephesus, Mark Eugenikos." After being miraculously healed, she made an <a href="/wiki/Icon" title="Icon">icon</a> of Saint Mark and lived devoutly for another 15 years.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:SaintMarkRochesterMinnesota.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/SaintMarkRochesterMinnesota.jpg/220px-SaintMarkRochesterMinnesota.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/SaintMarkRochesterMinnesota.jpg/330px-SaintMarkRochesterMinnesota.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/SaintMarkRochesterMinnesota.jpg/440px-SaintMarkRochesterMinnesota.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3024" data-file-height="4032" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Icon" title="Icon">Icon</a> of Saint Mark in an Orthodox parish in the United States</figcaption></figure>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Orthodox Church</a> considers Mark of Ephesus a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>, calling him, together with <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_Photios_I_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Patriarch Photios I of Constantinople">Photius the Great</a> and <a href="/wiki/Gregory_Palamas" title="Gregory Palamas">Gregory Palamas</a>, a <b>Pillar of Orthodoxy</b>. <a href="/wiki/Nicodemus_the_Hagiorite" title="Nicodemus the Hagiorite">Nikodimos of the Holy Mountain</a>, in his service to the saint, called him "the Atlas of Orthodoxy." His feast day is January 19, the day his <a href="/wiki/Relics" class="mw-redirect" title="Relics">relics</a> were moved to the monastery of Lazarus in Galata.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Theology">Theology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Theology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Mark's theological output was extensive and covered a wide range of genres and topics typical of monastic writers.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Hymnography">Hymnography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Hymnography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Mark composed a wealth of poetic texts honoring God and the saints, many of which were intended for use in a liturgical setting. In addition to canons and services to Jesus Christ, the Mother of God, and the angels, Mark honored his favorite Fathers of the Church: Gregory Palamas, John Damascene, Symeon Metaphrastes, along with a wealth of more ancient saints. Additionally, Mark composed verses celebrating the lives and achievements of his heroes, such as Joseph Bryennios.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Hesychasm">Hesychasm</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Hesychasm">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Mark was a devoted disciple of Gregory Palamas. Throughout his life he composed several treatises in defense of the essence-energies distinction, and he defended the unique contributions of Hesychast theology in the face of charges of innovation.
</p>
<h3><span id="Dogmatic.2Fpolemical_theology"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Dogmatic/polemical_theology">Dogmatic/polemical theology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Dogmatic/polemical theology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>One of Mark's most important theological contributions was his opposition to the Roman Catholic Filioque. At the Council of Florence, the examination of this controversy had both text-critical and exegetical dimensions, as the participants debated the authenticity of sources, the precision of grammatical constructions, and the canon of authoritative patristic texts. Mark had played an early role in the gathering of manuscripts, and his contested readings at the synod have since been vindicated for their precision and accuracy.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Echoing centuries of polemic, going back to Photius, the debates surrounding the Filioque admitted resonances of more recent discussions, such as those of John Bekkos and Gregory of Cyprus. In the end, Mark could not concede that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son as well as the Father, even by using the Orthodox phrase of "through the Son," since Mark considered this to be an equivocation in light of the obvious theological disagreements between East and West. For Eugenikos, the Holy Spirit proceeds only from the Father, and the phrase "through the Son" did not express anything like the theology of the Filioque.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Anthropology">Anthropology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Anthropology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Mark's criticism of the doctrine of Purgatory, the other major topic at Florence, involved him in questions relating to the nature of the human composite. Basing himself on Palamite conceptions of man, Mark articulated a theory of the human person rooted in Christology and Orthodox doctrines of Creation. Mark's discourses at Florence are, in this regard, supplemented by writings which he produced in response to the Platonism of Gemisthus Plethon, who preached a radical identification of the human person with the soul, to the detriment of embodied life.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Latin_influences">Latin influences</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Mark_of_Ephesus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Latin influences">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The Palaiologan period in which Mark lived witnessed the translation of the works of Thomas Aquinas into Greek, an event whose repercussions have yet to be fully documented and expounded. In spite of remaining ambiguities, though, it has become increasingly clear that the Hesychasts were not unaware of Latin theology.
</p><p>Some scholars have hypothesized that Mark adopts Thomas Aquinas' hylomorphism in his defense of the Resurrection, and that he experiments with arguments from Thomas Aquinas' Summa contra Gentiles in order to argue for the concomitance of mercy in God's condemnation of unrepentant sinners to hell. "Further, Eugenicus’ treatise Peri anastaseôs (ed. A. SCHMEMANN, “Une œuvre inédite de St Marc d’Éphèse: Peri anastaseôs”, in: Theología 22 (1951): 51–64; text on pp.&#160;53–60) is merely a defence of the rational possibility of the doctrine of resurrection and a rational refutation of some philosophical objections against it based almost entirely on Thomas Aquinas’ description of the natural unity of the human soul with the body in explicitly hylomorphic and anti-Platonic terms (see Summa contra Gentiles IV,79-81; cf. Vat. gr. 616, ff. 289r-294v)."<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Also, eminent Eastern Orthodox scholars have already suggested that, during Mark's courteous and apologetic period of preparation for the Council of Florence, the Ephesine explicitly omitted condemnations of Aquinas by name, even if his two "Antirrhetics against Manuel Kalekas" contained condemnation of Thomistic Trinitarian theology, especially Thomas' implicit rejection of the essence-energies distinction and the Thomas' assertion that Aristotelean "habits" were very similar to the charisms or the Holy Spirit (e.g., Faith, Hope, &amp; Charity).<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> What is more, Mark had a great love for <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</a>, spending money at Florence to buy manuscripts of the same. In fact, at Florence he copiously cites Augustinian and Ps.-Augustinian works approvingly as an authority in favor of Orthodoxy.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Finally, Mark was savvy enough to understand <a href="/wiki/Bernard_of_Clairvaux" title="Bernard of Clairvaux">Bernard of Clairvaux</a>'s writings from an unknown translation (likely through his former pupil Scholarius). He used Bernard to argue for the Palamite position on the beatific vision.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In effect, Mark of Ephesus' liberal use of Latin authorities paved the way for the fuller synthesis of his pupil Gennadius Scholarius after his master's death.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Even if Mark was initially zealous for the "divine work of union" (τὸ θεῖον ἔργον τῆς εἰρήνης καὶ ἑνώσεως τῶν ἐκκλησιῶν)<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> in his opening speech to Pope Eugene IV, Mark was disgusted at the Latin efforts and plots to prevent him from reading the acts of the Ecumenical Councils aloud, wherein the canons prohibited additions to the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed. At the beginning of the council, in the same opening speech, Mark noted that two issues alone were necessary to overcome the historical divisions between the Latin and Greek Churches; namely,  (1.) The issue of the Filioque or Latins' assertion that the Father and the Son are conjointly a cause of the Holy Spirit, and (2.) the issue of unleavened bread, whereby the Latins had historically abandoned the orthodox (vs. Armenian) practice of using leavened bread for the sake of azymes. Because Mark found reasons to reject uniquely Latin authorities, councils, and texts unavailable in Greek or unapproved by jointly canonical synods, he became increasingly exasperated at Latin attempts to introduce a host of new authorities into the debates that often turned out to be spurious.  In the end, Mark refused to sign the council documents following a debate with John Montenero, who claimed that there was a difference in the dignity of persons in the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. This had the effect of convincing Mark that the Thomists were subordinationists since Montenero was willing to defend a distinction in the persons according to "dignity" (dignitas or axiôma).<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Unsurprisingly, Mark concentrated on anti-Latin propaganda by recourse to differences that were most obvious to the simple Greek populace. For example, he noted that the Latins had no throne reserved for their hierarch in the sanctuary, the Latin priests shaved their faces "like women celebrating Mass," and various other minutiae.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> In this, Mark did not ignore various abstractions of metaphysics, yet he was more realistic and practical in his apologetics and an excellent propagandist. Therefore, one must read Mark's post-Florentine and anti-Latin propaganda through the optic of Mark's ends. Even if Mark dissented from Florence for traditional, theological motives he was quick to cite practical differences that emphasized the gulf between Latins and Greeks.
</p>
