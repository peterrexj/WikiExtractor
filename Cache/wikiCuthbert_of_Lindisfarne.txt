<p><b>Cuthbert of Lindisfarne</b><sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;634</span> – 20 March 687) was an <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxon</a> saint of the early Northumbrian church in the <a href="/wiki/Hiberno-Scottish_mission" title="Hiberno-Scottish mission">Celtic tradition</a>. He was a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a>, associated with the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monasteries</a> of <a href="/wiki/Melrose_Abbey#History" title="Melrose Abbey">Melrose</a> and <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of Northumbria">Kingdom of Northumbria</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> today in <a href="/wiki/North_East_England_(traditional)" class="mw-redirect" title="North East England (traditional)">north-eastern England</a> and south-eastern Scotland. Both during his life and after his death he became a popular medieval <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Northern_England" title="Northern England">Northern England</a>, with a <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cult</a> centred on his tomb at <a href="/wiki/Durham_Cathedral" title="Durham Cathedral">Durham Cathedral</a>. Cuthbert is regarded as the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of Northumbria. His feast days are 20 March (<a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a>, <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church</a><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>) and 4 September (<a href="/wiki/Church_in_Wales" title="Church in Wales">Church in Wales</a>, Catholic Church).
</p><p>Cuthbert grew up in or around <a href="/wiki/Lauderdale" title="Lauderdale">Lauderdale</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Melrose_Abbey" title="Melrose Abbey">Old Melrose Abbey</a>, a daughter-house of Lindisfarne, today in Scotland. He decided to become a monk after seeing a vision on the night in 651 that <a href="/wiki/Aidan_of_Lindisfarne" title="Aidan of Lindisfarne">Aidan</a>, the founder of Lindisfarne, died, but he seems to have experienced some period of  military service beforehand. He was made guest-master at the new monastery at <a href="/wiki/Ripon" title="Ripon">Ripon</a>, soon after 655, but had to return with <a href="/wiki/Eata_of_Hexham" title="Eata of Hexham">Eata of Hexham</a> to Melrose when <a href="/wiki/Wilfrid" title="Wilfrid">Wilfrid</a> was given the monastery instead.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956120–125_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956120–125-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer199557_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer199557-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> About 662 he was made prior at Melrose, and around 665 went as prior to Lindisfarne. In 684 he was made bishop of Lindisfarne, but by late 686 he resigned and returned to his hermitage as he felt he was about to die. He was probably in his early 50s.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956125–141_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956125–141-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer199560_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer199560-13">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Origins_and_background">Origins and background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Origins and background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Cuthbert was born (perhaps into a noble family) in <a href="/wiki/Dunbar" title="Dunbar">Dunbar</a>, then in Anglo-Saxon Northumbria, and now in East Lothian, <a href="/wiki/Scotland" title="Scotland">Scotland</a>, in the mid-630s, some ten years after the conversion of <a href="/wiki/Edwin_of_Northumbria" title="Edwin of Northumbria">King Edwin of Northumbria</a> to Christianity in 627, which was slowly followed by that of the rest of his people. The politics of the kingdom were violent, and there were later episodes of pagan rule, while spreading understanding of Christianity through the kingdom was a task that lasted throughout Cuthbert's lifetime. Edwin had been baptised by <a href="/wiki/Paulinus_of_York" title="Paulinus of York">Paulinus of York</a>, a Roman who had come with the <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_mission" title="Gregorian mission">Gregorian mission</a> from Rome, but his successor <a href="/wiki/Oswald_of_Northumbria" title="Oswald of Northumbria">Oswald</a> also invited Irish monks from <a href="/wiki/Iona" title="Iona">Iona</a> to found the monastery at Lindisfarne where Cuthbert was to spend much of his life. This was around 635, about the time Cuthbert was born.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956115–116_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956115–116-14">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The tension between the Roman and Celtic Christianity, often exacerbated by Cuthbert's near-contemporary <a href="/wiki/Wilfrid" title="Wilfrid">Wilfrid</a>, an intransigent and quarrelsome supporter of Roman ways, was to be a major feature of Cuthbert's lifetime. Cuthbert himself, though educated in the Celtic tradition, followed his mentor <a href="/wiki/Eata_of_Hexham" title="Eata of Hexham">Eata</a> in accepting the Roman forms, apparently without difficulty, after the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Whitby" title="Synod of Whitby">Synod of Whitby</a> in 664.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956122–129_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956122–129-15">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer199553–54,_60–66_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer199553–54,_60–66-16">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrown200364–66_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200364–66-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup> The earliest biographies concentrate on the many miracles that accompanied even his early life, but he was evidently indefatigable as a travelling priest spreading the Christian message to remote villages, and also well able to impress royalty and nobility. Unlike Wilfrid, his style of life was austere, and when he could, he lived the life of a hermit, though still receiving many visitors.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956115–141_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe1956115–141-19">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer199552–53,_57–60_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer199552–53,_57–60-20">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In Cuthbert's time the Anglo-Saxon Kingdom of Northumbria included, in modern terms, part of northern England as well as parts of south-eastern Scotland on an intermittent and fluid basis as far north as the <a href="/wiki/Firth_of_Forth" title="Firth of Forth">Firth of Forth</a>. Cuthbert may have been from the neighbourhood of Dunbar at the mouth of the Firth of Forth in modern-day Scotland, though <i>The Lives of the Fathers, Martyrs and Other Principal Saints</i> ("Butler's Lives"), by <a href="/wiki/Alban_Butler" title="Alban Butler">Alban Butler</a> records that he was fostered as a child near Melrose. Fostering is possibly a sign of noble birth, as are references to his riding a horse when young. One night while still a boy, employed as a shepherd, he had a vision of the soul of Aidan being carried to heaven by <a href="/wiki/Angel" title="Angel">angels</a>, and later found out that Aidan had died that night. Edwin Burton finds it a suggestion of lowly parentage that as a boy he used to tend sheep on the hills near that monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBurton1908_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurton1908-21">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> He appears to have undergone military service, but at some point he joined the very new monastery at Melrose, under the prior <a href="/wiki/Boisil" title="Boisil">Boisil</a>. Upon Boisil's death in 661, Cuthbert succeeded him as prior.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBurton1908_21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurton1908-21">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Cuthbert was possibly a second cousin of King <a href="/wiki/Aldfrith_of_Northumbria" title="Aldfrith of Northumbria">Aldfrith of Northumbria</a> (according to Irish genealogies), which may explain his later proposal that Aldfrith should be crowned as monarch.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHealy190978_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHealy190978-22">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEIreland199164_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEIreland199164-23">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Career">Career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Durham_St_Cuthbert.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a2/Durham_St_Cuthbert.jpg/220px-Durham_St_Cuthbert.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="298" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a2/Durham_St_Cuthbert.jpg/330px-Durham_St_Cuthbert.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a2/Durham_St_Cuthbert.jpg 2x" data-file-width="406" data-file-height="550" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Durham_St_Cuthbert.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>12th century wall-painting of St Cuthbert in <a href="/wiki/Durham_Cathedral" title="Durham Cathedral">Durham Cathedral</a></div></div></div>
<p>Cuthbert's fame for piety, diligence, and obedience grew. When <a href="/wiki/Alchfrith_of_Deira" class="mw-redirect" title="Alchfrith of Deira">Alchfrith</a>, king of <a href="/wiki/Deira_(kingdom)" class="mw-redirect" title="Deira (kingdom)">Deira</a>, founded a new monastery at <a href="/wiki/Ripon" title="Ripon">Ripon</a>, Cuthbert became its <i>praepositus hospitum</i> or guest master under Eata. When Wilfrid was made abbot of the monastery, Eata and Cuthbert returned to Melrose. Illness struck the monastery in 664 and while Cuthbert recovered, the <a href="/wiki/Prior" title="Prior">prior</a> died and Cuthbert was made prior in his place.<sup id="cite_ref-scl_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-scl-24">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Melrose_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Melrose-25">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> He spent much time among the people, ministering to their spiritual needs, carrying out missionary journeys, preaching, and performing miracles.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>After the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Whitby" title="Synod of Whitby">Synod of Whitby</a>, Cuthbert seems to have accepted the Roman customs, and his old abbot Eata called on him to introduce them at Lindisfarne as prior there. His <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">asceticism</a> was complemented by his charm and generosity to the poor, and his reputation for gifts of healing and insight led many people to consult him, gaining him the name of "Wonder Worker of Britain". He continued his missionary work, travelling the breadth of the country from <a href="/wiki/County_of_Berwick" class="mw-redirect" title="County of Berwick">Berwick</a> to <a href="/wiki/Galloway" title="Galloway">Galloway</a> to carry out pastoral work and founding an oratory at <a href="/wiki/Dull,_Perth_%26_Kinross" class="mw-redirect" title="Dull, Perth &amp; Kinross">Dull, Scotland</a>, complete with a large stone cross, and a little cell for himself. He is also said to have founded <a href="/wiki/The_Parish_Church_of_St_Cuthbert" class="mw-redirect" title="The Parish Church of St Cuthbert">St Cuthbert's Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Edinburgh" title="Edinburgh">Edinburgh</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Hermit.27s_life"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Hermit's_life">Hermit's life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Hermit&#039;s life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Cuthbert_and_%C3%86lfflaed_at_Coquet_Island.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/35/Cuthbert_and_%C3%86lfflaed_at_Coquet_Island.jpg/220px-Cuthbert_and_%C3%86lfflaed_at_Coquet_Island.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="319" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/35/Cuthbert_and_%C3%86lfflaed_at_Coquet_Island.jpg/330px-Cuthbert_and_%C3%86lfflaed_at_Coquet_Island.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/35/Cuthbert_and_%C3%86lfflaed_at_Coquet_Island.jpg/440px-Cuthbert_and_%C3%86lfflaed_at_Coquet_Island.jpg 2x" data-file-width="598" data-file-height="866" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Cuthbert_and_%C3%86lfflaed_at_Coquet_Island.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Cuthbert meets <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lffl%C3%A6d_of_Whitby" title="Ælfflæd of Whitby">Ælfflæd of Whitby</a> on Coquet Island, <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>'s Life of Cuthbert, 12th century</div></div></div>
<p>Cuthbert retired in 676, moved by the desire for a more contemplative life. With his abbot's leave, he moved to a spot which Archbishop Eyre identifies with St Cuthbert's Island near Lindisfarne, but which Raine<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERaine1828ii_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERaine1828ii-27">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> thinks was near <a href="/wiki/Holburn" title="Holburn">Holburn</a>, at a place now known as <a href="/wiki/St_Cuthbert%27s_Cave" title="St Cuthbert&#39;s Cave">St Cuthbert's Cave</a>. Shortly afterwards, Cuthbert moved to <a href="/wiki/Inner_Farne" class="mw-redirect" title="Inner Farne">Inner Farne</a> island, two miles from <a href="/wiki/Bamburgh" title="Bamburgh">Bamburgh</a>, off the coast of <a href="/wiki/Northumberland" title="Northumberland">Northumberland</a>, where he gave himself up to a life of great austerity.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBurton1908_21-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurton1908-21">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> At first he received visitors, but later he confined himself to his cell and opened his window only to give his blessing. He could not refuse an interview with the holy abbess and royal virgin <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lffl%C3%A6d_of_Whitby" title="Ælfflæd of Whitby">Elfleda</a>, the daughter of <a href="/wiki/Oswiu_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Oswiu of Northumbria">Oswiu of Northumbria</a>, who succeeded <a href="/wiki/Hilda_of_Whitby" title="Hilda of Whitby">St Hilda</a> as abbess of Whitby in 680. The meeting was held on <a href="/wiki/Coquet_Island,_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Coquet Island, England">Coquet Island</a>, further south off the Northumberland coast.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEButler1833371_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEButler1833371-28">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Election_as_Bishop.2C_Lindisfarne_and_death"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Election_as_Bishop,_Lindisfarne_and_death">Election as Bishop, Lindisfarne and death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Election as Bishop, Lindisfarne and death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 684, Cuthbert was elected <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Hexham" title="Bishop of Hexham">Bishop of Hexham</a> at a synod at Twyford (believed to be present-day <a href="/wiki/Alnmouth" title="Alnmouth">Alnmouth</a>),<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEUrban1852504_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEUrban1852504-29">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> but was reluctant to leave his retirement and take up his charge; it was only after a visit from a large group, including King <a href="/wiki/Ecgfrith_of_Northumbria" title="Ecgfrith of Northumbria">Ecgfrith</a>, that he agreed to return and take up the duties of bishop, but instead as <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Lindisfarne" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Lindisfarne">Bishop of Lindisfarne</a>, swapping with Eata, who went to Hexham in Cuthbert's place.  Cuthbert was consecrated at <a href="/wiki/York" title="York">York</a> by <a href="/wiki/Theodore_of_Tarsus" title="Theodore of Tarsus">Archbishop Theodore</a> and six bishops, on 26 March 685. But after Christmas 686 he returned to his cell on Inner Farne Island, where he died on 20 March 687, after a painful illness.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011108_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer2011108-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He was buried at Lindisfarne the same day, and after long journeys escaping the Danes his remains chose, as was thought, to settle at <a href="/wiki/Durham,_England" title="Durham, England">Durham</a>, causing the foundation of the city and Durham Cathedral. The <a href="/wiki/St_Cuthbert_Gospel" title="St Cuthbert Gospel">St Cuthbert Gospel</a> is among the objects later recovered from <a href="/wiki/St_Cuthbert%27s_coffin" title="St Cuthbert&#39;s coffin">St Cuthbert's coffin</a>, which is also an important artefact.
</p>

goatskin binding is the earliest surviving Western binding.</div></div></div>
<p>After Cuthbert's death, numerous <a href="/wiki/Miracle" title="Miracle">miracles</a> were attributed to his intercession and to intercessory <a href="/wiki/Prayer" title="Prayer">prayer</a> near his remains. In particular, <a href="/wiki/Alfred_the_Great" title="Alfred the Great">Alfred the Great</a>, <a href="/wiki/King_of_Wessex" class="mw-redirect" title="King of Wessex">King of Wessex</a>, was inspired and encouraged in his struggle against the Danes by a vision or dream he had of Cuthbert. Thereafter the royal <a href="/wiki/House_of_Wessex" title="House of Wessex">house of Wessex</a>, who became the kings of England, made a point of devotion to Cuthbert, which also gave a useful political message, as they came from opposite ends of the English nation. Cuthbert was "a figure of reconciliation and a rallying point for the reformed identity of Northumbria and England" after the absorption of the <a href="/wiki/Danelaw" title="Danelaw">Danish populations</a> into Anglo-Saxon society, as <a href="/wiki/Michelle_P._Brown" title="Michelle P. Brown">Michelle Brown</a> puts it.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe195631–34_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBattiscombe195631–34-30">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrown200364_(quoted)_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200364_(quoted)-31">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> The 8th-century historian Bede wrote both a verse and a prose life of St Cuthbert around 720. He has been described as "perhaps the most popular saint in England prior to the death of <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Becket" title="Thomas Becket">Thomas Becket</a> in 1170."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMarner20009_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarner20009-32">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> In 698 Cuthbert was reburied at Lindisfarne in the decorated oak coffin now usually meant by St Cuthbert's coffin, though he was to have many more coffins.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;d&#93;</a></sup> In 995 the "community of Cuthbert" founded and settled at Durham, guided by what they thought was the will of the saint, as the wagon carrying his coffin back to Chester-le-Street after a temporary flight from a Danish invasion became stuck hard on the road.
</p><p>During the medieval period, Cuthbert became important in defining the identity of the people living in Northumbria north of Tees. Symeon noted that it was the 'people of St Cuthbert', that is, 'the whole people between the river Tees and the river Tweed', who waged an unsuccessful campaign against the Scots at the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Carham" title="Battle of Carham">Battle of Carham</a> in 1018.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> By the later 11th century the Bishops of Durham had established a semi-autonomous region known as the <a href="/wiki/Liberty_of_Durham" title="Liberty of Durham">Liberty of Durham</a>, later the <a href="/wiki/Palatinate_of_Durham" class="mw-redirect" title="Palatinate of Durham">Palatinate of Durham</a>, between the Tyne and Tees. Within this area the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Durham" title="Bishop of Durham">Bishop of Durham</a> had almost as much power as the <a href="/wiki/Monarch" title="Monarch">king</a> of England himself, and the saint became a powerful symbol of the autonomy the region enjoyed. The inhabitants of the Palatinate became known as the <i>haliwerfolc</i>, which roughly translates as "people of the saint", and Cuthbert gained a reputation as fiercely protective of his domain.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELapsley1900_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELapsley1900-35">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> For example, there is a story that at the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Neville%27s_Cross" title="Battle of Neville&#39;s Cross">Battle of Neville's Cross</a> in 1346, the Prior of the Abbey at Durham received a vision of Cuthbert, ordering him to take the <a href="/wiki/Corporal_(liturgy)" title="Corporal (liturgy)">corporax</a> cloth of the saint and raise it on a spear point near the battlefield as a banner. Doing this, the Prior and his monks found themselves protected "by the mediation of holy St Cuthbert and the presence of the said holy Relic".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFowler1903107_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFowler1903107-36">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> Whether the story of the vision is true or not, the banner of St Cuthbert was regularly carried in battle against the Scots until the <a href="/wiki/English_Reformation" title="English Reformation">Reformation</a>, and it serves as a good example of how St Cuthbert was regarded as a protector of his people. A modern interpretation of the Banner, designed by Northumbria University academic Fiona Raeside-Elliott and embroidered by local textile artist Ruth O'Leary, is now on display at the saint's shrine in Durham Cathedral.
</p><p>Cuthbert's cult also appealed to the converted Danes who now made up much of the population of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_York" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of York">Kingdom of York</a>, and was also adopted by the Normans when they took over England. Cuthbert's shrine at Durham Cathedral was a major pilgrimage site throughout the Middle Ages, until stripped by Henry VIII's commissioners in the <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Monasteries" class="mw-redirect" title="Dissolution of the Monasteries">Dissolution of the Monasteries</a>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Relics">Relics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Relics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St-Cuthbert-Incorrupt.png" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/0/01/St-Cuthbert-Incorrupt.png/220px-St-Cuthbert-Incorrupt.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="315" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/0/01/St-Cuthbert-Incorrupt.png/330px-St-Cuthbert-Incorrupt.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/0/01/St-Cuthbert-Incorrupt.png/440px-St-Cuthbert-Incorrupt.png 2x" data-file-width="2794" data-file-height="4000" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St-Cuthbert-Incorrupt.png" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>The <a href="/wiki/Incorruptibility" title="Incorruptibility">incorrupt body</a> of Cuthbert from <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>'s Life of Cuthbert, 12th century</div></div></div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:252px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Cuthberts_Tomb.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/St_Cuthberts_Tomb.jpg/250px-St_Cuthberts_Tomb.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="333" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/St_Cuthberts_Tomb.jpg/375px-St_Cuthberts_Tomb.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/St_Cuthberts_Tomb.jpg/500px-St_Cuthberts_Tomb.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2736" data-file-height="3648" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Cuthberts_Tomb.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Location of St Cuthbert's tomb and reburial in Durham Cathedral; behind is a damaged statue of St Cuthbert, holding the head of the <a href="/wiki/Oswald_of_Northumbria" title="Oswald of Northumbria">king St Oswald</a> (whose head was reburied with Cuthbert)</div></div></div>
<p>According to <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>'s life of the saint, when Cuthbert's sarcophagus was opened eleven years after his death, his body was found to have been perfectly preserved or <a href="/wiki/Incorruptibility" title="Incorruptibility">incorrupt</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBede721_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBede721-37">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> This apparent miracle led to the steady growth of Cuthbert's posthumous <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cultus</a>, to the point where he became the most popular saint of Northern England. Numerous miracles were attributed to his intercession and to intercessory prayer near his remains.
</p><p>In 875 the <a href="/wiki/Denmark" title="Denmark">Danes</a> took the monastery of Lindisfarne and the monks fled, carrying St Cuthbert's body with them around various places including <a href="/wiki/Melrose_Abbey" title="Melrose Abbey">Melrose</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Melrose_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Melrose-25">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> After seven years' wandering it found a resting place at the still existing <a href="/wiki/St_Mary_and_St_Cuthbert,_Chester-le-Street" title="St Mary and St Cuthbert, Chester-le-Street">St Cuthbert's church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Chester-le-Street" title="Chester-le-Street">Chester-le-Street</a> until 995, when another Danish invasion led to its removal to Ripon. Then the saint intimated, as it was believed, that he wished to remain in Durham. A new stone church—the so-called "White Church"—was built, the predecessor of the present grand Cathedral. In 999, his relics were enshrined in the new church on 4 September, which is kept as the feast of his translation at Durham Cathedral<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> and as an optional memorial in the Catholic Church in England.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
In 1069 <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwine_(bishop_of_Durham)" title="Æthelwine (bishop of Durham)">Bishop Æthelwine</a> attempted to transport Cuthbert's body to Lindisfarne to escape from <a href="/wiki/William_the_Conqueror" title="William the Conqueror">King William</a> at the start of the <a href="/wiki/Harrying_of_the_North" title="Harrying of the North">Harrying of the North</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFletcher2003180_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFletcher2003180-40">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Journey_Cuthbert_of_Lindisfarne.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4c/The_Journey_Cuthbert_of_Lindisfarne.JPG/220px-The_Journey_Cuthbert_of_Lindisfarne.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4c/The_Journey_Cuthbert_of_Lindisfarne.JPG/330px-The_Journey_Cuthbert_of_Lindisfarne.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4c/The_Journey_Cuthbert_of_Lindisfarne.JPG/440px-The_Journey_Cuthbert_of_Lindisfarne.JPG 2x" data-file-width="3072" data-file-height="2304" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Journey_Cuthbert_of_Lindisfarne.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>The Journey</i>, a modern sculpture showing the travels of the Lindisfarne community, by <a href="/wiki/Fenwick_Lawson" title="Fenwick Lawson">Fenwick Lawson</a>. Shown here in the Millennium Square, <a href="/wiki/Durham,_England" title="Durham, England">Durham</a>.</div></div></div>
<p>In 1104 Cuthbert's tomb was opened again and his <a href="/wiki/Relics" class="mw-redirect" title="Relics">relics</a> translated to a new <a href="/wiki/Shrine" title="Shrine">shrine</a> behind the <a href="/wiki/Altar" title="Altar">altar</a> of the recently completed Cathedral. When the casket was opened, a small book of the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_John" title="Gospel of John">Gospel of John</a>, measuring 138 by 92 millimetres (5.4 × 3.6 inches), now known as the <a href="/wiki/Saint_Cuthbert_Gospel" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Cuthbert Gospel">Saint Cuthbert Gospel</a> (now <a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a> Additional MS 89000, formerly known as the Stonyhurst Gospel), was found. This is the oldest Western book to have retained its original <a href="/wiki/Bookbinding" title="Bookbinding">bookbinding</a>, in finely decorated leather.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> Also recovered much later were a set of <a href="/wiki/Vestments" class="mw-redirect" title="Vestments">vestments</a> of 909–916, made of <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_silk" title="Byzantine silk">Byzantine silk</a> with a "Nature Goddess" pattern, with a <a href="/wiki/Stole_(vestment)" title="Stole (vestment)">stole</a> and decoration in extremely rare Anglo-Saxon <a href="/wiki/Embroidery" title="Embroidery">embroidery</a> or <i><a href="/wiki/Opus_anglicanum" class="mw-redirect" title="Opus anglicanum">opus anglicanum</a></i>, which had been deposited in his tomb by King <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelstan" title="Æthelstan">Æthelstan</a> (r. 927–939) on a pilgrimage while Cuthbert's shrine was at Chester-le-Street.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWebster2012172_42-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWebster2012172-42">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJonesn.d._43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJonesn.d.-43">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cuthbert's shrine was destroyed in the Dissolution of the Monasteries, but, unusually, his relics survived and are still interred at the site, although they were also disinterred in the 19th century, when his wooden coffin and various relics were removed. St Cuthbert's coffin (actually one of a series of several coffins), as reconstructed by <a href="/wiki/Ernst_Kitzinger" title="Ernst Kitzinger">Ernst Kitzinger</a> and others, remains at the cathedral and is an important rare survival of Anglo-Saxon carving on wood. When the coffin was last inspected on 17 May 1827, a Saxon square cross of gold, embellished with garnets, in the characteristic splayed shape, used later as the heraldic emblem of St Cuthbert in the arms of Durham and Newcastle universities, was found.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Namesakes">Namesakes</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Namesakes">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Pectoral_Cross_of_St_Cuthbert.svg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Pectoral_Cross_of_St_Cuthbert.svg/220px-Pectoral_Cross_of_St_Cuthbert.svg.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="220" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Pectoral_Cross_of_St_Cuthbert.svg/330px-Pectoral_Cross_of_St_Cuthbert.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Pectoral_Cross_of_St_Cuthbert.svg/440px-Pectoral_Cross_of_St_Cuthbert.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="600" data-file-height="600" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Pectoral_Cross_of_St_Cuthbert.svg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Cross of Cuthbert</div></div></div>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Flag_of_County_Durham" title="Flag of County Durham">flag of County Durham</a> since 2013 features the Cross of St Cuthbert, <a href="/wiki/Counterchanged" class="mw-redirect" title="Counterchanged">counterchanged</a> in the county colours of blue and gold. The <a href="/wiki/Flag_of_Kirkcudbrightshire" title="Flag of Kirkcudbrightshire">flag of Kirkcudbrightshire</a> in Scotland since 2016 likewise features the Cross of St Cuthbert, whose name is the origin of the county's name. The Cross of St Cuthbert features as the principal charge on the coat of arms of the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Durham" class="mw-redirect" title="University of Durham">University of Durham</a>, granted in 1843, blazoned <i>Argent, a Cross of St Cuthbert Gules, on a canton Azure, a chevron Or, between three lions rampant of the first</i> ('A red Cross of St Cuthbert on a silver shield, with three silver fighting lions around a gold chevron on a blue square in the top left-hand corner'). The Cross also features in the arms of many of its constituent colleges. The <a href="/wiki/University_of_Newcastle_upon_Tyne" class="mw-redirect" title="University of Newcastle upon Tyne">University of Newcastle upon Tyne</a>, formerly King's College in the University of Durham, also features St Cuthbert's Cross on its arms, granted in 1937. The Newcastle University arms are blazoned <i>Azure, a Cross of St Cuthbert Argent, and on a chief of the last a lion passant guardant Gules.</i> ('A silver Cross of St Cuthbert on a blue shield, with a red lion walking and looking towards you on the silver top third portion of the shield.') The cross of St Cuthbert also features on the badges of the two Anglican secondary schools in <a href="/wiki/Tyne_and_Wear" title="Tyne and Wear">Tyne and Wear</a>, namely <a href="/wiki/Dame_Allan%27s_Schools" class="mw-redirect" title="Dame Allan&#39;s Schools">Dame Allan's Schools</a> and Sunderland High School.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St_Cuthbert%27s_Society" class="mw-redirect" title="St Cuthbert&#39;s Society">St Cuthbert's Society</a>, a college of Durham University established in 1888, is named after him and is located only a short walk from the coffin of the saint at Durham Cathedral. The Society celebrates St Cuthbert's Day on or around each 20 March with a feast. "Cuth's Day", the annual college day, is celebrated in the Easter term with music, entertainment, festivities and drinking. Cuddy's Corse is a <a href="/wiki/Waymarking" class="mw-redirect" title="Waymarking">waymarked</a> walking route between <a href="/wiki/Chester-Le-Street" class="mw-redirect" title="Chester-Le-Street">Chester-Le-Street</a> and Durham Cathedral; it marks the journey between two of the last resting places of the coffin.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Worksop_College" title="Worksop College">Worksop College</a>, founded as St Cuthbert's in 1895, was the last of the <a href="/wiki/List_of_Woodard_Schools" class="mw-redirect" title="List of Woodard Schools">Woodard Schools</a> to be opened.
</p><p>St Cuthbert is also the namesake of <a href="/wiki/St_Cuthbert%27s_College,_Auckland" title="St Cuthbert&#39;s College, Auckland">St Cuthbert's College</a> in <a href="/wiki/Epsom,_New_Zealand" title="Epsom, New Zealand">Epsom, New Zealand</a>; St Cuthbert's Day on 21 March is a day of school celebration. The school's houses are named after important locations in the life of the saint: <a href="/wiki/Dunblane_Cathedral" title="Dunblane Cathedral">Dunblane</a> (yellow), <a href="/wiki/Elgin,_Moray" title="Elgin, Moray">Elgin</a> (green), Iona (purple), <a href="/wiki/Kelso_Abbey" title="Kelso Abbey">Kelso</a> (blue), <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a> (white), <a href="/wiki/Melrose_Abbey" title="Melrose Abbey">Melrose</a> (red), York (orange) and Durham (pink).
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St_Cuthbert%27s_High_School" title="St Cuthbert&#39;s High School">St Cuthbert's High School</a>, a Roman Catholic school in <a href="/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne" title="Newcastle upon Tyne">Newcastle upon Tyne</a>, is named after the saint. St Cuthbert's Day is celebrated with Mass, and the <a href="/wiki/School_prayer" title="School prayer">school prayers</a> include reference to their patron saint (always ending with the invocation "St Cuthbert, pray for us"). The school badge features a bishop's crook in reference to St Cuthbert's time as a bishop, as well as ducks, reflecting his love of the animals.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St._Cuthbert%27s_Co-operative_Society" title="St. Cuthbert&#39;s Co-operative Society">St. Cuthbert's Co-operative Society</a> (now <a href="/wiki/Scotmid" title="Scotmid">Scotmid</a>) opened its first shop in Edinburgh in 1859, and expanded to become one of the largest co-ops in Scotland. Its dairy used horse-drawn delivery floats until 1985, and between 1944 and 1959 employed as a milkman <a href="/wiki/Sean_Connery" title="Sean Connery">Sean Connery</a>, who later played <a href="/wiki/James_Bond" title="James Bond">James Bond</a>.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Hexham_and_Newcastle" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of Hexham and Newcastle">Roman Catholic Diocese of Hexham and Newcastle</a> holds St Cuthbert as its patron saint, with the consecration of bishops in the diocese always taking place on 20 March, Cuthbert's feast day in the Catholic Church.
</p><p>Many churches are named after Cuthbert. An Orthodox Community in <a href="/wiki/Chesterfield,_Derbyshire" title="Chesterfield, Derbyshire">Chesterfield</a>, England, has taken St Cuthbert as their patron.<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Fossilised <a href="/wiki/Crinoid" title="Crinoid">crinoid</a> columnals extracted from limestone quarried on Lindisfarne, or found washed up along the foreshore, which were threaded into necklaces or rosaries, became known as <a href="/wiki/St._Cuthbert%27s_beads" title="St. Cuthbert&#39;s beads">St. Cuthbert's beads</a>.
</p><p>In Northumberland, the <a href="/wiki/Eider_duck" class="mw-redirect" title="Eider duck">eider duck</a> is known as the cuddy duck. While on the Farne Islands, Cuthbert instituted special laws to protect the ducks and other seabirds nesting on the islands.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-cduck_46-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cduck-46">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> They still breed in their thousands off the Northumberland coast.<sup id="cite_ref-cduck_46-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cduck-46">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In Cumbria, the civil parish and hamlet of <a href="/wiki/Holme_St_Cuthbert" title="Holme St Cuthbert">Holme St Cuthbert</a> are named after him, as is the parish church. It is a rural area, with one larger village and numerous smaller hamlets.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St_Cuthbert%27s_Way" title="St Cuthbert&#39;s Way">St Cuthbert's Way</a> is a long-distance walking route, one of <a href="/wiki/Scotland%27s_Great_Trails" title="Scotland&#39;s Great Trails">Scotland's Great Trails</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cuthbert is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Lesser_Festival_(Anglicanism)" title="Lesser Festival (Anglicanism)">Lesser Festival</a> on <a href="/wiki/March_20" title="March 20">20 March</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
