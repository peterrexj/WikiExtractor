<p>Saint <b>Odran</b> (<a href="/wiki/Floruit" title="Floruit">fl.</a> 430) was the charioteer of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Patrick" title="Saint Patrick">Saint Patrick</a> and the first <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyr" title="Christian martyr">Christian martyr</a> in Irish history.
</p><p>There are two different versions given about Odran's martyrdom. The first, in the <i><a href="/wiki/Vita_tripartita_Sancti_Patricii" title="Vita tripartita Sancti Patricii">Vita tripartita Sancti Patricii</a></i>, states that on the borders of the future counties of <a href="/wiki/County_Kildare" title="County Kildare">Kildare</a> and <a href="/wiki/County_Offaly" title="County Offaly">Offaly</a>, the chieftain of that district, <a href="/wiki/Failge_Berraide" class="mw-redirect" title="Failge Berraide">Failge Berraide</a>, worshiped the <a href="/wiki/Pagan" class="mw-redirect" title="Pagan">pagan</a> god <a href="/wiki/Crom_Cruach" title="Crom Cruach">Crom Cruach</a> and vowed to avenge the god's destruction at <a href="/wiki/Magh_Sl%C3%A9cht" title="Magh Slécht">Magh Slécht</a> by killing Patrick. Odran overheard the plot, and as he and Patrick set out in the chariot to continue their journey, requested that he be allowed to hold the place of honour instead of Patrick, who granted his wish; scarcely had they set out when a lance pierced Odran's heart, who by changing places saved Patrick's life.
</p><p>The second version, contained in the <a href="/wiki/Pseudo-history" class="mw-redirect" title="Pseudo-history">pseudo-historical</a> <a href="/wiki/Prologue" title="Prologue">prologue</a> (PHP) to the <i><a href="/wiki/Senchas_M%C3%A1r" class="mw-redirect" title="Senchas Már">Senchas Már</a></i>, the High-King <a href="/wiki/L%C3%B3egaire_mac_N%C3%A9ill" title="Lóegaire mac Néill">Lóegaire mac Néill</a> (died 462) suggests dispatching an assassin to kill someone from Patrick's household in order to test his preaching of forgiveness.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> The assassin is identified as King Lóegaire's brother Nuada (or nephew (?)<sup id="cite_ref-joyce_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-joyce-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>)<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> in the second paragraph quoted below, it being a paraphrase of the beginning sections of the PHP.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHancock18654–7_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHancock18654–7-10">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;d&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<blockquote>
<p>The cause of the Senchus [Mor] having been composed was this:—Patrick came to Erin to baptize and to disseminate religion among the Gaedhil, i.e. in the ninth year of Theodosius and in the fourth year of Laeghaire, King of Erin, son of Niall.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;e&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>But the cause of the Poem<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;f&#93;</a></sup> having been composed was <i>as follows</i>:<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;g&#93;</a></sup>—Laeghaire ordered his people to kill a man of Patrick's people; and Laeghaire agreed to give his own award to the person who should kill <i>the man</i>, that he might discover whether he (Patrick) would grant forgiveness for it. And Nuada Derg, the son of Niall, the brother of Laeghaire,<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;h&#93;</a></sup> who was in captivity in the hands of Laeghaire, heard this, and he said that if he were released and got other rewards, he would kill one of Patrick's people. And the command of Laeghaire's cavalry was given him, and he was released from captivity, and he gave guarantee that he would fulfil his promise; and he took his lance and went towards the clerics, and hurled the lance at them and slew Odran, Patrick's charioteer.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHancock18654–7_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHancock18654–7-10">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
</blockquote>
<p>St. Patrick then asked the <a href="/wiki/Chief_Ollam_of_Ireland" class="mw-redirect" title="Chief Ollam of Ireland">Chief Ollam of Ireland</a>, <a href="/wiki/Dubhthach_moccu_Lughair" class="mw-redirect" title="Dubhthach moccu Lughair">Dubhthach moccu Lughair</a> to try the case, and this places the poet in a quandary because if he didn't impose an <a href="/wiki/%C3%89raic" title="Éraic">eric-fine</a> this would seem an affront to Patrick, while if he did impose an eric, it would be an affront to God. However, Patrick assured him he would be inspired by God to speak what is right, and Dubhthach, after reciting the Senchus Mor as poetry,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHancock18656–9_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHancock18656–9-19">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> encompassing the <a href="/wiki/Brehon_law" class="mw-redirect" title="Brehon law">Brehon law</a> of Ireland, also referred to here as Patrick's law.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> pronounced judgment on the killer Nuada, who was condemned to death, though his soul went to Heaven.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHancock186510–15_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHancock186510–15-21">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Thus was the earliest judgement on the conflicting values of Christian and pagan laws in Ireland.
</p><p>Saint Odran's feast-day is 19 February.
</p><p>Due to the similarity of the name some people have identified Odran with <a href="/wiki/Oran_of_Iona" title="Oran of Iona">Odhran of Iona</a>. There is a link in the tradition that both men voluntarily sacrificed themselves in assisting the work of a greater saint.
</p>
