<p><b>Ulrich of Augsburg</b> (890 – 4 July 973), sometimes spelled <b>Uodalric</b> or <b>Odalrici</b>, was <a href="/wiki/Prince-Bishopric_of_Augsburg" title="Prince-Bishopric of Augsburg">Prince-Bishopric of Augsburg</a> in <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a>. He was the first <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> to be <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> not by a local authority but by the <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Pope</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ulrich_of_Augsburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_years">Early years</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ulrich_of_Augsburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early years">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Much of the information concerning Ulrich is derived from the <i>Life of St Ulrich</i> written by Gerhard of Augsburg sometime between 982 and 993. Ulrich was born in 890 at <a href="/wiki/Kyburg,_Zurich" class="mw-redirect" title="Kyburg, Zurich">Kyburg</a>, <a href="/wiki/Zurich" class="mw-redirect" title="Zurich">Zurich</a> in present-day <a href="/wiki/Switzerland" title="Switzerland">Switzerland</a>. He was the son of Hupald, Count of Dillingen (d. 909) and Dietpirch of Swabia (also known as Theoberga).<sup id="cite_ref-schmid_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schmid-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> His maternal grandfather was Adalbert II the Illustrious, Count of Thurgau. His family was connected with the dukes of Alamannia and the <a href="/wiki/Ottonian_dynasty" title="Ottonian dynasty">Ottonian dynasty</a>. An unnamed sister served as a nun in <a href="/wiki/Buchau" class="mw-redirect" title="Buchau">Buchau</a>.
</p><p>As was customary, his parents presented him as an <a href="/wiki/Oblate" title="Oblate">oblate</a> to the church while he was still a child.<sup id="cite_ref-Abels_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Abels-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> A sickly child, at the age of seven he was sent to the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_St._Gall" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of St. Gall">monastery of St. Gall</a>, where he proved to be an excellent scholar. While there, he became friends with <a href="/wiki/Wiborada" title="Wiborada">Wiborada</a>, a recluse who lived near the monastery and foretold that her young friend was destined to become a bishop.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He resolved to enter the priesthood, but was in doubt whether to enter the Benedictine Abbey of St. Gall or to become a secular priest. Sometime before April 910, he was sent for further training to a kinsman, Adalbero, Bishop of Augsburg, who made him chamberlain. Upon Adalbero's death (28 April 910) Ulrich returned home.<sup id="cite_ref-schmid_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schmid-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The Duke of Swabia presented him at the court of Henry I of Germany, where Ulrich became one of the household retainers.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop_of_Augsburg">Bishop of Augsburg</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ulrich_of_Augsburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Bishop of Augsburg">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Through the influence of his maternal uncle, <a href="/wiki/Burchard_II,_Duke_of_Swabia" title="Burchard II, Duke of Swabia">Burchard II, Duke of Swabia</a>, and other relatives, Ulrich was appointed <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Augsburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Augsburg">bishop of Augsburg</a> by <a href="/wiki/Henry_I_of_Germany" class="mw-redirect" title="Henry I of Germany">Henry I of Germany</a>, and was consecrated on 28 December 923.<sup id="cite_ref-Tyler_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Tyler-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He sought to improve the low moral and social condition of the clergy. The See of Augsburg reached the period of its greatest splendor under Ulrich; he raised the standard of training and discipline among the clergy by the reformation of existing schools and the establishment of new ones, and by canonical visitations and synods; he provided for the poor, and rebuilt decayed churches and monasteries. He built churches in honor of St. Afra and St. John, and founded the monastery of St. Stephen for Benedictine nuns.<sup id="cite_ref-lins_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lins-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> For purposes of obtaining relics he went on two journeys to Rome, in 910, and in 952 or 953.<sup id="cite_ref-schmid_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schmid-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> German emperor <a href="/wiki/Otto_I_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Otto I the Great">Otto I the Great</a> granted Ulrich the right to mint coins.
</p><p>During the struggle between Otto I and his son <a href="/wiki/Liudolf,_Duke_of_Swabia" title="Liudolf, Duke of Swabia">Liudolf, Duke of Swabia</a>, Ulrich remained loyal to Otto,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> holding for him the castle of <a href="/wiki/Schwabm%C3%BCnchen" title="Schwabmünchen">Schwabmünchen</a>, which was within the territorial jurisdiction of the Bishop of Augsburg.<sup id="cite_ref-Abels_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Abels-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> When in the summer of 954 father and son were ready to attack each other at <a href="/wiki/Illertissen" title="Illertissen">Illertissen</a> in Swabia, at the last moment Ulrich and Bishop Hartbert of <a href="/wiki/Chur" title="Chur">Chur</a> were able to mediate between Otto and Liudolf. Ulrich succeeded in persuading Liudolf and <a href="/wiki/Conrad,_Duke_of_Lorraine" title="Conrad, Duke of Lorraine">Conrad, Duke of Lorraine</a>, Otto's son-in-law, to ask the king's pardon on 17 December 954.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Against_the_Magyars">Against the Magyars</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ulrich_of_Augsburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Against the Magyars">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><a href="/wiki/Hungarian_invasions_of_Europe" title="Hungarian invasions of Europe">Magyars repeatedly attacked</a> in the territories of <a href="/wiki/Bavaria" title="Bavaria">Bavaria</a> and <a href="/wiki/Swabia" title="Swabia">Swabia</a>. Ulrich served as general in the defense of Augsburg. He built a stone wall fortification around the city. During these attacks many churches and buildings were destroyed, which Ulrich later rebuilt. Ulrich attended several imperial meetings and <a href="/wiki/Synod" title="Synod">synods</a>, such as at <a href="/wiki/Ingelheim" class="mw-redirect" title="Ingelheim">Ingelheim</a> in 948, Augsburg 952, Rome in 972 and again at <a href="/wiki/Ingelheim" class="mw-redirect" title="Ingelheim">Ingelheim</a> in 972.
</p><p>Soon after, the Magyars entered Germany, plundering and burning as they went, and in 955 advanced as far as Augsburg, which they besieged. It was due to Ulrich's ability and courage that Augsburg was able to hold out against the besiegers until the Emperor Otto arrived. According to his biographer Gerhard, Bishop Ulrich took the lead in the defense of the city. On the first day of the attack, Bishop Ulrich rode out to encourage the towns’ soldiers in their defense of the city's gate. While the battle raged, the bishop, dressed in his ecclesiastical robes, inspired his men, with the <a href="/wiki/Psalm_23" title="Psalm 23">23rd Psalm</a> ("Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death"). While this defense was going on, the King was raising an army to march south.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelbrück1982115_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelbrück1982115-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The fiercest fighting probably took place on 8 August at the eastern gate, which the Hungarians tried to storm in large numbers. The Bishop's men defended the gate bravely and killed the leader of the attack, forcing the Hungarians to withdraw. That evening Ulrich returned to the city to direct throughout the night the repair and strengthening of its walls.<sup id="cite_ref-Abels_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Abels-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The next day the Hungarians launched a wider general attack. During the battle, Berchtold of Risinesburg arrived, which heralded the approach of the German army. At the end of the day, the siege was suspended.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelbrück1982678_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelbrück1982678-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Ulrich's ability to hold out during the siege bought precious time for the emperor.<sup id="cite_ref-Tyler_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Tyler-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Ulrich subsequently contributed much to the decisive victory at the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Lechfeld" title="Battle of Lechfeld">Battle of Lechfeld</a> (10 August 955), where the invaders were finally defeated.<sup id="cite_ref-lins_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lins-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> However, Ulrich Schmid maintains that "The later assertion that Ulrich himself took part in the battle is incorrect."<sup id="cite_ref-schmid_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schmid-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="His_character">His character</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ulrich_of_Augsburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: His character">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:152px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Gora_Oljka_-_Saint_Ulrich.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Gora_Oljka_-_Saint_Ulrich.jpg/150px-Gora_Oljka_-_Saint_Ulrich.jpg" decoding="async" width="150" height="368" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Gora_Oljka_-_Saint_Ulrich.jpg/225px-Gora_Oljka_-_Saint_Ulrich.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Gora_Oljka_-_Saint_Ulrich.jpg/300px-Gora_Oljka_-_Saint_Ulrich.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1320" data-file-height="3240" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Gora_Oljka_-_Saint_Ulrich.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Statue of S. Ulrich in a church in Gora Oljka (Slovenia).</div></div></div>
<p>Ulrich demanded a high moral standard of himself and others. A hundred years after his death, a letter apparently written by him, which opposed <a href="/wiki/Clerical_celibacy_(Catholic_Church)" class="mw-redirect" title="Clerical celibacy (Catholic Church)">celibacy</a>, and supported the marriage of priests, suddenly appeared. The forger of the letter counted on the opinion of the common people, who would regard celibacy as unjust if Ulrich, known for the rigidity of his morals, upheld the marriage of priests.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Ulrich was also steadfastly loyal, as a prince of the empire, to the emperor. He was one of the most important props of the Ottonian policy, which rested mainly upon the ecclesiastical princes. He constantly attended the judicial courts held by the king and in the <a href="/wiki/Imperial_Diet_(Holy_Roman_Empire)" title="Imperial Diet (Holy Roman Empire)">Imperial Diets</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Later_life">Later life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ulrich_of_Augsburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Later life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Ulrich took part in the Diet held on 20 September 972, when he defended himself against the charge of <a href="/wiki/Nepotism" title="Nepotism">nepotism</a> in regard to his nephew Adalbero, whom he had appointed his coadjutor on account of his own illness and desire to retire to a Benedictine abbey.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Ulrich did, in fact, resign as prince-bishop and retired to <a href="/wiki/Ottobeuren_Abbey" title="Ottobeuren Abbey">Ottobeuren Abbey</a>, where he became abbot.
</p><p>As morning dawned on 4 July 973, Ulrich had ashes strewn on the ground in the shape of a cross; the cross sprinkled with <a href="/wiki/Holy_water" title="Holy water">holy water</a>, and he was placed upon it. His nephew Richwin came with a message and greeting from the Emperor Otto II as the sun rose, and immediately upon this, while the clergy sang the Litany, Ulrich died. He was buried at the <a href="/wiki/St._Ulrich%27s_and_St._Afra%27s_Abbey_(Augsburg)" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Ulrich&#39;s and St. Afra&#39;s Abbey (Augsburg)">St. Afra church</a> he had rebuilt in Augsburg; the burial was performed by Bishop Wolfgang of <a href="/wiki/Ratisbon" class="mw-redirect" title="Ratisbon">Ratisbon</a>. Later the St. Ulrich and Afra church was built in the same spot. He was succeeded by <a href="/wiki/Henry_I,_Bishop_of_Augsburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Henry I, Bishop of Augsburg">Henry</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-cath_encyc_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cath_encyc-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Maniple_(vestment)" title="Maniple (vestment)">maniple</a> of Ulrich was woven in red and white <a href="/wiki/Silk" title="Silk">silk</a> using <a href="/wiki/Tablet_weaving" title="Tablet weaving">tablet weaving</a> and Ulrich's <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relic</a> was later analyzed by Peter Collingwood in his <i>The Techniques of Tablet Weaving</i>; Collingwood regarded it as a 'masterpiece'.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ulrich_of_Augsburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>When Ulrich was too old and weak to say Mass, angels are said to have come to him to assist him. Places that were named after him are said to be host to healing abilities. Attesting to his early <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cultus</a>, there is a very beautiful miniature from the tenth century in a manuscript now in the library of <a href="/wiki/Einsiedeln_Abbey" title="Einsiedeln Abbey">Einsiedeln</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Other miniatures are at the Royal Library of Munich, in manuscripts dating from the year 1454.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Many <a href="/wiki/Miracle" title="Miracle">miracles</a> are said to have been wrought at his grave; only 20 years after his death, Ulrich was canonized by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_XV" title="Pope John XV">Pope John XV</a> on 4 July 993. He was the first saint to be canonized by a Pope, rather than by a local authority.<sup id="cite_ref-CNA_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CNA-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Walter_of_Pontoise" title="Walter of Pontoise">Walter of Pontoise</a> was the last saint in <a href="/wiki/Western_Europe" title="Western Europe">Western Europe</a> to have been canonized by an authority other than the Pope; he was canonized by <a href="/wiki/Hugh_de_Boves" class="mw-redirect" title="Hugh de Boves">Hugh de Boves</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Rouen" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Rouen">Archbishop of Rouen</a> in 1153.<sup id="cite_ref-ofsnlu_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ofsnlu-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage">Patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ulrich_of_Augsburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Along with Afra and Simpert, Ulrich is a patron saint of Augsburg. Legend held that pregnant women who drank from his chalice had easy deliveries, and thus developed his patronage of pregnant women and easy births. The touch of his pastoral cross was used to heal people bitten by rabid dogs.<sup id="cite_ref-CNA_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CNA-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
