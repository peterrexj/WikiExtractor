<p><b>Pelagius</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="&#39;p&#39; in &#39;pie&#39;">p</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;l&#39; in &#39;lie&#39;">l</span><span title="/eɪ/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;face&#39;">eɪ</span><span title="/dʒ/: &#39;j&#39; in &#39;jam&#39;">dʒ</span><span title="/i/: &#39;y&#39; in &#39;happy&#39;">i</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;s&#39; in &#39;sigh&#39;">s</span></span>/</a></span></span>; c. 354–418) was a British theologian known for promoting a system of doctrines (termed <a href="/wiki/Pelagianism" title="Pelagianism">Pelagianism</a> by his opponents) which emphasized human choice in salvation and denied <a href="/wiki/Original_sin" title="Original sin">original sin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Pelagius was accused of heresy at the synod of Jerusalem in 415 and his doctrines were harshly criticized by <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</a>, especially the Pelagian views about humankind's good nature and individual responsibility for choosing <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetism</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Pelagius especially stressed the freedom of human will.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Very little is known about the personal life and career of Pelagius.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Beginnings">Beginnings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Beginnings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Pelagius was active between about 390 and 418.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He was said by his contemporaries, such as Augustine of Hippo, <a href="/wiki/Prosper_of_Aquitaine" title="Prosper of Aquitaine">Prosper of Aquitaine</a>, <a href="/wiki/Marius_Mercator" title="Marius Mercator">Marius Mercator</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Orosius" title="Orosius">Paul Orosius</a>, to have been of Celtic British origin.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> apparently thought that Pelagius was Irish, suggesting that he was "stuffed with Irish porridge" (<span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Scotorum pultibus praegravatus</i></span>).<sup id="cite_ref-early_medieval_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-early_medieval-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 206">&#58;&#8202;206&#8202;</span></sup> He was tall in stature and portly in appearance. Pelagius was also highly educated, spoke and wrote Latin and Greek with great fluency, and was well versed in theology. His name has traditionally been understood as a Graecized form (from <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn">pélagos</i></span>, "sea") of the Welsh name Morgan ("sea-born"), or another Celtic equivalent.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Pelagius became better known around 380 when he moved to Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-Fletcher_11_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fletcher_11-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> There he enjoyed a reputation of austerity; he also corresponded with <a href="/wiki/Paulinus_of_Nola" title="Paulinus of Nola">Paulinus of Nola</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-pohle_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pohle-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Twenty-five years after the fact, Augustine related that Pelagius had reacted strongly to the statement from Augustine's <i><a href="/wiki/Confessions_(Augustine)" title="Confessions (Augustine)">Confessions</a></i> (397–401) "Give what you command and command what you will", as he believed that it undermined human responsibility.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHarrison201679_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarrison201679-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETeselle20144_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETeselle20144-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEScheck201281_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEScheck201281-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> However, this incident's historicity is questioned by scholars.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEScheck201281–82_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEScheck201281–82-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When <a href="/wiki/Alaric_I" title="Alaric I">Alaric</a> sacked Rome in 410, Pelagius and his follower <a href="/wiki/Caelestius" title="Caelestius">Caelestius</a> fled to <a href="/wiki/Carthage" title="Carthage">Carthage</a>, where he continued his work.<sup id="cite_ref-Fletcher_11_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fletcher_11-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Opponents">Opponents</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Opponents">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:182px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_Augustine_of_Hippo.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ed/Saint_Augustine_of_Hippo.jpg/180px-Saint_Augustine_of_Hippo.jpg" decoding="async" width="180" height="305" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ed/Saint_Augustine_of_Hippo.jpg/270px-Saint_Augustine_of_Hippo.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ed/Saint_Augustine_of_Hippo.jpg 2x" data-file-width="277" data-file-height="470" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_Augustine_of_Hippo.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Saint_Augustine_of_Hippo" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Augustine of Hippo">Saint Augustine of Hippo</a></div></div></div>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Augustine_of_Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Augustine of Hippo">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Pelagianism quickly spread, especially around Carthage. Augustine wrote <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">De peccatorum meritis et remissione libri III</i></span> (<i>Three Books on the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins</i>) in 412, and <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">De spiritu et littera</i></span> (<i>On the Spirit and the Letter</i>) in 414. When in 414 disquieting rumours arrived from Sicily and the so-called <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Definitiones Caelestii</i></span>, said to be the work of Caelestius, were sent to him, he at once (414 or 415) published the rejoinder, <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">De perfectione justitiae hominis</i></span>. In these, he strongly affirmed the existence of original sin, the need for infant baptism, the impossibility of a sinless life without Christ, and the necessity of Christ's grace. Augustine stands as an important source on the life and theology of Pelagius, and wrote about him extensively.<sup id="cite_ref-pohle_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pohle-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Jerome">Jerome</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Jerome">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Pelagius soon left for Palestine, befriending the bishop there. Jerome, who also lived there, became involved as well. Pelagius had criticized his commentary on the <a href="/wiki/Epistle_to_the_Ephesians" title="Epistle to the Ephesians">Epistle to the Ephesians</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-pohle_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pohle-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Jerome wrote against Pelagius in his Letter to Ctesiphon and <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Dialogus contra Pelagianos</i></span>. With Jerome at the time was <a href="/wiki/Paulus_Orosius" class="mw-redirect" title="Paulus Orosius">Orosius</a>, a visiting pupil of Augustine, who had similar views on the dangers of Pelagianism. Together, they publicly condemned Pelagius. Bishop <a href="/wiki/John_II,_Bishop_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="John II, Bishop of Jerusalem">John of Jerusalem</a>, a personal friend of Pelagius, called a council in July 415. Church sources claim Orosius' lack of fluency in Greek rendered him unconvincing and John's Eastern background made him more willing to accept that humans did not have inherent sinfulness, yet the council rendered no verdict and passed the controversy to the Latin Church because Pelagius, Jerome, and Orosius were all Latin.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Diospolis">Diospolis</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Diospolis">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>A few months later in December of 415, another synod in <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Diospolis" title="Synod of Diospolis">synod in Diospolis</a> (<a href="/wiki/Lydda" class="mw-redirect" title="Lydda">Lydda</a>) under the <a href="/wiki/Caesarea_in_Palaestina_(diocese)" title="Caesarea in Palaestina (diocese)">bishop of Cæsarea</a> was called by two deposed bishops who came to the Holy Land. However, neither bishop attended for unrelated reasons and Orosius had left after consultation with Bishop John. Pelagius explained to the synod that he did believe God was necessary for salvation because every human is created by God. He also claimed that many works of <a href="/wiki/Celestius" class="mw-redirect" title="Celestius">Celestius</a> did not represent his own views. He showed letters of recommendation by other authoritative figures including Augustine himself, who for all their disagreements, thought highly of Pelagius' character.
</p><p>The Synod of Diospolis therefore concluded: "Now since we have received satisfaction in respect of the charges brought against the monk Pelagius in his presence and since he gives his assent to sound doctrines but condemns and anathematises those contrary to the faith of the Church, we adjudge him to belong to the communion of the Catholic Church."<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:217px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Pelagius_from_Nuremberg_Chronicle.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/48/Pelagius_from_Nuremberg_Chronicle.jpg" decoding="async" width="215" height="288" class="thumbimage" data-file-width="215" data-file-height="288" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Pelagius_from_Nuremberg_Chronicle.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Pelagius, as depicted in the <a href="/wiki/Nuremberg_Chronicle" title="Nuremberg Chronicle">Nuremberg Chronicle</a></div></div></div>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Teachings">Teachings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Teachings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Manichaeism" title="Manichaeism">Manichaeism</a> stressed that the spirit was God-created, while material substance was corrupt and evil. Theologian Gerald Bonner felt that part of Pelagius' analysis was an over-reaction to Manicheanism. Pelagius held that everything created by God was good, therefore, he could not see how God had made humans fallen creatures.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> (Augustine's teaching on the <a href="/wiki/Fall_of_Adam" class="mw-redirect" title="Fall of Adam">Fall of Adam</a> was not a settled doctrine at the time the Augustinian/Pelagian dispute began.) The Pelagians accused Augustine of bringing Manichaeian theology into the Christian church, which Augustine himself denied.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The view that mankind can avoid sinning, and that humans can freely choose to obey God's commandments, stands at the core of Pelagian teaching. Pelagius stressed human autonomy and freedom of the will;<sup id="cite_ref-Filippo_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Filippo-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> an illustration of Pelagius' views on man's "moral ability" not to sin can be found in his Letter to Demetrias.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>For Pelagius, "<a href="/wiki/Grace_in_Christianity" title="Grace in Christianity">grace</a>" consisted of the gift of <a href="/wiki/Free_will" title="Free will">free will</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Law_of_Moses" title="Law of Moses">Law of Moses</a>, and the teachings of Jesus.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> According to Augustine, Pelagians saw baptism of infants as useless because they had no sin.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Celestius, who was a disciple of Pelagius, also denied original sin and the necessity of infant baptism for salvation.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Pope_Zosimus">Pope Zosimus</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Pope Zosimus">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Seeking to undo his condemnation, Pelagius wrote a letter and statement of belief to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Zosimus" title="Pope Zosimus">Pope Zosimus</a>, Innocent I's successor, arguing that he was orthodox. In these he articulated his beliefs so as not to contradict what the synods condemned. Zosimus was persuaded by Celestius to reopen the case, but opposition from the African bishops and <a href="/wiki/Honorius_(emperor)" title="Honorius (emperor)">Emperor Honorius</a> forced Zosimus to condemn and excommunicate Celestius and Pelagius in 418.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Pelagianism was condemned at the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Carthage_(418)" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Carthage (418)">Council of Carthage in 418</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Filippo_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Filippo-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Augustine, shocked that Pelagius and Celestius were not denounced as heretics, had called the Council of Carthage in 418. The synod issued eight canons<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<ul><li>Canon I:  Adam was not created subject to death.</li></ul>
<ul><li>Canon II: Infants are to be baptized for the remission of sins.</li></ul>
<ul><li>Canon III: Grace not only gives remission of sins but aid that we sin no more.</li></ul>
<ul><li>Canon IV: Grace gives knowledge, inspiration and desire to perform required duty.</li></ul>
<ul><li>Canon V: Without the grace of God we can do no good thing.</li></ul>
<ul><li>Canon VI: The statement “If we say that we have no sin we deceive ourselves” should not be said out of humility but because it is true.</li></ul>
<ul><li>Canon VII: In the <a href="/wiki/Lord%27s_Prayer" title="Lord&#39;s Prayer">Lord's Prayer</a>, the Saints pray “Forgive us our trespasses” not only for others but also for themselves.</li></ul>
<ul><li>Canon VIII: The Saints pray “Forgive us our trespasses” not out of humility but because they have sinned.</li></ul>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_aftermath">Death and aftermath</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Death and aftermath">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After his condemnation, Pelagius was expelled from Jerusalem, and Saint <a href="/wiki/Cyril_of_Alexandria" title="Cyril of Alexandria">Cyril of Alexandria</a> allowed him to settle in Egypt. He is not heard of thereafter.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His death did not end his teachings, although those who followed him may have modified those teachings. Because little information remains with regard to Pelagius' actual teachings, some of his doctrines possibly were subject to revision and suppression by his enemies (followers of Augustine and the Church leadership as a whole at that time).
</p><p>Pelagius and Caelestius were declared heretics by the <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Ephesus" class="mw-redirect" title="First Council of Ephesus">First Council of Ephesus</a> in 431.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Belief in Pelagianism and <a href="/wiki/Semipelagianism" class="mw-redirect" title="Semipelagianism">Semipelagianism</a> was common for the next few centuries, especially in Britain, the Holy Land and North Africa. <a href="/wiki/Germanus_of_Auxerre" title="Germanus of Auxerre">St Germanus</a> visited Britain to combat Pelagianism in or around AD 429. In Wales, <a href="/wiki/Saint_David" title="Saint David">Saint David</a> was credited with convening the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Brefi" title="Synod of Brefi">Synod of Brefi</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Victory" title="Synod of Victory">Synod of Victory</a> against the followers of Pelagius in the sixth century.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Evaluation">Evaluation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Evaluation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Because of the fifth-century condemnations of him, Pelagius became known as "a <a href="/wiki/Heresiarch" title="Heresiarch">heresiarch</a> of the deepest dye". Evaluation of him changed after the publication of a 1943 biography by <a href="/w/index.php?title=Georges_de_Plinval&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Georges de Plinval (page does not exist)">Georges de Plinval</a> and more recent scholars have viewed him as an orthodox Christian theologian who was a victim of denunciation. His Pauline commentaries were popular during the Middle Ages but frequently claimed to be the work of other authors.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>An objective view of Pelagius and his effect is difficult. His name has been used as an epithet for centuries by both Protestants and Catholics, and he has had few defenders. The very early church denounced his ideas and the Reformation accused Roman Catholics of adhering to his beliefs and condemned both Pelagius and the Catholic Church. Modern scholarship suggests that Pelagius did not take the more extreme positions later associated with his followers.<sup id="cite_ref-Clark_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Clark-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Ronald_Hutton" title="Ronald Hutton">Ronald Hutton</a> describes him as "a first-rate theologian".<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The theologian <a href="/wiki/Carol_Harrison_(theologian)" title="Carol Harrison (theologian)">Carol Harrison</a> commented that Pelagius presented "a radically different alternative to Western understandings of the human person, human responsibility and freedom, ethics and the nature of salvation" which might have come about if Augustine had not been victorious in the Pelagian controversy.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHarrison201678_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarrison201678-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> According to Harrison, "Pelagianism represents an attempt to safeguard God's justice, to preserve the integrity of human nature as created by God, and of human beings' obligation, responsibility and ability to attain a life of perfect righteousness."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHarrison201682_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarrison201682-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> However, this is at the expense of downplaying human frailty and presenting "the operation of divine grace as being merely external".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHarrison201682_26-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarrison201682-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> According to the scholar <a href="/wiki/Rebecca_Weaver_(theologian)" class="mw-redirect" title="Rebecca Weaver (theologian)">Rebecca Weaver</a>, "what most distinguished Pelagius was his conviction of an unrestricted freedom of choice, given by God and immune to alteration by sin or circumstance."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWeaver2014xviii_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWeaver2014xviii-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>
In 1956, John Ferguson wrote: <style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style></p><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>If a heretic is one who emphasizes one truth to the exclusion of others, it would at any rate appear that [Pelagius] was no more a heretic than Augustine. His fault was in exaggerated emphasis, but in the final form his philosophy took, after necessary and proper modifications as a result of criticism, it is not certain that any statement of his is totally irreconcilable with the Christian faith or indefensible in terms of the <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a>. It is by no means so clear that the same may be said of Augustine.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBeck2007694_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeck2007694-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Of his surviving works, only few are known in full. These are:
</p>
<ul><li><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">De fide Trinitatis libri III</i></span> ("On Faith in the Trinity: Three Books")</li>
<li><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Eclogarum ex divinis Scripturis liber primus</i></span> ("Excerpts out of Divine Scriptures: Book One")</li>
<li><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Commentarii in epistolas S. Pauli</i></span> ("Commentary on the Epistles of Saint Paul")</li></ul>
<p>Unfortunately, most of his work survives only in the quotations of his opponents. Only in the past century have works attributable to Pelagius been identified as such.
</p><p>Other writings include <i>On Nature</i>, parts of which are quoted in Augustine's <i><a href="/wiki/On_Nature_and_Grace" title="On Nature and Grace">On Nature and Grace</a></i>, and <i>Defense of the Freedom of the Will</i>, quoted in Augustine's <i>On the Grace of Christ</i>. Also surviving are his letter to Demetrias, along with fragments of other letters, and the written statement of faith which was received by Pope Zosimus.
</p>
