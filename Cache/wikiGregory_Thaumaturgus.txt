<p><b>Gregory Thaumaturgus</b> or <b>Gregory the Miracle-Worker</b> (<a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Greek language">Ancient Greek</a>: <span lang="grc">Γρηγόριος ὁ Θαυματουργός</span>, <i>Grēgórios ho Thaumatourgós</i>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Gregorius Thaumaturgus</i>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr>&#160;213&#160;– 270), also known as <b>Gregory of Neocaesarea</b>, was a <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a> of the 3rd century. He has been <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic</a> and <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Orthodox Churches</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_Thaumaturgus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gregory was born around AD 213 to a wealthy <a href="/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Rome" title="Religion in ancient Rome">pagan</a> family in <a href="/wiki/Neocaesarea" class="mw-redirect" title="Neocaesarea">Neocaesarea</a> (modern <a href="/wiki/Niksar" title="Niksar">Niksar</a>, then the capital of the area of <a href="/wiki/Pontus_(region)" title="Pontus (region)">Pontus</a> in <a href="/wiki/Asia_Minor" class="mw-redirect" title="Asia Minor">Asia Minor</a>). Little is known of his pastoral work, and his surviving theological writings are in an incomplete state. This lack of knowledge partially obscures his personality, despite his historical importance, and his immemorial title <a href="/wiki/Thaumaturgus" class="mw-redirect" title="Thaumaturgus">Thaumaturgus</a>, "the wonder-worker" in Latinized Greek, casts an air of legend about him. Nevertheless, the lives of few bishops of the third century are so well authenticated; the historical references to him permit a fairly detailed reconstruction of his work.<sup id="cite_ref-leclercq_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-leclercq-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Originally he was known as Theodore ("gift of God"), not an exclusively Christian name. He was introduced to the Christian religion at the age of fourteen, after the death of his father. He had a brother Athenodorus, and on the advice of one of their <a href="/wiki/Tutor" class="mw-redirect" title="Tutor">tutors</a>, the young men were eager to study at the <a href="/wiki/Law_School_of_Beirut" class="mw-redirect" title="Law School of Beirut">Berytus</a> in <a href="/wiki/Beirut" title="Beirut">Beirut</a>, then one of the four or five famous schools in the Hellenic world. At this time, their brother-in-law was appointed <i>assessor</i> (legal counsel) to the Roman Governor of <a href="/wiki/Palestine_(region)" title="Palestine (region)">Palestine</a>; the youths had therefore an occasion to act as an escort to their sister as far as <a href="/wiki/Caesarea_Palaestina" class="mw-redirect" title="Caesarea Palaestina">Caesarea</a> in Palestine. On arrival in that town they learned that the celebrated scholar <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>, head of the <a href="/wiki/Catechetical_School_of_Alexandria" title="Catechetical School of Alexandria">Catechetical School of Alexandria</a>, resided there. Curiosity led them to hear and converse with the master.<sup id="cite_ref-leclercq_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-leclercq-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Soon both youths forgot all about Beirut and Roman law, and gave themselves up to the great Christian teacher, who gradually won them over to Christianity.
</p><p>In his <a href="/wiki/Panegyric" title="Panegyric">panegyric</a> on Origen, Gregory describes the method employed by that master to win the confidence and esteem of those he wished to convert; how he mingled a persuasive candour with outbursts of temper and theological argument put cleverly at once and unexpectedly. Persuasive skill rather than bare reasoning, and evident sincerity and an ardent conviction were the means Origen used to make converts. Gregory took up at first the study of philosophy; theology was afterwards added, but his mind remained always inclined to philosophical study, so much so indeed that in his youth he cherished strongly the hope of demonstrating that the Christian religion was the only true and good philosophy. For seven years he underwent the mental and moral discipline of Origen (231 to 238 or 239). There is no reason to believe that his studies were interrupted by the persecutions of <a href="/wiki/Maximinus_Thrax" title="Maximinus Thrax">Maximinus of Thrace</a>; his alleged journey to Alexandria, at this time, may therefore be considered at least doubtful, and probably never occurred.
</p><p>Before leaving Palestine, Gregory delivered in presence of Origen a public farewell oration in which he returned thanks to the illustrious master he was leaving. This oration is valuable from many points of view. As a rhetorical exercise it exhibits the excellent training given by Origen, and his skill in developing literary taste and the amount of adulation then permissible towards a living person in an assembly composed mostly of Christians, and Christian in temper. It contains, moreover, much useful information concerning the youth of Gregory and his master's method of teaching. A letter of Origen refers to the departure of the two brothers, but it is not easy to determine whether it was written before or after the delivery of this oration. In it Origen exhorts his pupils to bring the intellectual treasures of the Greeks to the service of Christian philosophy, and thus imitate the Jews who employed the golden vessels of the Egyptians to adorn the <a href="/wiki/Holy_of_Holies" title="Holy of Holies">Holy of Holies</a>.
</p><p>Gregory returned to Pontus with the intention of practising law. His plan, however, was again laid aside, for he was soon consecrated bishop of his native Neocaesarea by Phoedimus, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Amasea" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Amasea">Bishop of Amasea</a> and <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop" title="Metropolitan bishop">metropolitan</a> of Pontus.<sup id="cite_ref-history_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-history-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability" title="Wikipedia:Verifiability"><span title="The material near this tag failed verification of its source citation(s). (December 2014)">failed verification</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> This fact illustrates in an interesting way the growth of the hierarchy in the primitive Church; the Christian community at Caesarea was very small, being only seventeen souls, and yet it was given a bishop. Ancient canonical documents indicate that it was possible for a community of even ten Christians to have their own bishop. When Gregory was consecrated he was forty years old, and he ruled his diocese for thirteen years.
</p><p>Nothing definite is known about his methods, but he must have shown much zeal in increasing the little flock with which he began his episcopal administration. An ancient source attests to his <a href="/wiki/Missionary" title="Missionary">missionary</a> zeal by recording a curious coincidence: Gregory began with only seventeen Christians, but at his death there remained only seventeen pagans in the whole town of Caesarea.<sup id="cite_ref-leclercq_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-leclercq-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Presumably the many miracles which won for him the title of Thaumaturgus were performed during these years.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Historicity_of_life">Historicity of life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_Thaumaturgus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Historicity of life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Sources on the life, teaching, and actions of Gregory Thaumaturgus are all more or less open to criticism. Besides the details given by Gregory himself, there are four other sources of information, according to Kötschau all derived from oral tradition; indeed, the differences between them force the conclusion that they cannot all be derived from one common written source. They are: 
</p>
<ul><li><i>Life and Panegyric of Gregory</i> by <a href="/wiki/St._Gregory_of_Nyssa" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Gregory of Nyssa">St. Gregory of Nyssa</a> (P.G., XLVI, col. 893 sqq.);</li>
<li><i>Historia Miraculorum</i>, by <a href="/wiki/Tyrannius_Rufinus" title="Tyrannius Rufinus">Rufinus</a>;</li>
<li>an account in <a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a> of the great actions of Blessed Gregory (sixth-century manuscript);</li>
<li><a href="/wiki/St._Basil" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Basil">St. Basil</a>, <i>De Spiritu Sancto</i>.</li></ul>
<p>Drawing on family traditions and a knowledge of the neighbourhood, the account by Gregory of Nyssa is more reliably historical than other known versions of the Thaumaturge's life. By the time of Rufinus (ca. 400), the original story was becoming confused; the Syriac account is at times obscure and contradictory. Even the life by Gregory of Nyssa exhibits a legendary element, though the facts were supplied to the writer by his grandmother, <a href="/wiki/St._Macrina_the_Elder" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Macrina the Elder">St. Macrina the Elder</a>. He relates that before his episcopal consecration Gregory retired from Neocaesarea into a solitude, and was favoured by an apparition of the Blessed Virgin and <a href="/wiki/John_the_Apostle" title="John the Apostle">John the Apostle</a>, and that the latter dictated to him a creed or formula of Christian faith, of which the autograph existed at Neocaesarea when the biography was being written. The creed itself is important for the history of Christian doctrine.<sup id="cite_ref-leclercq_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-leclercq-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gregory of Nyssa describes at length the miracles that gained for the Bishop of Caesarea the title of Thaumaturgus. It is clear that Gregory's influence must have been considerable, and his miraculous power undoubted. It might have been expected that Gregory's name would appear among those who took part in the First Council of Antioch against <a href="/wiki/Paul_of_Samosata" title="Paul of Samosata">Paul of Samosata</a>;<sup id="cite_ref-leclercq_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-leclercq-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> probably he took part also in the second council held there against the same heresiarch, for the letter of that council is signed by a bishop named Theodore, which had been originally Gregory's name.<sup id="cite_ref-leclercq_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-leclercq-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> To attract the people to the festivals in honour of the martyrs, Gregory organized profane amusements that might appeal to pagans, who were accustomed to religious ceremonies that combined solemnity with pleasure and merrymaking.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings_of_Gregory">Writings of Gregory</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_Thaumaturgus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Writings of Gregory">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Gregorius_Thaumaturgus_%E2%80%93_Canones_paenitentiales,_1584_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13691873.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Gregorius_Thaumaturgus_%E2%80%93_Canones_paenitentiales%2C_1584_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13691873.jpg/220px-Gregorius_Thaumaturgus_%E2%80%93_Canones_paenitentiales%2C_1584_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13691873.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="307" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Gregorius_Thaumaturgus_%E2%80%93_Canones_paenitentiales%2C_1584_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13691873.jpg/330px-Gregorius_Thaumaturgus_%E2%80%93_Canones_paenitentiales%2C_1584_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13691873.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Gregorius_Thaumaturgus_%E2%80%93_Canones_paenitentiales%2C_1584_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13691873.jpg/440px-Gregorius_Thaumaturgus_%E2%80%93_Canones_paenitentiales%2C_1584_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13691873.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1149" data-file-height="1605" /></a><figcaption><i>Canones paenitentiales</i>, 1584 edition<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup></figcaption></figure>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Oratio_Panegyrica">Oratio Panegyrica</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_Thaumaturgus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Oratio Panegyrica">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <i>Oratio Panegyrica</i> in honour of <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a> describes in detail that master's pedagogical methods. Its literary value consists less in its style than in its novelty: it is the first attempt at autobiography in Christian literature. This youthful work is full of enthusiasm and genuine talent; moreover, it proves how fully Origen had won the admiration of his pupils, and how the training Gregory received influenced the remainder of a long and well spent life.
</p><p>Gregory tells us in this work (xiii) that under Origen he read the works of many philosophers, without restriction as to school, except that of the atheists. From this reading of the old philosophers he learned to insist frequently on the unity of God; and his long experience of pagan or crudely Christian populations taught him how necessary this was. Traces of this insistence are to be met with in the Tractatus ad Theopompum, concerning the passability and impassibility of God; this work seems to belong to Gregory, though in its general arrangement it reminds us of Methodius.
</p><p>A similar trait was probably characteristic of the lost <i>Dialogus cum Aeliano</i> (Pros Ailianon Dialexis), which we learn of through St. Basil, who frequently attests the orthodoxy of the Thaumaturgus (Ep. xxviii, 1, 2; cciv, 2; ccvii, 4) and even defends him against the Sabellians, who claimed him for their teaching and quoted as his formula: <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn">patera kai ouion epinoia men einai duo, hypostasei de en</i></span> (that the Father and the Son were two in intelligence, but one in substance) from the aforesaid Dialogus cum Aeliano. St. Basil replied that Gregory was arguing against a pagan, and used the words <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn">agonistikos not dogmatikos</i></span>, i.e. in the heat of combat, not in calm exposition; in this case he was insisting, and rightly, on the Divine unity. He added, moreover, that a like explanation must be given to the words <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn">ktisma, poiema</i></span> (created, made) when applied to the Son, reference being to Christ Incarnate. Basil added that the text of the work was corrupt.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Epistola_Canonica">Epistola Canonica</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_Thaumaturgus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Epistola Canonica">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <i>Epistola Canonica</i> (Ἐπιστολὴ κανονική) (Routh, <i>Reliquiae Sacrae</i>, III, 251–83) is valuable to both historian and canonist as evidence of the organization of the Church of Caesarea and the other Churches of Pontus under Gregory's influence, at a time when the invading Goths had begun to aggravate a situation made difficult enough by the imperial persecutions. We learn from this work how absorbing the episcopal charge was for a man of conscience and a strict sense of duty. Moreover, it helps us to understand how a man so well equipped mentally, and with the literary gifts of Gregory, has not left a greater number of works.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Exposition_of_the_Faith">Exposition of the Faith</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_Thaumaturgus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Exposition of the Faith">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <i>Ἔκθεσις τῆς πίστεως</i> (Exposition of the Faith) is in its kind a theological document not less precious than the foregoing. It makes clear Gregory's orthodoxy a propos of the Trinity. Its authenticity and date seem now definitely settled, the date lying between 260 and 270. Caspari has shown that this confession of faith is a development of the premises laid down by Origen. Its conclusion leaves no room for doubt: "There is therefore nothing created, nothing greater or less (literally, nothing subject) in the Trinity (oute oun ktiston ti, he doulon en te triadi), nothing superadded, as though it had not existed before, but never been without the Son, nor the Son without the Spirit; and this same Trinity is immutable and unalterable forever". Such a formula, stating clearly the distinction between the Persons in the Trinity, and emphasizing the eternity, equality, immortality, and perfection, not only of the Father, but of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, proclaims a marked advance on the theories of Origen.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Epistola_ad_Philagrium">Epistola ad Philagrium</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_Thaumaturgus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Epistola ad Philagrium">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <i>Epistola ad Philagrium</i> has reached us in a <a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a> version. It treats of the <a href="/wiki/Consubstantiality" title="Consubstantiality">Consubstantiality</a> of the Son and has also been attributed to Gregory of Nazianzus (Ep. ccxliii; formerly Orat. xiv); <a href="/wiki/Tillemont" class="mw-redirect" title="Tillemont">Tillemont</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">Benedictines</a>, however, deny this because it offers no expression suggestive of the <a href="/wiki/Arian_controversy" title="Arian controversy">Arian controversy</a>. Draeseke, nevertheless, calls attention to numerous views and expressions in this treatise that recall the writings of Gregory of Nazianzus.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_attributed_works">Other attributed works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_Thaumaturgus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Other attributed works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>A <i>Metaphrasis eis ton Ekklesiasten tou Solomontos</i>, or paraphrase of <a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastes" title="Ecclesiastes">Ecclesiastes</a>, is attributed to him by some manuscripts; others ascribe it to <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Nazianzus" title="Gregory of Nazianzus">Gregory of Nazianzus</a>; <a href="/wiki/St._Jerome" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Jerome">St. Jerome</a> (<a href="/wiki/Jerome%27s_De_Viris_Illustribus" class="mw-redirect" title="Jerome&#39;s De Viris Illustribus">De vir. illust., chapter 65</a>, and Com. in eccles., iv) ascribes it to our Gregory.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The brief Treatise on the Soul addressed to one Tatian, in favour of which may be cited the testimony of <a href="/wiki/Nicholas_of_Methone" title="Nicholas of Methone">Nicholas of Methone</a> (probably from <a href="/wiki/Procopius_of_Gaza" title="Procopius of Gaza">Procopius of Gaza</a>), is now claimed for Gregory.
</p><p>The <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn">Kephalaia peri pisteos dodeka</i></span> or Twelve Chapters on Faith do not seem to be the work of Gregory. According to Caspari, the <i>Kata meros pistis</i> or brief exposition of doctrine concerning the <a href="/wiki/Trinity" title="Trinity">Trinity</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Incarnation" title="Incarnation">Incarnation</a>, attributed to Gregory, was composed by <a href="/wiki/Apollinaris_of_Laodicea" title="Apollinaris of Laodicea">Apollinaris of Laodicea</a> about 380, and circulated by his followers as a work of Gregory (<a href="/wiki/Otto_Bardenhewer" title="Otto Bardenhewer">Otto Bardenhewer</a>).
</p><p>Finally, the Greek, Syriac, and Armenian Catenæ contain fragments attributed more or less correctly to Gregory. The fragments of the De Resurrectione belong rather to <a href="/wiki/Pamphilus_of_Caesarea" title="Pamphilus of Caesarea">Pamphilus</a>' <i>Apologia</i> for Origen.
</p>
