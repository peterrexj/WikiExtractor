<p><b>Kentigern</b> (<a href="/wiki/Welsh_language" title="Welsh language">Welsh</a>: <i lang="cy">Cyndeyrn Garthwys</i>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Kentigernus</i>), known as <b>Mungo</b>, was a missionary in the <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Britons" title="Celtic Britons">Brittonic</a> <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Strathclyde" title="Kingdom of Strathclyde">Kingdom of Strathclyde</a> in the late sixth century, and the founder and <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the city of <a href="/wiki/Glasgow" title="Glasgow">Glasgow</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Name">Name</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Mungo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Name">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In <a href="/wiki/Wales" title="Wales">Wales</a> and <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a>, this saint is known by his birth and baptismal name Kentigern (<a href="/wiki/Welsh_language" title="Welsh language">Welsh</a>: <i lang="cy">Cyndeyrn</i>). This name probably comes from the <a href="/wiki/Common_Brittonic" title="Common Brittonic">British</a> <span title="Celtic languages collective text">&#42;<i lang="cel">Cuno-tigernos</i></span>, which is composed of the elements <span title="Celtic languages collective text">&#42;<i lang="cel">cun</i></span>, a <a href="/wiki/Hound" title="Hound">hound</a>, and <span title="Celtic languages collective text">&#42;<i lang="cel">tigerno</i></span>, a lord, prince, or king. The evidence is based on the <a href="/wiki/Old_Welsh" title="Old Welsh">Old Welsh</a> record <span title="Old Welsh-language text"><i lang="owl">Conthigirn(i)</i></span>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Other etymologies have been suggested, including British <span title="Celtic languages collective text">&#42;<i lang="cel">Kintu-tigernos</i></span> 'chief prince' based on the English form Kentigern, but the Old Welsh form above and <a href="/wiki/Old_English" title="Old English">Old English</a> <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">Cundiʒeorn</i></span> do not appear to support this.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Particularly in Scotland, he is known by the <a href="/wiki/Nickname" title="Nickname">pet name</a> Mungo, possibly derived from the <a href="/wiki/Cumbric_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Cumbric language">Cumbric</a> equivalent of the <a href="/wiki/Welsh_language" title="Welsh language">Welsh</a>: <i lang="cy">fy nghu</i> 'my dear (one)'.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The Mungo pet name or <a href="/wiki/Hypocorism" title="Hypocorism">hypocorism</a> has a Gaelic parallel in the form <span title="Scottish Gaelic-language text"><i lang="gd">Mo Choe</i></span> or <span title="Scottish Gaelic-language text"><i lang="gd">Mo Cha</i></span>, under which guise Kentigern appears in <a href="/wiki/Kirkmahoe" title="Kirkmahoe">Kirkmahoe</a>, for example, in Dumfriesshire, which appears as <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">ecclesia Sancti Kentigerni</i></span> in the <i>Arbroath Liber</i> in 1321. An ancient church in <a href="/wiki/Bromfield,_Cumbria" title="Bromfield, Cumbria">Bromfield, Cumbria</a>, is named after him, as are <a href="/wiki/Crosthwaite_Parish_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Crosthwaite Parish Church">Crosthwaite Parish Church</a> and some other churches in the northern part of <a href="/wiki/Cumbria" title="Cumbria">Cumbria</a>, for example <a href="/wiki/St_Mungo%27s_Church,_Dearham" title="St Mungo&#39;s Church, Dearham">St Mungo's Church, Dearham</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biographers">Biographers</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Mungo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Biographers">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <i>Life of Saint Mungo</i> was written by the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastic</a> <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographer</a> <a href="/wiki/Jocelyn_of_Furness" title="Jocelyn of Furness">Jocelyn of Furness</a> in about 1185.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Jocelyn states that he rewrote the 'life' from an earlier Glasgow legend and an <a href="/wiki/Old_Irish" title="Old Irish">Old Irish</a> document. There are certainly two other medieval lives: the earlier partial life in the <a href="/wiki/Cotton_library" title="Cotton library">Cottonian</a> manuscript now in the <a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>, and the later <i>Life</i>, based on Jocelyn, by <a href="/wiki/John_of_Tynemouth_(chronicler)" title="John of Tynemouth (chronicler)">John of Tynemouth</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Mungo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:GlasgowButeHallStKentigern.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f8/GlasgowButeHallStKentigern.jpg/220px-GlasgowButeHallStKentigern.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="522" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f8/GlasgowButeHallStKentigern.jpg/330px-GlasgowButeHallStKentigern.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f8/GlasgowButeHallStKentigern.jpg/440px-GlasgowButeHallStKentigern.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1029" data-file-height="2441" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:GlasgowButeHallStKentigern.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Saint Mungo (University of Glasgow)</div></div></div>
<p>Mungo's mother <a href="/wiki/Teneu" title="Teneu">Teneu</a> was a princess, the daughter of <a href="/wiki/King_Lot" title="King Lot">King Lleuddun</a> (Latin: Leudonus) who ruled a territory around what is now <a href="/wiki/Lothian" title="Lothian">Lothian</a> in Scotland, perhaps the kingdom of <a href="/wiki/Gododdin" title="Gododdin">Gododdin</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Y_Gogledd_Hen" class="mw-redirect" title="Y Gogledd Hen">Old North</a>. She became pregnant after being raped by <a href="/wiki/Owain_mab_Urien" title="Owain mab Urien">Owain mab Urien</a> according to the <a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a> manuscript. However, other historic accounts claim Owain and Teneu (also known as Thaney) had a love affair whilst he was still married to his wife Penarwen and that her father, King Lot, separated the pair after she became pregnant. Later, allegedly, after Penarwen died, Tenue/Thaney returned to King Owain and the pair were able to marry before King Owain met his death battling Bernicia in 597 AD.
</p><p>Her furious father had her thrown from the heights of <a href="/wiki/Traprain_Law" title="Traprain Law">Traprain Law</a>. Surviving, she was then abandoned in a <a href="/wiki/Coracle" title="Coracle">coracle</a> in which she drifted across the <a href="/wiki/Firth_of_Forth" title="Firth of Forth">Firth of Forth</a> to <a href="/wiki/Culross" title="Culross">Culross</a> in <a href="/wiki/Fife" title="Fife">Fife</a>. There Mungo was born.<sup id="cite_ref-hale_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hale-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Mungo was brought up by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Serf" title="Saint Serf">Saint Serf</a> who was ministering to the <a href="/wiki/Picts" title="Picts">Picts</a> in that area. It was Serf who gave him his popular pet-name. At the age of twenty-five, Mungo began his missionary labours on the <a href="/wiki/River_Clyde" title="River Clyde">Clyde</a>, on the site of modern Glasgow. He built his church across the water from an extinct volcano, next to the Molendinar Burn, where the present medieval cathedral now stands. For some thirteen years, he laboured in the district, living a most austere life in a small cell and making many converts by his holy example and his preaching.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A strong anti-Christian movement in Strathclyde, headed by a certain King Morken, compelled Mungo to leave the district, and he retired to Wales, via Cumbria, staying for a time with <a href="/wiki/Saint_David" title="Saint David">Saint David</a> at <a href="/wiki/St_David%27s" class="mw-redirect" title="St David&#39;s">St David's</a>, and afterwards moving on to <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Gwynedd" title="Kingdom of Gwynedd">Gwynedd</a> where he founded a <a href="/wiki/St_Asaph_Cathedral" title="St Asaph Cathedral">cathedral</a> at <a href="/wiki/St_Asaph" title="St Asaph">Llanelwy</a> (St Asaph in English). While there, he undertook a pilgrimage to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>. However, the new King of Strathclyde, <a href="/wiki/Riderch_I_of_Alt_Clut" class="mw-redirect" title="Riderch I of Alt Clut">Riderch Hael</a>, invited Mungo to return to his kingdom.<sup id="cite_ref-hunter_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hunter-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He decided to go and appointed <a href="/wiki/Saint_Asaph" title="Saint Asaph">Saint Asaph/Asaff</a> as <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_St_Asaph" title="Bishop of St Asaph">Bishop of Llanelwy</a> in his place.
</p><p>For some years, Mungo fixed his <a href="/wiki/Cathedra" title="Cathedra">Episcopal seat</a> at <a href="/wiki/Hoddom" title="Hoddom">Hoddom</a> in <a href="/wiki/Dumfriesshire" title="Dumfriesshire">Dumfriesshire</a>, evangelising thence the district of <a href="/wiki/Galloway" title="Galloway">Galloway</a>. He eventually returned to Glasgow where a large community grew up around him. It was nearby, in <a href="/wiki/Kilmacolm" title="Kilmacolm">Kilmacolm</a>, that he was visited by <a href="/wiki/Columba" title="Columba">Saint Columba</a>, who was at that time labouring in Strathtay. The two saints embraced, held long converse, and exchanged their pastoral staves.<sup id="cite_ref-hunter_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hunter-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In old age, Mungo became very feeble and his chin had to be set in place with a bandage. He is said to have died in his bath, on Sunday 13 January.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Miracles">Miracles</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Mungo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Miracles">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In the <i>Life of Saint Mungo</i>, he performed four miracles in Glasgow. The following verse is used to remember Mungo's four miracles:
</p>
<blockquote><div class="poem">
<p>Here is the bird that never flew<br />
Here is the tree that never grew<br />
Here is the bell that never rang<br />
Here is the fish that never swam<sup id="cite_ref-scotsman_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-scotsman-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
</div></blockquote>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Verse_(poetry)" title="Verse (poetry)">verses</a> refer to the following:
</p>
<ul><li><i>The Bird</i>: Mungo restored life to a robin that had been killed by some of his classmates.<sup id="cite_ref-hale_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hale-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>The Tree</i>: Mungo had been left in charge of a fire in Saint Serf's monastery. He fell asleep and the fire went out. Taking a hazel branch, he restarted the fire.<sup id="cite_ref-foghlam_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foghlam-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>The Bell</i>: the bell is thought to have been brought by Mungo from Rome. It was said to have been used in services and to mourn the dead. The original bell no longer exists, and a replacement, created in the 1640s, is now on display in Glasgow.</li>
<li><i>The Fish</i>: refers to the story about Queen Languoreth of Strathclyde who was suspected of infidelity by her husband. King Riderch demanded to see her ring, which he claimed she had given to her lover. In reality the King had thrown it into the River Clyde. Faced with execution she appealed for help to Mungo, who ordered a messenger to catch a fish in the river. On opening the fish, the ring was miraculously found inside, which allowed the Queen to clear her name. (This story may be confused with an almost identical one concerning King <a href="/wiki/Maelgwn_Hir_ap_Cadwallon" class="mw-redirect" title="Maelgwn Hir ap Cadwallon">Maelgwn</a> of Gwynedd and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Asaph" title="Saint Asaph">Saint Asaph</a>.)</li></ul>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Analysis">Analysis</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Mungo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Analysis">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Mungo's ancestry is recorded in the <i><a href="/wiki/Bonedd_y_Saint" title="Bonedd y Saint">Bonedd y Saint</a></i>. His father, Owain was a King of <a href="/wiki/Rheged" title="Rheged">Rheged</a>. His maternal grandfather, Lleuddun, was probably a King of the <a href="/wiki/Gododdin" title="Gododdin">Gododdin</a>; <a href="/wiki/Lothian" title="Lothian">Lothian</a> was named after him. There seems little reason to doubt that Mungo was one of the first evangelists of Strathclyde, under the patronage of King Rhiderch Hael, and probably became the first <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Glasgow" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Glasgow">Bishop of Glasgow</a>.
</p><p>Jocelin seems to have altered parts of the original life that he did not understand; while adding others, like the trip to Rome, that served his own purposes, largely the promotion of the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Glasgow" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Glasgow">Bishopric of Glasgow</a>. Some new parts may have been collected from genuine local stories, particularly those of Mungo's work in <a href="/wiki/Cumbria" title="Cumbria">Cumbria</a>. S. Mundahl-Harris has shown that Mungo's associations with <a href="/wiki/St_Asaph" title="St Asaph">St Asaph</a> were a <a href="/wiki/Normans" title="Normans">Norman</a> invention.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (August 2009)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> However, in Scotland, excavations at Hoddom have brought confirmation of early Christian activity there, uncovering a late 6th-century stone baptistery.
</p><p>Details of Mungo's infirmity have a ring of authenticity about them. The year of Mungo's death is sometimes given as 603, but is recorded in the <a href="/wiki/Annales_Cambriae" title="Annales Cambriae">Annales Cambriae</a> as 612. 13 January was a Sunday in both 603 and 614. David McRoberts has argued that his death in the bath is a garbled version of his collapse during a baptismal service.
</p><p>In a late 15th-century fragmentary manuscript generally called '<a href="/wiki/Lailoken" title="Lailoken">Lailoken</a> and Kentigern', Mungo appears in conflict with the mad prophet, Lailoken alias <a href="/wiki/Merlin_(wizard)" class="mw-redirect" title="Merlin (wizard)">Merlin</a>. Lailoken's appearance at the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Arfderydd" title="Battle of Arfderydd">Battle of Arfderydd</a> in 573 has led to a connection being made between this battle, the rise of Riderch Hael and the return of Mungo to Strathclyde.
</p><p>The <i>Life of Saint Mungo</i> bears similarities with <a href="/wiki/Chr%C3%A9tien_de_Troyes" title="Chrétien de Troyes">Chrétien de Troyes</a>'s French romance <i><a href="/wiki/Yvain,_the_Knight_of_the_Lion" title="Yvain, the Knight of the Lion">Yvain, the Knight of the Lion</a></i>. In Chrétien's story, <a href="/wiki/Ywain" title="Ywain">Yvain</a>, a version of Owain mab Urien, courts and marries <a href="/wiki/Laudine" title="Laudine">Laudine</a>, only to leave her for a period to go adventuring. This suggests that the works share a common source.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Mungo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Stmungotomb.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bf/Stmungotomb.JPG/200px-Stmungotomb.JPG" decoding="async" width="200" height="150" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bf/Stmungotomb.JPG/300px-Stmungotomb.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bf/Stmungotomb.JPG/400px-Stmungotomb.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1600" data-file-height="1200" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Stmungotomb.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Tomb of St. Mungo in the crypt of <a href="/wiki/Glasgow_Cathedral" title="Glasgow Cathedral">Glasgow Cathedral</a></div></div></div>
<p>On the spot where Mungo was buried now stands the <a href="/wiki/Glasgow_Cathedral" title="Glasgow Cathedral">cathedral</a> dedicated in his honour. His <a href="/wiki/Shrine" title="Shrine">shrine</a> was a great centre of Christian pilgrimage until the <a href="/wiki/Scottish_Reformation" title="Scottish Reformation">Scottish Reformation</a>. His remains are said to still rest in the crypt. A spring called "St. Mungo's Well"
fell eastwards from the apse.
</p><p>His festival was kept throughout Scotland on 13 January. The <a href="/wiki/Bollandist" title="Bollandist">Bollandists</a> have printed a special mass for this feast, dating from the 13th century. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> in the West is 13 January. His feast day in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> is 14 January.
</p><p>Mungo's four religious miracles in Glasgow are represented in the city's <a href="/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_Glasgow" title="Coat of arms of Glasgow">coat of arms</a>. Glasgow's current <a href="/wiki/Motto" title="Motto">motto</a> <i>Let Glasgow flourish by the preaching of His word and the praising of His name</i> and the more secular <i>Let Glasgow flourish</i>, are both inspired by Mungo's original call <i>"Let Glasgow flourish by the preaching of the word"</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-foghlam_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foghlam-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Saint Mungo's Well was a cold water spring and bath at Copgrove, near <a href="/wiki/Ripon" title="Ripon">Ripon</a>, North Yorkshire, formerly believed effective for treating <a href="/wiki/Rickets" title="Rickets">rickets</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Glasgow Fire Brigade also named their <a href="/wiki/Fireboat" title="Fireboat">fireboat</a> <a href="/wiki/St._Mungo_(fireboat)" title="St. Mungo (fireboat)"><i>St. Mungo</i></a>, which served the around the Clyde from 1959 to 1975.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>One of <a href="/wiki/LNER_Peppercorn_Class_A1" title="LNER Peppercorn Class A1">Arthur H. Peppercorn's A1</a> Pacific locomotives (ordered by the <a href="/wiki/London_and_North_Eastern_Railway" title="London and North Eastern Railway">LNER</a> but not built until after nationalisation of Britain's railways) was named Saint Mungo, entering service in 1949 and carrying the <a href="/wiki/British_Railways" class="mw-redirect" title="British Railways">BR</a> number 60145. This was the last of the design to be withdrawn in 1966.
</p><p>Mungo is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(observance)" class="mw-redirect" title="Commemoration (observance)">commemoration</a> on 13 January.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Namesake_churches.2C_schools_and_charities"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Namesake_churches,_schools_and_charities">Namesake churches, schools and charities</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Mungo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Namesake churches, schools and charities">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:147px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Stmungorc.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Stmungorc.JPG/145px-Stmungorc.JPG" decoding="async" width="145" height="193" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Stmungorc.JPG/218px-Stmungorc.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Stmungorc.JPG/290px-Stmungorc.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1200" data-file-height="1600" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Stmungorc.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/St_Mungo%27s_Church,_Townhead" class="mw-redirect" title="St Mungo&#39;s Church, Townhead">St Mungo's Church, Townhead</a>, <a href="/wiki/Glasgow" title="Glasgow">Glasgow</a></div></div></div>
<p>Saint Mungo founded a number of churches during his period as Archbishop of Strathclyde of which <a href="/wiki/Stobo_Kirk" title="Stobo Kirk">Stobo Kirk</a> is a notable example. At <a href="/wiki/Townhead" title="Townhead">Townhead</a> and <a href="/wiki/Dennistoun" title="Dennistoun">Dennistoun</a> in <a href="/wiki/Glasgow" title="Glasgow">Glasgow</a> there is a modern <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> church and a traditional <a href="/wiki/Scottish_Episcopal_Church" title="Scottish Episcopal Church">Scottish Episcopal Church</a><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> respectively dedicated to the saint.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St_Mungo%27s_Academy" title="St Mungo&#39;s Academy">St Mungo's Academy</a> is a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a>, <a href="/wiki/Co-educational" class="mw-redirect" title="Co-educational">co-educational</a>, <a href="/wiki/Comprehensive_school" title="Comprehensive school">comprehensive</a>, <a href="/wiki/Secondary_school" title="Secondary school">secondary school</a> located in <a href="/wiki/Bridgeton,_Glasgow" title="Bridgeton, Glasgow">Bridgeton, Glasgow</a>.
</p><p>Another church established by the saint himself was <a href="/wiki/St_Kentigern%27s_Church,_Lanark_(Hyndford_Road)" title="St Kentigern&#39;s Church, Lanark (Hyndford Road)">St Kentigern's Church</a> of <a href="/wiki/Lanark" title="Lanark">Lanark</a>, founded shortly before his death, and which now stands in ruins. Another church called <a href="/w/index.php?title=St_Kentigern%27s_Church,_Lanark_(Hope_Street)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="St Kentigern&#39;s Church, Lanark (Hope Street) (page does not exist)">St Kentigern's</a> was built in the town in the late 19th century. It is still present but has been converted to housing and office space.<sup id="cite_ref-Clydesdale&#39;s_Heritage_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Clydesdale&#39;s_Heritage-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-HES_Lanark_Church_(Hope_St)_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-HES_Lanark_Church_(Hope_St)-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Kilmarnock" title="Kilmarnock">Kilmarnock</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Scotland" title="Church of Scotland">Church of Scotland</a> congregation is named St Kentigern's.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St._Kentigern%27s_Academy" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Kentigern&#39;s Academy">St. Kentigern's Academy</a> opened in <a href="/wiki/Blackburn,_West_Lothian" title="Blackburn, West Lothian">Blackburn, West Lothian</a> in September 1974.
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Alloa" title="Alloa">Alloa</a>, a chapel dedicated to St. Mungo is thought to have been erected during the fourteenth or fifteenth-century. The present <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Scotland" title="Church of Scotland">Church of Scotland</a> <a href="/wiki/St._Mungo%27s_Parish_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Mungo&#39;s Parish Church">St. Mungo's Parish Church</a> in Alloa was built in 1817.
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Cumbernauld" title="Cumbernauld">Cumbernauld</a>, there is St. Mungo's Parish Church in the centre of the New Town.
</p><p>In the <a href="/wiki/Lake_District" title="Lake District">Lake District</a> village of <a href="/wiki/Caldbeck" title="Caldbeck">Caldbeck</a> there is a church and a well named after him. The Cumbrian parish churches at Crossthwaite in Keswick, Mungrisdale, Castle Sowerby, and Irthington are also dedicated to St Kentigern. There are two Cumbrian churches dedicated to St Mungo, one at Bromfield (also a well and castle) and one at <a href="/wiki/St_Mungo%27s_Church,_Dearham" title="St Mungo&#39;s Church, Dearham">Dearham</a>.
</p><p>There is a St Kentigern's school and church in <a href="/wiki/Blackpool" title="Blackpool">Blackpool</a>.
</p><p>In Falkirk, there is a St. Mungo's High School.
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Grinsdale" title="Grinsdale">Grinsdale</a>, Cumbria there is a church venerated to St. Kentigern.
</p><p>Also in Cumbria, there are two Greek Orthodox Communities venerated to St. Mungo/Kentigern, one in <a href="/wiki/Dalton-in-Furness" title="Dalton-in-Furness">Dalton-in-Furness</a> and the other in <a href="/wiki/Keswick,_Cumbria" title="Keswick, Cumbria">Keswick</a>.
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Fallowfield" title="Fallowfield">Fallowfield</a>, a suburb of the city of Manchester, a Roman Catholic church is dedicated to Saint Kentigern.
</p><p>St Kentigern's is a small Roman Catholic Church in the village of <a href="/wiki/Eyeries" title="Eyeries">Eyeries</a>, on the Beara peninsula in West Cork, Ireland.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Mungo or Kentigern is the patron of a <a href="/wiki/Presbyterian_church" class="mw-redirect" title="Presbyterian church">Presbyterian church</a> school in <a href="/wiki/Auckland" title="Auckland">Auckland</a>, <a href="/wiki/New_Zealand" title="New Zealand">New Zealand</a>, which has three campuses: <a href="/wiki/Saint_Kentigern_College" title="Saint Kentigern College">Saint Kentigern College</a>, a secondary co-ed college in the suburb of <a href="/wiki/Pakuranga" title="Pakuranga">Pakuranga</a>, Saint Kentigern Boys School, a boys-only private junior primary school in the suburb of <a href="/wiki/Remuera" title="Remuera">Remuera</a>, and Saint Kentigern Girls School, a girls-only private junior primary school also in Remuera.
</p><p>There is a United Church of Canada charge in Cushing Quebec Canada, Saint Mungo's United Church. Built in the 1836 originally as a Church of Scotland, it has recently been restored for its 180th anniversary.
</p><p>Although secular, the English charity for the support and empowerment of the homeless, <a href="/wiki/St_Mungo%27s_(charity)" title="St Mungo&#39;s (charity)">St. Mungo's</a>, was named after the saint by its founder. The Glasgow-born Harry Stone named it in honour of the patron saint of his birth city when the charity was established in 1969.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Saint Mungo's runs hostels, outreach, emergency shelters, and employment and training services. It provides an online and in-person "Recovery College" free to its students.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Fiction">Fiction</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Mungo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Fiction">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>St. Mungo is mentioned in the <i><a href="/wiki/Father_Brown" title="Father Brown">Father Brown</a></i> series of books by <a href="/wiki/G._K._Chesterton" title="G. K. Chesterton">G. K. Chesterton</a>, as the titular saint of Father Brown's parish.
</p><p>St. Mungo's Hospital for Magical Maladies and Injuries is the primary hospital of Magical Britain in the <i><a href="/wiki/Harry_Potter" title="Harry Potter">Harry Potter</a></i> series of books by <a href="/wiki/J._K._Rowling" title="J. K. Rowling">J. K. Rowling</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-scotsman_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-scotsman-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Kentigern Gardens is the location of a murder in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Cuckoo%27s_Calling" title="The Cuckoo&#39;s Calling">The Cuckoo's Calling</a></i>, a novel published under <a href="/wiki/J._K._Rowling" title="J. K. Rowling">J. K. Rowling</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Pseudonym" title="Pseudonym">pseudonym</a> of Robert Galbraith.
</p><p>Mungo is the main antagonist in the historical novel <i>The Lost Queen</i> by Signe Pike.
</p>
