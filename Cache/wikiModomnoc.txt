<p><b>St. Modomnóc of Ossory</b> (also <i>Domnóc</i> and <i>Dominic</i>) (died c. 550) was an <a href="/wiki/Ireland" title="Ireland">Irish</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and <a href="/wiki/Missionary" title="Missionary">missionary</a> in <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ossory" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of Ossory">Osraige</a> who was a disciple of <a href="/wiki/Saint_David" title="Saint David">St. David of Wales</a> and a member of the <a href="/wiki/U%C3%AD_N%C3%A9ill" title="Uí Néill">O'Neill</a> royal family.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> His feast day is February 13.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Modomnoc&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Modomnoc's name is likely to have been Dominic<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> or Donogh, as the words "my", ("<i>mo</i>") "little" and "dear" were added to Irish saint's names; hence Domnóc's name is often rendered <i>mo Domnóc</i> or <i>Modomnóc</i>. He left Ireland to practice priesthood and crossed the sea to Wales to study under St. David at Menevia.<sup id="cite_ref-haggerty_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-haggerty-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Beekeeper">Beekeeper</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Modomnoc&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Beekeeper">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>One of the best known stories regarding Saint Modomnoc concerns his work as a beekeeper. Bees were kept both for their honey and the production of wax.<sup id="cite_ref-haggerty_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-haggerty-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was never stung. When the time came for him to return to Ireland, three times the bees followed in great swarm and settled on the mast.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p><i>In a little boat, from the east, over the pure-colored sea, my Domnoc brought the gifted race of Ireland's bees.</i> <b>~ Félire Óengusso</b><sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Modomnoc's talking to his bees is in keeping with an Irish folklore custom of ‘Telling the Bees’ which ensures that the bees not feel any offence due to exclusion from family affairs and so will remain with the hive. It was believed that if one didn’t tell the bees of a wedding, a birth, or a death they would take offence and leave.<sup id="cite_ref-bee_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bee-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> This same custom forms the basis of <a href="/wiki/John_Greenleaf_Whittier" title="John Greenleaf Whittier">John Greenleaf Whittier's</a> poem, "Telling the Bees".<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Upon returning home he continued his religious services at <a href="/wiki/Tybroughney" title="Tybroughney">Tybroughney</a>. He is said to have been honoured with the episcopal dignity, about the middle of the 6th century.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-butler_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
