<p><b>Edmund of Abingdon</b> (also known as  <b>Edmund Rich</b>, <b>St Edmund of Canterbury</b>, <b>Edmund of Pontigny</b>, French: <b>St Edme</b>; c. 1174&#160;&#8211;&#32;1240) was an English-born prelate who served as <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>. He became a respected lecturer in <a href="/wiki/Mathematics" title="Mathematics">mathematics</a>, <a href="/wiki/Dialectics" class="mw-redirect" title="Dialectics">dialectics</a> and theology at the Universities of <a href="/wiki/University_of_Paris" title="University of Paris">Paris</a> and <a href="/wiki/University_of_Oxford" title="University of Oxford">Oxford</a>, promoting the study of <a href="/wiki/Aristotle" title="Aristotle">Aristotle</a>. Having already an unsought reputation as an ascetic, he was ordained a priest, took a doctorate in divinity and soon became known not only for his lectures on theology but as a popular preacher, spending long years travelling within England, and engaging in 1227 preaching the <a href="/wiki/Sixth_Crusade" title="Sixth Crusade">Sixth Crusade</a>. Obliged to accept an appointment as <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_IX" title="Pope Gregory IX">Pope Gregory IX</a>, he combined a gentle personal temperament with a strong public stature and severity towards King <a href="/wiki/Henry_III_of_England" title="Henry III of England">Henry III</a> in defence of <a href="/wiki/Magna_Carta" title="Magna Carta">Magna Carta</a> and in general of good civil and Church government and justice. He also worked for strict observance in monastic life and negotiated peace with <a href="/wiki/Llywelyn_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Llywelyn the Great">Llywelyn the Great</a>. His policies earned him hostility and jealousy from the king, and opposition from several monasteries and from the clergy of Canterbury Cathedral. He died in France at the beginning of a journey to Rome in 1240. He was <a href="/wiki/Canonised" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonised">canonised</a> in 1246.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edmund_of_Abingdon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_career">Early life and career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edmund_of_Abingdon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early life and career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Edmund was born circa 1174, possibly on 20 November (the feast of St <a href="/wiki/Edmund_the_Martyr" title="Edmund the Martyr">Edmund the Martyr</a>), in <a href="/wiki/Abingdon,_Oxfordshire" class="mw-redirect" title="Abingdon, Oxfordshire">Abingdon</a> in Berkshire (now <a href="/wiki/Oxfordshire" title="Oxfordshire">Oxfordshire</a>), 7 miles south of <a href="/wiki/Oxford" title="Oxford">Oxford</a>, England. Edmund had two sisters and at least one brother.<sup id="cite_ref-edmonds_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-edmonds-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>"Rich" was an epithet sometimes given to his wealthy merchant father, Reynold.<sup id="cite_ref-monks_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-monks-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> It was never applied to Edmund or his siblings in their lifetimes. His father retired, with his wife's consent, to the monastery at <a href="/wiki/Eynsham_Abbey" title="Eynsham Abbey">Eynsham Abbey</a>, leaving in her hands the education of their family. Her name was Mabel; she was a <a href="/wiki/Faith" title="Faith">devout</a> woman who lived an <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a> life and encouraged her children to do the same. Both her daughters <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">took the veil</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edmund may have been educated at the <a href="/wiki/Abingdon_School" title="Abingdon School">monastic school</a> in <a href="/wiki/Abingdon_Abbey" title="Abingdon Abbey">Abingdon</a>. He developed a taste for religious learning, saw visions while still at school, and at the age of twelve took a vow of perpetual <a href="/wiki/Chastity" title="Chastity">chastity</a> in the <a href="/wiki/University_Church_of_St_Mary_the_Virgin" title="University Church of St Mary the Virgin">Virgin's church</a> at Oxford.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His early studies were in England, but he completed his higher learning in France at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Paris" title="University of Paris">University of Paris</a>. About 1195, in company with his brother Richard, he was sent to the schools of Paris. He studied at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Oxford" title="University of Oxford">universities of Oxford</a> and Paris and became a teacher about 1200, or a little earlier. For six years he lectured on <a href="/wiki/Mathematics" title="Mathematics">mathematics</a> and <a href="/wiki/Dialectics" class="mw-redirect" title="Dialectics">dialectics</a>, apparently dividing his time between Oxford and Paris, and helped introduce the study of <a href="/wiki/Aristotle" title="Aristotle">Aristotle</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Edmund became one of Oxford's first lecturers with a Master of Arts, but was not Oxford's first Doctor of Divinity.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Long hours at night spent in prayer had the result that he often "nodded off" during his lectures. There is a long-established tradition that he utilised his lecture-fees to build the Lady Chapel of <a href="/wiki/St_Peter%27s-in-the-East" class="mw-redirect" title="St Peter&#39;s-in-the-East">St Peter's in the East</a> at Oxford.<sup id="cite_ref-dover_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dover-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The site where he lived and taught was formed into a mediaeval <a href="/wiki/Academic_halls_of_the_University_of_Oxford" title="Academic halls of the University of Oxford">academic hall</a> in his name and later incorporated as the college of <a href="/wiki/St_Edmund_Hall,_Oxford" title="St Edmund Hall, Oxford">St Edmund Hall</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His mother's influence then led to his taking up the study of <a href="/wiki/Theology" title="Theology">theology</a>.
Though for some time Edmund resisted the change, he finally entered upon his new career between 1205 and 1210. He spent a year in retirement with the <a href="/wiki/Canons_regular#Canons_Regular_of_Saint_Augustine" class="mw-redirect" title="Canons regular">Augustinian</a> canons of <a href="/wiki/Merton_Priory" title="Merton Priory">Merton Priory</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> received ordination, took a doctorate in divinity and soon became known as a lecturer on theology and as an extemporaneous preacher. In this capacity he gained some reputation for eloquence. He spent the fees which he received in charity,<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and refused to spend upon himself the revenues which he derived from several <a href="/wiki/Benefice" title="Benefice">benefices</a>. He often retired for solitude to <a href="/wiki/Reading_Abbey" title="Reading Abbey">Reading Abbey</a>, and it is possible that he would have become a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> if that profession had afforded more scope for his gifts as a preacher and expositor.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Some time between 1219 and 1222 he was appointed vicar of the parish of <a href="/wiki/Calne" title="Calne">Calne</a> in Wiltshire, and treasurer of <a href="/wiki/Salisbury_Cathedral" title="Salisbury Cathedral">Salisbury Cathedral</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He held this position for eleven years, during which time he also engaged in preaching. In 1227 he preached the <a href="/wiki/Sixth_crusade" class="mw-redirect" title="Sixth crusade">sixth crusade</a> through a large part of England.<sup id="cite_ref-edmonds_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-edmonds-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He formed a friendship with <a href="/wiki/Ela_of_Salisbury,_3rd_Countess_of_Salisbury" title="Ela of Salisbury, 3rd Countess of Salisbury">Ela, countess of Salisbury</a>, and her husband, <a href="/wiki/William_Longesp%C3%A9e,_3rd_Earl_of_Salisbury" title="William Longespée, 3rd Earl of Salisbury">William Longespée</a>, and was noted for his works of charity and the austerity of his life.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Archbishop_of_Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edmund_of_Abingdon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Archbishop of Canterbury">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_John_the_Evangelist_Church_(Logan,_Ohio)_-_high_altar_statue,_St._Edmund_of_Abingdon.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Saint_John_the_Evangelist_Church_%28Logan%2C_Ohio%29_-_high_altar_statue%2C_St._Edmund_of_Abingdon.jpg/170px-Saint_John_the_Evangelist_Church_%28Logan%2C_Ohio%29_-_high_altar_statue%2C_St._Edmund_of_Abingdon.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="255" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Saint_John_the_Evangelist_Church_%28Logan%2C_Ohio%29_-_high_altar_statue%2C_St._Edmund_of_Abingdon.jpg/255px-Saint_John_the_Evangelist_Church_%28Logan%2C_Ohio%29_-_high_altar_statue%2C_St._Edmund_of_Abingdon.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Saint_John_the_Evangelist_Church_%28Logan%2C_Ohio%29_-_high_altar_statue%2C_St._Edmund_of_Abingdon.jpg/340px-Saint_John_the_Evangelist_Church_%28Logan%2C_Ohio%29_-_high_altar_statue%2C_St._Edmund_of_Abingdon.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3648" data-file-height="5472" /></a><figcaption>Statue of St. Edmund on the <a href="/wiki/Altar_in_the_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Altar in the Catholic Church">altar</a> in <a href="/wiki/Saint_John_the_Evangelist_Catholic_Church_Complex" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint John the Evangelist Catholic Church Complex">St. John the Evangelist Church</a> (<a href="/wiki/Logan,_Ohio" title="Logan, Ohio">Logan, Ohio</a>)</figcaption></figure>

<p>In 1233 came the news of Edmund's appointment, by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_IX" title="Pope Gregory IX">Pope Gregory IX</a>, to the Archbishopric of Canterbury. The chapter had already made three selections which the pope had declined to confirm.  Edmund's name had been proposed as a compromise by Gregory, perhaps on account of his work for the crusade.  He was consecrated on 2 April 1234.<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook233_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook233-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Before his consecration Edmund became known for supporting ecclesiastical independence from Rome, maintenance of the <a href="/wiki/Magna_Carta" title="Magna Carta">Magna Carta</a> and the exclusion of foreigners from civil and ecclesiastical office. Reluctant to accept appointment as archbishop, Edmund was persuaded when it was pointed out that if he refused, the Pope might very well appoint a foreign ecclesiastic.<sup id="cite_ref-dover_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dover-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He chose as his chancellor <a href="/wiki/Richard_of_Wich" class="mw-redirect" title="Richard of Wich">Richard of Wich</a>, known to later ages as St Richard of Chichester.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
In the name of his fellow bishops Edmund admonished <a href="/wiki/Henry_III_of_England" title="Henry III of England">Henry III of England</a> at <a href="/wiki/Westminster" title="Westminster">Westminster</a>, on 2 February 1234, to heed the example of his father, <a href="/wiki/John_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="John of England">John of England</a>. A week after his consecration he again appeared before the king with the barons and bishops, this time threatening Henry with excommunication if he refused to dismiss his councillors, many of them foreign,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDavis1911_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavis1911-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> and particularly <a href="/wiki/Peter_des_Roches" title="Peter des Roches">Peter des Roches</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Winchester" title="Bishop of Winchester">Bishop of Winchester</a>. Henry yielded, and the favourites were dismissed, <a href="/wiki/Hubert_de_Burgh" class="mw-redirect" title="Hubert de Burgh">Hubert de Burgh</a> (whom they had imprisoned) was released and reconciled to the king and soon the archbishop was sent to <a href="/wiki/Wales" title="Wales">Wales</a> to negotiate peace with <a href="/wiki/Llywelyn_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Llywelyn the Great">Llywelyn the Great</a>. Edmund's success, however, turned the king against him.<sup id="cite_ref-dover_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dover-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Edmund was valued by the local people for his teaching, preaching, study, and his prayer; but his uncompromising stand in favour of good discipline in both civil and ecclesial government, of strict observance in monastic life, and of justice in high quarters brought him into conflict with Henry III, with several monasteries, and with the priests of Canterbury cathedral.<sup id="cite_ref-millwall_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-millwall-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> He claimed and exercised metropolitan rights of visitation, this was often challenged and he had to resort to litigation to maintain his authority, not the least with his own monastic chapter at Canterbury.<sup id="cite_ref-hayward_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hayward-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Edmund_bronze.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8c/St_Edmund_bronze.jpg/170px-St_Edmund_bronze.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="220" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8c/St_Edmund_bronze.jpg/255px-St_Edmund_bronze.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8c/St_Edmund_bronze.jpg/340px-St_Edmund_bronze.jpg 2x" data-file-width="824" data-file-height="1068" /></a><figcaption>Bronze statue of Edmund at <a href="/wiki/St_Edmund_Hall,_Oxford" title="St Edmund Hall, Oxford">St Edmund Hall, Oxford</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>In 1236, with the object of emancipating himself from Edmund's control, the king asked the pope to send him a <a href="/wiki/Papal_legate" title="Papal legate">legate</a>. On the arrival of <a href="/wiki/Odo_of_Ch%C3%A2teauroux" title="Odo of Châteauroux">Cardinal Odo</a> in 1237 the archbishop found himself thwarted and insulted at every point. The politically significant marriage between <a href="/wiki/Simon_de_Montfort,_6th_Earl_of_Leicester" title="Simon de Montfort, 6th Earl of Leicester">Simon de Montfort</a> and Henry's sister <a href="/wiki/Eleanor_of_England,_Countess_of_Leicester" title="Eleanor of England, Countess of Leicester">Eleanor</a>, which Edmund had pronounced invalid, was ratified at Rome upon appeal. The king and legate upheld the monks of Canterbury in their opposition to Edmund's authority. Edmund protested to the king, and <a href="/wiki/Excommunication" title="Excommunication">excommunicated</a> in general terms all who had infringed the liberties of Canterbury. These measures had no impact, and the pope could not be moved to reverse the legate's decisions. Edmund complained that the discipline of the national church was ruined by this conflict of powers, and began to consider retirement.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Journey_to_Rome">Journey to Rome</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edmund_of_Abingdon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Journey to Rome">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Notwithstanding the gentleness of his disposition, Edmund firmly defended the rights of Church and State against the exactions and usurpations of Henry III. In December 1237 Edmund set out for Rome to plead his cause in person.<sup id="cite_ref-edmonds_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-edmonds-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> From this futile mission he returned to England in August 1238 where his efforts to foster reform were frustrated. Edmund submitted to the papal demands and, early in 1240 paid to the pope's agents one fifth of his revenue, which had been levied for the pope's war against <a href="/wiki/Emperor_Frederick_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Emperor Frederick II">Emperor Frederick II</a>.  Other English prelates followed his example.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>The papacy then ordered that 300 English benefices should be assigned to Romans. In 1240 Edmund set out for Rome. At the <a href="/wiki/Cistercian" class="mw-redirect" title="Cistercian">Cistercian</a> <a href="/wiki/Pontigny_Abbey" title="Pontigny Abbey">Pontigny Abbey</a> in France he became sick, began travelling back to England, but died only 50 miles further north, on 16 November 1240,<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook233_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook233-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> at the house of <a href="/wiki/Augustinian_Canons" class="mw-redirect" title="Augustinian Canons">Augustinian Canons</a> at <a href="/wiki/Soisy-Bouy" title="Soisy-Bouy">Soisy-Bouy</a> and was taken back to Pontigny.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edmund_of_Abingdon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Less than a year after Edmund's death, miracles were alleged to be wrought at his grave.  Despite Henry's opposition,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDavis1911_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavis1911-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> he was <a href="/wiki/Canonised" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonised">canonised</a> only six years after his death, in December 1246. His feast day is 16 November.<sup id="cite_ref-Walsh_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Walsh-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> A few years later, the first chapel dedicated to him, <a href="/wiki/St_Edmund%27s_Chapel" title="St Edmund&#39;s Chapel">St Edmund's Chapel</a>, was consecrated in <a href="/wiki/Dover,_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Dover, England">Dover</a> by his friend <a href="/wiki/Richard_of_Chichester" title="Richard of Chichester">Richard of Chichester</a>, making it the only chapel dedicated to one English saint by another; this small building still stands.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At Salisbury, a <a href="/wiki/Collegiate_church" title="Collegiate church">collegiate church</a> founded in 1269 by <a href="/wiki/Walter_de_la_Wyle" title="Walter de la Wyle">Bishop de la Wyle</a> was dedicated to Edmund; rebuilt in 1407, the church is now <a href="/wiki/Salisbury_Arts_Centre" title="Salisbury Arts Centre">Salisbury Arts Centre</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> An altar in <a href="/wiki/Salisbury_Cathedral" title="Salisbury Cathedral">the cathedral</a> is also dedicated to him.<sup id="cite_ref-hayward_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hayward-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Today he is remembered in the name of <a href="/wiki/St_Edmund_Hall,_Oxford" title="St Edmund Hall, Oxford">St Edmund Hall, Oxford</a> and <a href="/wiki/St_Edmund%27s_College,_Cambridge" title="St Edmund&#39;s College, Cambridge">St Edmund's College, Cambridge</a>. His name is given also to <a href="/wiki/St_Edmund%27s_College,_Ware" title="St Edmund&#39;s College, Ware">St Edmund's College, Ware</a>; <a href="/wiki/St_Edmund%27s_School,_Canterbury" class="mw-redirect" title="St Edmund&#39;s School, Canterbury">St Edmund's School, Canterbury</a>; <a href="/wiki/St_Edmund%27s_School,_Hindhead" title="St Edmund&#39;s School, Hindhead">St Edmund's School, Hindhead</a>; <a href="/wiki/St_Edmund%27s_Catholic_School,_Portsmouth" title="St Edmund&#39;s Catholic School, Portsmouth">St Edmund's Catholic School, Portsmouth</a>; St Edmund's Roman Catholic Primary School, Abingdon, Oxfordshire and St Edmund Preparatory High School, Brooklyn, NY.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Edmund Rich of Abingdon is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(Anglicanism)" title="Commemoration (Anglicanism)">commemoration</a> on <a href="/wiki/November_16" title="November 16">16 November</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Relics">Relics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edmund_of_Abingdon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Relics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Edmund's body was never translated to Canterbury, because the Benedictine community there resented what they regarded as Edmund's attacks on their independence.<sup id="cite_ref-hayward_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hayward-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> After his death he was taken back to <a href="/wiki/Pontigny_Abbey" title="Pontigny Abbey">Pontigny Abbey</a>, where his main relics are now found in a baroque reliquary tomb dating to the 17th century.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>An arm is enshrined in the Chapel of Our Lady of the Assumption at St. Edmund's Retreat on <a href="/wiki/Enders_Island" title="Enders Island">Enders Island</a> off the coast of Mystic, Connecticut. The retreat is operated by the Society of the Fathers and Brothers of St. Edmund.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1853, the fibula of Edmund's left leg was presented to St Edmund's College, <a href="/wiki/Ware,_Hertfordshire" title="Ware, Hertfordshire">Ware</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> by Cardinal Wiseman. Many local cures of serious illnesses were attributed to the intercession of St Edmund; one of the earliest of these was of a student who nearly died after a fall in 1871. His complete healing led to the accomplishment of a vow to extend the beautiful Pugin chapel with a side chapel to honour the saint.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Islamic silk <a href="/wiki/Chasuble" title="Chasuble">chasuble</a>, with the main fabric probably made in <a href="/wiki/Al-Andalus" title="Al-Andalus">Al-Andalus</a>, that Edmund had with him at his death remains in a local church, with a stole and <a href="/wiki/Maniple_(vestment)" title="Maniple (vestment)">maniple</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Character">Character</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edmund_of_Abingdon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Character">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Edmund's life was one of self-sacrifice and devotion to others. From boyhood he practised <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a>; such as fasting on Saturdays on bread and water, and wearing a <a href="/wiki/Cilice" title="Cilice">hair shirt</a>. After snatching a few hours' sleep, most of the night he spent in prayer and meditation.<sup id="cite_ref-edmonds_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-edmonds-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edmund_of_Abingdon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Besides his "Constitutions," issued in 1236 (printed in <a href="/wiki/W._Lynwood" class="mw-redirect" title="W. Lynwood">W. Lynwood</a>'s <i>Constitutiones Angliae</i>, Oxford, 1679), Edmund wrote a work in the genre of the <a href="/wiki/Speculum_literature" title="Speculum literature">Speculum literature</a> entitled <i>Speculum ecclesiae</i> (London, 1521; Eng. transl., 1527; reprinted in <a href="/wiki/M._de_la_Bigne" class="mw-redirect" title="M. de la Bigne">M. de la Bigne</a>'s <i>Bibliotheca veterum patrum</i>, v., Paris, 1609),<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> which was translated into <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Norman_language" title="Anglo-Norman language">Anglo-Norman</a> around the second half of the thirteenth century as <i>Mirour de Seinte Eglyse</i> ('a mirror of the holy Church'), a treatise about the <a href="/wiki/Christian_faith" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian faith">Christian faith</a> aimed at religious practitioners. (The French text was then revised in a version labelled by its editor as a '<a href="/wiki/Laity" title="Laity">lay</a> version', which is attested principally in the manuscript London, British Library, MS Arundel 288).<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Religious_Congregation_of_the_Society_of_St_Edmund">Religious Congregation of the Society of St Edmund</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Edmund_of_Abingdon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Religious Congregation of the Society of St Edmund">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Saint_Edmund" title="Society of Saint Edmund">Society of Saint Edmund</a></div>
<p>Edmund's life inspired the formation of the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Saint_Edmund" title="Society of Saint Edmund">Society of Saint Edmund</a> at <a href="/wiki/Pontigny" title="Pontigny">Pontigny</a>, France, in 1843 by <a href="/wiki/Jean_Baptiste_Muard" class="mw-redirect" title="Jean Baptiste Muard">Jean Baptiste Muard</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pierre_Boyer" class="mw-redirect" title="Pierre Boyer">Pierre Boyer</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> The Society intended to keep Edmund's memory and life alive by conducting parish missions in the archdiocese of Sens, so as to revitalize the faith of the people who had become alienated from the Church. Members of the Society, based in Pontigny, fled to the United States in 1889 after widespread <a href="/wiki/Anticlericalism" class="mw-redirect" title="Anticlericalism">anticlericalism</a> in France.  The Society of Saint Edmund settled in Winooski Park, Vermont and in 1904 established <a href="/wiki/Saint_Michael%27s_College" title="Saint Michael&#39;s College">Saint Michael's College</a> where the deeds and values of Edmund's life continue through the college mission.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Today members of the Society of Saint Edmund devote themselves to parochial work in the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Burlington" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Burlington">Diocese of Burlington</a>, Vermont, ministry to the African American community through the Edmundite Missions in <a href="/wiki/Selma,_Alabama" title="Selma, Alabama">Selma, Alabama</a>, to higher education at Saint Michael's College, and retreat ministry at Saint Edmund's Retreat (Enders Island) in Connecticut as well as <a href="/wiki/Saint_Anne%27s_Shrine" title="Saint Anne&#39;s Shrine">Saint Anne's Shrine</a> in Vermont.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
