<p><b>Nicodemus</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="&#39;n&#39; in &#39;nigh&#39;">n</span><span title="/ɪ/: &#39;i&#39; in &#39;kit&#39;">ɪ</span><span title="&#39;k&#39; in &#39;kind&#39;">k</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;d&#39; in &#39;dye&#39;">d</span><span title="/iː/: &#39;ee&#39; in &#39;fleece&#39;">iː</span><span title="&#39;m&#39; in &#39;my&#39;">m</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;s&#39; in &#39;sigh&#39;">s</span></span>/</a></span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Νικόδημος</span></span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek" title="Romanization of Greek">translit.</a></small>&#160;<span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn"><i>Nikódēmos</i></i></span>; <a href="/wiki/Imperial_Aramaic_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Imperial Aramaic language">Imperial Aramaic</a>: <span lang="arc" dir="rtl">𐡍𐡒𐡃𐡉𐡌𐡅𐡍</span>, <small>romanized:&#160;</small><span title="Imperial Aramaic-language romanization"><i lang="arc-Latn">Naqdīmūn</i></span>; <a href="/wiki/Hebrew_language" title="Hebrew language">Hebrew</a>: <span lang="he" dir="rtl">נַקְדִּימוֹן</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Hebrew" title="Romanization of Hebrew">romanized</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Hebrew-language romanization"><i lang="he-Latn">Naqdīmōn</i></span>) is a New Testament figure venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in a number of Christian traditions.  Depicted as a <a href="/wiki/Pharisee" class="mw-redirect" title="Pharisee">Pharisee</a> and a member of the <a href="/wiki/Sanhedrin" title="Sanhedrin">Sanhedrin</a> who is drawn to hear Jesus' teachings, he is mentioned in three places in the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_John" title="Gospel of John">Gospel of John</a>:
</p>
<ul><li>He first visits Jesus one night to discuss Jesus' teachings (<a href="/wiki/John_3" title="John 3">John 3</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>The second time Nicodemus is mentioned, he reminds his colleagues in the Sanhedrin that the <a href="/wiki/Jewish_law" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish law">law</a> requires that a person be heard before being judged (<a href="/wiki/John_7" title="John 7">John 7</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Finally, Nicodemus appears after <a href="/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus" title="Crucifixion of Jesus">Jesus' crucifixion</a> to provide the customary spices for anointing the dead, and assists <a href="/wiki/Joseph_of_Arimathea" title="Joseph of Arimathea">Joseph of Arimathea</a> in preparing the <a href="/wiki/Burial_of_Jesus" title="Burial of Jesus">body of Jesus for burial</a> (<a href="/wiki/John_19" title="John 19">John 19</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<p>An <a href="/wiki/Apocrypha" title="Apocrypha">apocryphal</a> work under his name—the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_Nicodemus" title="Gospel of Nicodemus">Gospel of Nicodemus</a>—was produced in the mid-4th century, and is mostly a reworking of the earlier <a href="/wiki/Acts_of_Pilate" class="mw-redirect" title="Acts of Pilate">Acts of Pilate</a>, which recounts the <a href="/wiki/Harrowing_of_Hell" title="Harrowing of Hell">Harrowing of Hell</a>.
</p><p>Although there is no clear source of information about Nicodemus outside the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_John" title="Gospel of John">Gospel of John</a>, Ochser and <a href="/wiki/Kaufmann_Kohler" title="Kaufmann Kohler">Kohler</a>, writing in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Jewish_Encyclopedia" title="The Jewish Encyclopedia">The Jewish Encyclopedia</a></i> in 1905,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> identify him with <a href="/wiki/Nicodemus_ben_Gurion" title="Nicodemus ben Gurion">Nicodemus ben Gurion</a>, mentioned in the <a href="/wiki/Talmud" title="Talmud">Talmud</a> as a wealthy and popular holy man reputed to have had miraculous powers. Some 21st-century historians make the same connection.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Other scholars reject this identification, arguing that the biblical Nicodemus is likely an older man at the time of his conversation with Jesus, while Nicodemus ben Gurion was on the scene forty years later, at the time of the <a href="/wiki/First_Jewish-Roman_War" class="mw-redirect" title="First Jewish-Roman War">First Jewish-Roman War</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Carson_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Carson-7">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Nicodemus is considered by both <a href="/wiki/Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic">Catholic</a> and <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Churches</a> to have secretly been a <a href="/wiki/Disciple_(Christianity)" title="Disciple (Christianity)">disciple</a> of Jesus on the basis of the narrative in <a href="/wiki/John_19" title="John 19">John 19</a>; there is no explicit mention of his discipleship in the Gospel of John. Owing to his insistence on a hearing for Jesus according to Jewish law, Nicodemus is sometimes referred to as "defender of Jesus".<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Gospel_narrative">Gospel narrative</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Gospel narrative">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

Nicodemus (left) talking to <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">Jesus</a>, by <a href="/wiki/Henry_Ossawa_Tanner" title="Henry Ossawa Tanner">Henry Ossawa Tanner</a></figcaption></figure>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_(ca.1886).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg/270px-Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="270" height="236" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg/405px-Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg/540px-Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="936" data-file-height="819" /></a><figcaption>Christus und Nicodemus, by <a href="/wiki/Fritz_von_Uhde" title="Fritz von Uhde">Fritz von Uhde</a> (1848–1911)</figcaption></figure>
<p>As is the case with <a href="/wiki/Lazarus_of_Bethany" title="Lazarus of Bethany">Lazarus</a>, Nicodemus is not part of the tradition of the <a href="/wiki/Synoptic_Gospels" title="Synoptic Gospels">synoptic Gospels</a>, and is mentioned only by John,<sup id="cite_ref-driscoll_CE_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-driscoll_CE-10">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> who devotes more than half of <a href="/wiki/John_3" title="John 3">Chapter 3</a> of his gospel  and a few verses of <a href="/wiki/John_7" title="John 7">Chapter 7</a> to Nicodemus, and lastly mentions him in <a href="/wiki/John_19" title="John 19">Chapter 19</a>.
</p><p>The first time Nicodemus is mentioned, he is identified as a Pharisee who comes to see Jesus at night. According to the scripture, Jesus went to Jerusalem for the <a href="/wiki/Passover" title="Passover">Passover</a> feast. While in Jerusalem he <a href="/wiki/Cleansing_of_the_Temple" title="Cleansing of the Temple">chased the moneychangers from the temple and overturned their tables</a>. His disciples remembered then the words of <a href="/wiki/Psalm_69" title="Psalm 69">Psalm 69</a>: "Zeal for your house will consume me." After these events "many believed in his name when they saw the signs that he was doing".<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;d&#93;</a></sup> When Nicodemus visits Jesus he makes reference to these events: "Rabbi, we know that you are a teacher who has come from God. For no one could perform the signs you are doing if God were not with him".<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;e&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Jesus replies: "Unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God." Then follows a conversation with Nicodemus about the meaning of being "<a href="/wiki/Born_again_(Christianity)" class="mw-redirect" title="Born again (Christianity)">born again</a>" or "born from above" (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">ἄνωθεν</span>): Nicodemus explores the notion of being literally born again from one's mother's <a href="/wiki/Womb" class="mw-redirect" title="Womb">womb</a>, but most theologians recognise that Nicodemus knew Jesus was not speaking of literal rebirth. Theologian <a href="/wiki/Charles_Ellicott" title="Charles Ellicott">Charles Ellicott</a> wrote that "after the method of Rabbinic dialogue, [Nicodemus] presses the impossible meaning of the words in order to exclude it, and to draw forth the true meaning. 'You cannot mean that a man is to enter the second time into his mother's womb, and be born. What is it, then, that you do mean?<span class="nowrap" style="padding-left:0.1em;">&#39;</span>"<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In this instance, Nicodemus chooses the literal (rather than the figurative) meaning of <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn">anōthen</i></span> and assumes that that meaning exhausts the significance of the word.
</p><p>Jesus expresses surprise, perhaps ironically, that "a <a href="/wiki/Rabbi" title="Rabbi">teacher of Israel</a>" does not understand the concept of spiritual rebirth:<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;f&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>Jesus answered him, "Are you the teacher of Israel and yet you do not understand these things? Truly, truly, I say to you, we speak of what we know, and bear witness to what we have seen, but you do not receive our testimony."</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite>John 3:10–11</cite></div></blockquote>
<p>In Chapter 7, Nicodemus advises his colleagues among "the chief priests and the Pharisees", to hear and investigate before making a judgment concerning Jesus, reminding them that Jewish law requires that a person must be heard before they can be condemned. Their mocking response argues that no prophet comes from <a href="/wiki/Galilee" title="Galilee">Galilee</a>. Nonetheless, it is probable that he wielded a certain influence in the Sanhedrin.<sup id="cite_ref-driscoll_CE_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-driscoll_CE-10">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Finally, when Jesus is buried, Nicodemus brought a mixture of <a href="/wiki/Myrrh" title="Myrrh">myrrh</a> and <a href="/wiki/Aloes" class="mw-redirect" title="Aloes">aloes</a>—about 100 <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Roman_units_of_measurement#Weight" title="Ancient Roman units of measurement">Roman pounds</a> (33 kilograms, or 73&#160;lb).<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;g&#93;</a></sup> Nicodemus must have been a man of means; in his book <i><a href="/wiki/Jesus_of_Nazareth:_Holy_Week" title="Jesus of Nazareth: Holy Week">Jesus of Nazareth: Holy Week</a></i>, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a> observes that, "The quantity of the <a href="/wiki/Liniment" title="Liniment">balm</a> is extraordinary and exceeds all normal proportions. This is a royal burial."<sup id="cite_ref-Burke_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burke-16">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nicodemus is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in Eastern and <a href="/wiki/Oriental_Orthodoxy" class="mw-redirect" title="Oriental Orthodoxy">Oriental Orthodoxy</a> and in Catholicism. The Eastern Orthodox and <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Catholic_Churches" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine Catholic Churches">Byzantine Catholic Churches</a> commemorate him on the Sunday of the Holy <a href="/wiki/Myrrhbearers" title="Myrrhbearers">Myrrhbearers</a>, which is celebrated on the Third Sunday of <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Pascha</a> (i.e., the second Sunday after Easter).<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Sacred_tradition" title="Sacred tradition">Sacred tradition</a> holds that his <a href="/wiki/Relics" class="mw-redirect" title="Relics">relics</a> were found on 2 August, along with those of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Stephen" title="Saint Stephen">Saint Stephen</a> the <a href="/wiki/Protomartyr" class="mw-redirect" title="Protomartyr">Protomartyr</a>; <a href="/wiki/Gamaliel" title="Gamaliel">Gamaliel</a>; and Gamaliel's second son, <a href="/wiki/Abibon" title="Abibon">Abibon</a> (also, "Abibas"; "Abibo"). This event is commemorated on that date in the Eastern churches,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> while the Catholic <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> marks the anniversary of the <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">translation</a> of Nicodemus' remains, rather than their discovery, which by the same tradition occurred the following day, 3 August.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> In the <i><a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a></i>, Nicodemus' feast day is 31 August, celebrated jointly with Saint <a href="/wiki/Joseph_of_Arimathea" title="Joseph of Arimathea">Joseph of Arimathea</a>, and generally followed in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Rite" title="Roman Rite">Roman Rite</a> tradition.<sup id="cite_ref-driscoll_CE_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-driscoll_CE-10">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>This does not preclude local preferences and traditions from being applied, as dioceses, national churches and religious institutes may have their own days of observance approved.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> For example, at the <a href="/wiki/St._Nicodemus_and_St._Joseph_of_Arimathea_Church" title="St. Nicodemus and St. Joseph of Arimathea Church">St. Nicodemus and St. Joseph of Arimathea Church</a>, which the <a href="/wiki/Franciscan_Order" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan Order">Franciscan Order</a> established in <a href="/wiki/Ramla" title="Ramla">Ramla</a> in the 19th century, permission from the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Patriarch of Jerusalem">Latin Patriarch of Jerusalem</a> was obtained to celebrate the feast day of the two saints on the Saturday of the third week of Easter, so that the link between their role in the burial of Jesus and Easter <a href="/wiki/Solemnities" class="mw-redirect" title="Solemnities">solemnities</a> would be highlighted in the Holy Land.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The church has a painting above its altar, attributed to <a href="/wiki/Titian" title="Titian">Titian</a>, <i>The Deposition from the Cross</i>, which shows the two saints.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Days_of_observance">Days of observance</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Days of observance">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<ul><li>Eastern Christianity (including Eastern and Oriental Orthodoxy and Eastern-rite Catholics):

<li>As a Holy Myrrhbearer: Third Sunday of Pascha (Easter)</li></ul></li>
<li>Roman-rite Catholicism:
<ul><li>Translation of the saint's relics: 3 August</li>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Art">Art</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Art">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Entierro_de_Cristo_(Tiziano).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg/270px-Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="270" height="211" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg/405px-Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg/540px-Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3051" data-file-height="2381" /></a><figcaption>Entombment, by <a href="/wiki/Titian" title="Titian">Titian</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>Nicodemus figures prominently in <a href="/wiki/Medieval" class="mw-redirect" title="Medieval">medieval</a> depictions of the Deposition or <a href="/wiki/Descent_from_the_Cross" title="Descent from the Cross">Descent from the Cross</a> in which he and Joseph of Arimathea are shown removing the dead Christ from the cross, often with the aid of a ladder.<sup id="cite_ref-schiller_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schiller-24">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Like Joseph, Nicodemus became the object of various pious legends during the Middle Ages, particularly in connection with <a href="/wiki/Monumental_crosses" class="mw-redirect" title="Monumental crosses">monumental crosses</a>. He was reputed to have carved both the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Face_of_Lucca" title="Holy Face of Lucca">Holy Face of Lucca</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Batll%C3%B3_Crucifix" class="mw-redirect" title="Batlló Crucifix">Batlló Crucifix</a>, receiving <a href="/wiki/Angel" title="Angel">angelic</a> assistance with the face in particular and thus rendering the works instances of <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn"><a href="/wiki/Acheiropoieta" title="Acheiropoieta">acheiropoieta</a></i></span>.<sup id="cite_ref-schiller_24-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schiller-24">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Both of these sculptures date from at least a millennium after Nicodemus' life, but the ascriptions attest to the contemporary interest in Nicodemus as a character in medieval Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-schiller_24-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-schiller-24">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Literature">Literature</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Literature">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><i>Persuaded: The Story of Nicodemus</i> by David Harder is a fictionalized account of the life of Nicodemus. According to the author, he used episodes and timetables sourced from all four gospels and the <a href="/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles" title="Acts of the Apostles">Acts of the Apostles</a> to develop his novel's timeline of events and extensively researched the culture, politics and history of the period. He consulted the Passion Translation<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;h&#93;</a></sup> version of the Bible to do so; scripture quoted within the novel is taken from there.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>  Harder has spoken of his attempts to bring biblical characters to life in a relatable way, while maintaining overall historical and scriptural accuracy.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Henry_Vaughan" title="Henry Vaughan">Henry Vaughan</a>'s "The Night," Nicodemus is significant to the poem's theme: he serves as the departure point and illustration of its meditation on night's relationship with experience of God.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Music">Music</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Music">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 18th-century <a href="/wiki/Lutheranism" title="Lutheranism">Lutheranism</a>, prescribed readings were assigned throughout the year; the gospel text of the meeting of Jesus and Nicodemus at night was assigned to <a href="/wiki/Trinity_Sunday" title="Trinity Sunday">Trinity Sunday</a>. <a href="/wiki/Johann_Sebastian_Bach" title="Johann Sebastian Bach">Johann Sebastian Bach</a> composed several <a href="/wiki/Church_cantata_(Bach)#Trinity" title="Church cantata (Bach)">cantatas</a> for the occasion, of which <span title="German-language text"><span lang="de"><a href="/wiki/O_heilges_Geist-_und_Wasserbad,_BWV_165" title="O heilges Geist- und Wasserbad, BWV 165"><i>O heilges Geist- und Wasserbad</i>, BWV 165</a></span></span>, composed in 1715, stays close to the gospel based on a libretto by the court poet in <a href="/wiki/Weimar" title="Weimar">Weimar</a>, <a href="/wiki/Salomo_Franck" class="mw-redirect" title="Salomo Franck">Salomo Franck</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In popular music, Nicodemus' name was used figuratively in <a href="/wiki/Henry_Clay_Work" title="Henry Clay Work">Henry Clay Work</a>'s 1864 <a href="/wiki/American_Civil_War" title="American Civil War">American Civil War</a>-era piece "Wake Nicodemus", an abolitionist song hailing the end of slavery,<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> which at that time was popular in <a href="/wiki/Minstrel_show" title="Minstrel show">minstrel shows</a>. In 1978 <a href="/wiki/Tim_Curry" title="Tim Curry">Tim Curry</a> covered the song on his debut album <i><a href="/wiki/Read_My_Lips_(Tim_Curry_album)" title="Read My Lips (Tim Curry album)">Read My Lips</a></i>. The song's layered connotations have given it an association with the civil rights movement in the <a href="#United_States">§&#160;United States</a>. 
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Ernst_Pepping" title="Ernst Pepping">Ernst Pepping</a> composed an <span title="German-language text"><i lang="de">Evangelienmotette</i></span> (<a href="/wiki/Motet" title="Motet">motet</a> on gospel text) <span title="German-language text"><i lang="de"><a href="/wiki/Jesus_und_Nikodemus" title="Jesus und Nikodemus">Jesus und Nikodemus</a></i></span> in 1937. In 1941, <a href="/wiki/The_Golden_Gate_Quartet" class="mw-redirect" title="The Golden Gate Quartet">The Golden Gate Quartet</a>, sang the Gospel "God Told Nicodemus", in the African-American Jubilee style. The song "Help Yourself" by <a href="/wiki/The_Devil_Makes_Three_(band)" title="The Devil Makes Three (band)">The Devil Makes Three</a> contains a very informal retelling of the relationship between Nicodemus and Jesus.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Film_and_television">Film and television</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Film and television">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>

</p><p>The figure of Nicodemus appears in several television productions:
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Social_influence">Social influence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Social influence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Reformation_and_religious_conflict">The Reformation and religious conflict</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: The Reformation and religious conflict">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Nicodemite" title="Nicodemite">Nicodemite</a></div><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Reformation" title="Reformation">Reformation</a></div>
<p>During the struggle between <a href="/wiki/Protestant" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant">Protestants</a> and <a href="/wiki/Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic">Catholics</a> in Europe, from the 16th century to the 18th, a person professing a creed different from the locally approved one often risked severe penalties—in many cases, capital punishment. There developed the use of "<a href="/wiki/Nicodemite" title="Nicodemite">Nicodemite</a>", usually a <a href="/wiki/Term_of_disparagement" class="mw-redirect" title="Term of disparagement">term of disparagement</a>, referring to a person who is suspected of public misrepresentation of their actual religious beliefs.<sup id="cite_ref-overell_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-overell-36">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 117–118">&#58;&#8202;117–118&#8202;</span></sup><sup id="cite_ref-CODoCC_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CODoCC-37">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
The term is recorded from at least 1529 (in, at first, a slightly different sense, although still related to religious concealment or reticence). It is a reference to the clandestine night visit of Nicodemus to Jesus, suggesting an analogy between the undeclared belief of Nicodemus and the reluctance of some dissenters from Catholicism to risk being open about their true creed. To <a href="/wiki/John_Calvin" title="John Calvin">John Calvin</a>, who opposed all veneration of saints, the fact of Nicodemus becoming a Catholic saint in no way exonerated this "duplicity". Calvin used the word in his 1544 <span title="French-language text"><i lang="fr">Excuse à messieurs les Nicodemites</i></span> referring to religious dissemblers in France—outwardly, conforming Catholics; inwardly, adherents of Protestantism. He was apparently not completely comfortable with the term's allusion to Nicodemus, however; subsequent editions of the work reduced the label's use and later French editions replaced the word with "<span title="French-language text"><i lang="fr">faux</i></span> ('false') Nicodemus" instead.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> While the epithet initially applied to <a href="/wiki/Crypto-protestantism" class="mw-redirect" title="Crypto-protestantism">crypto-protestants</a>, it later came to be used broadly for anyone suspected of exhibiting a false appearance of their religious adherence and concealing their genuine beliefs.<sup id="cite_ref-CODoCC_37-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CODoCC-37">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="United_States">United States</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: United States">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The discussion with Jesus is the source of several common expressions of contemporary <a href="/wiki/American_Christianity" class="mw-redirect" title="American Christianity">American Christianity</a>, specifically, the descriptive phrase "<a href="/wiki/Born_again_(Christianity)" class="mw-redirect" title="Born again (Christianity)">born again</a>" used to describe salvation or <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptism</a> by some groups, and <a href="/wiki/John_3:16" title="John 3:16">John 3:16</a>, a commonly quoted verse used to describe God's plan of salvation.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Nicodemus_National_Historic_Site" title="Nicodemus National Historic Site">Nicodemus National Historic Site</a>, commemorating the only remaining western town established by African Americans during the Reconstruction Period following the <a href="/wiki/American_Civil_War" title="American Civil War">American Civil War</a>, is in <a href="/wiki/Kansas" title="Kansas">Kansas</a>. The <a href="/wiki/National_Park_Service" title="National Park Service">National Park Service</a> indicates the town's name came from the 1864 piece, "Wake Nicodemus" by <a href="/wiki/Henry_Clay_Work" title="Henry Clay Work">Henry Clay Work</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> Some point to the lyrics' use of the name <i>Nicodemus</i> as a figurative reference to the biblical figure. In the case of the song, Nicodemus is an enslaved African, long deceased, but acknowledged in the lyrics as both prophet and herald of freedom. The contrast in the lyrics between <i>night</i> and <i>morning</i>, and the enslaved Nicodemus' certainty in what the metaphorical "morning" would bring, have been seen as drawing a parallel to, and being inspired by, the story of the biblical figure. In one story, emancipation, and in the other, salvation, is to come "in the morning"; each Nicodemus has faith in their eventual advent. The lyrics of first verse (in part), the chorus, and the fourth verse, say:<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"><blockquote class="templatequote"><div class="poem">
<p>Nicodemus, the slave, was of African birth,<br />
And was bought for a bagful of gold;<br />
<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>[...]<br />
"Wake me up!" was his charge,<br />
at the first break of day–<br />
Wake me up for the great Jubilee!<br />
<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span><br />
The "Good Coming" is almost here!<br />
It was long, long, long on the way!<br />
Now run and tell Elijah to hurry up, Pomp<br />
And meet up at the gum tree down in the swamp,<br />
To wake Nicodemus today.<br />
<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>[...]<br />
'Twas a long weary night – we were almost in fear<br />
That the future was more than he knew;<br />
'Twas a long weary night – but the morning is near,<br />
And the words of our prophet are true.
</p>
</div><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite>Extract from "Wake Nicodemus" by Henry Clay Work (1864)</cite></div></blockquote>
<p>Rosamund Rodman noted in a 2008 article that slaves who learnt to read generally did so in secret and at night, due to risks of punishment for this forbidden activity. The gospel's Nicodemus came to Jesus to learn from him, also in secret and at night, for fear of repercussions. Such connections and allusions lead Rodman to conclude that the town's name has its ultimate origin with the biblical figure.<sup id="cite_ref-rodman_41-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rodman-41">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Daniel Burke notes that, "To blacks after the <a href="/wiki/American_Civil_War" title="American Civil War">Civil War</a>, he was a model of rebirth as they sought to cast off their old identity as slaves".<sup id="cite_ref-Burke_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burke-16">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 16 August 1967, <a href="/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr." title="Martin Luther King Jr.">Martin Luther King Jr.</a> invoked Nicodemus as a metaphor concerning the need for the United States to be "born again" in order to effectively address social and economic inequality. The speech was called "Where Do We Go From Here?", and delivered at the 11th Annual SCLC Convention in <a href="/wiki/Atlanta" title="Atlanta">Atlanta</a>, Georgia.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
