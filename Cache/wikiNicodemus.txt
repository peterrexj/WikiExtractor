<p><b>Nicodemus</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="&#39;n&#39; in &#39;nigh&#39;">n</span><span title="/ɪ/: &#39;i&#39; in &#39;kit&#39;">ɪ</span><span title="&#39;k&#39; in &#39;kind&#39;">k</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;d&#39; in &#39;dye&#39;">d</span><span title="/iː/: &#39;ee&#39; in &#39;fleece&#39;">iː</span><span title="&#39;m&#39; in &#39;my&#39;">m</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;s&#39; in &#39;sigh&#39;">s</span></span>/</a></span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Νικόδημος</span></span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek" title="Romanization of Greek">translit.</a></small>&#160;<span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn"><i>Nikódēmos</i></i></span>) was a <a href="/wiki/Pharisee" class="mw-redirect" title="Pharisee">Pharisee</a> and a member of the <a href="/wiki/Sanhedrin" title="Sanhedrin">Sanhedrin</a> mentioned in three places in the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_John" title="Gospel of John">Gospel of John</a>:
</p>
<ul><li>He first visits Jesus one night to discuss Jesus' teachings (<a href="/wiki/John_3" title="John 3">John 3</a>, <span class="nourlexpansion nowrap"><a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+3%3A1%E2%80%9321&amp;version=NRSV">John 3:1–21</a></span>).</li>
<li>The second time Nicodemus is mentioned, he reminds his colleagues in the Sanhedrin that the <a href="/wiki/Jewish_law" class="mw-redirect" title="Jewish law">law</a> requires that a person be heard before being judged (<a href="/wiki/John_7" title="John 7">John 7</a>, <span class="nourlexpansion nowrap"><a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+7%3A50%E2%80%9351&amp;version=NRSV">John 7:50–51</a></span>).</li>
<li>Finally, Nicodemus appears after the <a href="/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus" title="Crucifixion of Jesus">Crucifixion of Jesus</a> to provide the customary embalming spices, and assists <a href="/wiki/Joseph_of_Arimathea" title="Joseph of Arimathea">Joseph of Arimathea</a> in preparing the body of Jesus for <a href="/wiki/Burial_of_Jesus" title="Burial of Jesus">burial</a> (<a href="/wiki/John_19" title="John 19">John 19</a>, <span class="nourlexpansion nowrap"><a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+19%3A39%E2%80%9342&amp;version=NRSV">John 19:39–42</a></span>).</li></ul>
<p>An <a href="/wiki/Apocrypha" title="Apocrypha">apocryphal</a> work under his name—the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_Nicodemus" title="Gospel of Nicodemus">Gospel of Nicodemus</a>—was produced in the mid-4th century, and is mostly a reworking of the earlier <a href="/wiki/Acts_of_Pilate" class="mw-redirect" title="Acts of Pilate">Acts of Pilate</a>, which recounts the <a href="/wiki/Harrowing_of_Hell" title="Harrowing of Hell">Harrowing of Hell</a>.
</p><p>Although there is no clear source of information about Nicodemus outside the Gospel of John, Ochser and <a href="/wiki/Kaufmann_Kohler" title="Kaufmann Kohler">Kohler</a> (in an article in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Jewish_Encyclopedia" title="The Jewish Encyclopedia">The Jewish Encyclopedia</a></i>) and some historians<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> have speculated that he could be identical to <a href="/wiki/Nicodemus_ben_Gurion" title="Nicodemus ben Gurion">Nicodemus ben Gurion</a>, mentioned in the <a href="/wiki/Talmud" title="Talmud">Talmud</a> as a wealthy and popular holy man reputed to have had miraculous powers. Others point out that the biblical Nicodemus is likely an older man at the time of his conversation with Jesus, while Nicodemus ben Gurion was on the scene 40 years later, at the time of the <a href="/wiki/First_Jewish-Roman_War" class="mw-redirect" title="First Jewish-Roman War">First Jewish-Roman War</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Carson_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Carson-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span id="In_John.27s_Gospel"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="In_John's_Gospel">In John's Gospel</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: In John&#039;s Gospel">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:272px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Henry_Ossawa_Tanner_-_Jesus_and_nicodemus.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Henry_Ossawa_Tanner_-_Jesus_and_nicodemus.jpg/270px-Henry_Ossawa_Tanner_-_Jesus_and_nicodemus.jpg" decoding="async" width="270" height="231" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Henry_Ossawa_Tanner_-_Jesus_and_nicodemus.jpg/405px-Henry_Ossawa_Tanner_-_Jesus_and_nicodemus.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Henry_Ossawa_Tanner_-_Jesus_and_nicodemus.jpg/540px-Henry_Ossawa_Tanner_-_Jesus_and_nicodemus.jpg 2x" data-file-width="701" data-file-height="600" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Henry_Ossawa_Tanner_-_Jesus_and_nicodemus.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Nicodemus (left) talking to <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">Jesus</a>, by <a href="/wiki/Henry_Ossawa_Tanner" title="Henry Ossawa Tanner">Henry Ossawa Tanner</a></div></div></div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:272px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_(ca.1886).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg/270px-Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="270" height="236" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg/405px-Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg/540px-Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_%28ca.1886%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="936" data-file-height="819" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Fritz_von_Uhde_-_Christus_und_Nikodemus_(ca.1886).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Christus und Nicodemus, by <a href="/wiki/Fritz_von_Uhde" title="Fritz von Uhde">Fritz von Uhde</a> (1848–1911)</div></div></div>
<p>As is the case with <a href="/wiki/Lazarus_of_Bethany" title="Lazarus of Bethany">Lazarus</a>, Nicodemus does not belong to the tradition of the <a href="/wiki/Synoptic_Gospels" title="Synoptic Gospels">Synoptic Gospels</a>, and is mentioned only by <a href="/wiki/John_the_Apostle" title="John the Apostle">John</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Driscoll_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Driscoll-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> who devotes more than half of <a href="/wiki/John_3" title="John 3">Chapter 3</a> of his gospel  and a few verses of <a href="/wiki/John_7" title="John 7">Chapter 7</a> to Nicodemus, and lastly mentions him in <a href="/wiki/John_19" title="John 19">Chapter 19</a>.
</p><p>The first time Nicodemus is mentioned, he is identified as a Pharisee who comes to see Jesus at night. According to the scripture, Jesus went to Jerusalem for the <a href="/wiki/Passover" title="Passover">Passover</a> feast. While in Jerusalem he <a href="/wiki/Cleansing_of_the_Temple" title="Cleansing of the Temple">chased the moneychangers from the temple and overturned their tables</a>. His disciples remembered then the words of <a href="/wiki/Psalm_69" title="Psalm 69">Psalm 69</a>: "Zeal for your house will consume me." After these events "many believed in his name when they saw the signs that he was doing" (<span class="nourlexpansion nowrap"><a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+2%3A23%E2%80%9325&amp;version=NRSV">John 2:23–25</a></span>). When Nicodemus visits Jesus he makes reference to these events: "Rabbi, we know that you are a teacher who has come from God. For no one could perform the signs you are doing if God were not with him."(<span class="nourlexpansion nowrap"><a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+3%3A2&amp;version=NRSV">John 3:2</a></span>).
</p><p>Jesus replies: "Unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God." Then follows a conversation with Nicodemus about the meaning of being "<a href="/wiki/Born_again_(Christianity)" class="mw-redirect" title="Born again (Christianity)">born again</a>" or "born from above" (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">ἄνωθεν</span>): Nicodemus explores the notion of being literally born again from one's mother's <a href="/wiki/Womb" class="mw-redirect" title="Womb">womb</a>, but most theologians recognise that Nicodemus knew Jesus was not speaking of literal rebirth. Theologian <a href="/wiki/Charles_Ellicott" title="Charles Ellicott">Charles Ellicott</a> wrote that "after the method of Rabbinic dialogue, [Nicodemus] presses the impossible meaning of the words in order to exclude it, and to draw forth the true meaning. 'You cannot mean that a man is to enter the second time into his mother’s womb, and be born. What is it, then, that you do mean?'"<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In this instance, Nicodemus chooses the literal (rather than the figurative) meaning of <i>anōthen</i> and assumes that that meaning exhausts the significance of the word.
</p><p>Jesus expresses surprise, perhaps ironically, that "a <a href="/wiki/Rabbi" title="Rabbi">teacher of Israel</a>" does not understand the concept of spiritual rebirth:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>Art thou a master of Israel, and knowest not these things? Verily, verily, I say unto thee, We speak that we do know, and testify that we have seen; and ye receive not our <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">witness</a>.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8201;<cite>John 3:10–11, KJV</cite></div></blockquote>
<p>In Chapter 7, Nicodemus advises his colleagues among "the chief priests and the Pharisees", to hear and investigate before making a judgment concerning Jesus. Their mocking response argues that no prophet comes from <a href="/wiki/Galilee" title="Galilee">Galilee</a>. Nonetheless, it is probable that he wielded a certain influence in the Sanhedrin.<sup id="cite_ref-Driscoll_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Driscoll-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Finally, when Jesus is buried, Nicodemus brought a mixture of <a href="/wiki/Myrrh" title="Myrrh">myrrh</a> and <a href="/wiki/Aloe" title="Aloe">aloes</a>—about 100 <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Roman_units_of_measurement#Weight" title="Ancient Roman units of measurement">Roman pounds</a> (33 kilograms (73&#160;lb))—despite embalming being generally against Jewish custom (with the exceptions of <a href="/wiki/Jacob" title="Jacob">Jacob</a> and <a href="/wiki/Joseph_(Genesis)" title="Joseph (Genesis)">Joseph</a>).<sup class="nourlexpansion reference"><a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+19%3A39&amp;version=NRSV">&#91;John 19:39&#93;</a></sup> Nicodemus must have been a man of means; in his book <i><a href="/wiki/Jesus_of_Nazareth:_Holy_Week" title="Jesus of Nazareth: Holy Week">Jesus of Nazareth: Holy Week</a></i>, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a> observes that, "The quantity of the <a href="/wiki/Liniment" title="Liniment">balm</a> is extraordinary and exceeds all normal proportions. This is a royal burial."<sup id="cite_ref-Burke_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burke-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration_and_liturgical_commemoration">Veneration and liturgical commemoration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration and liturgical commemoration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Nicodemus is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in the various <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy" title="Eastern Orthodoxy">Eastern</a> <a href="/wiki/Oriental_Orthodoxy" class="mw-redirect" title="Oriental Orthodoxy">Churches</a> and in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox</a> and <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Catholic_Churches" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine Catholic Churches">Byzantine-rite Catholic</a> churches commemorate Nicodemus on the <a href="/wiki/Myrrhbearers" title="Myrrhbearers">Sunday of the Myrrhbearers</a>, celebrated on the <i>Third Sunday of <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Pascha</a></i> (i.e., the second Sunday after Easter) as well as 2 August, the date when <a href="/wiki/Sacred_tradition" title="Sacred tradition">tradition</a> holds that his <a href="/wiki/Relics" class="mw-redirect" title="Relics">relics</a> were found, along with those of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Stephen" title="Saint Stephen">Stephen</a> the <a href="/wiki/Protomartyr" class="mw-redirect" title="Protomartyr">Protomartyr</a>, <a href="/wiki/Gamaliel" title="Gamaliel">Gamaliel</a>, and Abibas (Gamaliel's second son). The traditional <a href="/wiki/Western_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Western Church">Roman-rite Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Liturgical_calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Liturgical calendar">liturgical calendar</a> lists the same feast of the finding of their relics on the following day, 3 August.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2018)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>In the current <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, Nicodemus is commemorated along with <a href="/wiki/Joseph_of_Arimathea" title="Joseph of Arimathea">Saint Joseph of Arimathea</a> on 31 August. The <a href="/wiki/Franciscan_Order" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan Order">Franciscan Order</a> erected a church under the patronage of <a href="/wiki/St._Nicodemus_and_St._Joseph_of_Arimathea_Church" title="St. Nicodemus and St. Joseph of Arimathea Church">Saints Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathea</a> in <a href="/wiki/Ramla" title="Ramla">Ramla</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="In_art">In art</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: In art">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:272px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Entierro_de_Cristo_(Tiziano).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg/270px-Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="270" height="211" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg/405px-Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg/540px-Entierro_de_Cristo_%28Tiziano%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3051" data-file-height="2381" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Entierro_de_Cristo_(Tiziano).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Entombment, by <a href="/wiki/Titian" title="Titian">Titian</a></div></div></div>
<p>Nicodemus figures prominently in <a href="/wiki/Medieval" class="mw-redirect" title="Medieval">medieval</a> depictions of the <a href="/wiki/Descent_from_the_Cross" title="Descent from the Cross">Deposition</a> in which he and Joseph of Arimathea are shown removing the dead Christ from the cross, often with the aid of a ladder.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2018)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Like Joseph, Nicodemus became the object of various pious <a href="/wiki/Legend" title="Legend">legends</a> during the Middle Ages, particularly in connection with <a href="/wiki/Monumental_crosses" title="Monumental crosses">monumental crosses</a>. He was reputed to have carved both the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Face_of_Lucca" title="Holy Face of Lucca">Holy Face of Lucca</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Batllo_Crucifix" class="mw-redirect" title="Batllo Crucifix">Batlló Crucifix</a>, receiving <a href="/wiki/Angel" title="Angel">angelic</a> assistance with the face in particular and thus rendering the works instances of <a href="/wiki/Acheiropoieta" title="Acheiropoieta">acheiropoieta</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Both of these sculptures date from at least a millennium after Nicodemus' life, but the ascriptions attest to the contemporary interest in Nicodemus as a character in medieval Europe.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2018)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="In_poetry">In poetry</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: In poetry">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In <a href="/wiki/Henry_Vaughan" title="Henry Vaughan">Henry Vaughan's</a> "The Night," mentioning Nicodemus is significant to elaborate the poem's depiction of the night's relationship with God.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="In_music">In music</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: In music">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In the <a href="/wiki/Lutheranism" title="Lutheranism">Lutheran</a> prescribed readings of the 18th century, the gospel text of the meeting of Jesus and Nicodemus at night was assigned to <a href="/wiki/Trinity_Sunday" title="Trinity Sunday">Trinity Sunday</a>. <a href="/wiki/Johann_Sebastian_Bach" title="Johann Sebastian Bach">Johann Sebastian Bach</a> composed several <a href="/wiki/Church_cantata_(Bach)#Trinity" title="Church cantata (Bach)">cantatas</a> for the occasion, of which <span title="German-language text"><span lang="de"><a href="/wiki/O_heilges_Geist-_und_Wasserbad,_BWV_165" title="O heilges Geist- und Wasserbad, BWV 165"><i>O heilges Geist- und Wasserbad</i>, BWV 165</a></span></span>, composed in 1715, stays close to the gospel based on a libretto by the court poet in <a href="/wiki/Weimar" title="Weimar">Weimar</a>, <a href="/wiki/Salomo_Franck" class="mw-redirect" title="Salomo Franck">Salomo Franck</a>.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Ernst_Pepping" title="Ernst Pepping">Ernst Pepping</a> composed in 1937 an <span title="German-language text"><i lang="de">Evangelienmotette</i></span> (<a href="/wiki/Motet" title="Motet">motet</a> on gospel text) <span title="German-language text"><i lang="de"><a href="/wiki/Jesus_und_Nikodemus" title="Jesus und Nikodemus">Jesus und Nikodemus</a></i></span>.
</p><p>In popular music, Nicodemus' name was figuratively used in <a href="/wiki/Henry_Clay_Work" title="Henry Clay Work">Henry Clay Work</a>'s 1864 <a href="/wiki/American_Civil_War" title="American Civil War">American Civil War</a>-era piece "Wake Nicodemus",<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> which at that time was popular in <a href="/wiki/Minstrel_show" title="Minstrel show">minstrel shows</a>. In 1978 <a href="/wiki/Tim_Curry" title="Tim Curry">Tim Curry</a> covered the song on his debut album <i><a href="/wiki/Read_My_Lips_(Tim_Curry_album)" title="Read My Lips (Tim Curry album)">Read My Lips</a></i>. The song "Help Yourself" by <a href="/wiki/The_Devil_Makes_Three_(band)" title="The Devil Makes Three (band)">The Devil Makes Three</a> contains a very informal retelling of the relationship between Nicodemus and Jesus.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The second verse of the song "Help yourself" performed by <a href="/wiki/The_Devil_Makes_Three_(band)" title="The Devil Makes Three (band)">The Devil Makes Three</a> is dedicated to Nicodemus.
</p><p>More recently, in 1941, "<i><a href="/wiki/Golden_Gate_Quartet" title="Golden Gate Quartet">The Golden Gate Quartet</a></i>", in the African-American Jubilee style, sang the Gospel "God Told Nicodemus".
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="In_film">In film</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: In film">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Nicodemus is portrayed by <a href="/wiki/Diego_Matamoros" title="Diego Matamoros">Diego Matamoros</a> in the 2003 film <i><a href="/wiki/The_Gospel_of_John_(2003_film)" title="The Gospel of John (2003 film)">The Gospel of John</a></i>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="In_television">In television</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: In television">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Nicodemus is portrayed by <a href="/wiki/Forrest_Taylor" title="Forrest Taylor">Forrest Taylor</a> in the 1952 television series <i>The Living Bible</i> and <a href="/wiki/Erick_Avari" title="Erick Avari">Erick Avari</a> in the 2019 web television series <i><a href="/wiki/The_Chosen_(TV_series)" title="The Chosen (TV series)">The Chosen</a></i>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="In_literature">In literature</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: In literature">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><i>Persuaded: The Story of Nicodemus</i> by author David Harder is a historical fictional account on the life of Nicodemus.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Harder used events and timetables for his novel found within the pages of the Passion Translation version of the Bible<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> and brought biblical characters to life in a realistic story with the goal of keeping his book historically and scripturally accurate.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="During_the_Protestant_vs._Catholic_struggle">During the Protestant vs. Catholic struggle</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: During the Protestant vs. Catholic struggle">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Nicodemite" title="Nicodemite">Nicodemite</a></div>
<p>During the struggle between <a href="/wiki/Protestant" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant">Protestants</a> and <a href="/wiki/Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic">Catholics</a> in Europe, from the 16th century to the 18th, a person belonging to a Church different from the locally dominant one often risked severe punishment – in many cases, capital. At that time, there developed the use of "<a href="/wiki/Nicodemite" title="Nicodemite">Nicodemite</a>", usually a <a href="/wiki/Term_of_disparagement" class="mw-redirect" title="Term of disparagement">term of disparagement</a> referring to a person who is suspected of public misrepresentation of their actual religious beliefs by exhibiting false appearance and concealing true beliefs.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
The term was apparently introduced by <a href="/wiki/John_Calvin" title="John Calvin">John Calvin</a>  in his 1544 <i>Excuse à messieurs les Nicodemites</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> To Calvin, who opposed all veneration of saints, the fact of Nicodemus becoming a Catholic saint in no way exonerated his "duplicity". The term was originally applied mainly to <a href="/wiki/Crypto-protestantism" class="mw-redirect" title="Crypto-protestantism">crypto-protestants</a> – hidden Protestants in a Catholic environment – later used more broadly.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2018)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="United_States">United States</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nicodemus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: United States">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The discussion with Jesus is the source of several common expressions of contemporary <a href="/wiki/American_Christianity" class="mw-redirect" title="American Christianity">American Christianity</a>, specifically, the descriptive phrase "<a href="/wiki/Born_again_(Christianity)" class="mw-redirect" title="Born again (Christianity)">born again</a>" used to describe salvation or <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptism</a> by some groups, and <a href="/wiki/John_3_16" class="mw-redirect" title="John 3 16">John 3:16</a>, a commonly quoted verse used to describe God's plan of salvation.
</p><p>Daniel Burke notes that, "To blacks after the <a href="/wiki/American_Civil_War" title="American Civil War">Civil War</a>, he was a model of rebirth as they sought to cast off their old identity as slaves".<sup id="cite_ref-Burke_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burke-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Rosamond Rodman asserts that freed slaves who moved to <a href="/wiki/Nicodemus,_Kansas" title="Nicodemus, Kansas">Nicodemus, Kansas</a>, after the Civil War named their town after him.<sup id="cite_ref-Burke_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burke-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> However, the <a href="/wiki/National_Park_Service" title="National Park Service">National Park Service</a> indicates that it was more likely based on an 1864 song, "Wake Nicodemus" by <a href="/wiki/Henry_Clay_Work" title="Henry Clay Work">Henry Clay Work</a>, used to promote settlement in the area.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 16 August 1967, <a href="/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr." title="Martin Luther King Jr.">Martin Luther King Jr.</a> invoked Nicodemus as a metaphor concerning the need for the United States to be "born again" in order to effectively address social and economic inequality. The speech was called "Where Do We Go From Here?", and delivered at the 11th Annual SCLC Convention in <a href="/wiki/Atlanta" title="Atlanta">Atlanta</a>, Georgia.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
