<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1066479718">.mw-parser-output .infobox-subbox{padding:0;border:none;margin:-3px;width:auto;min-width:100%;font-size:100%;clear:none;float:none;background-color:transparent}.mw-parser-output .infobox-3cols-child{margin:auto}.mw-parser-output .infobox .navbar{font-size:100%}body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-header,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-subheader,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-above,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-title,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-image,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-full-data,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-below{text-align:center}</style><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div style="display:inline;" class="fn">Saint Magnus Felix Ennodius</div></th></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Born</th><td class="infobox-data">473 or 474<br /><a href="/wiki/Arles" title="Arles">Arles</a>, <a href="/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire" title="Western Roman Empire">Western Roman Empire</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Died</th><td class="infobox-data">17 July 521</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Venerated&#160;in</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Feast</a></th><td class="infobox-data">17 July</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p><b>Magnus Felix Ennodius</b> (473 or 474&#160;&#8211;&#32;17 July 521 AD) was <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Pavia" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Pavia">Bishop of Pavia</a> in 514, and a <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> <a href="/wiki/Rhetoric" title="Rhetoric">rhetorician</a> and <a href="/wiki/Poet" title="Poet">poet</a>.
</p><p>He was one of four <a href="/wiki/Gallo-Roman" class="mw-redirect" title="Gallo-Roman">Gallo-Roman</a> aristocrats of the fifth to sixth-century whose letters survive in quantity: the others are <a href="/wiki/Sidonius_Apollinaris" title="Sidonius Apollinaris">Sidonius Apollinaris</a>, <a href="/wiki/Prefect" title="Prefect">prefect</a> of <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> in 468 and <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Clermont" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Clermont">bishop of Clermont</a> (died 485), <a href="/wiki/Ruricius" title="Ruricius">Ruricius</a> <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Limoges" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Limoges">bishop of Limoges</a> (died 507) and <a href="/wiki/Avitus_of_Vienne" title="Avitus of Vienne">Alcimus Ecdicius Avitus</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Vienne" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Vienne">bishop of Vienne</a> (died 518). All of them were linked in the tightly bound aristocratic Gallo-Roman network that provided the bishops of Catholic Gaul.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He is regarded as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>, with a <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> of 17 July.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Magnus_Felix_Ennodius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Ennodius was born at Arelate (<a href="/wiki/Arles" title="Arles">Arles</a>) and belonged to a distinguished but impecunious family. As Mommaerts and Kelley observe, "Ennodius claimed in his letters to them to be related to a large number of individuals. Unfortunately, he seldom specified the nature of the relationship."<sup id="cite_ref-MomKelly_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MomKelly-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Because his sister Euprepia (born 465 or 470) is known to have had a son named <a href="/wiki/Flavius_Licerius_Firminus_Lupicinus" title="Flavius Licerius Firminus Lupicinus">Flavius Licerius Firminus Lupicinus</a>, who was named for his grandfather, Vogel argued that Ennodius' father was named Firminus. <a href="/wiki/Jacques_Sirmond" title="Jacques Sirmond">Jacques Sirmond</a> suggested that Ennodius was the son of one Camillus of Arles, whose father was a proconsular and the brother of <a href="/wiki/Magnus_(consul_460)" title="Magnus (consul 460)">Magnus</a>, the consul of 460; but Mommaerts and Kelley dismiss Sirmond's identification as untenable.<sup id="cite_ref-MomKelly_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MomKelly-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Having lost his parents at an early age, Ennodius was brought up by an aunt at <a href="/wiki/Ticinum" title="Ticinum">Ticinum</a> (Pavia); according to some, at Mediolanum (<a href="/wiki/Milan" title="Milan">Milan</a>). After her death he was received into the family of a pious and wealthy young lady, to whom he was betrothed. It is not certain whether he actually married this lady; she seems to have lost her money and retired to a convent, whereupon Ennodius entered the Church, and was ordained <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a> (about 493) by <a href="/wiki/Epiphanius,_bishop_of_Pavia" class="mw-redirect" title="Epiphanius, bishop of Pavia">Epiphanius, bishop of Pavia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>From Pavia he went to Milan, which Ennodius made his home until his elevation to the <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_see" title="Episcopal see">see</a> of Pavia about 515. During his stay at Milan he visited Rome and other places, where he gained a reputation as a teacher of rhetoric. As bishop of Pavia he played a considerable part in ecclesiastical affairs. On two occasions (in 515 and 517) he was sent to <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> on an embassy to the <a href="/wiki/Anastasius_I_(emperor)" class="mw-redirect" title="Anastasius I (emperor)">emperor Anastasius</a>, to endeavour to bring about a reconciliation over the <a href="/wiki/Acacian_schism" title="Acacian schism">Acacian schism</a> that divided the Eastern and Western churches.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Ennodius' <a href="/wiki/Epitaph" title="Epitaph">epitaph</a> still exists in the <a href="/wiki/Basilica" title="Basilica">basilica</a> of <a href="/wiki/San_Michele_Maggiore,_Pavia" title="San Michele Maggiore, Pavia">San Michele Maggiore, Pavia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Magnus_Felix_Ennodius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Ennodius is one of the best representatives of the two-fold (<a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">pagan</a> and <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a>) tendency of 5th century literature, and of the Gallo-Roman clergy who upheld the cause of civilization and classical literature against the inroads of barbarism. But his anxiety not to fall behind his classical models—the chief of whom was <a href="/wiki/Virgil" title="Virgil">Virgil</a>—his striving after elegance and grammatical correctness, and a desire to avoid the commonplace have produced a turgid and affected style, which, aggravated by rhetorical exaggerations and popular <a href="/wiki/Barbarism_(linguistics)" title="Barbarism (linguistics)">barbarisms</a>, makes his works difficult to understand. It has been remarked that his poetry is less unintelligible than his prose.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The numerous writings of this ecclesiastic may be grouped into four types: letters, miscellanies, discourses, and poems. His letters on a variety of subjects, addressed to high church and state officials, are valuable for the religious and political history of the period. Of the miscellanies, the most important are:
</p>
<ul><li>The <i>Panegyric of Theodoric</i>, written to thank the <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arian</a> king for his tolerance of <a href="/wiki/Catholicism" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholicism">Catholicism</a> and support of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Symmachus" title="Pope Symmachus">Pope Symmachus</a> (probably delivered before the king on the occasion of his entry into <a href="/wiki/Ravenna" title="Ravenna">Ravenna</a> or Milan); like all similar works, it is full of flattery and exaggeration, but if used with caution is a valuable authority</li>
<li>The <i>Life of St Epiphanius, bishop of Pavia</i>, the best written and perhaps the most important of all his writings, an interesting picture of the political activity and influence of the church</li>
<li><i>Eucharisticon de Vita Sua</i>, a sort of confessions, after the manner of <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</a></li>
<li>the description of the enfranchisement of a slave with religious formalities in the presence of a bishop</li>
<li><i>Paraenesis didascalica</i>, an educational guide, in which the claims of grammar as a preparation for the study of rhetoric, the mother of all the sciences, are strongly insisted on.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<p>The discourses (<i>Dictiones</i>) are on sacred, scholastic, controversial and ethical subjects. The discourse on the anniversary of Laurentius, <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Milan" title="Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Milan">bishop of Milan</a>, is the chief authority for the life of that prelate; the scholastic discourses, rhetorical exercises for the schools, contain eulogies of classical learning, distinguished professors and pupils; the controversial deal with imaginary charges, the subjects being chiefly borrowed from the <i>Controversiae</i> of <a href="/wiki/Seneca_the_Elder" title="Seneca the Elder">Seneca the Elder</a>; the ethical harangues are put into the mouth of mythological personages (e.g. the speech of <a href="/wiki/Thetis" title="Thetis">Thetis</a> over the body of <a href="/wiki/Achilles" title="Achilles">Achilles</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Amongst the poems mention may be made of two <i>Itineraria</i>, descriptions of a journey from Milan to Brigantium (<a href="/wiki/Brian%C3%A7on" title="Briançon">Briançon</a>) and of a trip on the <a href="/wiki/Po_River" class="mw-redirect" title="Po River">Po River</a>; an apology for the study of profane literature; an <i><a href="/wiki/Epithalamium" title="Epithalamium">epithalamium</a>,</i> in which Love is introduced as execrating Christianity; a dozen <a href="/wiki/Hymn" title="Hymn">hymns</a>, after the manner of <a href="/wiki/Ambrose" title="Ambrose">Ambrose</a>, probably intended for church use; <a href="/wiki/Epigram" title="Epigram">epigrams</a> on various subjects, some being epigrams proper—inscriptions for tombs, basilicas, baptisteries—others imitations of <a href="/wiki/Martial" title="Martial">Martial</a>, satiric pieces and descriptions of scenery.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Critical_editions">Critical editions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Magnus_Felix_Ennodius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Critical editions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <i><a href="/wiki/Editio_princeps" title="Editio princeps">editio princeps</a></i> of Ennodius was published by <a href="/wiki/Johann_Jakob_Grynaeus" title="Johann Jakob Grynaeus">Johann Jakob Grynaeus</a> in 1569 at Basel. Sirmond edited his works in 1611, organizing the individual works into the four groupings described above; this presentation remained "the classic text" until <a href="/wiki/Wilhelm_Hartel" class="mw-redirect" title="Wilhelm Hartel">Guilelmus Hartel</a> (vol. vi. of <i><a href="/wiki/Corpus_Scriptorum_Ecclesiasticorum_Latinorum" title="Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum">Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum</a></i>, Vienna, 1882). However, it was not until 1885 that <a href="/w/index.php?title=Friedrich_Vogel_(historian)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Friedrich Vogel (historian) (page does not exist)">Friedrich Vogel</a> prepared an edition for the <i><a href="/wiki/Monumenta_Germaniae_Historica" title="Monumenta Germaniae Historica">Monumenta Germaniae Historica</a></i> (<i>Auctores Antiquissimi</i>, vol. vii), that the individual works were once again presented in the miscellaneous order of the manuscripts.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Vogel did so seeing traces of a chronological sequence in that order, which Sr. Genevieve Cook notes led to "a series of studies on the chronology of the works of Ennodius".<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A modern edition of Ennodius' correspondence is under way: Stéphane Gioanni, <i>Ennode de Pavie, Lettres</i>, tome I: Livres I et II, Paris, Les Belles Lettres, 2006, based on his 2004 Ph.D. thesis. See a first <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://web.archive.org/web/20070316001648/http://home.hccnet.nl/j.a.van.waarden/download/Gioanni%20Ennode.pdf">review</a> (Joop van Waarden) and Stéphane Gioanni, <i>Ennode de Pavie, Lettres</i>, tome II, livres III et IV, Paris, Les Belles Lettres, 2010.
</p>
