<p><b>Boris</b> and <b>Gleb</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_East_Slavic_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old East Slavic language">Old East Slavic</a>: <span lang="orv">Борисъ и Глѣбъ</span>, <small>romanized:&#160;</small><span title="Old East Slavic-language romanization"><i lang="orv-Latn">Borisŭ i Glěbŭ</i></span>; <a href="/wiki/Russian_language" title="Russian language">Russian</a>: <span lang="ru">Борис и Глеб</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Russian" title="Romanization of Russian">romanized</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Russian-language romanization"><i lang="ru-Latn">Boris i Gleb</i></span>; <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_language" title="Ukrainian language">Ukrainian</a>: <span lang="uk">Борис і Гліб</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Ukrainian" title="Romanization of Ukrainian">romanized</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Ukrainian-language romanization"><i lang="uk-Latn">Borys i Hlib</i></span>), respective Christian names <b>Roman</b> (<span title="Old East Slavic-language text"><span lang="orv">Романъ</span></span>, <span title="Old East Slavic-language romanization"><i lang="orv-Latn">Romanŭ</i></span>) and <b>David</b> (<span title="Old East Slavic-language text"><span lang="orv">Давꙑдъ</span></span>, <span title="Old East Slavic-language romanization"><i lang="orv-Latn">Davydŭ</i></span>),  were the first <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saints</a> canonized in <a href="/wiki/Kievan_Rus%27" title="Kievan Rus&#39;">Kievan Rus'</a> after the <a href="/wiki/Christianization_of_Kievan_Rus%27" title="Christianization of Kievan Rus&#39;">Christianization of the country</a>. Their feast day is observed on July 24 (August 6).
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Boris_and_Gleb&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to the two 11th-century <i>Lives of Boris and Gleb</i>, ascribed to <a href="/wiki/Nestor_the_Chronicler" title="Nestor the Chronicler">Nestor the Chronicler</a> and <a href="/wiki/Jacob_the_Monk" class="mw-redirect" title="Jacob the Monk">Jacob the Monk</a>, they were younger children of <a href="/wiki/Vladimir_the_Great" title="Vladimir the Great">Vladimir the Great</a>, who favored them over his other children. The <i><a href="/wiki/Primary_Chronicle" title="Primary Chronicle">Primary Chronicle</a></i> claims that their mother was a <a href="/wiki/Bulgaria" title="Bulgaria">Bulgarian</a> woman.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Most modern scholars, however, argue that Boris and Gleb had different mothers and were of different ages. Boris, the elder, who was already married and ruled the town of <a href="/wiki/Rostov" title="Rostov">Rostov</a>, was probably regarded as <a href="/wiki/Heir_apparent" title="Heir apparent">heir apparent</a> to the <a href="/wiki/Kiev" class="mw-redirect" title="Kiev">Kievan</a> throne. Gleb, who was still a minor, ruled the easternmost town of <a href="/wiki/Murom" title="Murom">Murom</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-roca_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-roca-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Both brothers were murdered during the <a href="/wiki/Internecine_war" class="mw-redirect" title="Internecine war">internecine wars</a> of 1015–1019. The <i>Primary Chronicle</i> blames <a href="/wiki/Sviatopolk_I_of_Kiev" title="Sviatopolk I of Kiev">Sviatopolk the Accursed</a> for plotting their assassinations. Boris learns of his father's death upon his return with the Rus' army to Alta. Informed of Sviatopolk's accession to the throne and urged to replace him, Boris replies: "Be it not for me to raise my hand against my elder brother. Now that my father has passed away, let him take the place of my father in my heart."<sup id="cite_ref-roca_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-roca-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Despite Boris' acquiescence, Sviatopolk sends Putsha and the <a href="/wiki/Boyars" class="mw-redirect" title="Boyars">boyars</a> of Vyshegorod to execute his brother. Boris and his manservant are stabbed to death while asleep in a tent. The prince is discovered still breathing in a bodybag being transported to Kiev, but the <a href="/wiki/Varangians" title="Varangians">Varangians</a> end his life with the thrust of a sword.<sup id="cite_ref-roca_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-roca-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Sent for by Sviatopolk, Gleb believes his father is still alive and rushes to his father's deathbed. On the way, their brother Yaroslav learns of Sviatopolk's treachery and urges Gleb not to meet him. In the middle of praying to his deceased brother and God, Gleb is assassinated by his own cook, Torchin, who cuts his throat with a kitchen knife.<sup id="cite_ref-Hackel_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hackel-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Life</i> contains many picturesque details of Boris and Gleb's last hours, such as their sister's warning about the murderous plans of Sviatopolk. The narrative is a masterpiece of <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> that weaves together numerous literary traditions. The factual circumstances of Boris and Gleb's lives and deaths cannot, however, be extrapolated from their hagiography. Perhaps the crucial evidence comes from several unbiased foreign sources, which mention that Boris succeeded his father in Kyiv and was not lurking in Rostov as the Russian <i>Primary Chronicle</i> seems to imply.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>The Norse <i><a href="/wiki/Eymund%27s_saga" class="mw-redirect" title="Eymund&#39;s saga">Eymund's saga</a></i> relates a tale of the <a href="/wiki/Varangian" class="mw-redirect" title="Varangian">Varangian</a> warriors who were hired by <a href="/wiki/Yaroslav_I_the_Wise" class="mw-redirect" title="Yaroslav I the Wise">Yaroslav I the Wise</a> to kill his brother <i>Burizleif</i>. Some historians<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions" title="Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch"><span title="The material near this tag possibly uses too-vague attribution or weasel words. (June 2020)">who?</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> trust the saga more than sources from Rus', claiming that it was Yaroslav and not Sviatopolk who was interested in removing his political rivals and was guilty of his brothers' murder. Others<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions" title="Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch"><span title="The material near this tag possibly uses too-vague attribution or weasel words. (June 2020)">who?</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> consider "<i>Burizleif</i>" a misreading of <a href="/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_I_of_Poland" class="mw-redirect" title="Bolesław I of Poland">Bolesław</a>, the Polish ruler allied to Sviatopolk.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Boris_and_Gleb&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Boris and Gleb received the crown of martyrdom in 1015. The brothers became known as "Strastoterptsy" (Passion-Bearers), since they did not resist evil with violence.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> 
Boris and Gleb's relics were housed in the Church of St. Basil in <a href="/wiki/Vyshhorod" title="Vyshhorod">Vyshhorod</a>, later destroyed.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Boris and Gleb were <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">glorified</a> (canonized) by the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Orthodox church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Rus%27_people" title="Rus&#39; people">Rus'</a> in 1071. The <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> canonized the brothers in 1724, during the papacy of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XIII#Beatifications_and_canonizations" title="Pope Benedict XIII">Benedict XIII</a>. They were interred at the <a href="/wiki/Vyshhorod" title="Vyshhorod">Vyshhorod</a> Cathedral, which was re<a href="/wiki/Consecrated" class="mw-redirect" title="Consecrated">consecrated</a> in their name. Many other Ukrainian and Russian churches were later named after them.
</p><p>In 2011 a monument to Boris and Gleb was erected in Vyshhorod, Ukraine. The authors of the monument are <a href="/wiki/Boris_Krylov" class="mw-redirect" title="Boris Krylov">Boris Krylov</a> and Oles Sydoruk.
</p>
