<p><b>Arnulf of Metz</b> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 582 – 645) was a <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frankish</a> <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Metz" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Metz">bishop of Metz</a> and advisor to the <a href="/wiki/Merovingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingian">Merovingian</a> court of <a href="/wiki/Austrasia" title="Austrasia">Austrasia</a>. He later retired to the <a href="/wiki/Remiremont_Abbey" title="Remiremont Abbey">Abbey of Remiremont</a>. In French he is also known as <b>Arnoul</b> or <b>Arnoulf</b>. In English he is known as <b>Arnold</b>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Genealogy">Genealogy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Arnulf_of_Metz&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Genealogy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <i>Vita Sancti Arnulfi</i> (c. 657), written shortly after the saint's death, states that he was of <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frankish</a> ancestry, from "sufficiently elevated and noble parentage, and very rich in worldly goods".<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Tenth and eleventh century texts like <i>Genealogiae Breves Regum Francorum</i> often claim that <a href="/wiki/Arnoald" title="Arnoald">Arnoald</a> is Arnulf's father, but this is considered a fabrication.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Medieval Frankish historians <a href="/wiki/Patrick_J._Geary" title="Patrick J. Geary">Patrick Geary</a> and <a href="/wiki/Richard_Gerberding" title="Richard Gerberding">Richard Gerberding</a> observe that the lack of contemporary written texts makes it impossible to trace Arnulf's ancestry.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> <sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Arnulf of Metz is traditionally considered the earliest named ancestor of Charlemagne and the Carolingian Dynasty. This belief originates from two sources written after Arnulf's death. <a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Deacon" title="Paul the Deacon">Paul the Deacon</a>'s <i>Liber de episcopis Mettensibus</i> (c. 784) presents Arnulf as Charlemagne's third great-grandfather, while <i>Annales Mettenses Priores</i> (805) depicts him as only a "close relative."<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> <sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Because these two accounts are inconsistent and written a century after Arnulf's death, there is widespread scholarly agreement that the familial connection between Arnulf and Charlemagne is an invention by Charlemagne.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Arnulf_of_Metz&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Arnulf was born to an important Frankish family near <a href="/wiki/Nancy,_France" title="Nancy, France">Nancy</a> in <a href="/wiki/Lorraine" title="Lorraine">Lorraine</a> around 582.<sup id="cite_ref-monks_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-monks-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> The family owned vast domains between the <a href="/wiki/Moselle" title="Moselle">Moselle</a> and <a href="/wiki/Meuse" title="Meuse">Meuse</a> rivers.<sup id="cite_ref-Riche_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Riche-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> As an adolescent, he was called to the Merovingian court of king <a href="/wiki/Theudebert_II" title="Theudebert II">Theudebert II</a> (595–612) of <a href="/wiki/Austrasia" title="Austrasia">Austrasia</a><sup id="cite_ref-Schaefer_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Schaefer-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> where he was educated by <a href="/wiki/Gondulf_of_Provence" title="Gondulf of Provence">Gondulf of Provence</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-monks_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-monks-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Arnulf was later sent to serve as <i><a href="/wiki/Dux" title="Dux">dux</a></i> at the <a href="/wiki/Schelde" class="mw-redirect" title="Schelde">Schelde</a>.
</p><p>Arnulf gave distinguished service at the Austrasian court under Theudebert II. He distinguished himself both as a military commander and in the civil administration; at one time he had under his care six distinct provinces.<sup id="cite_ref-Schaefer_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Schaefer-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Arnulf married in 596 to a noblewoman whom later sources give the name of Dode, or Doda (born c. 584), the paternal aunt of Saint <a href="/wiki/Glodesind" title="Glodesind">Glodesind</a> of France, an abbess of a convent in Metz.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Chlodulf_of_Metz" title="Chlodulf of Metz">Chlodulf of Metz</a> was their oldest son, but more important is his second son <a href="/wiki/Ansegisel" title="Ansegisel">Ansegisel</a>, who married <a href="/wiki/Begga" title="Begga">Begga</a> daughter of Pepin I, Pepin of Landen. Arnulf is thus the male-line grandfather of <a href="/wiki/Pepin_of_Herstal" title="Pepin of Herstal">Pepin of Herstal</a>, great-grandfather of <a href="/wiki/Charles_Martel" title="Charles Martel">Charles Martel</a> and great-great-great-grandfather of <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a>.
</p><p>Around 611, he and his friend Romaricus, likewise an officer of the court, planned to make a pilgrimage to the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_L%C3%A9rins" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of Lérins">Abbey of Lérins</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Schaefer_12-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Schaefer-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Chlothachar, who appreciated Arnulf's administrative skills, offered him the vacant see of <a href="/wiki/Metz" title="Metz">Metz</a>, the capital of the Austrasian kingdom. His wife took the veil as a nun in a convent at Treves, and Arnulf saw it as a sign of God and became a priest and bishop afterwards.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Arnulf continued to serve as the king's steward and courtier.<sup id="cite_ref-Riche_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Riche-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The rule of Austrasia came into the hands of <a href="/wiki/Brunhilda_of_Austrasia" title="Brunhilda of Austrasia">Brunhilda</a>, the grandmother of Theudebert, who ruled also in <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Burgundy" title="Kingdom of Burgundy">Burgundy</a> in the name of her great-grandchildren. In 613 Arnulf joined his politics with Pepin of Landen and led the opposition of Frankish nobles against Queen Brunhilda. The revolt led to her overthrow, torture and eventual execution, and the subsequent reunification of Frankish lands under <a href="/wiki/Chlothar_II" title="Chlothar II">Chlothachar II</a>.
</p><p>Chlothachar later made his son <a href="/wiki/Dagobert_I" title="Dagobert I">Dagobert I</a> king of Austrasia, which he ruled with the help of his adviser Arnulf. Pepin of Landen, became the <a href="/wiki/Mayor_of_the_palace" title="Mayor of the palace">mayor of the palace</a>. In 624 Pepin and Arnulf encouraged Dagobert in the murder of Chrodoald, an important leader of the Frankish <a href="/wiki/Agilolfings" title="Agilolfings">Agilolfings</a> family. In 625 Arnulf took part in a council held by the Frankish bishops at Reims.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During his career he was attracted to religious life, and around 628 he retired to a hermitage at a mountain site in his domains in the <a href="/wiki/Vosges" title="Vosges">Vosges</a> to become a monk. His friend <a href="/wiki/Romaric" title="Romaric">Romaric</a>, whose parents had been killed by Brunhilda, had preceded him to the mountains around 613. Romaric and <a href="/wiki/Amatus" class="mw-redirect" title="Amatus">Amatus</a> had already established Remiremont Abbey there. After the death of Chlothachar in 629, Arnulf settled near Habendum, where he died some time between 643 and 647. He was buried at Remiremont.<sup id="cite_ref-Riche_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Riche-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Arnulf is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>. In <a href="/wiki/Iconography" title="Iconography">iconography</a>, he is typically portrayed with a pastoral staff or a rake in his hand.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (July 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legends">Legends</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Arnulf_of_Metz&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Legends">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There are three legends associated with Arnulf:
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Legend_of_the_Ring">The Legend of the Ring</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Arnulf_of_Metz&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: The Legend of the Ring">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Arnulf was tormented by the violence that surrounded him and feared that he had played a role in the wars and murders that plagued the ruling families. Obsessed by these sins, Arnulf went to a bridge over the Moselle river. There he took off his bishop's ring and threw it into the river, praying to God to give him a sign of absolution by returning the ring to him. Many penitent years later, a fisherman brought to the bishop's kitchen a fish in the stomach of which was found the bishop's ring. Arnulf repaid the sign of God by immediately retiring as bishop and becoming a hermit for the remainder of his life.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Legend_of_the_Fire">The Legend of the Fire</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Arnulf_of_Metz&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: The Legend of the Fire">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>At the moment Arnulf resigned as bishop, a fire broke out in the cellars of the royal palace and threatened to spread throughout the city of Metz. Arnulf, full of courage and feeling unity with the townspeople, stood before the fire and said, "If God wants me to be consumed, I am in His hands." He then made the sign of the cross at which point the fire immediately receded.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Legend_of_the_Beer_Mug">The Legend of the Beer Mug</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Arnulf_of_Metz&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: The Legend of the Beer Mug">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>It was July 642 and very hot when the parishioners of Metz went to Remiremont to recover the remains of their former bishop. They had little to drink and the terrain was inhospitable. At the point when the exhausted procession was about to leave Champigneulles, one of the parishioners, Duc Notto, prayed "By his powerful intercession the Blessed Arnold will bring us what we lack." Immediately the small remnant of beer at the bottom of a pot multiplied in such amounts that the pilgrims' thirst was quenched and they had enough to enjoy the next evening when they arrived in Metz. For this reason he is known as the patron saint of Brewers.
</p>
