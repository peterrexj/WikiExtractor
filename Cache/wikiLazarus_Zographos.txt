<p><b>Lazarus</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Λάζαρος</span>), surnamed <b>Zographos</b> (Ζωγράφος, "the Painter"), is a 9th-century <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine</a> <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> saint.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He is also known as <i>Lazarus the Painter</i> and <i>Lazarus the Iconographer</i>. Born in <a href="/wiki/Armenia" title="Armenia">Armenia</a> on November 17, 810, he lived before and during the second period of <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm" title="Byzantine Iconoclasm">Byzantine Iconoclasm</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Lazarus was the first saint to be <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> specifically as an iconographer. He was later followed by <a href="/wiki/Catherine_of_Bologna" title="Catherine of Bologna">Saint Catherine of Bologna</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life_and_times">Life and times</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lazarus_Zographos&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life and times">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Lazarus became a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> at an early age and is thought to have studied the art of painting at the <a href="/wiki/Monastery_of_Stoudios" title="Monastery of Stoudios">Stoudios Monastery</a> in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:2_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Lazarus was noted to possess the following virtues: love for Christ, asceticism, prayer, and rejection of the vanities of the world.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He was further recognized for his acts of self-control, discipline and alms-giving, then made a priest. In his lifetime he was highly regarded and well known for his <a href="/wiki/Fresco" title="Fresco">frescos</a>. He used faith and ritual as a means to transcribe his inner contemplation onto the images he painted.<sup id="cite_ref-:3_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>  Thus, his ability to paint icons was seen as a gift given by God. During the reign of <a href="/wiki/Theophilos_(emperor)" title="Theophilos (emperor)">Theophilos</a> (<span style="white-space:nowrap;"><abbr title="reigned">r.</abbr>&#160;829–842</span>), an <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm" title="Byzantine Iconoclasm">iconoclast</a> emperor opposed to all holy images, Lazarus stubbornly continued his craft of painting icons and began restoring images defaced by heretics.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Theophilos sought out Lazarus, who was then famous for his painting, and intended to make an example of him. After being asked several times to cease painting, Lazarus was brought before the emperor where he refused to destroy any of the images he painted. The emperor soon found that Lazarus was above flattery and bribery.<sup id="cite_ref-:4_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:4-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He was then threatened with the death penalty, which at the time was not an uncommon outcome for those who favored icons (<a href="/wiki/Iconodules" class="mw-redirect" title="Iconodules">iconodules</a>). However, Lazarus being a man of the cloth, could not be put to death and so he was instead thrown in prison. During his imprisonment he was subjected to such “severe torture that the ladders flesh melted away along with his blood.”<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> He was left to die of his wounds but recovered. He then began to paint holy images on panels from his prison cell. Hearing of this, Theophilos gave orders to have “sheets of red hot iron to be applied to the palms of his hands where, as a result, he lost consciousness and lay half dead.”<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> It is also said his hands were burned with red-hot horseshoes until his flesh melted to the bone.
</p><p>As Lazarus lay on his deathbed, the <a href="/wiki/Theodora_(wife_of_Theophilos)" title="Theodora (wife of Theophilos)">Empress Theodora</a>, an iconodule, convinced Theophilos to release Lazarus from prison. Lazarus found refuge at <i>Tou Phoberou</i>, a secluded church of <a href="/wiki/St._John_the_Forerunner" class="mw-redirect" title="St. John the Forerunner">St. John the Forerunner</a> once located in Phoberos on the Asiatic shore of the <a href="/wiki/Bosphorus" class="mw-redirect" title="Bosphorus">Bosporus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The Church is believed to have once functioned as an imperial monastery that housed as many as one-hundred and seventy monks.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> After the death of Theophilos in 842, Theodora asked Lazarus to forgive her husband's actions, to which he replied “God is not so unjust, O, Empress, as to forget our love and labors on his behalf, and attach greater value to that mans hatred and extraordinary insanity.”<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Lazarus served as a model of perseverance for those who had suffered from iconoclast persecution.<sup id="cite_ref-:5_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:5-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Attributed_artworks">Attributed artworks</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lazarus_Zographos&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Attributed artworks">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After the <a href="/wiki/Triumph_of_Orthodoxy" class="mw-redirect" title="Triumph of Orthodoxy">restoration of the icons</a> in 843, Lazarus was again free to pursue his painting. Despite his previous wounds, Lazarus was said to have painted a large fresco of St. John at the Phoberos Monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The painted icon was known to have the power to perform cures and miracles.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> That same year, he also famously restored a portrait of Christ known as the <a href="/wiki/Christ_Chalkites" class="mw-redirect" title="Christ Chalkites">Christ Chalkites</a> (Christ of the Chalke) over the <a href="/wiki/Chalke_Gate" class="mw-redirect" title="Chalke Gate">Chalke Gate</a>, a ceremonial entrance of the <a href="/wiki/Great_Palace_of_Constantinople" title="Great Palace of Constantinople">Great Palace of Constantinople</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Neither of these two works survive today. Lazarus was also accredited with the mosaic decoration of the apse of <a href="/wiki/Hagia_Sophia" title="Hagia Sophia">Hagia Sophia</a> within the pilgrim accounts of <a href="/wiki/Anthony_of_Novgorod" title="Anthony of Novgorod">Antony, Archbishop of Novgorod</a> during a visit to Constantinople. Antony described the mosaic as depicting the Mother of God holding a Child Christ flanked by two angels, which was noted to have been seen by both Emperor <a href="/wiki/Basil_I" title="Basil I">Basil l</a> and <a href="/wiki/Michael_III" title="Michael III">Michael III</a> (<span style="white-space:nowrap;"><abbr title="reigned">r.</abbr>&#160;842–867</span>) before his death the same year. However, these accounts are dated several centuries later in <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1200</span>.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Ambassador_to_Rome">Ambassador to Rome</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lazarus_Zographos&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Ambassador to Rome">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 856, Lazarus was served as a diplomat for Michael III, Theophilos and Theodora's son, who sent him as an emissary to visit <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_III" title="Pope Benedict III">Pope Benedict III</a> to discuss the possibility of reconciliation between the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> of Rome and the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Orthodox Church</a>, who at this point had very strained relations.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> In 865, during his second mission to the Pope, Lazarus died at Rome on 28 September, although <a href="/wiki/Raymond_Janin" title="Raymond Janin">Raymond Janin</a> disputes the date.<sup id="cite_ref-ODB_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODB-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> He was buried in the Monastery of Evanderes, near Constantinople.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a> of Saint Lazarus Zographos is 17 November in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church_liturgical_calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Orthodox Church liturgical calendar">Orthodox calendar</a>, and 23 February in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Calendar_of_Saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Calendar of Saints">Roman Catholic calendar</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
