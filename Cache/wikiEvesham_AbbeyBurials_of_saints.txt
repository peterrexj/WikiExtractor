					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><p class="mw-empty-elt">

</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Evesham_Abbey_Bell_Tower.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/41/Evesham_Abbey_Bell_Tower.jpg/220px-Evesham_Abbey_Bell_Tower.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="424" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/41/Evesham_Abbey_Bell_Tower.jpg/330px-Evesham_Abbey_Bell_Tower.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/41/Evesham_Abbey_Bell_Tower.jpg/440px-Evesham_Abbey_Bell_Tower.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1204" data-file-height="2323" /></a><figcaption>Evesham Abbey bell tower</figcaption></figure>
<p><b>Evesham Abbey</b> was founded by <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">Saint</a> <a href="/wiki/Egwin" class="mw-redirect" title="Egwin">Egwin</a> at <a href="/wiki/Evesham" title="Evesham">Evesham</a> in Worcestershire, England between 700 and 710 following an alleged vision of the <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Blessed Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a> by a swineherd by the name of <a href="/wiki/Eof" title="Eof">Eof</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to the monastic history, Evesham came through the <a href="/wiki/Norman_Conquest" title="Norman Conquest">Norman Conquest</a> unusually well, because of a quick approach by Abbot <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwig" title="Æthelwig">Æthelwig</a> to <a href="/wiki/William_the_Conqueror" title="William the Conqueror">William the Conqueror</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Abbey" title="Abbey">abbey</a> is of <a href="/wiki/St._Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Benedict">Benedictine</a> origin, and became in its heyday one of the wealthiest in the country. <a href="/wiki/Simon_de_Montfort,_6th_Earl_of_Leicester" title="Simon de Montfort, 6th Earl of Leicester">Simon de Montfort</a> (1208–1265) is buried near the <a href="/wiki/High_altar" class="mw-redirect" title="High altar">high altar</a> of the ruined abbey, the spot marked by an altar-like memorial monument dedicated by the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a> in 1965.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During the 16th-century <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries" title="Dissolution of the monasteries">dissolution of the monasteries</a>, almost all of the abbey was demolished. Today, only one section of walling survives from the abbey itself, although fragments of the 13th-century <a href="/wiki/Chapter_house" title="Chapter house">chapter house</a>, together with the <a href="/wiki/Almonry" title="Almonry">almonry</a>, the 16th-century <a href="/wiki/Bell_tower" title="Bell tower">bell tower</a> and a gateway remain. The abbey's site is a scheduled monument and has several listed structures within it and adjacent to it, of which four are designated at the highest <a href="/wiki/Grade_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Grade I">Grade I</a> level.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Foundation">Foundation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Evesham_Abbey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Foundation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The year of the foundation of the abbey (that is, when a monastic community was first established) is problematic. <a href="/wiki/William_Tindal" title="William Tindal">William Tindal</a> (1794) comments that "I have a MS. but without name or reference, which says that he [i.e. <a href="/wiki/Egwin_of_Evesham" title="Egwin of Evesham">Ecgwine</a>] began his Abbey in the year 682. This is before he was made bishop, and seems improbable. <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Tanner_(bishop)" title="Thomas Tanner (bishop)">Tanner</a> [Not. Mon. p.168] says in 701. The date of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Constantine" title="Pope Constantine">Pope Constantine</a>’s charter may decide the point as to the consecration of his Abbey, but there is reason to suppose that Egwin began to build as early as the year 702".<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> George May gives 701 as the year that <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_of_Mercia" title="Æthelred of Mercia">Ethelred</a> conferred on Ecgwine the whole peninsula <sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> with the erection of the monastery commencing in the same year.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On the other hand, the year of the consecration derives from the grant of the first privilege to the abbey from Pope Constantine "written in the seven hundred and ninth year of our Lord’s incarnation."<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Egwin_of_Evesham" title="Egwin of Evesham">Ecgwine</a> allegedly returned from Rome bearing this charter, which was apparently read out by <a href="/wiki/Berhtwald" title="Berhtwald">Archbishop Berhtwald</a> at a council of "the whole of England" held at <a href="/wiki/Alcester" title="Alcester">Alcester</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> although that meeting was probably fictitious.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Thomas_of_Marlborough" title="Thomas of Marlborough">Thomas of Marlborough</a> records that, in accordance with the apostolic command, a community of monks was then established<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> (meaning the foundation has also been dated to 709):
</p><p>"When the blessed Ecgwine saw that longed-for day when the place which he had built would be consecrated, and a monastic order established to serve God in that place, he then abandoned all concerns for worldly matters, and devoted himself to a contemplative way of life. Following the example of the Lord by humbling himself, he resigned his bishop's see, and became abbot of the monastery."<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The alleged charter of Ecgwine (purportedly written in 714) records that on the feast of All Saints "Bishop Wilfrid and I consecrated the church which I had built to God, the Blessed Mary, and to all Christ’s elect".<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>  The feast of All Saints became established in the West after 609 or 610 under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_IV" title="Pope Boniface IV">Pope Boniface IV</a>; its observance on 1 November dates from the time of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_III" title="Pope Gregory III">Pope Gregory III</a> (died 741).<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> A <a href="/wiki/Wilfrith_I_(bishop_of_Worcester)" title="Wilfrith I (bishop of Worcester)">Bishop Wilfrid</a> was Egwin’s successor to the see of <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Worcester" title="Bishop of Worcester">Worcester</a> (though he is sometimes confused with <a href="/wiki/Wilfrid" title="Wilfrid">Wilfrid, Bishop of York</a>, who died c. 709).
</p><p>Although the exact year of the foundation remains unclear, it has sometimes been assumed that the date of the abbey's consecration was the <a href="/wiki/All_Saints%27_Day" title="All Saints&#39; Day">feast of All Saints</a> in 709.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> That the consecration occurred on this feast day would provide a neat connection with <a href="/wiki/All_Saints_Church,_Evesham" title="All Saints Church, Evesham">All Saints' Church</a> in Evesham. That Abbot Clement Lichfield lies buried beneath the Chantry Chapel, now known as the Lichfield Chapel in consequence, provides a link to the closing days of the life of the abbey.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Surviving_structures">Surviving structures</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Evesham_Abbey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Surviving structures">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:19th_Century_oil_painting_of_the_abbey%E2%80%99s_bell_tower_and_churches_at_Evesham.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/19th_Century_oil_painting_of_the_abbey%E2%80%99s_bell_tower_and_churches_at_Evesham.jpg/230px-19th_Century_oil_painting_of_the_abbey%E2%80%99s_bell_tower_and_churches_at_Evesham.jpg" decoding="async" width="230" height="269" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/19th_Century_oil_painting_of_the_abbey%E2%80%99s_bell_tower_and_churches_at_Evesham.jpg/345px-19th_Century_oil_painting_of_the_abbey%E2%80%99s_bell_tower_and_churches_at_Evesham.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/19th_Century_oil_painting_of_the_abbey%E2%80%99s_bell_tower_and_churches_at_Evesham.jpg/460px-19th_Century_oil_painting_of_the_abbey%E2%80%99s_bell_tower_and_churches_at_Evesham.jpg 2x" data-file-width="598" data-file-height="700" /></a><figcaption>A view over the river to the abbey's tower, a 19th century oil painting</figcaption></figure>
<p>During the <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries" title="Dissolution of the monasteries">dissolution of the monasteries</a>, on its surrender to the king in 1540 the abbey was plundered and razed to the ground, although the <a href="/wiki/Evesham_Bell_Tower" title="Evesham Bell Tower">bell tower</a> built earlier that century was saved.<sup id="cite_ref-vch_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vch-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> The tower stands 110 feet (34&#160;m) tall and is a <a href="/wiki/Grade_I_listed_building" class="mw-redirect" title="Grade I listed building">Grade I listed building</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Other remains include:
</p>
<ul><li>The abbey's <a href="/wiki/Almonry" title="Almonry">almonry</a>, from the 15th century and earlier, Grade I listed,<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> has been restored and houses the <a href="/wiki/Almonry_Museum_and_Heritage_Centre" title="Almonry Museum and Heritage Centre">Almonry Museum and Heritage Centre</a></li>
<li>The L-shaped house which is now numbers 53 and 54, Merstow Green, incorporates the abbey's Great Gate from the early 14th century and a vaulted passage from the same period; Grade I listed<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Abbot Reginald's gateway and wall, c.1120 with 15th-century room above; Grade I listed<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Part of the south wall of the abbey precinct, restored, including an arched doorway<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>A 13th-century archway which led to the chapter house<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Fragmentary remains of the west wall of the north transept of the abbey<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>A wall, blocked arches and a window which are the remains of the abbey stables, 14th and 15th centuries; Grade II* listed<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<p>The full area of the abbey precincts, most of which is today a public park, was designated as a <a href="/wiki/Scheduled_monuments_in_Greater_Manchester" title="Scheduled monuments in Greater Manchester">scheduled monument</a> in 1949.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> As of 2022<sup class="plainlinks noexcerpt noprint asof-tag update" style="display:none;"><a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Evesham_Abbey&amp;action=edit">&#91;update&#93;</a></sup>, the site in general and in particular the almonry and Abbot Reginald's wall, are on Historic England's <a href="/wiki/Heritage_at_Risk_Register" title="Heritage at Risk Register">Heritage at Risk register</a> owing to their vulnerable condition.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Other buildings linked to the history of the abbey include <a href="/wiki/Middle_Littleton_Tythe_Barn" title="Middle Littleton Tythe Barn">Middle Littleton tythe barn</a>.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Prints_and_paintings">Prints and paintings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Evesham_Abbey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Prints and paintings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:J._M._W._TURNER_Evesham_Abbey.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/J._M._W._TURNER_Evesham_Abbey.jpg/220px-J._M._W._TURNER_Evesham_Abbey.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="170" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/J._M._W._TURNER_Evesham_Abbey.jpg/330px-J._M._W._TURNER_Evesham_Abbey.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/J._M._W._TURNER_Evesham_Abbey.jpg/440px-J._M._W._TURNER_Evesham_Abbey.jpg 2x" data-file-width="900" data-file-height="695" /></a><figcaption>J. M. W. Turner’s watercolour of the old abbey gateway, 1793</figcaption></figure>
<p>Prints of picturesque ruins accompanied the growth of domestic tourism in Britain during the second half of the 18th century, among which can be found striking images of the remains of the abbey walls and bell tower at Evesham. These, accompanied by scenic and historical descriptions, were collected, for example, in such works as <a href="/wiki/Francis_Grose" title="Francis Grose">Francis Grose</a>'s <i>Antiquities of England and Wales</i> (1786)<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> and, more particularly, <a href="/wiki/Samuel_Ireland" title="Samuel Ireland">Samuel Ireland</a>'s <i>Picturesque Views on the Upper or Warwickshire Avon</i> (1795).<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> Among the visiting artists who came to paint them was <a href="/wiki/J._M._W._Turner" title="J. M. W. Turner">J. M. W. Turner</a>, who in 1793 made pen and wash studies of the abbey gateway<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> and of the Church of St Laurence seen through the bell tower's arch.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> An anonymous artist of the period also left a panorama of the tower and churches behind it as seen from the river.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the following century, the antiquary <a href="/wiki/Edward_Rudge" title="Edward Rudge">Edward Rudge</a> began excavations of the abbey remains on parts of his property, between 1811 and 1834. The results were given to the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Antiquaries_of_London" title="Society of Antiquaries of London">Society of Antiquaries of London</a>; illustrations of the discoveries were published in their <i>Vetusta Monumenta</i> with a memoir by his son, <a href="/wiki/Edward_John_Rudge" title="Edward John Rudge">Edward John Rudge</a> and illustrations by his wife, <a href="/wiki/Anne_Rudge" title="Anne Rudge">Anne Rudge</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB00_47-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB00-47">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Conservation">Conservation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Evesham_Abbey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Conservation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Evesham Abbey Trust, a charity and <a href="/wiki/Charitable_incorporated_organisation" title="Charitable incorporated organisation">charitable incorporated organisation</a> registered in 2016, aims to conserve, preserve and improve the abbey site and environs.<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup> Since May 2017, the trust owns the freehold of much of the site following its gifting by the Rudge family.<sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> The trust in 2019 obtained over £1m of funding from the <a href="/wiki/National_Lottery_Heritage_Fund" title="National Lottery Heritage Fund">National Lottery Heritage Fund</a>, <a href="/wiki/Historic_England" title="Historic England">Historic England</a> and other local and regional funders to begin the conservation and restoration of the abbey walls and the creation of a set of interpretive gardens.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Commemoration">Commemoration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Evesham_Abbey&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Commemoration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>One of the <a href="/wiki/Great_Western_Railway" title="Great Western Railway">Great Western Railway</a> <a href="/wiki/Star_class_locomotive" class="mw-redirect" title="Star class locomotive">Star class locomotives</a> was named Evesham Abbey and numbered 4065.  It was subsequently rebuilt as a <a href="/wiki/Castle_class_locomotive" class="mw-redirect" title="Castle class locomotive">Castle class locomotive</a>, being renumbered as 5085 while retaining the name Evesham Abbey.
</p>
