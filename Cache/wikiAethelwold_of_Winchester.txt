in</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a><sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><br /><a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><br /><a <p><b>Æthelwold of Winchester</b> (also <b>Aethelwold</b><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and <b>Ethelwold</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> 904/9 – 984)  was <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Winchester" title="Bishop of Winchester">Bishop of Winchester</a> from 963 to 984 and one of the leaders of the tenth-century <a href="/wiki/English_Benedictine_Reform" title="English Benedictine Reform">monastic reform movement</a> in <a href="/wiki/History_of_Anglo-Saxon_England" title="History of Anglo-Saxon England">Anglo-Saxon England</a>.
</p><p>Monastic life had declined to a low ebb in England in the ninth century, partly because of the ravages caused by <a href="/wiki/Viking" class="mw-redirect" title="Viking">Viking</a> attacks, and partly because of a preference for <a href="/wiki/Secular_clergy" title="Secular clergy">secular clergy</a>, who were cheaper and were thought to serve the spiritual needs of the laity better. Kings from <a href="/wiki/Alfred_the_Great" title="Alfred the Great">Alfred the Great</a> onwards took an interest in the <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> rule, but it was only in the middle of the tenth century that kings became ready to commit substantial funds to its support. Æthelwold became the leading propagandist for the <a href="/wiki/English_Benedictine_Reform" title="English Benedictine Reform">monastic reform movement</a>, although he made enemies by his ruthless methods, and he was more extreme in his opposition to secular clergy than his fellow reformers, <a href="/wiki/Saint_Dunstan" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Dunstan">Saint Dunstan</a> and <a href="/wiki/Oswald_of_Worcester" title="Oswald of Worcester">Oswald of Worcester</a>. He is nevertheless recognised as a key figure in the reform movement, who also made a major contribution to the revival of learning and the arts. He was an important political figure, backing <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_the_Unready" title="Æthelred the Unready">Æthelred the Unready</a> against <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Martyr" title="Edward the Martyr">Edward the Martyr</a>, and playing a major advisory role during Æthelred's minority.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Barrow_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Barrow-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Æthelwold was born to noble parents in <a href="/wiki/Winchester" title="Winchester">Winchester</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> From the late 920s he served in a secular capacity at the court of <a href="/wiki/Athelstan_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Athelstan of England">King Athelstan</a>, and according to Æthelwold's biographer, <a href="/wiki/Wulfstan_the_Cantor" title="Wulfstan the Cantor">Wulfstan</a>, "he spent a long time in the royal burh there as the king's inseparable companion, learning much from the king's witan that was useful and profitable to him".<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The king arranged for him to be ordained a priest by <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfheah_the_Bald" title="Ælfheah the Bald">Ælfheah the Bald</a>, Bishop of Winchester, on the same day as Saint <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">Dunstan</a>. After a period in the late 930s studying under Ælfheah at Winchester, Æthelwold moved to <a href="/wiki/Glastonbury_Abbey" title="Glastonbury Abbey">Glastonbury Abbey</a>, where Dunstan had been made abbot. Here Æthelwold studied grammar, metrics and patristics, subsequently being made <a href="/wiki/Dean_(religion)" class="mw-redirect" title="Dean (religion)">dean</a>. During the reign of <a href="/wiki/Eadred" title="Eadred">King Eadred</a> (946–955), Æthelwold wished to travel to Europe to learn more about the monastic life, but Eadred refused permission, and instead appointed him abbot of the former monastic site of <a href="/wiki/Abingdon_Abbey" title="Abingdon Abbey">Abingdon Abbey</a>, which was then served by secular priests.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The years he spent in Abingdon were extremely productive, and he undertook the building of a church, the rebuilding of the cloister and the establishment at Abingdon of the <a href="/wiki/Benedictine_Rule" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine Rule">Benedictine Rule</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (July 2019)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>When Eadred died, he was succeeded by his nephew, <a href="/wiki/Eadwig" title="Eadwig">Eadwig</a>, who drove Eadred's chief advisor, Dunstan, into exile. However, Æthelwold attended Eadwig's court in at least some of the years of his reign, 955–59. The future <a href="/wiki/Edgar_the_Peaceful" class="mw-redirect" title="Edgar the Peaceful">King Edgar</a> had been taught from boyhood by Æthelwold, who evidently inspired his pupil to take an interest in the rule of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Benedict">Saint Benedict</a>. When Eadwig died, Æthelwold naturally backed Edgar's succession.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> He seems to have been in the personal service of King Edgar in 960–963, as he wrote many of the charters of this period.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop_of_Winchester">Bishop of Winchester</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Bishop of Winchester">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Entry_into_Jerusalem_-_Benedictional_of_St._Aethelwold_(971-984),_f.45v_-_BL_Add_MS_49598.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/The_Entry_into_Jerusalem_-_Benedictional_of_St._Aethelwold_%28971-984%29%2C_f.45v_-_BL_Add_MS_49598.jpg/220px-The_Entry_into_Jerusalem_-_Benedictional_of_St._Aethelwold_%28971-984%29%2C_f.45v_-_BL_Add_MS_49598.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="305" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/The_Entry_into_Jerusalem_-_Benedictional_of_St._Aethelwold_%28971-984%29%2C_f.45v_-_BL_Add_MS_49598.jpg/330px-The_Entry_into_Jerusalem_-_Benedictional_of_St._Aethelwold_%28971-984%29%2C_f.45v_-_BL_Add_MS_49598.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/The_Entry_into_Jerusalem_-_Benedictional_of_St._Aethelwold_%28971-984%29%2C_f.45v_-_BL_Add_MS_49598.jpg/440px-The_Entry_into_Jerusalem_-_Benedictional_of_St._Aethelwold_%28971-984%29%2C_f.45v_-_BL_Add_MS_49598.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1289" data-file-height="1785" /></a><figcaption><i>The Entry into Jerusalem</i> from the <a href="/wiki/Benedictional_of_Saint_%C3%86thelwold" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictional of Saint Æthelwold">Benedictional of Saint Æthelwold</a> (<a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>)</figcaption></figure>
<p>On 29 November 963, Æthelwold was consecrated Bishop of Winchester, and the following year, with the connivance of King Edgar and the support of an armed force led by a royal official, he had the clerics of the Winchester Old and New Minsters expelled and replaced by monks from Abingdon. The king had sought the permission of the pope for the expulsion the previous autumn. Between 964 and 971, Æthelwold refounded monasteries at Chertsey, Milton Abbas, Peterborough, Ely and Thorney, and the Nunnaminster nunnery in Winchester. He was also zealous in recovering land which he believed had once belonged to religious communities and subsequently been alienated, and if necessary charters were forged to prove claims to title.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Æthelwold was one of the principal advocates for the <a href="/wiki/English_Benedictine_Reform" title="English Benedictine Reform">Benedictine reform movement</a> during Edgar's reign, the author of all the major works of propaganda produced in England. He had the strong support of Edgar and his wife, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfthryth,_wife_of_Edgar" class="mw-redirect" title="Ælfthryth, wife of Edgar">Ælfthryth</a>, and his works emphasise the role of Edgar, who he saw as Christ's representative, in restoring the monasteries. He envisaged a major role for Edgar in supervising monasteries, and for Queen Ælthryth supervising Benedictine nunneries.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> However, he was more extreme in his espousal of monasticism than Dunstan and Oswald, the other great leaders of monasticism in the reign of King Edgar. They followed continental practice in maintaining both monks and secular priests in their households, and did not follow Æthelwold in his dramatic expulsions of secular clerks and replacement by monks.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_6-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Æthelwold links the terms 'filth' and 'clergy' several times in his writings, regarding them (like other Benedictines) as impure and unfit to serve altars or engage in any form of divine service, because many of them were married and they did not follow a monastic rule.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>To Æthelwold's admirers, the epithets "father of monks" and "benevolent bishop" summarize his character as reformer and friend of Christ's poor;<sup id="cite_ref-CathSaints_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CathSaints-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> though he suffered much from ill-health, his life as scholar, teacher, prelate and Royal counsellor was ever austere, and he was said to be "terrible as a lion" to the rebellious, yet "gentler than a dove" to the meek. He is said to have written a treatise on the circle and to have compiled the "<a href="/wiki/Regularis_Concordia_(Winchester)" title="Regularis Concordia (Winchester)">Regularis Concordia</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-Walsh184_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Walsh184-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Benedictines were greatly superior to the secular clergy in their learning and their schools. Æthelwold personally taught the older pupils at Winchester, and their works show that they regarded him with great respect and affection. His surviving works in both Latin and Old English show that he was a great scholar, and his vernacular writings are believed to have played an important role in the development of Standard Old English.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_6-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Some of the wealth he accumulated was used to rebuild churches, and he was also a major patron of ecclesiastical art, although unfortunately none of his works survive, and only written accounts remain. The artistic workshops he established continued to be influential after his death, both at home and abroad.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_6-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A century later Æthelwold had acquired a great reputation as a goldsmith, and was credited with the production of a range of metal objects at Abingdon, including many figures and objects in precious metal, bells and even a pipe organ.  While his later, disreputable successor at Abingdon <a href="/wiki/Spearhafoc" title="Spearhafoc">Spearhafoc</a> clearly was, like Dunstan, a significant artist, that <a href="/wiki/Wulfstan_the_Cantor" title="Wulfstan the Cantor">Wulfstan</a>'s contemporary <i>Life of Æthelwold</i> mentions him undertaking other forms of manual work, in the gardens and in building, but nothing about metalwork, suggests this legend was a later elaboration, though one that shows the high status of goldsmithing at the time.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Æthelwold was certainly bishop during the period when the Winchester school of <a href="/wiki/Manuscript_illumination" class="mw-redirect" title="Manuscript illumination">manuscript illumination</a> reached its peak, and the most important surviving manuscript of the school, the <a href="/wiki/Benedictional_of_Saint_%C3%86thelwold" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictional of Saint Æthelwold">Benedictional of Saint Æthelwold</a> (<a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>), was commissioned by him.  He also rebuilt the <a href="/wiki/Old_Minster,_Winchester" title="Old Minster, Winchester">Old Minster at Winchester</a>, completed in 980.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (July 2019)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Political_role">Political role</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Political role">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Æthelwold also played an important political role. When the succession became an issue late in Edgar's reign, Æthelwold supported the claim of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_the_Unready" title="Æthelred the Unready">Æthelred</a>, the son of his major patron, Ælfthryth, whereas Dunstan and Oswald appear to have supported Edgar's son by an earlier wife, <a href="/wiki/Edward_the_Martyr" title="Edward the Martyr">Edward the Martyr</a>, who succeeded to the throne. After Edward was murdered in 978, Æthelwold seems to have played a major advisory role in Æthelred's minority. It is significant that it was only after Æthelwold's death in 984 that Æthelred started acting against the interests of some of the reformed monastic houses. One victim was <a href="/wiki/Abingdon_Abbey" title="Abingdon Abbey">Abingdon Abbey</a>, and in a charter restoring its privileges in 993 the king acknowledged that Æthelwold's passing had deprived the country "of one whose industry and pastoral care ministered not only to my interest but also to that of all the inhabitants of the country."<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_reputation">Death and reputation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Death and reputation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Æthelwold died on 1 August 984<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook223_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook223-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> at <a href="/wiki/Municipal_Borough_of_Beddington_and_Wallington" title="Municipal Borough of Beddington and Wallington">Beddington</a> in <a href="/wiki/Surrey" title="Surrey">Surrey</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Walsh184_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Walsh184-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> He was buried in the crypt of the Old Minster at Winchester, but twelve years later Ælfhelm, a citizen of Wallingford, claimed to have been cured of blindness by visiting Æthelwold's tomb. This was taken as the necessary sign for his formal recognition as a saint, and his body was translated from the crypt to the choir. By the 12th century, <a href="/wiki/Abingdon_Abbey" title="Abingdon Abbey">Abingdon Abbey</a> had acquired an arm and a leg.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>One of Æthelwold's pupils, Wulfstan of Winchester, wrote a biography which seems to have played a major role in promoting his cult, and in about 1004 <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfric_of_Eynsham" title="Ælfric of Eynsham">Ælfric</a>, another disciple and abbot of Eynsham, abridged Wulfstan's work in Latin and Old English.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> However, the Æthelwold of Wulfstan's life inspired respect rather than devotion, and his cult never seems to have achieved great popularity. Wulfstan's saint is a formidable authoritarian, who, for instance, commands a monk to show his devotion by plunging his hand into a pot of boiling stew. He has a reputation for ruthless insensitivity which is not shared by the other tenth-century monastic reformers. His importance to the reform movement has always been appreciated, but the range of his contributions to scholarship has only been recognised in recent years.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_6-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Æthelwold's liturgical feast is kept on 1 August.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
