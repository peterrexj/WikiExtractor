<p><b>Josaphata Hordashevska</b>, born <b>Michaelina Hordashevska</b> (20 November 1869 &#8211; 7 April 1919) an ethnic Ukrainian Greek-Catholic in the Austro-Hungarian Empire <a href="/wiki/Nun#Religious_Sister" title="Nun">Religious Sister</a>, was the first member and co-foundress of the <a href="/wiki/Sisters_Servants_of_Mary_Immaculate" title="Sisters Servants of Mary Immaculate">Sisters Servants of Mary Immaculate</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josaphata_Hordashevska&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Michaelina Hordashevska was born 20 November 1869 in <a href="/wiki/Lviv" title="Lviv">Lviv</a>, then part of the <a href="/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Empire" class="mw-redirect" title="Austro-Hungarian Empire">Austro-Hungarian Empire</a> and now <a href="/wiki/Ukraine" title="Ukraine">Ukraine</a>, into a family who were members of the <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church">Ukrainian Catholic Church</a>. At the age of 18, she considered consecrating her life to God in a <a href="/wiki/Contemplative" class="mw-redirect" title="Contemplative">contemplative</a> <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_St_Basil_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of St Basil the Great">Basilian nuns</a>, then the only Eastern-rite women's <a href="/wiki/Religious_congregation" title="Religious congregation">religious congregation</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-vat_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vat-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> She attended a <a href="/wiki/Spiritual_retreat" class="mw-redirect" title="Spiritual retreat">spiritual retreat</a> which was preached by a <a href="/wiki/Order_of_St._Basil_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of St. Basil the Great">Basilian monk</a>, Father <a href="/wiki/Jeremiah_Lomnytskyj" title="Jeremiah Lomnytskyj">Jeremiah Lomnytskyj</a>, whose spiritual guidance she sought. With his permission, Hordashevska took a private <a href="/wiki/Vow" title="Vow">vow</a> of <a href="/wiki/Chastity" title="Chastity">chastity</a> for one year. She was to renew this vow twice.
</p><p>At that time, Lomnytsky, seeing that there was a need of active Religious Sisters to meet the social needs of the poor and needy faithful of the church, had decided to establish a women's congregation which would follow an active life of service. He did so in conjunction with Father <a href="/wiki/Cyril_Sielecki" class="mw-redirect" title="Cyril Sielecki">Cyril Sielecki</a>, <a href="/wiki/Pastor" title="Pastor">pastor</a> of the village of Zhuzhelyany. Lomnytsky felt that Hordashevska would be an appropriate candidate to found such a congregation. Thus she was asked to be the foundress of such a group, rather than follow the monastic life she had been considering. When she agreed, she was sent in June 1892 to the <a href="/wiki/Poland" title="Poland">Polish</a> <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Felician_Sisters" title="Felician Sisters">Felician Sisters</a> to experience the life of community which followed an active <a href="/wiki/Consecrated_life" title="Consecrated life">consecrated life</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-vat_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vat-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hordashevska returned to Lviv two months later and, on 24 August 1892, took the <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">religious habit</a> of the new Congregation and received the name <b>Josaphata</b>, in honor of the Ukrainian Catholic <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a>, <a href="/wiki/Josaphat_Kuntsevych" title="Josaphat Kuntsevych">Josaphat Kuntsevych</a>. She then went to Zhuzhelyany, and became the first <a href="/wiki/Superior_(hierarchy)" title="Superior (hierarchy)">Superior</a> of the seven young women who had been recruited for the new institute, training them in the spirit and charism of the Sisters Servants: "Serve your people where the need is greatest".<sup id="cite_ref-vat_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vat-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>For the rest of her life, Hordashevska led the new Congregation, through its growth and development. She oversaw the development of the various new ministries the Sisters entered. For this, she had to steer a new path for the Sisters in the Eastern Church, sometimes being caught between the conflicting visions of Fathers  Lomnytsky and Sielecki.<sup id="cite_ref-icon_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-icon-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>By 1902 the Congregation numbered 128 Sisters, in 26 convents across the country. They were able to hold their first <a href="/wiki/General_Chapter" class="mw-redirect" title="General Chapter">General Chapter</a> in August of that year, at which Hordashevska was elected the first <a href="/wiki/Superior_General" class="mw-redirect" title="Superior General">Superior General</a> of the Congregation and Lomnytsky resigned from that office. Soon, however, internal divisions led Hordashevska to tender her resignation to the <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Catholic_Archeparchy_of_Lviv" title="Ukrainian Catholic Archeparchy of Lviv">Metropolitan Archbishop of Lviv</a>, <a href="/wiki/Andrey_Sheptytsky" title="Andrey Sheptytsky">Andrey Sheptytsky</a>. Under the new Superior General appointed by the Metropolitan Archbishop, Hordashevska and her natural sister, Sister Arsenia Hordashevska, were denied permission to take permanent vows.<sup id="cite_ref-icon_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-icon-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Due to her <a href="/wiki/Canon_law" title="Canon law">canonical</a> status of still being in temporary vows, Hordashevska was ineligible to participate in the next General Chapter of the Congregation. Nonetheless, she was elected <a href="/wiki/Vicar_General" class="mw-redirect" title="Vicar General">Vicaress General</a> of the Congregation <i>in absentia</i>, with the delegates of the Chapter petitioning the Metropolitan that she be allowed to make her permanent vows. This request was granted, and Hordashevska did so the following day, 11 May 1909, and assumed the office to which she had been voted.<sup id="cite_ref-icon_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-icon-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Three years later, Hordashevska was diagnosed with <a href="/wiki/Tuberculosis" title="Tuberculosis">tuberculosis</a> of the bone. In 1919, at the age of 49 and on the day she had predicted, she died amidst terrible suffering. Her mortal remains were exhumed in 1982 and taken to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, where they are kept in a <a href="/wiki/Reliquary" title="Reliquary">reliquary</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Motherhouse" title="Motherhouse">General Motherhouse</a> of the Sisters Servants in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>. The process of her beatification started in Rome in 1983 and on 27 June 2001 she was beatified by Pope <a href="/wiki/John_Paul_II" class="mw-redirect" title="John Paul II">John Paul II</a> in <a href="/wiki/Lviv" title="Lviv">Lviv</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Numerous miracles are ascribed due to her intercession after her death.
</p><p>As of 2001, her religious order, the Sisters Servants of Mary Immaculate is now the largest female religious community in the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Influence">Influence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josaphata_Hordashevska&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Influence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to the testimony of Sister Philomena Yuskiv, "She[Josaphata Hordashevska] showed her love for her people through her heart-felt desire to lift them up morally and spiritually; she taught children, youth and women, served the sick, visited the poor and needy, taught liturgical chant and looked after the Church's beauty."<sup id="cite_ref-:0_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Relics">Relics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josaphata_Hordashevska&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Relics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In November 1982, Sister Josaphata's remains were transferred to a small chapel in the General Chapter of the Congregation of the Sisters Servants of Mary Immaculate in Rome. Small parts of the relics remain in various places around Ukraine. This includes a monastery in the city of Lviv, located on Pasichna Street.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> <sup id="cite_ref-:1_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Beatification">Beatification</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Josaphata_Hordashevska&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Beatification">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On 27 June 2001, she was proclaimed Blessed by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> in Lviv. The beatification ceremony took place on 27 June 2001 in Lviv during the Holy Liturgy in the Byzantine rite with the participation of <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
