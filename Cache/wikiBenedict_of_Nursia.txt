<p><b>Benedict of Nursia</b> <span class="noexcerpt nowraplinks" style="font-size:85%; font-weight:normal;"><a href="/wiki/Order_of_Saint_Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of Saint Benedict">OSB</a></span> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Benedictus Nursiae</i>; <a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Benedetto da Norcia</i>; 2 March AD 480 – 21 March AD 548) was an <a href="/wiki/Christianity_in_Italy" title="Christianity in Italy">Italian Christian</a> monk, writer, and theologian who is venerated in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Oriental_Orthodoxy" class="mw-redirect" title="Oriental Orthodoxy">Oriental Orthodox Churches</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Old_Catholic_Church" title="Old Catholic Church">Old Catholic Churches</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Barry1995_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Barry1995-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He is a <a href="/wiki/Patron_saints_of_Europe" class="mw-redirect" title="Patron saints of Europe">patron saint of Europe</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Benedict founded twelve communities for monks at <a href="/wiki/Subiaco,_Lazio" title="Subiaco, Lazio">Subiaco, Lazio</a>, Italy (about 65 kilometres (40&#160;mi) to the east of Rome), before moving to <a href="/wiki/Monte_Cassino" title="Monte Cassino">Monte Cassino</a> in the mountains of <a href="/wiki/Central_Italy" title="Central Italy">central Italy</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Saint_Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of Saint Benedict">Order of Saint Benedict</a> is of later origin and, moreover, is not an "order" as is commonly understood but merely a confederation of autonomous congregations.<sup id="cite_ref-Holder2009_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Holder2009-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Benedict's main achievement, his <i><a href="/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict" title="Rule of Saint Benedict">Rule of Saint Benedict</a></i>, contains a set of <a href="/wiki/Decree_(canon_law)" class="mw-redirect" title="Decree (canon law)">rules</a> for his monks to follow. Heavily influenced by the writings of <a href="/wiki/John_Cassian" title="John Cassian">John Cassian</a>, it shows strong affinity with the <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_the_Master" title="Rule of the Master">Rule of the Master</a>, but it also has a unique spirit of balance, moderation and reasonableness (<span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">ἐπιείκεια</span></span>, <i>epieíkeia</i>), which persuaded most Christian religious communities founded throughout the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a> to adopt it. As a result, his Rule became one of the most influential religious rules in Western <a href="/wiki/Christendom" title="Christendom">Christendom</a>. For this reason, Giuseppe Carletti regarded Benedict as the founder of <a href="/wiki/Christian_monasticism#Western_Christian_monasticism" title="Christian monasticism">Western Christian monasticism</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Benedict_of_Nursia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Apart from a short poem attributed to Mark of Monte Cassino,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> the only ancient account of Benedict is found in the second volume of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I" title="Pope Gregory I">Pope Gregory&#160;I</a>'s four-book <i>Dialogues</i>, thought to have been written in 593,<sup id="cite_ref-ford_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ford-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> although the authenticity of this work is disputed.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gregory's account of Benedict's life is not, however, a biography in the modern sense of the word. It provides instead a <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">spiritual portrait</a> of the gentle, disciplined abbot. In a letter to Bishop Maximilian of Syracuse, Gregory states his intention for his <i>Dialogues</i>, saying they are a kind of <i>floretum</i> (an <i>anthology</i>, literally, 'flowers') of the most striking miracles of Italian holy men.<sup id="cite_ref-fn_4_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fn_4-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gregory did not set out to write a chronological, historically anchored story of Benedict, but he did base his anecdotes on direct testimony. To establish his authority, Gregory explains that his information came from what he considered the best sources: a handful of Benedict's disciples who lived with him and witnessed his various miracles. These followers, he says, are Constantinus, who succeeded Benedict as <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">Abbot</a> of Monte Cassino, Honoratus, who was abbot of Subiaco when St. Gregory wrote his <i>Dialogues</i>, Valentinianus, and Simplicius.
</p><p>In Gregory's day, history was not recognised as an independent field of study; it was a branch of grammar or rhetoric, and <i>historia</i> was an account that summed up the findings of the learned when they wrote what was, at that time, considered history.<sup id="cite_ref-fn_6_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fn_6-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Gregory's <i>Dialogues</i>, Book Two, then, an authentic <a href="/wiki/Hagiography#Medieval_England" title="Hagiography">medieval hagiography</a> cast as a conversation between the Pope and his deacon Peter,<sup id="cite_ref-Colgrave_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Colgrave-13">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> is designed to teach spiritual lessons.<sup id="cite_ref-ford_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ford-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Benedict_of_Nursia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was the son of a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman</a> noble of <a href="/wiki/Nursia" class="mw-redirect" title="Nursia">Nursia</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-ford_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ford-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup class="noprint Inline-Template noprint Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:AGE_MATTERS" class="mw-redirect" title="Wikipedia:AGE MATTERS"><span title="This claim needs references to more current sources. (December 2021)">obsolete&#160;source</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> the modern <a href="/wiki/Norcia" title="Norcia">Norcia</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Umbria" title="Umbria">Umbria</a>. A tradition which <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> accepts makes him a twin with his sister <a href="/wiki/Scholastica" title="Scholastica">Scholastica</a>. If 480 is accepted as the year of his birth, the year of his abandonment of his studies and leaving home would be about 500. Gregory's narrative makes it impossible to suppose him younger than 20 at the time.
</p><p>Benedict was sent to Rome to study, but was disappointed by the life he found there. He does not seem to have left Rome for the purpose of becoming a <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a>, but only to find some place away from the life of the great city. He took his old nurse with him as a servant and they settled down to live in <a href="/wiki/Enfide" class="mw-redirect" title="Enfide">Enfide</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Crawley_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Crawley-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Enfide, which the tradition of Subiaco identifies with the modern <a href="/wiki/Affile" title="Affile">Affile</a>, is in the <a href="/wiki/Monti_Simbruini" title="Monti Simbruini">Simbruini</a> mountains, about forty miles from Rome and two miles from Subiaco.
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Benedetto,_Mauro_e_Placido.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a5/Benedetto%2C_Mauro_e_Placido.jpg/200px-Benedetto%2C_Mauro_e_Placido.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="113" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a5/Benedetto%2C_Mauro_e_Placido.jpg/300px-Benedetto%2C_Mauro_e_Placido.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a5/Benedetto%2C_Mauro_e_Placido.jpg/400px-Benedetto%2C_Mauro_e_Placido.jpg 2x" data-file-width="671" data-file-height="380" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Benedetto,_Mauro_e_Placido.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Saint Benedict orders <a href="/wiki/Saint_Maurus" title="Saint Maurus">Saint Maurus</a> to the rescue of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Placidus" title="Saint Placidus">Saint Placidus</a></i>, by <a href="/wiki/Fra_Filippo_Lippi" class="mw-redirect" title="Fra Filippo Lippi">Fra Filippo Lippi</a>, AD 1445</div></div></div>
<p>A short distance from Enfide is the entrance to a narrow, gloomy valley, penetrating the mountains and leading directly to Subiaco. The path continues to ascend, and the side of the ravine, on which it runs, becomes steeper, until a cave is reached above which the mountain now rises almost perpendicularly; while on the right, it strikes in a rapid descent down to where, in Benedict's day, 500 feet (150&#160;m) below, lay the blue waters of a lake. The cave has a large triangular-shaped opening and is about ten feet deep.
On his way from Enfide, Benedict met a monk, <a href="/wiki/Romanus_of_Subiaco" title="Romanus of Subiaco">Romanus of Subiaco</a>, whose monastery was on the mountain above the cliff overhanging the cave. Romanus discussed with Benedict the purpose which had brought him to Subiaco, and gave him the monk's habit. By his advice Benedict became a hermit and for three years, unknown to men, lived in this cave above the lake.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Later_life">Later life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Benedict_of_Nursia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Later life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gregory tells us little of these years. He now speaks of Benedict no longer as a youth (<i>puer</i>), but as a man (<i>vir</i>) of God. <a href="/wiki/Romanus_of_Subiaco" title="Romanus of Subiaco">Romanus</a>, Gregory tells us, served Benedict in every way he could. The monk apparently visited him frequently, and on fixed days brought him food.<sup id="cite_ref-Crawley_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Crawley-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During these three years of solitude, broken only by occasional communications with the outer world and by the visits of Romanus, Benedict matured both in mind and character, in knowledge of himself and of his fellow-man, and at the same time he became not merely known to, but secured the respect of, those about him; so much so that on the death of the abbot of a monastery in the neighbourhood (identified by some with <a href="/wiki/Vicovaro" title="Vicovaro">Vicovaro</a>), the community came to him and begged him to become its abbot. Benedict was acquainted with the life and discipline of the monastery, and knew that "their manners were diverse from his and therefore that they would never agree together: yet, at length, overcome with their entreaty, he gave his consent" (ibid., 3). The experiment failed; the monks tried to poison him. The legend goes that they first tried to poison his drink. He prayed a blessing over the cup and the cup shattered. Thus he left the group and went back to his cave at Subiaco. There lived in the neighborhood a priest called Florentius who, moved by envy, tried to ruin him. He tried to poison him with poisoned bread. When he prayed a blessing over the bread, a raven swept in and took the loaf away. From this time his miracles seem to have become frequent, and many people, attracted by his sanctity and character, came to Subiaco to be under his guidance. Having failed by sending him poisonous bread, Florentius tried to seduce his monks with some prostitutes. To avoid further temptations, in about 530 Benedict left Subiaco.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> He founded 12 monasteries in the vicinity of Subiaco, and, eventually, in 530 he founded the great Benedictine monastery of <a href="/wiki/Monte_Cassino" title="Monte Cassino">Monte Cassino</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> which lies on a hilltop between Rome and Naples.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:302px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Totila_e_San_Benedetto.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ef/Totila_e_San_Benedetto.jpg/300px-Totila_e_San_Benedetto.jpg" decoding="async" width="300" height="198" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ef/Totila_e_San_Benedetto.jpg/450px-Totila_e_San_Benedetto.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ef/Totila_e_San_Benedetto.jpg 2x" data-file-width="600" data-file-height="396" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Totila_e_San_Benedetto.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Totila" title="Totila">Totila</a> and Saint Benedict, painted by <a href="/wiki/Spinello_Aretino" title="Spinello Aretino">Spinello Aretino</a></div></div></div>
<p>During the invasion of Italy, <a href="/wiki/Totila" title="Totila">Totila</a>, King of the <a href="/wiki/Ostrogoths" title="Ostrogoths">Goths</a>, ordered a general to wear his kingly robes and to see whether Benedict would discover the truth. Immediately Benedict detected the impersonation, and Totila came to pay him due respect.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Benedict_of_Nursia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He is believed<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions" title="Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch"><span title="The material near this tag may use weasel words or too-vague attribution. (December 2021)">by whom?</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> to have died of a fever at <a href="/wiki/Monte_Cassino" title="Monte Cassino">Monte Cassino</a> not long after his twin sister, <a href="/wiki/Scholastica" title="Scholastica">Scholastica</a>, and was buried in the same place as his sister. According to tradition, this occurred on 21 March 547.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> He was named patron protector of Europe by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI" title="Pope Paul VI">Pope Paul VI</a> in 1964.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> In 1980, <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> declared him co-<a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron</a> of Europe, together with <a href="/wiki/Saints_Cyril_and_Methodius" class="mw-redirect" title="Saints Cyril and Methodius">Cyril and Methodius</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Egregiae_Virtutis_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Egregiae_Virtutis-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Furthermore, he is the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Speleology" title="Speleology">speleologists</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> On the island of <a href="/wiki/Tenerife" title="Tenerife">Tenerife</a> (<a href="/wiki/Spain" title="Spain">Spain</a>) he is the patron saint of fields and farmers. An important <a href="/wiki/Romeria" class="mw-redirect" title="Romeria">romeria</a> (<i><a href="/wiki/Romer%C3%ADa_Regional_de_San_Benito_Abad" title="Romería Regional de San Benito Abad">Romería Regional de San Benito Abad</a></i>) is held on this island in his honor, one of the most important in the country.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the pre-1970 <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a>, his feast is kept on 21 March, the day of his death according to some manuscripts of the <i><a href="/wiki/Martyrologium_Hieronymianum" title="Martyrologium Hieronymianum">Martyrologium Hieronymianum</a></i> and that of <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>. Because on that date his <a href="/wiki/Catholic_liturgy" title="Catholic liturgy">liturgical</a> <a href="/wiki/Memorial_(liturgy)" title="Memorial (liturgy)">memorial</a> would always be impeded by the observance of <a href="/wiki/Lent" title="Lent">Lent</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Mysterii_Paschalis" title="Mysterii Paschalis">1969 revision</a> of the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> moved his memorial to 11 July, the date that appears in some Gallic liturgical books of the end of the 8th century as the feast commemorating his birth (<i>Natalis S. Benedicti</i>). There is some uncertainty about the origin of this feast.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Accordingly, on 21 March the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a> mentions in a line and a half that it is Benedict's day of death and that his memorial is celebrated on 11 July, while on 11 July it devotes seven lines to speaking of him, and mentions the tradition that he died on 21 March.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> commemorates Saint Benedict on 14 March.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a> has no single universal calendar, but a provincial <a href="/wiki/List_of_Anglican_Church_calendars" title="List of Anglican Church calendars">calendar of saints</a> is published in each province. In almost all of these, Saint Benedict is commemorated on 11 July.
</p><p>Benedict is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Lesser_Festival_(Anglicanism)" title="Lesser Festival (Anglicanism)">Lesser Festival</a> on 11 July.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Rule_of_Saint_Benedict"><i>Rule of Saint Benedict</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Benedict_of_Nursia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Rule of Saint Benedict">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict" title="Rule of Saint Benedict">Rule of Saint Benedict</a></div>
<p>Benedict wrote the <i>Rule</i> in 516<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> for monks living communally under the authority of an abbot. The <i>Rule</i> comprises seventy-three short chapters. Its wisdom is twofold: spiritual (how to live a Christocentric life on earth) and administrative (how to run a monastery efficiently). More than half of the chapters describe how to be obedient and humble, and what to do when a member of the community is not. About one-fourth regulate the work of God (the "opus Dei"). One-tenth outline how, and by whom, the monastery should be managed.
</p><p>Following the golden rule of <i>Ora et Labora – pray and work</i>, the monks each day devoted eight hours to prayer, eight hours to sleep, and eight hours to manual work, sacred reading and/or works of charity.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Saint_Benedict_Medal">Saint Benedict Medal</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Benedict_of_Nursia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Saint Benedict Medal">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Saint_Benedict_Medal" title="Saint Benedict Medal">Saint Benedict Medal</a></div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:212px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Benediktusmedaille.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Benediktusmedaille.jpg/210px-Benediktusmedaille.jpg" decoding="async" width="210" height="105" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Benediktusmedaille.jpg/315px-Benediktusmedaille.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Benediktusmedaille.jpg/420px-Benediktusmedaille.jpg 2x" data-file-width="426" data-file-height="214" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Benediktusmedaille.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Benedict depicted on a Jubilee <a href="/wiki/Saint_Benedict_Medal" title="Saint Benedict Medal">Saint Benedict Medal</a> for the 1,400th anniversary of his birth in 1880</div></div></div>
<p>This <a href="/wiki/Devotional_medal" title="Devotional medal">devotional medal</a> originally came from a cross in honor of Saint Benedict. On one side, the medal has an image of Saint Benedict, holding the Holy Rule in his left hand and a cross in his right. There is a raven on one side of him, with a cup on the other side of him. Around the medal's outer margin are the words <i>"Eius in obitu nostro praesentia muniamur"</i> ("May we be strengthened by his presence in the hour of our death"). The other side of the medal has a cross with the initials CSSML on the vertical bar which signify <i>"Crux Sacra Sit Mihi Lux"</i> ("May the Holy Cross be my light") and on the horizontal bar are the initials NDSMD which stand for <i>"Non-Draco Sit Mihi Dux"</i> ("Let not the dragon be my guide"). The initials CSPB stand for <i>"Crux Sancti Patris Benedicti"</i> ("The Cross of the Holy Father Benedict") and are located on the interior angles of the cross. Either the inscription <i>"PAX"</i> (Peace) or the <a href="/wiki/Christogram" title="Christogram">Christogram</a> <i>"IHS"</i> may be found at the top of the cross in most cases. Around the medal's margin on this side are the <i><a href="/wiki/Vade_Retro_Satana" class="mw-redirect" title="Vade Retro Satana">Vade Retro Satana</a></i> initials VRSNSMV which stand for <i>"Vade Retro Satana, Nonquam Suade Mihi Vana"</i> ("Begone Satan, do not suggest to me thy vanities") then a space followed by the initials SMQLIVB which signify <i>"Sunt Mala Quae Libas, Ipse Venena Bibas"</i> ("Evil are the things thou profferest, drink thou thine own poison").<sup id="cite_ref-Benedict_pp_60_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Benedict_pp_60-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:182px;"><a href="/wiki/File:StBenedictVadeRetroSatana.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/StBenedictVadeRetroSatana.jpg/180px-StBenedictVadeRetroSatana.jpg" decoding="async" width="180" height="300" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4f/StBenedictVadeRetroSatana.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="219" data-file-height="365" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:StBenedictVadeRetroSatana.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Image of Saint Benedict with a cross (which is inscribed, "Crux sacra sit mihi lux! Non-draco sit mihi dux!" ("May the holy cross be my light! May the dragon never be my guide!")) and a scroll stating "Vade retro Satana! Nunquam suade mihi vana! Sunt mala quae libas. Ipse venena bibas! ("Begone Satan! Never tempt me with your vanities! The drink you offer is evil. Drink that poison yourself!", or in brief,<i><a href="/wiki/Vade_Retro_Satana" class="mw-redirect" title="Vade Retro Satana">Vade Retro Satana</a></i> which is abbreviated on the <a href="/wiki/Saint_Benedict_Medal" title="Saint Benedict Medal">Saint Benedict Medal</a>.</div></div></div>
<p>This medal was first struck in 1880 to commemorate the fourteenth centenary of Benedict's birth and is also called the Jubilee Medal; its exact origin, however, is unknown. In 1647, during a witchcraft trial at <a href="/w/index.php?title=Natternberg&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Natternberg (page does not exist)">Natternberg</a> near <a href="/wiki/Metten_Abbey" title="Metten Abbey">Metten Abbey</a> in Bavaria, the accused women testified they had no power over Metten, which was under the protection of the cross. An investigation found a number of painted crosses on the walls of the abbey with the letters now found on St Benedict medals, but their meaning had been forgotten. A manuscript written in 1415 was eventually found that had a picture of Benedict holding a scroll in one hand and a staff which ended in a cross in the other. On the scroll and staff were written the full words of the initials contained on the crosses. Medals then began to be struck in Germany, which then spread throughout Europe. This medal was first approved by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XIV" title="Pope Benedict XIV">Pope Benedict XIV</a> in his briefs of 23 December 1741 and 12 March 1742.<sup id="cite_ref-Benedict_pp_60_27-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Benedict_pp_60-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Benedict has been also the motif of many collector's coins around the world. The <a href="/wiki/Euro_gold_and_silver_commemorative_coins_(Austria)#2002_coinage" title="Euro gold and silver commemorative coins (Austria)">Austria 50 euro 'The Christian Religious Orders'</a>, issued on 13 March 2002 is one of them.
</p>

<p>The early Middle Ages have been called "the Benedictine centuries."<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> In April 2008, Pope <a href="/wiki/Benedict_XVI" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedict XVI">Benedict XVI</a> discussed the influence St Benedict had on Western Europe. The pope said that "with his life and work St Benedict exercised a fundamental influence on the development of European civilization and culture" and helped Europe to emerge from the "dark night of history" that followed the fall of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_empire" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman empire">Roman empire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Benedict contributed more than anyone else to the rise of monasticism in the West. His Rule was the foundational document for thousands of religious communities in the Middle Ages.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> To this day, The Rule of St. Benedict is the most common and influential Rule used by monasteries and monks, more than 1,400 years after its writing. Today the Benedictine family is represented by two branches: the Benedictine Federation and the Cistercians.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A basilica was built upon the birthplace of Benedict and Scholastica in the 1400s. Ruins of their familial home were excavated from beneath the church and preserved. The <a href="/wiki/October_2016_Central_Italy_earthquakes" title="October 2016 Central Italy earthquakes">earthquake of 30 October 2016</a> completely devastated the structure of the basilica, leaving only the front facade and altar standing.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
