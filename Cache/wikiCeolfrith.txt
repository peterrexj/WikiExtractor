<p><b>Saint Ceolfrid</b> (or <b>Ceolfrith</b>, <small>Old English:&#160;</small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="ang-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Old_English" title="Help:IPA/Old English">[ˈtʃeːolfriθ]</a></span>; c. 642 – 716) was an <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon">Anglo-Saxon</a> <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> and <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>. He is best known as the warden of <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> from the age of seven until his death in 716. He was the Abbot of <a href="/wiki/Monkwearmouth-Jarrow_Abbey" class="mw-redirect" title="Monkwearmouth-Jarrow Abbey">Monkwearmouth-Jarrow Abbey</a>, and a major contributor to the project to produce the <a href="/wiki/Codex_Amiatinus" title="Codex Amiatinus">Codex Amiatinus</a> Bible. He died in <a href="/wiki/Burgundy_(region)" class="mw-redirect" title="Burgundy (region)">Burgundy</a> while en route to deliver a copy of the codex to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_II" title="Pope Gregory II">Pope Gregory II</a> in Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ceolfrith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Not much is known about the earlier period of Ceolfrid's life.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> His desire to join the <a href="/wiki/Monastic_community" class="mw-redirect" title="Monastic community">monastic community</a> was likely due to his own brother Cynefrid's devotion to the traditions of <a href="/wiki/Christian_monasticism" title="Christian monasticism">Christian monasticism</a>. Historians date Ceolfrid's induction into monastic tradition around the date of Cynefrid's death in 660. Ceolfrid is known to have a strong family connection to monastic tradition. In addition to his brother, his cousin Tunbert was the first Abbot of the Monastery of <a href="/wiki/Hexham" title="Hexham">Hexham</a>. His first four years in <a href="/wiki/Cloister" title="Cloister">cloister</a> took place at <a href="/wiki/Gilling_Abbey" title="Gilling Abbey">Gilling Abbey</a> in what is now <a href="/wiki/North_Yorkshire" title="North Yorkshire">North Yorkshire</a>, which was also attended by Cynefrid, prior to his departure to Ireland. Ceolfrid is described as having "behaved of the greater devotion, giving his mind continually to reading, to labour, and monastic discipline".<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> After these four years, Ceolfrid left Gilling as he "sought a monastery of a stricter character".<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He soon took in with a band of men, led by Wilfrid, later canonized as <a href="/wiki/Saint_Wilfrid" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Wilfrid">Saint Wilfrid</a>. These monks are identified by Boutflower as being the Benedictines of <a href="/wiki/Ripon" title="Ripon">Ripon</a> at a monastery under the same name. During this time, he came to refine his own understanding of proper monastic principles. At the age of 27, Ceolfrid was ordained as a priest, and began to acquaint himself to the utmost with the practices of monastic life.
</p><p>Very little is revealed about the period between the end of his days at Ripon, and his appointment under <a href="/wiki/Benedict_Biscop" title="Benedict Biscop">Benedict Biscop</a>, except that he spent some time in the institutions of Abbot <a href="/wiki/Botolph" class="mw-redirect" title="Botolph">Botolph</a>, whom he describes as being filled with "the grace of spirit".<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> While having been revered as an inspiration for the way of divine living, Botolph also served to inspire a greater sense of humility within Ceolfrid.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Relationship_with_Benedict_Biscop">Relationship with Benedict Biscop</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ceolfrith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Relationship with Benedict Biscop">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:CeolfridBibled11v.jpeg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c1/CeolfridBibled11v.jpeg/220px-CeolfridBibled11v.jpeg" decoding="async" width="220" height="318" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c1/CeolfridBibled11v.jpeg/330px-CeolfridBibled11v.jpeg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c1/CeolfridBibled11v.jpeg/440px-CeolfridBibled11v.jpeg 2x" data-file-width="3808" data-file-height="5496" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:CeolfridBibled11v.jpeg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>One of the few surviving pages from the <a href="/wiki/Ceolfrid_Bible" title="Ceolfrid Bible">Ceolfrid Bible</a>, one of three versions of the <a href="/wiki/Vulgate_Bible" class="mw-redirect" title="Vulgate Bible">Vulgate Bible</a> created in Wearmouth and Jarrow under Ceolfrid at the turn of the eighth century.<br /><a href="/wiki/British_Library" title="British Library">British Library</a>, London.</div></div></div>
<p>In c.672/3, <a href="/wiki/Benedict_Biscop" title="Benedict Biscop">Benedict Biscop</a> received a land grant from King <a href="/wiki/Ecgfrith_of_Northumbria" title="Ecgfrith of Northumbria">Ecgfrith of Northumbria</a> for the explicit purpose of erecting a monastery. During the construction of his first monastery at <a href="/wiki/Monkwearmouth" title="Monkwearmouth">Wearmouth</a>, Biscop appointed Abbot Eosterwini (anglicized as "<a href="/wiki/Easterwine" class="mw-redirect" title="Easterwine">Easterwine</a>") as his primary Abbot and Coadjutor. The monastery took eight years to build. This institute had left Ecgfrith so enamoured that soon after the completion of the Wearmouth Monastery, he granted Biscop another segment of land for the construction of a second monastery, <a href="/wiki/Jarrow" title="Jarrow">Jarrow</a>, with the intention that the two should be administered as one.
</p><p>It is during the construction of the Wearmouth Monastery that Benedict Biscop sought out Ceolfrid, who would become "his most zealous assistant from the first foundation of the former monastery",<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> as well as a close friend. It appears that Biscop's invitation came at a most opportune time, for Ceolfrid had been contemplating the idea of leaving the post he held at the time. He had grown rather disenchanted with the power stratification within the institution (the name of which is unknown), and had had enough of the "jealousies and very bitter persecutions of certain men of rank",<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and had been looking to return to his own monastery (assumed to be Ripon).
</p><p>Upon the completion of the Jarrow Monastery, Ceolfrid became the Abbot of the St. Paul's Church on the monasterial grounds. Conflicting reports state that the presence of Ceolfrid during Jarrow's construction varied. Some papers state that Jarrow came into his hands after its completion, while another identified Ceolfrid as being paramount to the actual construction of the monastery; as the individual who directed the construction of the monastery itself.
</p><p>The friendship between the two was fairly close. When Benedict sailed across the English channel to Rome for the last time, he chose only Ceolfrid to join him in his journey. This trip was to be the very trip that would lead to both Abbot's immortalization in the works of Ceolfrid's ward and later contemporary, <a href="/wiki/The_Venerable_Bede" class="mw-redirect" title="The Venerable Bede">The Venerable Bede</a>. Ceolfrid also used the trip as an opportunity to explore his role in Biscop's institution, feeling that Rome would be an opportune place to learn his position's responsibilities. Twelve years later, upon the death of Abbot Eosterwini, Ceolfrid was appointed as the sole Abbot for both Monasteries of Wearmouth and Jarrow, an honour never heard of before. In 690, Benedict Biscop died, after being bedridden for a lengthy period of time, and Ceolfrid became the leading head of both monasteries, "[whose] libraries of both monasteries, which Abbot Benedict had so actively begun, under his zealous care became doubled in extent".<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Relationship_with_Bede">Relationship with Bede</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ceolfrith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Relationship with Bede">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> came into Ceolfrid's care at the young age of seven, and became the pupil of the Abbot as well as friend. In his early years at the twin Monasteries of Wearmouth – Jarrow (686), the Plague had struck Northumbria, and ravaged most of the countryside, including the twin Monasteries. Ceolfrid and Bede appeared to have remained untouched by the epidemic, and took the duties of caring for the infected and dying monks of the monasteries with unyielding fervour. They further worked together in maintaining the regular sermons when fear had gripped the population. When the Plague finally passed over, master and pupil began to rebuild the monastic foundations and succeeded effectively. Bede remained in Jarrow for the majority of his life, never straying more than 70 miles from the monastery at any time. He was a loyal pupil until Ceolfrid's death, and he died in Jarrow in 735.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Codex_Amiatinus_Project">The Codex Amiatinus Project</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ceolfrith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: The Codex Amiatinus Project">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:232px;"><a href="/wiki/File:CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg/230px-CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg" decoding="async" width="230" height="320" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg/345px-CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg/460px-CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1475" data-file-height="2052" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Portrait of Ezra from the <a href="/wiki/Codex_Amiatinus" title="Codex Amiatinus">Codex Amiatinus</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Laurentian_Library" title="Laurentian Library">Laurentian Library</a>, Florence.</div></div></div>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Codex_Amiatinus" title="Codex Amiatinus">Codex Amiatinus</a> is the oldest manuscript with a complete text of the <a href="/wiki/Vulgate" title="Vulgate">Vulgate</a>. The Codex Amiatinus is described as a brilliant display of the beauty that is Early British, Pre-<a href="/wiki/Carolingian_Renaissance" title="Carolingian Renaissance">Carolingian</a> <a href="/wiki/Calligraphy" title="Calligraphy">calligraphy</a>. The composition of the Vulgate was part of the project to expand Wearmouth and Jarrow's extensive library, and Ceolfrid ordered three copies of this Bible manuscript to be composed; one of which would be dedicated to the Pope Gregory II, while the other two copies were meant to stay in the respective churches of Wearmouth and Jarrow. There are no official records that state that the text made it to Rome. It is said that instead, it made its way into Florence, where it was presented by the Lombard Abbot Peter to the <a href="/wiki/Abbazia_di_San_Salvatore" title="Abbazia di San Salvatore">Abbazia di San Salvatore</a> at <a href="/wiki/Mount_Amiata" class="mw-redirect" title="Mount Amiata">Mount Amiata</a> in <a href="/wiki/Tuscany" title="Tuscany">Tuscany</a>. It is believed that he changed the dedicatory note inscribed within the leaves as donated to the monastery. This occurred in the 9th century. The document remained at Mount Amiata until 1786, when it was relocated to the <a href="/wiki/Laurentian_Library" title="Laurentian Library">Laurentian Library</a> in Florence. There is some dispute over what consisted of this Vulgate Codex. Over the past few hundred years, additional leaves that appear to be related to this text have been located in Britain, some having been used as book wrappings. These new discoveries have led scholars to question the total length of the codex, as there are still fragments missing from it today.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Ceolfrid_and_the_Picts">Ceolfrid and the Picts</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ceolfrith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Ceolfrid and the Picts">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Sometime after 711, <a href="/wiki/Nechtan_mac_Der-Ilei" title="Nechtan mac Der-Ilei">Nechtan mac Der-Ilei</a>, King of the <a href="/wiki/Picts" title="Picts">Picts</a>, sought authoritative advice from Abbot Ceolfrid on the reform of paschal cycles with a view to harmonising the celebration of Easter within his kingdom.  By 716 both the Picts and the Columban clergy of <a href="/wiki/Iona" title="Iona">Iona</a> had adopted the <i>pascha catholica</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Final_days">Final days</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ceolfrith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Final days">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Ceolfrid apparently knew that he was coming to the end of his life, and so he resigned his post and was succeeded by <a href="/wiki/Hwaetberht" title="Hwaetberht">Hwaetberht</a>. He then set sail for Rome with the intent of delivering the Codex Amiatinus Bible to Pope Gregory II. He made it as far as <a href="/wiki/Langres" title="Langres">Langres</a> in Burgundy, where he died on 29 September 716 and was buried.
</p>
