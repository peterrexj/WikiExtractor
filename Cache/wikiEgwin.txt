<p><b>Saint Egwin of Evesham</b>, <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">OSB</a><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> (died 30 December 717) was a <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict" title="Rule of Saint Benedict">Benedictine</a> <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> and, later, the third <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Worcester" title="Bishop of Worcester">Bishop of Worcester</a> in England.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Egwin_of_Evesham&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Egwin was born in <a href="/wiki/Worcester,_England" title="Worcester, England">Worcester</a> of a <a href="/wiki/Nobility" title="Nobility">noble</a> family, and was a descendant of <a href="/wiki/List_of_monarchs_of_Mercia" title="List of monarchs of Mercia">Mercian kings</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-CathEnc_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CathEnc-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He may possibly have been a nephew of King <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelred_of_Mercia" title="Æthelred of Mercia">Æthelred of Mercia</a>. Having become a monk, his biographers say that king, clergy, and commoners all united in demanding Egwin's elevation to <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a>; but the popularity which led him to the episcopal office dissipated in response to his performance as bishop.<sup id="cite_ref-CathEnc_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CathEnc-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He was <a href="/wiki/Consecrated" class="mw-redirect" title="Consecrated">consecrated</a> bishop after 693.<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook223_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook223-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As a bishop he was known as a protector of orphans and widows and a fair judge. <sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He struggled with the local population over the acceptance of Christian morality, especially <a href="/wiki/Christian_views_on_marriage" title="Christian views on marriage">Christian marriage</a> and <a href="/wiki/Clerical_celibacy" title="Clerical celibacy">clerical celibacy</a>. Egwin's stern discipline created a resentment which, as King Æthelred was his friend, eventually found its way to his ecclesiastical superiors. He undertook a <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> to seek vindication from the <a href="/wiki/Pope" title="Pope">pope</a> himself. According to a legend, he prepared for his journey by locking shackles on his feet, and throwing the key into the <a href="/wiki/River_Avon,_Warwickshire" title="River Avon, Warwickshire">River Avon</a>.
</p><p>According to one account, as Egwin and his companions were passing through the Alps, they began to thirst. Those among his companions who did not acknowledge the bishop's sanctity asked him mockingly to pray for water as Moses once did in the desert. But others, who did believe in him, rebuked the unbelievers and asked him in a different tone, with true faith and hope. Egwin prostrated himself in prayer. On arising, they saw a pure stream of water gush forth out of the rock.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While he prayed before the tomb of the <a href="/wiki/Apostles_in_the_New_Testament" title="Apostles in the New Testament">Apostles</a> in Rome, one of his servants brought him the very key—found in the mouth of a fish that had just been caught in the <a href="/wiki/Tiber" title="Tiber">Tiber</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-CathEnc_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CathEnc-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Egwin then released himself from his self-imposed bonds and straight away obtained from the pope an authoritative release from his enemies' obloquy.
</p><p>Upon his return to England, he founded <a href="/wiki/Evesham_Abbey" title="Evesham Abbey">Evesham Abbey</a>, which became one of the great Benedictine houses of medieval England. It was dedicated to the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a>, who had reportedly made known to a <a href="/wiki/Swineherd" title="Swineherd">swineherd</a> named <a href="/wiki/Eof" title="Eof">Eof</a> just where a church should be built in her honour.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>One of the last important acts of his <a href="/wiki/Episcopate" class="mw-redirect" title="Episcopate">episcopate</a> was his participation in the first great <a href="/wiki/Councils_of_Clovesho" title="Councils of Clovesho">Council of Clovesho</a>. According to the Benedictine historian, <a href="/wiki/Jean_Mabillon" title="Jean Mabillon">Jean Mabillon</a>, he died on 30 December 720,<sup id="cite_ref-CathEnc_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CathEnc-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> although his death is generally accepted as having occurred three years earlier on 30 December 717.<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook223_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook223-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He died at the abbey he had founded, and his remains were enshrined there.
</p><p>A <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> was written by <a href="/wiki/Byrhtferth_of_Ramsey" class="mw-redirect" title="Byrhtferth of Ramsey">Byrhtferth of Ramsey</a>, a monk at <a href="/wiki/Ramsey_Abbey" title="Ramsey Abbey">Ramsey Abbey</a>, around 1016.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Another <i>Vita Sancti Egwini</i>, was written by <a href="/wiki/Dominic_of_Evesham" title="Dominic of Evesham">Dominic of Evesham</a>, a medieval prior of Evesham Abbey around 1130.<sup id="cite_ref-Writings298_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Writings298-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> His tomb was destroyed, along with the abbey church, at the time of the <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Monasteries" class="mw-redirect" title="Dissolution of the Monasteries">dissolution</a> of the abbey in 1540.
</p>
