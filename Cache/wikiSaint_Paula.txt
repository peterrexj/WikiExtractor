<p><b>Paula of Rome</b> (AD&#160;347–404)<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> was an ancient <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Rome" title="Ancient Rome">Roman</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and early <a href="/wiki/Desert_Mothers" title="Desert Mothers">Desert Mother</a>. A member of one of the richest <a href="/wiki/Roman_Senate" title="Roman Senate">senatorial</a> families which claimed descent from <a href="/wiki/Agamemnon" title="Agamemnon">Agamemnon</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-umilta_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-umilta-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Paula was the daughter of Blesilla and Rogatus, from the great clan of the <a href="/wiki/Category:Furii" title="Category:Furii">Furii Camilli</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Kelley_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kelley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> At the age of 16,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-AOCA_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AOCA-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Paula was married to the nobleman Toxotius, with whom she had four daughters, <a href="/wiki/Blaesilla" title="Blaesilla">Blaesilla</a>, <a href="/wiki/Paulina_(wife_of_Pammachius)" title="Paulina (wife of Pammachius)">Paulina</a>, <a href="/wiki/Eustochium" title="Eustochium">Eustochium</a>, and Rufina. She also had a boy, also named Toxotius.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Information about Paula's early life is recorded by <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>. In his Letter 108, he states that she had led a luxurious life and held a great status. She dressed in <a href="/wiki/Silk" title="Silk">silks</a>, and had been carried about the city by her <a href="/wiki/Eunuch" title="Eunuch">eunuch</a> slaves.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Entering_the_religious_life">Entering the religious life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Entering the religious life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>At the age of 32, Paula was <a href="/wiki/Widow" title="Widow">widowed</a>. She continued to dedicate herself to her family, but became more interested in religion as time went on. 
</p><p>Through the influence of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Marcella" title="Saint Marcella">Marcella</a> and her group, Paula became an enthusiastic member of this semi-monastic group of women. She met and learned from <a href="/wiki/Paulinus_II_of_Antioch" title="Paulinus II of Antioch">Paulinus, bishop of Antioch</a> and <a href="/wiki/Epiphanius_of_Salamis" title="Epiphanius of Salamis">Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis</a><sup id="cite_ref-:0_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> In 382, she met <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>, who had come to Rome with <a href="/wiki/Epiphanius_of_Salamis" title="Epiphanius of Salamis">Epiphanius</a> and <a href="/wiki/Paulinus_II_of_Antioch" title="Paulinus II of Antioch">Paulinus of Antioch</a>. Born in <a href="/wiki/Dalmatia" title="Dalmatia">Dalmatia</a>, Jerome had studied in Rome as a youth and had traveled to <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a> and <a href="/wiki/Aquileia" title="Aquileia">Aquileia</a>, and for some years had lived in the East as an <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a> and scholar.
</p><p>While on pilgrimage to the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Land" title="Holy Land">Holy Land</a> and <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a>, with Jerome and her daughter, Eustochium, she visited monks and other ascetics including <a href="/wiki/Paulinus_of_Nola" title="Paulinus of Nola">Paulinus of Nola</a>, <a href="/wiki/Melania_the_Elder" title="Melania the Elder">Melania the Elder</a>, <a href="/wiki/Tyrannius_Rufinus" title="Tyrannius Rufinus">Rufinus of Aquileia</a> and Isidore the Confessor. She then settled in <a href="/wiki/Bethlehem" title="Bethlehem">Bethlehem</a> and established a monastery for men and a convent for women and a hostel for pilgrims.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:0_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> The monks' monastery was run by men with Jerome living and writing in one of its cells and Paula was abbess of the nuns.<sup id="cite_ref-Cain_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cain-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Family">Family</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Family">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Paula married her daughter, Paulina (d. 395), to the senator <a href="/wiki/Pammachius" title="Pammachius">Pammachius</a>; Blesilla soon became a widow and died in 384. Of her two other daughters, Rufina died in 386, and Eustochium accompanied her mother to the Orient where she died in 419. Eustochium succeeded Paula as abbess and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_the_Younger&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Paula the Younger (page does not exist)">Paula the Younger</a> (Paula's granddaughter) succeeded Eustochium. Paula's son, Toxotius, at first not a Christian, but baptized in 385, married in 389 Laeta, daughter of the pagan priest Albinus. Of this marriage was born Paula the Younger, who in 404 rejoined Eustochium in the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Land" title="Holy Land">Holy Land</a> and in 420 closed the eyes of Jerome. These are the names which recur frequently in the letters of Jerome, where they are inseparable from that of Paula. It has been argued that <a href="/wiki/Eustochius" title="Eustochius">Eustochius</a> of <a href="/wiki/Tours" title="Tours">Tours</a> was the brother of Paula the Younger and the son of Toxotius.<sup id="cite_ref-Kelley_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kelley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Pilgrimage">Pilgrimage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Pilgrimage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>A year after the death of her husband, Paula pursued a pilgrimage to tour all of the holy sites, traveling with large entourages of both men and women<sup id="cite_ref-Whiting_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Whiting-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> including her daughter Eustochium and Jerome himself.<sup id="cite_ref-Cain_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cain-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Paula could undertake this voyage due to her widow status which left her a significant fortune allowing her exemption from remarriage. Additionally, having had a male heir and two married daughters provided supplementary financial insurance.<sup id="cite_ref-Whiting_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Whiting-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Her travels are documented by Jerome in his later writing addressed to Eustochium which discusses how Paula participated in the environments they toured. He discusses that Paula exemplified an intimate and emotional connection with the sights, experiencing visual vividness of biblical events at each locale.<sup id="cite_ref-Cain_12-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cain-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Concluding her journey, Paula decided to remain in Bethlehem to develop a monastery and spiritual retreat with Jerome.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastery_establishment">Monastery establishment</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Monastery establishment">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Once settled in Bethlehem, Paula and Jerome built a double monastery including one for Paula and her nuns and another for Jerome and his monks. The addition of a roadside hostel was also constructed to serve as an economic source to fund the monasteries.<sup id="cite_ref-Whiting_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Whiting-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> This development took three years to complete and was primarily sourced by Paula who,<sup id="cite_ref-Cain_12-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cain-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> during this time of construction, stayed at another double monastery called <a href="/wiki/Mount_of_Olives" title="Mount of Olives">Mount Olives</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Whiting_13-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Whiting-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Once completed, the monastery segregated each gender from one another during manual labor and meals, but practiced prayer in the same locale. Additional separation, within the nun monastery, included three different communities of women based on social rank who were divided in separate living quarters.<sup id="cite_ref-Cain_12-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cain-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During its functioning, Jerome and Paula's retreat attracted large crowds of visitors both from <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christian</a> backgrounds and general travelers from a variety of regions including <a href="/wiki/Ethiopia" title="Ethiopia">Ethiopia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Iran" title="Iran">Persia</a>, and <a href="/wiki/India" title="India">India</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Cain_12-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cain-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Along with this, aristocratic refugees were also drawn to the locale due to Jerome's extensive network of followers. The result of this inclusion, alongside their growing admittance of monks and nuns, left Paula and Jerome's retreat to face financial hardship, having their resources strained. In order to recover costs, which were also depleted by Paula's considerable donations to the needy, Jerome sold his family's property in <a href="/wiki/Italy" title="Italy">Italy</a> and <a href="/wiki/Dalmatia" title="Dalmatia">Dalmatia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Whiting_13-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Whiting-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Ascetic_life">Ascetic life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Ascetic life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>It is Jerome's writing's in a letter to Eustochium that provide the most insight on Paula's life during her years of service at the monastery. She is noted as maintaining her ascetic devotion through intensive studies of the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old</a> and <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testaments</a>, often under the guidance of Jerome.<sup id="cite_ref-Ex_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ex-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> With this, she also practiced a strict fasting regimen, abstinence, and pursued a destitute lifestyle "to preserve a singular attachment to God" as stated by Jerome.<sup id="cite_ref-Cain_12-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cain-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> While practicing this life of isolation, Paula still continued to interact with local clergy and bishops and maintained devout attention to teaching the nuns under her sovereignty.<sup id="cite_ref-Whiting_13-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Whiting-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Jerome's letter from 404 moreover indicates Paula's first-hand connection with relics from Christ's passion, "she was shown the pillar of the church which supports the colonnade and is stained with the Lord's blood. He is said to have been tied to it when he was scourged."<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Jerome made explicit in his letter how Paula, through these practices, became a recognized figure in the Christian community. At one point, in traveling to <a href="/wiki/Nitria_(monastic_site)" title="Nitria (monastic site)">Nitria</a>, she was earnestly received by renowned monks from Egypt, and once her death arrived on 26 January 404, her funeral was noted as having a significant portion of the population of Palestine arrive in her honor.<sup id="cite_ref-Ex_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ex-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> A year after her passing, Paula was recognized by the Latin Church as a saint, with feast day on 26 January.<sup id="cite_ref-Cain_12-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cain-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Relationship_with_Jerome">Relationship with Jerome</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Relationship with Jerome">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/06/Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg/220px-Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="316" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/06/Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg/330px-Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/06/Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg/440px-Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n_043.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2850" data-file-height="4096" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Saint_Jerome" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Jerome">Saint Jerome</a> with Saint Paula and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Eustochium" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Eustochium">Saint Eustochium</a> (painting of <a href="/wiki/Francisco_de_Zurbar%C3%A1n" title="Francisco de Zurbarán">Francisco de Zurbarán</a> at National Gallery of Art in Washington)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Jerome's enemies found that his denunciations of clerical indulgence and advocacy of self-denial were odd when they considered his close relationship with Paula.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> An amorous relationship between Jerome and Paula was suggested as having occurred.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Palladius_of_Galatia" title="Palladius of Galatia">Palladius</a>, a contemporary of Jerome, believed that Paula was hindered by Jerome: "For though she was able to surpass all, having great abilities, he hindered her by his jealousy, having induced her to serve his own plan."<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>What is known is that Paula financed Jerome's translation of the bible into Latin, now known as the Latin Vulgate bible and he dedicated many of his commentaries and books to her.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Jerome died in late 419 or early 420, he was buried beneath the north aisle of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Nativity" title="Church of the Nativity">Church of the Nativity</a>, near the graves of Paula and Eustochium.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Medieval_interpretations">Medieval interpretations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Medieval interpretations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>An anecdote told of Jerome, of twelfth-century origin, tells that Roman clergy hostile to Jerome planned to have him expelled from the city by planting a woman's robe next to his bed. When Jerome awoke in the middle of the night to attend the service of <a href="/wiki/Matins" title="Matins">matins</a>, he absentmindedly put on the female robes. He was thus accused of having had a woman in his bed. This story acknowledges, while at the same time discrediting as a malicious <a href="/wiki/Slander" class="mw-redirect" title="Slander">slander</a>, Jerome's relationship with women, such as he is presumed to have had with Paula.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Chaucer" class="mw-redirect" title="Chaucer">Chaucer</a> played upon the relationship between Jerome and Paula when he writes the <a href="/wiki/Wife_of_Bath" class="mw-redirect" title="Wife of Bath">Wife of Bath's</a> Prologue. Chaucer has the Wife visit the same pilgrimage sites as did Paula, and has her constantly cite not classical authors, but Jerome.<sup id="cite_ref-umilta_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-umilta-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Many of her comments are counter-arguments to those put forth by Jerome, mainly in his work <i><a href="/wiki/Against_Jovinianus" title="Against Jovinianus">Against Jovinianus</a></i>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Modern_interpretations">Modern interpretations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Modern interpretations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><a href="/wiki/Feminist" class="mw-redirect" title="Feminist">Feminist</a> authors writing in the late 19th and early 20th century, such as <a href="/wiki/Ellen_Battelle_Dietrick" title="Ellen Battelle Dietrick">Ellen Battelle Dietrick</a> and <a href="/wiki/John_Augustine_Zahm" title="John Augustine Zahm">John Augustine Zahm</a>, are attributing to Paula (and, to a degree, to her daughter Eustochium) a much more comprehensive role in Jerome's work, crediting Paula with first suggesting to him the translation of the <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">Bible</a> from <a href="/wiki/Hebrew" class="mw-redirect" title="Hebrew">Hebrew</a> and <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a> into <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a>, resulting in his major oeuvre, the <a href="/wiki/Vulgate" title="Vulgate">Vulgate</a>, as well as in helping him along with the translation, editing Jerome's manuscripts, providing him the money needed for purchasing the necessary works, and eventually copying the text and putting it into wider circulation.<sup id="cite_ref-EBD_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EBD-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Zahm_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Zahm-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Dietrick, the women's rights activist, also maintains that Paula "co-labor[ed] with Jerome", being a "woman of fine intellect, highly trained, and an excellent Hebrew scholar," who "revised and corrected Jerome's work" and takes case with the "Churchmen" attributing the Vulgate solely to Jerome, while this fundamental work would have never taken shape without Paula's contribution.<sup id="cite_ref-EBD_22-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EBD-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> Nancy Hardesty, a leading figure in the US evangelical feminist movement<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> whose publishing and public activity career started in the 1960s and peaked in the 70s, wrote in 1988 about Paula in a popular Christian history magazine, speaking of how she paid Jerome's living expenses, and repeating several of the old tropes known from Dietrick and Zahm<sup id="cite_ref-Hardesty_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hardesty-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> However, <a href="/wiki/William_Fremantle_(nephew)" class="mw-redirect" title="William Fremantle (nephew)">W.H. Fremantle</a>, who wrote the Jerome chapter of the classical <a href="/wiki/Nicene_and_Post-Nicene_Fathers" title="Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers">Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers</a> (NPNF) series, published in 1892 and including all of Jerome's surviving letters to Paula and Eustachium, doesn't mention in Jerome's biography under "The Vulgate" Paula's or Eustochium's name even once, and only mentions two members of the next generation of "virgins", the younger Paula and Melania, as those who attended to him during his last years.<sup id="cite_ref-NPNF2vol3_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-NPNF2vol3-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> The famous <i>Epistula</i> 108 written by Jerome at the death of Paula and addressed to Eustochium, while including a biography of his late friend, focuses on what Jerome conceives as Paula's main merits, her ascetic lifestyle and Christian values, but does not mention their working relationship.<sup id="cite_ref-Lamp_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lamp-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> He does paraphrase <a href="/wiki/Horace" title="Horace">Horace</a> when he writes that "I have built" (to your memory) "a monument more lasting than bronze",<sup id="cite_ref-Lamp_27-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lamp-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> but while the poet is predicting the long-lasting influence of the entire literary oeuvre left behind by him and his contemporaries of the Augustan age,<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> Jerome is equivocal enough as to allow the Paula enthusiast, Rev. J.A. Zahm (1912), to see Jerome alluding here to his entire life-work, "but above all, ... the Vulgate" as Paula's monument,<sup id="cite_ref-Zahm_23-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Zahm-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> while the very rigurous Johanna C. Lamprecht (2017) only understands him as referring to that very letter.<sup id="cite_ref-Lamp_27-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lamp-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> However, the fact remains that Jerome dedicated many of his reworked or new translations to Paula: <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Job" title="Book of Job">Job</a>, <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Isaiah" title="Book of Isaiah">Isaiah</a>, <a href="/wiki/1_and_2_Samuel" class="mw-redirect" title="1 and 2 Samuel">1 and 2 Samuel</a>, <a href="/wiki/1_and_2_Kings" class="mw-redirect" title="1 and 2 Kings">1 and 2 Kings</a>, <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Esther" title="Book of Esther">Esther</a>, three of the <a href="/wiki/Pauline_epistles" title="Pauline epistles">epistles</a> (<a href="/wiki/Epistle_to_the_Galatians" title="Epistle to the Galatians">Galatians</a>, <a href="/wiki/Epistle_to_Philemon" title="Epistle to Philemon">Philemon</a>, <a href="/wiki/Epistle_to_Titus" title="Epistle to Titus">Titus</a>), and the <a href="/wiki/Twelve_minor_prophets" class="mw-redirect" title="Twelve minor prophets">twelve minor prophets</a>, with the Book of Esther being dedicated to both Paula and Eustochium.<sup id="cite_ref-Hardesty_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hardesty-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> The official <a href="/wiki/Vatican_News" title="Vatican News">Vatican News</a> is presenting as Paula's main merit the fact that it was her who "suggested the need" for the Bible's translation into Latin, and together with her daughter "copied the work so it could be shared far and wide."<sup id="cite_ref-HSee_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-HSee-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paula_of_Rome&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 2022, Paula was officially added to the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church liturgical calendar</a> with a feast day together with her daughter, <a href="/wiki/Eustochium" title="Eustochium">Eustochium</a> on 28 September.<sup id="cite_ref-gc2022_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-gc2022-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
