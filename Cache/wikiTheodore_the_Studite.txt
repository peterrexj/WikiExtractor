<p><b>Theodore the Studite</b> (<a href="/wiki/Medieval_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Medieval Greek language">Medieval Greek</a>: <span lang="grc">Θεόδωρος ό Στουδίτης</span>; 759–826), also known as <b>Theodorus Studita</b> and <b>Saint Theodore of Stoudios/Studium</b>, was a <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Greeks" title="Byzantine Greeks">Byzantine Greek</a> <a href="/wiki/Monasticism" title="Monasticism">monk</a> and abbot of the <a href="/wiki/Stoudios_Monastery" class="mw-redirect" title="Stoudios Monastery">Stoudios Monastery</a> in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrowne193376_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrowne193376-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911769_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911769-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He played a major role in the revivals both of Byzantine monasticism and of classical literary genres in Byzantium. He is known as a zealous opponent of <a href="/wiki/Iconoclasm_(Byzantine)" class="mw-redirect" title="Iconoclasm (Byzantine)">iconoclasm</a>, one of several conflicts that set him at odds with both emperor and patriarch. Throughout his life he maintained letter correspondences with many important political and cultural figures of the Byzantine empire;<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKazhdanTalbot1991–1992396_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKazhdanTalbot1991–1992396-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> this included many women, such as the composer and nun <a href="/wiki/Kassia" title="Kassia">Kassia</a>, who was much influenced by his teachings.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKazhdanTalbot1991–1992399_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKazhdanTalbot1991–1992399-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Family_and_childhood">Family and childhood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Family and childhood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Theodore was born in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> in 759.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911769_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911769-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He was the oldest son of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Photeinos_(fl._8th_century)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Photeinos (fl. 8th century) (page does not exist)">Photeinos</a>, an important financial official in the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_aristocracy_and_bureaucracy" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine aristocracy and bureaucracy">palace bureaucracy</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> and <a href="/wiki/Theoktiste_(740%E2%80%93802)" title="Theoktiste (740–802)">Theoktiste</a>, herself the offspring of a distinguished Constantinopolitan family.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The brother of Theoktiste, Theodore's uncle <a href="/wiki/Plato_of_Sakkoudion" title="Plato of Sakkoudion">Platon</a>, was an important official in the imperial financial administration.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The family therefore controlled a significant portion, if not all, of the imperial financial administration during the reign of <a href="/wiki/Constantine_V" title="Constantine V">Constantine V</a> (r. 741–775).<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Theodore had two younger brothers (<a href="/wiki/Joseph_I_of_Thessalonica" class="mw-redirect" title="Joseph I of Thessalonica">Joseph</a>, later <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Thessalonica" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Thessalonica">Archbishop of Thessalonica</a>, and Euthymios) and one sister, whose name we do not know.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It has often been assumed that Theodore's family belonged to the <a href="/wiki/Iconodule" class="mw-redirect" title="Iconodule">iconodule</a> party during the first period of <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm" title="Byzantine Iconoclasm">Byzantine Iconoclasm</a>. There is however no evidence to support this, and their high position in the imperial bureaucracy of the time renders any openly iconodule position highly unlikely. Furthermore, when Platon left his office and entered the priesthood in 759, he was ordained by an abbot who, if he was not actively iconoclastic himself, at the very least offered no resistance to the iconoclastic policies of Constantine V. The family as a whole was most likely indifferent to the question of icons during this period.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to the later hagiographical literature, Theodore received an education befitting his family's station and from the age of seven was instructed by a private tutor, eventually concentrating in particular on theology. It is however not clear that these opportunities were available to even the most well-placed Byzantine families of the eighth century, and it is possible that Theodore was at least partially an autodidact.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_monastic_career">Early monastic career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Early monastic career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Following the death of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Leo_IV_the_Khazar" title="Leo IV the Khazar">Leo IV</a> (r. 775–780) in 780, Theodore's uncle Platon, who had lived as a monk in the Symbola Monastery in <a href="/wiki/Bithynia" title="Bithynia">Bithynia</a> since 759, visited Constantinople, and persuaded the entire family of his sister, Theoktiste, to likewise take monastic vows. Theodore, together with his father and brothers, sailed back to Bithynia with Platon in 781, where they set about transforming the family estate into a religious establishment, which became known as the Sakkudion Monastery. Platon became abbot of the new foundation, and Theodore was his "right hand." The two sought to order the monastery according to the writings of <a href="/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea" title="Basil of Caesarea">Basil of Caesarea</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During the period of the regency of <a href="/wiki/Irene_(empress)" class="mw-redirect" title="Irene (empress)">Eirene</a>, Abbot Platon emerged as a supporter of the <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_Tarasios_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Patriarch Tarasios of Constantinople">Patriarch Tarasios</a>, and was a member of Tarasios's iconodule party at the <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Nicaea" title="Second Council of Nicaea">Second Council of Nicaea</a>, where the veneration of icons was declared orthodox. Shortly thereafter Tarasios himself ordained Theodore as a priest. In 794, Theodore became abbot of the Sakkudion Monastery, while Platon withdrew from the daily operation of the monastery and dedicated himself to silence.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Conflict_with_Constantine_VI">Conflict with Constantine VI</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Conflict with Constantine VI">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Also in 794, Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantine_VI" title="Constantine VI">Constantine VI</a> (r. 776–797) decided to separate from his first wife, <a href="/wiki/Maria_of_Amnia" title="Maria of Amnia">Maria of Amnia</a>, and to marry Maria's <i>kubikularia</i> (<a href="/wiki/Lady-in-waiting" title="Lady-in-waiting">Lady-in-waiting</a>), <a href="/wiki/Theodote_(empress)" title="Theodote (empress)">Theodote</a>, a cousin of Theodore the Studite.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Although the Patriarch may initially have resisted this development, as a divorce without proof of adultery on the part of the wife could be construed as illegal, he ultimately gave way. The marriage of Constantine and Theodote was celebrated in 795, although not by the patriarch, as was normal, but by a certain Joseph, a priest of Hagia Sophia.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A somewhat obscure chain of events followed (the so-called "Moechian controversy," from the Greek <i>moichos</i>, "adulterer"), in which Theodore initiated a protest against the marriage from the Sakkudion Monastery, and appears to have demanded the excommunication, not only of the priest Joseph, but also of all who had received communion from him, which, as Joseph was a priest of the imperial church, included implicitly the emperor and his court.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> This demand had no official weight, however, and Constantine appears to have attempted to make peace with Theodore and Platon (who, on account of his marriage, were now his relatives), inviting them to visit him during a sojourn at the imperial baths of Prusa in Bithynia. In the event neither appeared.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As a result, imperial troops were sent to the Sakkudion Monastery, and the community was dispersed. Theodore was flogged, and, together with ten other monks, banished to Thessaloniki, while Platon was imprisoned in Constantinople.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> The monks arrived in Thessaloniki in March 797, but did not remain for long; in August of the same year Constantine VI was blinded and overthrown, and his mother Irene, the new empress, lifted the exile.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Abbot_of_the_Studites">Abbot of the Studites</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Abbot of the Studites">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Theodore_Studite_(Menologion_of_Basil_II).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Theodore_Studite_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg/200px-Theodore_Studite_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="135" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Theodore_Studite_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg/300px-Theodore_Studite_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Theodore_Studite_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg/400px-Theodore_Studite_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="871" data-file-height="588" /></a><figcaption>The Stoudios Monastery as depicted in an 11th-century manuscript.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Following the accession of Irene, the priest Joseph was stripped of his office, and Theodoros was received in the imperial palace.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> The monks then returned to the Sakkudion Monastery, but were forced back to the capital in either 797 or 798 on account of an Arab raid on Bithynia. At this time, Irene offered Theodore the leadership of the ancient <a href="/wiki/Stoudios" class="mw-redirect" title="Stoudios">Stoudios</a> Monastery in Constantinople, which he accepted.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Theodore then set about building various workshops within the monastery to guarantee <a href="/wiki/Autarky" title="Autarky">autarky</a>, constructing a library and a <a href="/wiki/Scriptorium" title="Scriptorium">scriptorium</a>, and restoring and decorating the church. He also composed a series of poems on the duties of the various members of the community, which were likely inscribed and displayed within the monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> He furthermore composed a rule for the governance of the monastery,<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> and made the Studios community the center of an extensive congregation of dependent monasteries, including the Sakkudion. He maintained contact with these other monasteries above all through his prodigious literary output (letters as well as <a href="/wiki/Catechism" title="Catechism">catechisms</a>), which reached a quantitative peak at this time, and developed a system of messengers that was so elaborate as to resemble a private postal service.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>To this period may also date the so-called iconodule epigrams, <a href="/wiki/Iamb_(foot)" class="mw-redirect" title="Iamb (foot)">iambic</a> <a href="/wiki/Acrostic" title="Acrostic">acrostics</a> composed by Theodore that replaced the "iconoclastic epigrams" which were previously exhibited on the <a href="/wiki/Chalke" title="Chalke">Chalke</a> gate of the <a href="/wiki/Great_Palace_of_Constantinople" title="Great Palace of Constantinople">Great Palace</a>. It has been suggested that these were commissioned by Irene, as another sign of her good favor toward Theodore, although a commission under <a href="/wiki/Michael_I_Rangabe" title="Michael I Rangabe">Michael I Rangabe</a> (r. 811–813) is also possible; in any case, they were removed in 815 by <a href="/wiki/Leo_V_the_Armenian" title="Leo V the Armenian">Leo V the Armenian</a> (r. 813–820) and replaced by new "iconoclastic" verses.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 806, the Patriarch Tarasios died, and Emperor <a href="/wiki/Nikephoros_I" title="Nikephoros I">Nikephoros I</a> (r. 802–811) set about seeking his replacement.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> It appears likely that Platon at this time put forth Theodore's name,<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> but <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_Nikephoros_I_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Ecumenical Patriarch Nikephoros I of Constantinople">Nikephoros</a>, a layman who held the rank of <i><a href="/wiki/Asekretis" title="Asekretis">asekretis</a></i> in the imperial bureaucracy, was chosen instead.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> The selection of Nikephoros gave rise to an immediate protest on the part of the Studites, and in particular Theodore and Platon, who objected to the elevation of a layman to the patriarchal throne. Theodore and Platon were jailed for 24 days before the Emperor Nikephoros allowed them to return to their congregations.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Conflict_with_Nikephoros">Conflict with Nikephoros</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Conflict with Nikephoros">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Emperor Nikephoros soon requested that his new patriarch rehabilitate the priest Joseph, who had officiated at the wedding of Constantine and Theodote, possibly because Joseph had aided in the peaceful resolution of the revolt of <a href="/wiki/Bardanes_Tourkos" title="Bardanes Tourkos">Bardanes Tourkos</a>. In 806, the Patriarch Nikephoros convened a <a href="/wiki/Synod" title="Synod">synod</a> to address the case, at which Theodore was present. The Synod decided to readmit Joseph to the priesthood, a decision to which Theodore did not at the time object.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Therefore, relations between the Studite Abbot and the Patriarch appear to have been initially untroubled, an impression which is reinforced by the choice (806/807) of Theodore's brother, Joseph, as Archbishop of Thessaloniki.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> However, soon after this ordination, perhaps in 808, Theodore began to express his unwillingness to associate with the rehabilitated priest Joseph, or for that matter with anyone else who knowingly associated with him, as he held the rehabilitation to be uncanonical. As in the first dispute over the priest Joseph, the extension of this refusal beyond Joseph to those who associated with him included implicitly the patriarch and the emperor himself.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Early in 808, Theodoros offered in a series of letters to explain his position to the emperor, and furthermore to perform the customary <a href="/wiki/Proskynesis" title="Proskynesis">proskynesis</a> at his feet, which offer Nikephoros declined, instead setting off for the summer military campaign.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> In the winter of the same year, Theodore's brother Joseph visited him in Constantinople, but refused to attend the Christmas mass in Hagia Sophia, at which the emperor, the patriarch, and the priest Joseph would have been present. As a result, he was stripped of his archbishopric.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> At around the same time a small military division was dispatched to the Stoudios Monastery to arrest Theodore, Joseph, and Platon.<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> A synod was then held in January of 809, at which Theodore and his followers were anathematized as schismatic.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Theodore, Joseph, and Platon were thereafter banished to the <a href="/wiki/Princes%27_Islands" title="Princes&#39; Islands">Princes' Islands</a>: Theodore to <a href="/wiki/Heybeliada" title="Heybeliada">Chalke</a>, Joseph to <a href="/wiki/K%C4%B1nal%C4%B1ada" title="Kınalıada">Prote</a>, and Platon to <a href="/wiki/Sivriada" title="Sivriada">Oxeia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Theodore maintained an extensive literary activity in exile, writing numerous letters to correspondents including his brother, various Studite monks, influential family members, and even <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_III" title="Pope Leo III">Pope Leo III</a>. He also continued to compose catechisms for the Studite congregation, as well as a number of poems.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Rehabilitation_under_Michael_I">Rehabilitation under Michael I</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Rehabilitation under Michael I">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 811, the new emperor <a href="/wiki/Michael_I_Rangabe" title="Michael I Rangabe">Michael I Rangabe</a> called the Studites back from exile. The priest Joseph was once more defrocked, and Theodore was, at least superficially, reconciled with the Patriarch Nikephoros.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There are, however, indications that a certain rivalry between the Studite Abbot and the Patriarch persisted. In 812, Michael I resolved to persecute certain heretics in <a href="/wiki/Phrygia" title="Phrygia">Phrygia</a> and <a href="/wiki/Lycaonia" title="Lycaonia">Lycaonia</a>, namely the <a href="/wiki/Paulicianism" title="Paulicianism">Paulicians</a> and the "Athinganoi" (sometimes identified with the <a href="/wiki/Romani_people" title="Romani people">Roma</a>). Theodore and Nikephoros were called before the emperor to debate the legality of punishing heresy by death, Theodore arguing against and Nikephoros for. Theodore is said to have won the day.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The second affair concerned a peace treaty proposed by <a href="/wiki/Krum_of_Bulgaria" class="mw-redirect" title="Krum of Bulgaria">Krum of Bulgaria</a> (r. 803–814), also in 812, according to which the Byzantine and Bulgarian states should exchange refugees. It is likely that Krum sought the return of certain Bulgarians who had betrayed him to the Byzantines. In this instance Theodore argued against the exchange, as it would require that Christians be cast to barbarians, while Nikephoros urged the emperor to accept the treaty. Once more Theodore's opinion prevailed, although this time with serious consequences; Krum attacked and took <a href="/wiki/Nesebar" title="Nesebar">Mesembria</a> in November the same year.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> Michael led a military campaign against the Bulgarians in 813, which ended in defeat, and as a result he abdicated in July and <a href="/wiki/Leo_V_the_Armenian" title="Leo V the Armenian">Leo V</a> was crowned emperor.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 4 April 814 Theodore's uncle Platon died in the Stoudios Monastery after a long illness. Theodore composed a long funeral oration, the <i>Laudatio Platonis</i>, which remains one of the most important sources for the history of the family.<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Second_Iconoclasm">Second Iconoclasm</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Second Iconoclasm">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Hosios_Loukas_(nave,_arch_by_north_west_bay)_-_Theodore_Studites.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Hosios_Loukas_%28nave%2C_arch_by_north_west_bay%29_-_Theodore_Studites.jpg/220px-Hosios_Loukas_%28nave%2C_arch_by_north_west_bay%29_-_Theodore_Studites.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="499" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Hosios_Loukas_%28nave%2C_arch_by_north_west_bay%29_-_Theodore_Studites.jpg/330px-Hosios_Loukas_%28nave%2C_arch_by_north_west_bay%29_-_Theodore_Studites.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Hosios_Loukas_%28nave%2C_arch_by_north_west_bay%29_-_Theodore_Studites.jpg/440px-Hosios_Loukas_%28nave%2C_arch_by_north_west_bay%29_-_Theodore_Studites.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1156" data-file-height="2620" /></a><figcaption>Mosaic of Theodore the Studite located in <a href="/wiki/Hosios_Loukas" title="Hosios Loukas">Hosios Loukas</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<p>At the very beginning of his reign, the emperor Leo V faced a new Bulgarian offensive that reached the walls of Constantinople and ravaged large sections of <a href="/wiki/Thrace" title="Thrace">Thrace</a>. This came to an end with the death of Krum on April 13, 814, and the internal power struggles that followed.<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> However, as the previous 30 years since the approval of icon-veneration at the Synod of 787 had represented for the Byzantines a string of military catastrophes, Leo resolved to reach back to the policies of the more successful Isaurian dynasty. He renamed his son Constantine, thus drawing a parallel to <a href="/wiki/Leo_III_the_Isaurian" title="Leo III the Isaurian">Leo III</a> (r. 717–741) and <a href="/wiki/Constantine_V" title="Constantine V">Constantine V</a>, and beginning in 814 began to discuss with various clerics and senators the possibility of reviving the iconoclastic policy of the Isaurians. This movement met with strong opposition from the Patriarch Nikephoros, who himself gathered a group of bishops and abbots about him and swore them to uphold the veneration of images. The dispute came to a head in a debate between the two parties before the emperor in the Great Palace in early 815, at which Theodore and his brother Joseph were present and took the side of the iconodules. Theodore told the emperor: "Know that though an angel came from heaven itself to pervert us we would not obey him. Far less would we obey you."<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Leo held fast by his plan to revive iconoclasm, and in March 815 the Patrarch Nikephoros was stripped of his office and exiled to Bithynia. At this point Theodore remained in Constantinople, and assumed a leading role in the iconodule opposition. On March 25, Palm Sunday, he commanded his monks to process through the monastery's vineyard, holding up icons so that they could be seen over the walls by the neighbors. This provocation elicited only a rebuke from the emperor.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A new patriarch, <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_Theodotos_I_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Ecumenical Patriarch Theodotos I of Constantinople">Theodotos</a>, was selected, and in April a synod was convened in Hagia Sophia, at which iconoclasm was re-introduced as dogma. Theodore composed a series of letters in which he called on "all, near and far," to revolt against the decision of the synod. Not long thereafter he was exiled by imperial command to a Metopa, a fortress on the eastern shore of <a href="/wiki/Lake_Uluabat" title="Lake Uluabat">Lake Apollonia</a> in Bithynia.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup> Shortly thereafter Leo had Theodore's poems removed from the Chalke Gate and replaced by a new set of "iconoclastic" epigrams.<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While Theodore was in exile, the leadership of the Studite congregation was assumed by the Abbot Leontios, who for a time adopted the iconoclast position and won over many individual monks to his party.<sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> He was, however, eventually won back to the iconodule party.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup> The Studite situation mirrored a general trend, with a number of bishops and abbots at first willing to reach a compromise with the iconoclasts,<sup id="cite_ref-51" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-51">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup> but then in the years between 816 and 819 renouncing the iconoclast position, a movement that was perhaps motivated by the martyrdom of the Studite monk Thaddaios.<sup id="cite_ref-52" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-52">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup> It was during this upswell in icondule sentiment that Theodore began to compose his own polemic against the iconoclasts, the <i>Refutatio</i>, concentrating in particular on refuting the arguments and criticizing the literary merits of the new iconoclastic epigrams on the Chalke.<sup id="cite_ref-53" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-53">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Theodore exercised a wide influence during the first year of his exile, primarily through a massive letter-writing campaign. Accordingly, he was transferred in 816 to Boneta, a fortress in the more remote <a href="/wiki/Anatolic_theme" class="mw-redirect" title="Anatolic theme">Anatolic theme</a>, whence he nevertheless remained abreast of developments in the capital and maintained a regular correspondence. This continued activity led to an imperial order that Theodore be whipped, which his captors however refused to carry out.<sup id="cite_ref-54" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-54">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup> In 817, Theodore wrote two letters to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paschal_I" title="Pope Paschal I">Pope Paschal I</a>, which were co-signed by several fellow iconodule abbots, in the first requesting that he summon an anti-iconoclastic Synod; letters to the Patriarchs of <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_Christopher_I_of_Alexandria" title="Patriarch Christopher I of Alexandria">Alexandria</a> and <a href="/wiki/Orthodox_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="Orthodox Patriarch of Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>, among other "foreign" clerics, followed.<sup id="cite_ref-55" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-55">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup> As a result, the emperor ordered at least once more that Theodore be flogged, and the command was this time carried out, with the result that Theodore became quite ill.<sup id="cite_ref-56" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-56">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup> After his recovery Theodore was moved to <a href="/wiki/Smyrna" title="Smyrna">Smyrna</a>. Early in 821, however, Leo V fell victim to a grisly murder at the altar of the Church of St. Stephen in the <a href="/wiki/Great_Palace_of_Constantinople" title="Great Palace of Constantinople">imperial palace</a>; Theodore was released from exile shortly thereafter.<sup id="cite_ref-57" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-57">&#91;57&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Final_years">Final years</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Final years">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Following his release, Theodore made his way back to Constantinople, travelling through north-western Anatolia and meeting with numerous monks and abbots on the way. At the time he appears to have believed that the new emperor, <a href="/wiki/Michael_II" title="Michael II">Michael II</a> (r. 820–829), would adopt a pro-icons policy, and he expressed this hope in two letters to Michael.<sup id="cite_ref-58" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-58">&#91;58&#93;</a></sup> An imperial audience was arranged for a group of iconodule clerics, including Theodore, at which however Michael expressed his intention to "leave the church as he had found it." The abbots were to be allowed to venerate images if they so wished, as long as they remained outside of Constantinople. Theodore returned to Anatolia, in what seems to have been a sort of self-imposed exile.<sup id="cite_ref-59" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-59">&#91;59&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Theodore's activities in his final years are somewhat difficult to trace. He continued to write numerous letters supporting the use of icons, and appears to have remained an important leader of the opposition to imperial iconoclasm.<sup id="cite_ref-60" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-60">&#91;60&#93;</a></sup> He was present at a meeting of "more than a hundred" iconodule clerics in 823 or 824, which ended in an argument between the Studites and the host, one Ioannikos, which may have represented a power struggle within the movement.<sup id="cite_ref-61" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-61">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup> Theodore also spoke against the second marriage of Michael II to the nun Euphrosyne, a daughter of Constantine VI, although in a very moderate fashion, and with none of the passion or effect of the Moechian controversy.<sup id="cite_ref-62" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-62">&#91;62&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Theodore's years of exile, regular fasting, and exceptional exertions had taken their toll, and in 826 he became quite ill.<sup id="cite_ref-63" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-63">&#91;63&#93;</a></sup> In this year, he dictated his <i>Testament</i>, a form of spiritual guidance for the future abbots of the Stoudios monastery, to his disciple Naukratios.<sup id="cite_ref-64" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-64">&#91;64&#93;</a></sup> He died on 11 November that year, while celebrating mass, apparently in the monastery of Hagios Tryphon on Cape Akritas in Bithynia. Eighteen years later, his remains, along with those of his brother Joseph, were brought back to the Stoudios Monastery, where they were interred beside the grave of their uncle Platon.<sup id="cite_ref-65" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-65">&#91;65&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Theodore's revival of the Stoudios monastery had a major effect on the later history of <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Christian_Monasticism" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Christian Monasticism">Byzantine monasticism</a>. His disciple, Naukratios, recovered control of the monastery after the end of iconoclasm in 842, and throughout the remainder of the ninth century the Studite abbots continued Theodore's tradition of opposition to patriarchal and imperial authority.<sup id="cite_ref-66" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-66">&#91;66&#93;</a></sup> Elements of Theodore's <i>Testament</i> were incorporated verbatim in the <a href="/wiki/Typikon" title="Typikon">typika</a> of certain early <a href="/wiki/Mount_Athos" title="Mount Athos">Athonite</a> monasteries.<sup id="cite_ref-67" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-67">&#91;67&#93;</a></sup> The most important elements of his reform were its emphases on <a href="/wiki/Cenobitic" class="mw-redirect" title="Cenobitic">cenobitic</a> (communal) life, manual labor, and a carefully defined administrative hierarchy.<sup id="cite_ref-68" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-68">&#91;68&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Theodore also built the Stoudios monastery into a major scholarly center, in particular through its library and scriptorium, which certainly surpassed all other contemporary Byzantine ecclesiastical institutions in this regard.<sup id="cite_ref-Pratsch,_Theodoros,_306_69-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Pratsch,_Theodoros,_306-69">&#91;69&#93;</a></sup> Theodore himself was a pivotal figure in the revival of classical literary forms, in particular iambic verse, in Byzantium, and his criticisms of the iconoclastic epigrams drew a connection between literary skill and orthodox faith.<sup id="cite_ref-70" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-70">&#91;70&#93;</a></sup> After his death the Stoudios monastery continued to be a vital center for Byzantine hymnography and hagiography, as well as for the copying of manuscripts.<sup id="cite_ref-Pratsch,_Theodoros,_306_69-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Pratsch,_Theodoros,_306-69">&#91;69&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Following the "triumph of Orthodoxy" (i.e. the reintroduction of icons) in 843, Theodore became one of the great heroes of the iconodule opposition. There was no formal process of <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a> in Byzantium, but Theodore was soon recognized as a saint. In the Latin West, Theodore’s recognition of <a href="/wiki/Primacy_of_the_Roman_Pontiff" class="mw-redirect" title="Primacy of the Roman Pontiff">papal primacy</a> on the basis of his letters to Pope <a href="/wiki/Paschal_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Paschal I">Paschal I</a> was part of what caused him to be formally canonized by the Catholic Church. His <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">feast day</a> is 11 November in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Christianity" title="Eastern Christianity">East</a> and 12 November in the <a href="/wiki/Western_Christianity" title="Western Christianity">West</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-71" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-71">&#91;71&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Commentary_on_Theodore">Commentary on Theodore</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Theodore_the_Studite&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Commentary on Theodore">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>As also mentioned by <a href="/wiki/Kirby_Page" title="Kirby Page">Kirby Page</a> in <i>Jesus or Christianity</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPage1929_72-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPage1929-72">&#91;72&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Charles_Loring_Brace" title="Charles Loring Brace">Charles Loring Brace</a> tells us in <i>Gesta Christi</i> that it was not until the 9th century that the first recorded stand against slavery itself was taken by Theodore:<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrace188842_73-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrace188842-73">&#91;73&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<blockquote>
<p>No direct word against slavery, however, came forth from the great Teacher [Jesus Christ]. It was not until the ninth century after, that one of his humble followers, Saint Theodore of Studium (Constantinople), ventured to put forth the command "Thou shalt possess no slave, neither for domestic service nor for the labor of the fields, for man is made in the image of God."
</p>
</blockquote>
