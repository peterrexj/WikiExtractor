<p><b>Philip Romolo Neri</b>, Cong. Orat. (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;n&#39; in &#39;nigh&#39;">n</span><span title="/ɪər/: &#39;ear&#39; in &#39;near&#39;">ɪər</span><span title="/i/: &#39;y&#39; in &#39;happy&#39;">i</span></span>/</a></span></span> <a href="/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key" title="Help:Pronunciation respelling key"><i title="English pronunciation respelling"><span style="font-size:90%">NEER</span>-ee</i></a>; <a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <span lang="it" style="font-style: normal;"><b>Filippo Romolo Neri</b></span>, <small>pronounced&#160;</small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="it-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Italian" title="Help:IPA/Italian">[fiˈlippo ˈrɔːmolo ˈneːri]</a></span>; 22 July 1515&#160;&#8211;&#32;26 May 1595), known as the "Second Apostle of Rome", after <a href="/wiki/Saint_Peter" title="Saint Peter">Saint Peter</a>, was an Italian priest noted for founding a society of <a href="/wiki/Secular_clergy" title="Secular clergy">secular clergy</a> called the <a href="/wiki/Congregation_of_the_Oratory" class="mw-redirect" title="Congregation of the Oratory">Congregation of the Oratory</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Philip was the son of Francesco di Neri, a lawyer, and his wife Lucrezia da Mosciano, whose family were <a href="/wiki/Nobility_of_Italy" title="Nobility of Italy">nobility</a> in the service of the state. He was carefully brought up, and received his early teaching from the friars at <a href="/wiki/San_Marco,_Florence" title="San Marco, Florence">San Marco</a>, the famous <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican</a> monastery in <a href="/wiki/Florence" title="Florence">Florence</a>. He was accustomed in later life to ascribing most of his progress to the teaching of two of them, Zenobio de' Medici and Servanzio Mini. At the age of 18, in 1533, Philip was sent to his uncle, Romolo, a wealthy merchant at San Germano (now <a href="/wiki/Cassino" title="Cassino">Cassino</a>), a then Neapolitan town near the base of <a href="/wiki/Monte_Cassino" title="Monte Cassino">Monte Cassino</a>, to assist him in his business, and with the hope that he might inherit his uncle's fortune.<sup id="cite_ref-Crawley_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Crawley-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He gained Romolo's confidence and affection, but soon after coming to San Germano, Philip had a religious conversion. From then onward, he no longer cared for things of the world and decided in 1533 to live in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>. <sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Founding_of_the_Oratory">Founding of the Oratory</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Founding of the Oratory">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Mission_work">Mission work</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Mission work">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>After arriving in Rome, Philip became a tutor in the house of a Florentine aristocrat named Galeotto Caccia. After two years he began to pursue his studies (for three years) under the guidance of the <a href="/wiki/Augustinians" title="Augustinians">Augustinians</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Crawley_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Crawley-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Following this, he began those labors amongst the sick and poor which, in later life, gained him the title of "Apostle of Rome". He also ministered to the prostitutes of the city. In 1538 he entered into the home mission work for which he became famous, traveling throughout the city, seeking opportunities of entering into conversation with people, and of leading them to consider the topics he set before them.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> For seventeen years Philip lived as a layman in Rome, probably without thinking of becoming a priest. Around 1544, he made the acquaintance of <a href="/wiki/Ignatius_of_Loyola" title="Ignatius of Loyola">Ignatius of Loyola</a>. Many of Philip's disciples found their vocations in the infant <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">Society of Jesus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ritchie_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ritchie-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Confraternity_of_the_Holy_Trinity">Confraternity of the Holy Trinity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Confraternity of the Holy Trinity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 1548, together with his <a href="/wiki/Confessor" title="Confessor">confessor</a>, Persiano Rossa, Philip founded the Confraternity of the Most Holy Trinity of Pilgrims and Convalescents (<a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Santissima Trinita de' Pellegrini e de' Convalescenti</i>),<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWalsh1991157_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWalsh1991157-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> whose primary object was to minister to the needs of the thousands of poor <a href="/wiki/Christian_pilgrimage" title="Christian pilgrimage">pilgrims</a> who flocked to Rome, especially in <a href="/wiki/Jubilee_(Christianity)" class="mw-redirect" title="Jubilee (Christianity)">jubilee years</a>, and also to relieve the patients discharged from hospitals but who were still too weak for labor.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Members met for prayer at the Church of San Salvatore in Campo<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> where the devotion of the <a href="/wiki/Forty_Hours_of_Exposition_of_the_Blessed_Sacrament" class="mw-redirect" title="Forty Hours of Exposition of the Blessed Sacrament">Forty Hours of Exposition of the Blessed Sacrament</a> was first introduced into Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-addington_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-addington-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Oratory">The Oratory</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: The Oratory">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 1551, Philip received all the <a href="/wiki/Minor_orders" title="Minor orders">minor orders</a>, and was ordained deacon and finally priest (on 23 May). He thought of going to India as a <a href="/wiki/Missionary" title="Missionary">missionary</a> but was dissuaded by his friends who saw that there was abundant work to be done in Rome. Accordingly, he settled down, with some companions, at the Hospital of San Girolamo della Carità, and while there tentatively began, in 1556, the institute with which his name is more especially connected, that of the Oratory. The scheme at first was no more than a series of evening meetings in a hall (the Oratory), at which there were prayers, hymns, and readings from Scripture, the church fathers, and the <a href="/wiki/Martyrology" title="Martyrology">Martyrology</a>, followed by a lect of some religious question proposed for consideration. The musical selections (settings of scenes from sacred history) were called <i><a href="/wiki/Oratorio" title="Oratorio">oratorios</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Giovanni_Palestrina" class="mw-redirect" title="Giovanni Palestrina">Giovanni Palestrina</a> was one of Philip's followers and composed music for the services.<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The program developed, and the members of the society undertook various kinds of mission work throughout Rome, notably the preaching of sermons in different churches every evening, a completely new idea at that time.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He also spent much of his time hearing confessions and effected many conversions in this way.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWalsh1991157_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWalsh1991157-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1564 the Florentines requested that Philip leave San Girolamo to oversee their newly built church in Rome, <a href="/wiki/San_Giovanni_dei_Fiorentini" title="San Giovanni dei Fiorentini">San Giovanni dei Fiorentini</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ritchie_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ritchie-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was at first reluctant, but by consent of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_IV" title="Pope Pius IV">Pope Pius IV</a> he accepted while remaining in charge of San Girolamo where the exercises of the Oratory were kept up. At this time the new society included among its members <a href="/wiki/Caesar_Baronius" title="Caesar Baronius">Caesar Baronius</a> (the ecclesiastical historian), Francesco Maria Tarugi (afterward <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Avignon" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Avignon">Archbishop of Avignon</a>), and Ottavio Paravicini – all of whom later became cardinals – and also Gallonius (Antonio Galloni, author of a well-known work on the <i>Sufferings of the Martyrs</i>), <a href="/wiki/Giovenale_Ancina" class="mw-redirect" title="Giovenale Ancina">Ancina</a>, Bordoni, and other men of ability and distinction. In 1574, the Florentines built a large oratory or mission room for the society, next to San Giovanni – to save them the fatigue of the daily journey to and from San Girolamo, and to provide a more convenient place of assembly – and the headquarters were transferred there.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As the community grew, and its mission work extended, the need for a church entirely its own made itself felt, and the small parish church of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_in_Vallicella" title="Santa Maria in Vallicella">Santa Maria in Vallicella</a>, conveniently situated in the middle of Rome, was offered and accepted. The building, however, not large enough for their purpose, was pulled down, and a splendid church was erected on the site. It was immediately after taking possession of their new quarters that Philip formally organized, under permission of a <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">papal bull</a> dated 15 July 1575, a community of secular priests, called the Congregation of the Oratory. The new church was consecrated early in 1577, and the clergy of the new society at once resigned the charge of San Giovanni dei Fiorentini; Philip himself did not leave San Girolamo until 1583, and then only by an injunction of the pope that he, as the superior, should reside at the chief house of his congregation. He was at first elected for a term of three years (as was common in modern societies) but in 1587 was nominated superior for life. He was, however, entirely free from personal ambition, and had no desire to be <a href="/wiki/Superior_general" class="mw-redirect" title="Superior general">superior general</a> over several dependent houses, so he desired that all congregations formed on his model outside Rome should be autonomous, governing themselves, and with no provision for Philip to retain control over any new foundation they might themselves make elsewhere – a regulation afterward formally confirmed by a brief of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_XV" title="Pope Gregory XV">Gregory XV</a> in 1622.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Political_activity">Political activity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Political activity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Guercino_San_Filippo_Neri._San_Marino.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/Guercino_San_Filippo_Neri._San_Marino.JPG/200px-Guercino_San_Filippo_Neri._San_Marino.JPG" decoding="async" width="200" height="260" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/Guercino_San_Filippo_Neri._San_Marino.JPG/300px-Guercino_San_Filippo_Neri._San_Marino.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/Guercino_San_Filippo_Neri._San_Marino.JPG/400px-Guercino_San_Filippo_Neri._San_Marino.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1758" data-file-height="2284" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Guercino_San_Filippo_Neri._San_Marino.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Philip Neri, as painted by <a href="/wiki/Guercino" title="Guercino">Guercino</a> in 1656</div></div></div>
<p>Although Philip refrained from becoming involved in political matters, he broke this rule in 1593–1595 when he persuaded Pope <a href="/wiki/Clement_VIII" class="mw-redirect" title="Clement VIII">Clement VIII</a> to revoke the <a href="/wiki/Ex-communication" class="mw-redirect" title="Ex-communication">ex-communication</a> and <a href="/wiki/Anathema" title="Anathema">anathema</a> pronounced against <a href="/wiki/Henry_IV_of_France" title="Henry IV of France">Henry IV of France</a><sup id="cite_ref-Crawley_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Crawley-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> and the refusal to receive his ambassador, even though the king had formally renounced <a href="/wiki/Calvinism" title="Calvinism">Calvinism</a>. Philip saw that the pope's attitude was more than likely to drive Henry to relapse and rekindle the civil war in France, and directed the future <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_(Catholicism)" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinal (Catholicism)">Cardinal</a> <a href="/wiki/Caesar_Baronius" title="Caesar Baronius">Caesar Baronius</a>, a member of the Oratory who was then the pope's confessor, to refuse the pope <a href="/wiki/Absolution" title="Absolution">absolution</a> and to resign his office of confessor unless the pope withdrew the anathema. Clement yielded at once, though the whole college of cardinals had supported his policy; and Henry, who did not learn the facts until several years afterward, testified lively gratitude for the timely and political intervention. Philip continued in the government of the Oratory until his death. He was succeeded by Baronius.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389–390_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911389–390-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Personal_character">Personal character</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Personal character">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Philip Neri embodied several contradictions, combining popular veneration with intensely individual piety. He became deeply involved with the Church while seeking to reform a corrupt Rome and an indifferent clergy.<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Philip possessed a playful sense of humor, combined with a shrewd wit. He considered a cheerful temper to be more Christian than a melancholy one, and carried this spirit into his whole life: "A joyful heart is more easily made perfect than a downcast one." This was the secret of Neri's popularity and his place in the folklore of the Roman poor. Many <a href="/wiki/Miracles" class="mw-redirect" title="Miracles">miracles</a> were attributed to him. When his body was examined after death, it was found that two of his ribs had been broken, which was attributed at the time to the expansion of his heart while fervently praying in the catacombs about the year 1545.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Ritchie_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ritchie-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Benedict_XIV" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedict XIV">Benedict XIV</a>, who reorganized the rules for <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a>, decided that Philip's enlarged heart was caused by an <a href="/wiki/Aneurism" class="mw-redirect" title="Aneurism">aneurism</a>. Ponnelle and Bordet, in their 1932 biography <i>St. Philip Neri and the Roman Society of His Times (1515–1595)</i>, conclude that it was partly natural and partly supernatural. What is certain is that Philip himself and his penitents associated it with divine love.<sup id="cite_ref-addington_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-addington-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>"Practical commonplaceness," says <a href="/wiki/Frederick_William_Faber" title="Frederick William Faber">Frederick William Faber</a> in his <a href="/wiki/Panegyric" title="Panegyric">panegyric</a> on Philip, "was the special mark that distinguishes his form of ascetic piety from the types accredited before his day. He looked like other men. ...He was emphatically a modern gentleman, of scrupulous courtesy, and sportive gaiety, acquainted with what was going on in the world, taking a real interest in it, giving and getting information, very neatly dressed, with a shrewd common sense always alive about him, in a modern room with modern furniture, plain, it is true, but with no marks of poverty about it – in a word, with all the ease, the gracefulness, the polish of a modern gentleman of good birth, considerable accomplishments, and widespread knowledge."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Accordingly, Philip was ready to meet the needs of his day to an extent and in a manner which even the versatile <a href="/wiki/Jesuits" title="Jesuits">Jesuits</a>, who much desired to enlist him in their company, did not rival; and, though an Italian priest and head of a new religious order, his genius was entirely unmonastic and unmedieval – frequent and popular preaching, unconventional prayer, and unsystematized, albeit fervent, private devotion.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Philip prayed, "Let me get through today, and I shall not fear tomorrow."<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Philip had no difficulties in respect of the teaching of his Church. His great merit was the instinctive tact that showed him that the system of monasticism could never be the leaven of secular life in the world of his day, but that something more homely, simple, and every day in character was needed for the new times than emerging.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_veneration">Death and veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Death and veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:S_Filipo_Neri_chapel.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/S_Filipo_Neri_chapel.JPG/200px-S_Filipo_Neri_chapel.JPG" decoding="async" width="200" height="150" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/S_Filipo_Neri_chapel.JPG/300px-S_Filipo_Neri_chapel.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/S_Filipo_Neri_chapel.JPG/400px-S_Filipo_Neri_chapel.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1600" data-file-height="1200" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:S_Filipo_Neri_chapel.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Philip Neri's <a href="/wiki/Effigy" title="Effigy">effigy</a> at his tomb</div></div></div>
<p>Philip Neri died around the end of the day on 25 May 1595, the Feast of <a href="/wiki/Corpus_Christi_(feast)" class="mw-redirect" title="Corpus Christi (feast)">Corpus Christi</a> that year, after having spent the day hearing confessions and receiving visitors.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWalsh1991157–158_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWalsh1991157–158-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> About midnight he began hemorrhaging, and Baronius read the commendatory prayers over him. Baronius asked that he bless his spiritual sons before dying and, though he could no longer speak, he blessed them with the sign of the cross and died.
</p><p>Philip Neri was beatified by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_V" title="Pope Paul V">Paul V</a> in 1615 and <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_XV" title="Pope Gregory XV">Pope Gregory XV</a> in 1622.<sup id="cite_ref-addington_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-addington-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> His <a href="/wiki/Memorial_(liturgy)" title="Memorial (liturgy)">memorial</a> is celebrated on 26 May. His body is venerated in the <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_in_Vallicella" title="Santa Maria in Vallicella">Chiesa Nuova</a> ("New Church") in Rome.
</p><p>Philip Neri is one of the influential figures of the Counter-Reformation and is noted for converting to personal holiness many of the influential people within the Church itself.<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Philip is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(observance)" class="mw-redirect" title="Commemoration (observance)">commemoration</a> on <a href="/wiki/May_26" title="May 26">26 May</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Oratory_2">The Oratory</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: The Oratory">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Oratory_of_Saint_Philip_Neri" title="Oratory of Saint Philip Neri">Oratory of Saint Philip Neri</a></div>
<p>The congregation Philip Neri founded is of an original stamp, little resembling a monastery of the older type, and its rules (not drawn up by Philip Neri, but approved by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_V" title="Pope Paul V">Pope Paul V</a> in 1612)<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEIngold1911_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEIngold1911-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> leave considerable freedom of action compared with traditional religious foundations.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390_9-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911390-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:132px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Filipe_de_Nery.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Filipe_de_Nery.JPG/130px-Filipe_de_Nery.JPG" decoding="async" width="130" height="229" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Filipe_de_Nery.JPG/195px-Filipe_de_Nery.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Filipe_de_Nery.JPG/260px-Filipe_de_Nery.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1960" data-file-height="3448" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Filipe_de_Nery.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Statue of Philip Neri in <a href="/wiki/Congregados_Basilica" title="Congregados Basilica">Congregados Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Braga" title="Braga">Braga</a>, Portugal</div></div></div>
<p>The Congregation of the Oratory of Saint Philip Neri is a pontifical society of apostolic life of Catholic priests and lay brothers who live together in a community bound together but without formal vows. They are commonly referred to as Oratorians (Oratorian Fathers). Oratorians commit themselves to membership in a particular, independent, self-governing local community (an Oratory, usually named for the place in which it is located). In some locations, the local Oratory has been designated to administer a particular parish; others may be tasked with campus ministry.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_French_Oratory">The French Oratory</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: The French Oratory">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Oratory_of_Jesus" title="Oratory of Jesus">Oratory of Jesus</a></div>
<p>The Oratory movement spread in the early period, especially in Italy. In France, a separate and distinct foundation from the <a href="/wiki/Oratory_of_Saint_Philip_Neri" title="Oratory of Saint Philip Neri">Oratory of Saint Philip Neri</a> was founded, though inspired by St Philip's model. Best known as the <b>French Oratory</b>, it was founded in 1611 in <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a>, France, by <a href="/wiki/Pierre_de_B%C3%A9rulle" title="Pierre de Bérulle">Pierre de Bérulle</a> (1575–1629), later a <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_(Catholic_Church)" title="Cardinal (Catholic Church)">cardinal</a> of the Catholic Church.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>  The French Oratory had a determinant influence on the <a href="/wiki/French_school_of_spirituality" title="French school of spirituality">French school of spirituality</a> throughout the 17th century. Unlike St Philip's Oratory, it operates under the central authority of a <a href="/wiki/Superior_General" class="mw-redirect" title="Superior General">Superior General</a>. Early members included <a href="/wiki/Nicolas_Malebranche" title="Nicolas Malebranche">Nicolas Malebranche</a>, <a href="/wiki/Louis_Thomassin" title="Louis Thomassin">Louis Thomassin</a>, <a href="/wiki/Jules_Mascaron" title="Jules Mascaron">Jules Mascaron</a> and <a href="/wiki/Jean_Baptiste_Massillon" title="Jean Baptiste Massillon">Jean Baptiste Massillon</a>. Suppressed at the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>, it was revived by Père Pététot, curé of St Roch, in 1852, as the "Oratory of Jesus and Mary Immaculate".
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Music">Music</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Music">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Philip Neri encouraged the singing of the <i>Lauda spiritual</i> (<a href="/wiki/Laude" class="mw-redirect" title="Laude">laude</a>) in his oratory services. The prominent composers <a href="/wiki/Tom%C3%A1s_Luis_de_Victoria" title="Tomás Luis de Victoria">Tomás Luis de Victoria</a> and <a href="/wiki/Giovanni_Pierluigi_da_Palestrina" title="Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina">Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina</a> probably participated in this music.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESmither_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmither-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> The result of Philip's approach was undoubtedly a unique and varied aesthetic experience. <sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDanieli2009_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDanieli2009-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Seven_Churches_Walk">Seven Churches Walk</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Philip_Neri&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Seven Churches Walk">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Philip sometimes led "excursions" to other churches, often with music and a picnic on the way.<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_7-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> In 1553, Neri started the tradition of making a one-day <a href="/wiki/Christian_pilgrimage" title="Christian pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a> to seven churches, starting from <a href="/wiki/St._Peter%27s_Basilica" title="St. Peter&#39;s Basilica">St. Peter's Basilica</a> and ending at the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_di_Santa_Maria_Maggiore" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica di Santa Maria Maggiore">Basilica di Santa Maria Maggiore</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The tradition of visiting all seven churches was started by Neri<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> to combine conviviality and the sharing of a common religious experience through discovering the heritage of the early saints. Neri drew up an itinerary that included visits to St. Peter's Basilica, then St. Paul Outside-the-Walls, St. Sebastian's, St. John Lateran, Holy Cross-in-Jerusalem, St. Lawrence-Outside-the Walls, and finally St. Mary Major. He and a few friends and acquaintances would gather before dawn and set out on their walk. At each church, there would be prayer, hymn singing, and a brief sermon by Neri.<sup id="cite_ref-picnic_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-picnic-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A simple meal was pre-arranged at the gardens of the <a href="/wiki/Villa_Celimontana" title="Villa Celimontana">Villa Mattei</a>. The Mattei family opened their grounds for pilgrims to rest and provided them with bread, wine, cheese, eggs, apples, and salami. During these "picnics", musicians would play and singers would perform. 
</p><p>The street which links <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint_Paul_Outside_the_Walls" title="Basilica of Saint Paul Outside the Walls">Basilica of Saint Paul Outside the Walls</a> with <a href="/w/index.php?title=San_Sebastiano_Fuori_le_mura&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="San Sebastiano Fuori le mura (page does not exist)">San Sebastiano Fuori le mura</a> is still called "Via Delle Sette Chiese" (Seven Churches Walk). These <a href="/wiki/Christian_pilgrimage" title="Christian pilgrimage">pilgrimages</a> were designed to be a counterpoint to the raucous behavior of Carnival.<sup id="cite_ref-picnic_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-picnic-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> The Walks became very popular and began to attract others. From this developed the custom of visiting seven churches on <a href="/wiki/Maundy_Thursday" title="Maundy Thursday">Maundy Thursday</a>. In Rome, the Seven Church Walk is traditionally done on Wednesday of Holy Week.
</p>
