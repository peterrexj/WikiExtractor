<p><b>Gertrude of Nivelles</b>, OSB (also spelled <i>Geretrude</i>, <i>Geretrudis</i>, <i>Gertrud</i>; c. 628<sup id="cite_ref-Butler_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Butler-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> – 17 March 659) was a seventh-century <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> who, with her mother <a href="/wiki/Itta_of_Metz" title="Itta of Metz">Itta</a>, founded the <a href="/wiki/Nivelles_Abbey" title="Nivelles Abbey">Abbey of Nivelles</a>, now in Belgium.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Family_and_childhood">Family and childhood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Family and childhood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The early history of Gertrude's family is not well documented. The anonymous author of her <a href="/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages" title="Early Middle Ages">Early Middle Ages</a> biography, <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Vita_Sanctae_Geretrudis&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Vita Sanctae Geretrudis (page does not exist)">Vita Sanctae Geretrudis</a></i>, only hints at her origins: "it would be tedious to insert in this account in what line of earthly origin she was descended. For who living in Europe does not know the loftiness, the names, and the localities of her lineage?"<sup id="cite_ref-vita_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vita-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
Gertrude's father, <a href="/wiki/Pepin_of_Landen" title="Pepin of Landen">Pepin of Landen</a> (Pippin the Elder), a nobleman from east <a href="/wiki/Francia" title="Francia">Francia</a>, had been instrumental in persuading King <a href="/wiki/Clothar_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Clothar II">Clothar II</a> to crown his son, <a href="/wiki/Dagobert_I" title="Dagobert I">Dagobert I</a>, as the King of <a href="/wiki/Austrasia" title="Austrasia">Austrasia</a>. Due to her position at the palace, Gertrude's mother, <a href="/wiki/Itta_of_Metz" title="Itta of Metz">Itta of Metz</a>, was likely acquainted with <a href="/wiki/Amandus" title="Amandus">Amandus</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Maastricht" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Maastricht">Bishop of Maastricht</a>.
</p><p>When Dagobert succeeded his father and the court moved to <a href="/wiki/Neustria" title="Neustria">Neustria</a>, Pippin and his family (including young Gertrude) moved with the king's court.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Thus, Gertrude became introduced to politics during her childhood in the royal court. <a href="/wiki/Arnulf_of_Metz" title="Arnulf of Metz">Arnulf of Metz</a>, Pippin's close ally, was one of several royal counselors who received ecclesiastical posts after a secular career. McNamara argues that Arnulf retired into religion at the time of Clothar's death in 628, but he kept close ties to the family by marrying his son to Gertrude's sister, <a href="/wiki/Begga" title="Begga">Begga</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> However, later scholars have disagreed.
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Marriage_proposal">Marriage proposal</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Marriage proposal">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:GetrudNivelles.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e4/GetrudNivelles.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="526" class="thumbimage" data-file-width="200" data-file-height="526" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:GetrudNivelles.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i> St. Gertrude of Nivelles</i> by an anonymous Flemish painter (ca. 1530)</div></div></div>
<p>Gertrude's biography begins with her father hosting a banquet when Gertrude was ten years old.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> That the king accepted Pippin's invitation to the dinner at all shows Pippin's standing as well as that of his family.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> At this feast, the King asked Gertrude if she would like to marry the "son of a duke of the Austrasians.... for the sake of his worldly ambition and mutual alliance."<sup id="cite_ref-vita_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vita-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Gertrude declined and "lost her temper and flatly rejected him with an oath, saying that she would have neither him nor any earthly spouse but Christ the Lord."<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara223_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara223-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Marriage alliances were important in this era although scholars disagree as to the extent parents or kings asserted power over spousal choice.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> The marriage between Gertrude's sister Begga and <a href="/wiki/Ansegisel" title="Ansegisel">Ansegisel</a> helped set the stage for a Carolingian takeover of Austrasia.<sup id="cite_ref-wemple54_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-wemple54-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> The marriage of their son <a href="/wiki/Pepin_the_Middle" class="mw-redirect" title="Pepin the Middle">Pepin the Middle</a> and <a href="/wiki/Plectrude" title="Plectrude">Plectrude</a> later secured the lands of Plectrude's parents <a href="/wiki/Hugobert" title="Hugobert">Hugobert</a> and <a href="/wiki/Irmina_of_Oeren" title="Irmina of Oeren">Irmina of Oeren</a> between the Rhine, Moselle and Meuse rivers, because Plectrude was an only child. Begga's sons enhanced Pepin's power by marrying women with political connections in the north and northwest.<sup id="cite_ref-wemple54_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-wemple54-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> All agree that the girl's personal feeling mattered little. One scholar speculated that if Pippin I had lived longer, he would likely have forced Gertrude to marry the son of the Austrasian duke, thus giving power to the <a href="/wiki/Pippinids" title="Pippinids">Pippinids</a> sooner to supplant the <a href="/wiki/Merovingians" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingians">Merovingians</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-wemple54_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-wemple54-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Dagobert died in 639 and was succeeded in Neutria by <a href="/wiki/Clovis_II" title="Clovis II">Clovis II</a> and in Austrasia by <a href="/wiki/Sigebert_III" title="Sigebert III">Sigebert III</a>. When Pippin died, Gertrude's brother <a href="/wiki/Grimoald_the_Elder" title="Grimoald the Elder">Grimoald</a> competed with Otto to become the new mayor of the palace.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> After Otto died in battle, "the dignity of mayor of Sigebert's palace and control of all the kingdom of Austrasia was thus decisively assured to Grimoald" and the Pippinids.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The mention of Gertrude's decided rejection of her Austrasian suitor is unique for the era. At least one scholar considers it to have been deliberately included by the chronicler as expressing her character.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> The reference to a prior betrothal to Christ becomes common in later saints' lives. The suitor, while irritated, is not emotionally affected by this rejection.
</p><p>After Dagobert's death, Pippin returned to the east in 640, taking Gertrude with him. Soon after, Pippin himself died, giving Gertrude the freedom to take the veil and enter the monastic life.<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara223_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara223-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Scholars debate the date of the death of Pippin. Some sources date it as late as 650,<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> although others date it much earlier.
</p>
<h4><span id="After_her_father.27s_death"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="After_her_father's_death">After her father's death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: After her father&#039;s death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The <i>Vita</i> describes how Itta, in order to prevent "violent abductors from tearing her daughter away by force," shaved her daughter's hair, leaving only a crown shape.<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara224_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara224-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> This action, known as <a href="/wiki/Tonsuring" class="mw-redirect" title="Tonsuring">tonsuring</a>, marked Gertrude for a life of religious service. There were constant requests by "violators of souls" who wished to gain wealth and power by marrying Gertrude. As detailed in the <i>Vita</i>, only Itta's foundation of the Abbey of Nivelles stopped the constant flow of suitors interested in marrying Gertrude in order to ally with her wealthy family.<sup id="cite_ref-peyroux52_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-peyroux52-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Susan Wemple argues that Gertrude's story is an example of mothers dominating their daughters in Merovingian times in an effort to "safeguard [their] daughters' sexual purity and secure [their] future."<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Mothers, she says, were required to raise their daughters to be obedient and disciplined, and the standard "maternal feelings" were "vigilance and worry"<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> The biographer of Gertrude mentions that after the death of Pippin the Elder in 640, his widow Itta pondered daily on what was to become of her and her daughter. Upon the advice of Amand, she ordered the construction of a monastery to which she and Gertrude could retire.<sup id="cite_ref-ott_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ott-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Wemple, 
</p>
<blockquote><p>"A mother's importance was acknowledged in law insofar as she had the right to assume the guardianship for her fatherless children. In the propertied classes, this meant that a widow could exercise considerable power by managing the estates of her minor children and arranging for their marriages."<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Itta lost this right after the death of her husband Pippin because their sons had come of age. She still had the option to find a suitable husband for Gertrude. Catherine Peyroux has said that Itta established the monastery in order to protect her and her daughter in the event that her sons fell out of favor with the ruling dynasty, as well as to safeguard the family lands from plunder or seizure through forced marriage.<sup id="cite_ref-peyroux52_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-peyroux52-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Foundress">Foundress</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Foundress">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:0_Nivelles_-_coll%C3%A9giale_Ste-Gertrude.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fb/0_Nivelles_-_coll%C3%A9giale_Ste-Gertrude.JPG/220px-0_Nivelles_-_coll%C3%A9giale_Ste-Gertrude.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fb/0_Nivelles_-_coll%C3%A9giale_Ste-Gertrude.JPG/330px-0_Nivelles_-_coll%C3%A9giale_Ste-Gertrude.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fb/0_Nivelles_-_coll%C3%A9giale_Ste-Gertrude.JPG/440px-0_Nivelles_-_coll%C3%A9giale_Ste-Gertrude.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1704" data-file-height="2272" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:0_Nivelles_-_coll%C3%A9giale_Ste-Gertrude.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Nivelles (Belgium), fore-part and southern door of the Saint Gertrude Collegiate church (eleventh or thirteenth century)</div></div></div>
<p>Christianity was not at all widespread in Gertrude's place and time. It was only the development of cities and the initiative of bishops that led to a vast movement of evangelism, and a flowering of monasteries in the 7th and 8th centuries.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gertrude's <i>Vita</i> describes how Bishop <a href="/wiki/Amand" class="mw-redirect" title="Amand">Amand</a> came to Itta's house, "preaching the word of God. At the Lord's bidding, he asked whether she would build a monastery for herself and Christ's handmaid, Gertrude."<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara224_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara224-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Itta founded Nivelles, a double monastery, one for men, the other for women. However, after they entered the religious life, Gertrude and her mother suffered, "no small opposition" from the royal family. During this period, trials for the family are mentioned involving the usurper Otto's bid to replace the Pippinids at the side of the king.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There is some precedent for Gertrude and Itta's move to the monastery at Nivelles. According to Wemple, "during the second half of the seventh century, women in Neustrian-Burgundian families concentrated on the creation of a network of monasteries rather than on the conclusion of politically advantageous unions, while families whose holdings were in the northeastern parts of the kingdom, centering around the city of Metz, were more concerned with the acquisition of power through carefully arranged marriages." Itta's move to start a monastery was thus not completely out of the ordinary, and may have in fact been the norm for a widowed noblewoman.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Upon Itta's death at about the age of 60 in the year 652, twelve years after the death of her husband Pippin,<sup id="cite_ref-vita_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vita-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Gertrude took over the monastery. At this time, Gertrude took the "whole burden of governing upon herself alone," placing affairs of the family in the hand of "good and faithful administrators from the brothers." Some have argued that this implies that Gertrude ruled the monastery with an abbot. Frankish double monasteries were almost always led by an abbess, or jointly by an abbess and abbot.<sup id="cite_ref-wemple162_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-wemple162-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> However, when Suzanne Wemple used Nivelles as an example of the latter, claiming that Gertrude ruled Nivelles jointly with Amand "around 640,"<sup id="cite_ref-wemple162_23-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-wemple162-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> she casts doubt on her own theory by mistaking the date. Many later scholars date the foundation of Nivelles between 647 and 650.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastic_life">Monastic life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Monastic life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Console_Oudegracht_321.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Console_Oudegracht_321.jpg/220px-Console_Oudegracht_321.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Console_Oudegracht_321.jpg/330px-Console_Oudegracht_321.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Console_Oudegracht_321.jpg/440px-Console_Oudegracht_321.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4000" data-file-height="3000" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Console_Oudegracht_321.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Console Oudegracht 321</div></div></div>
<p>The <i>Vita</i> states that in Gertrude, "temperance of character, the sobriety of her heart and the moderation of her words she anticipated maturity." She was "an intelligent young woman, scholarly and charitable, devoting herself to the sick, elderly, and poor," and as knowing much of the scripture by memory. Gertrude also memorized passages and books on divine law, and she "openly disclosed the hidden mysteries of allegory to her listeners." Her <i>Vita</i> describes Gertrude as building churches, and taking care of orphans, widows, captives, and pilgrims.
</p><p>Upon becoming abbess, Gertrude "obtained through her envoy's men of good reputation, relics of saints and holy books from Rome, and from regions across the sea, experienced men for the teaching of the divine law and to practice the chants for herself and her people."<sup id="cite_ref-vita_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vita-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Fouracre and Gerberding assert that the men from across the sea are from Britain and Ireland and also highlight this as an example of the importance of Rome to the Franks long before Charlemagne ever had a relationship with the Pope.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> This is supported by Peyroux, Wemple, and the ancient Chronicles of Fredegar.
</p><p>She welcomed foreigners, lay or religious. She especially welcomed Irish monks who, since the sixth century, traveled to evangelize.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> Among the numerous pilgrims that visited the monastery of Nivelles were the two brothers, <a href="/wiki/Foillan" title="Foillan">Foillan</a> and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Ultan" title="Saint Ultan">Ultan</a>, both Irish monks on their way from Rome to Peronne, where their brother <a href="/wiki/Saint_Fursey" title="Saint Fursey">Fursey</a>, lay buried.<sup id="cite_ref-ott_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ott-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> According to Wemple, "The Irish monasteries, with the ancient tradition of oral learning, were at the time the most distinguished centers of scholarship".<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_of_Foillan">Death of Foillan</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Death of Foillan">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In the <i>Additum Additamentum Nivialense de Fuilano</i>, an addendum to the <i>Vita Sanctae Gertrudis</i>, there is a story about several events involving Irish monks led by Foillan that involve Gertrude and the Abbey of Nivelles.<sup id="cite_ref-additamentum_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-additamentum-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Before the foundation of Nivelles, Irish monks led by Foillan traveled to Francia, from Fursey's monastery in Ireland to escape pagan raids. They were received by <a href="/wiki/Erchinoald" title="Erchinoald">Erchinoald</a>, mayor of the palace, but were later expelled by him and moved to live with Itta and Gertrude. Grimoald and the Pippinids were happy to accept them and built the monastery of Berbrona for them with the help of Itta and Gertrude. In other works this monastery is referred to as Fosses.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood190_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood190-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> There is much praise of Gertrude in the text.<sup id="cite_ref-additamentum_27-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-additamentum-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Sometime later, Foillan went on a journey, saying mass in Nivelles before leaving. Ian Wood says that the purpose of Foillan's journey was to visit his benefactors, but he provides no evidence for this claim other than a citation of the Additamentum.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood190_28-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood190-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> After only a day of traveling, Foillan and his three companions were betrayed and murdered by an evil man who offered them shelter for the night in his house and then sold their belongings. Upon learning that Foillan did not reach his destination, the brothers of his monastery began to search for him. However, it was Gertrude who succeeded in finding Foillan's body 77 days after he was murdered, on the anniversary of his brother Fursey's death. The four bodies were immediately brought to Nivelles.<sup id="cite_ref-additamentum_27-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-additamentum-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>"Dido, Bishop of Poitiers, and the mayor of the palace, Grimoald, a man of illustrious standing," arrived by chance, or, as the text hints, divine intervention at Nivelles shortly before the bodies and the two men carried Foillan into Nivelles "on their own shoulders." Foillan's body was then taken to his own monastery "and when noblemen had flocked from all sides to meet him and carried him on their own shoulders" he was buried at Fosses.<sup id="cite_ref-additamentum_27-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-additamentum-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Miracles">Miracles</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Miracles">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Gertude_nivelle_shrine02.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e4/Gertude_nivelle_shrine02.jpg/220px-Gertude_nivelle_shrine02.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="312" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e4/Gertude_nivelle_shrine02.jpg/330px-Gertude_nivelle_shrine02.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e4/Gertude_nivelle_shrine02.jpg/440px-Gertude_nivelle_shrine02.jpg 2x" data-file-width="632" data-file-height="896" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Gertude_nivelle_shrine02.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Shrine of St. Gertrude of Nivelles, originally made in 1272–1298 - this reproduction in the Pushkin Museum was cast from the original because in 1940 a German bomb smashed the original reliquary into 337 fragments that subsequently was rebuilt</div></div></div>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Miraculous_vision">Miraculous vision</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Miraculous vision">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The first miracle attributed to Gertrude in the <i>Vita</i> takes place at the altar of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Sixtus_II" title="Pope Sixtus II">Pope Sixtus II</a> the Martyr as Gertrude was standing in prayer. "She saw descending above her a flaming pellucid sphere such that the whole basilica was illuminated by its brightness." The vision persisted for about half an hour and later was revealed to some of the sisters at the monastery. The anonymous author of the <i>Vita</i> believes that this vision represents a "visitation of the True Light."<sup id="cite_ref-vita_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vita-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Salvation_of_the_sailors">Salvation of the sailors</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Salvation of the sailors">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The second miracle attributed to Gertrude in the <i>Vita</i> took place as the anonymous author and his friend were <i>peacefully sailing over the sea on the monastery's business</i>. This account is felt by some to indicate that the author was an Irish monk.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In the account, an incredible storm appears as well as a sea monster, causing great despair as "the sailors... turned to their idols," evidence of the persistence of paganism at the time.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In desperation, the author's friend cries out to Gertrude to save himself and his companions from the storm and monster. Immediately the storm subsides and the monster dives back into the deep.<sup id="cite_ref-vita_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vita-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Appointment_of_Wulfetrud">Appointment of Wulfetrud</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Appointment of Wulfetrud">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Before her death, Gertrude appointed her niece Wulfetrud as Abbess of Nivelles. Wulfetrud's position was precarious because her father, <a href="/wiki/Grimoald_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Grimoald I">Grimoald I</a>, had usurped the Austrasian throne.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> According to Ian Wood, "It was the Neustrian court that had ended Grimoald's usurpation of the Austrasian throne."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Vita</i> states that "out of hatred of her father that kings, queens, and even priests... wished to drag her from her place" and steal Wulfetrude's property. Wulfetrud was only 20 years old at the time.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Wilfetrud's appointment was a testament to Gertrude's power and influence within the abbey and the Catholic Church itself.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> According to the <i>Vita</i>, Wulfetrud kept her position "through the grace of God."<sup id="cite_ref-vita_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vita-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> At the same time, however, Gertrude was unable to help "Grimoald or his daughter against Clovis II."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.260_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.260-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:0_Gertrude_de_Nivelles_-_JPG.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/ff/0_Gertrude_de_Nivelles_-_JPG.jpg/220px-0_Gertrude_de_Nivelles_-_JPG.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="429" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/ff/0_Gertrude_de_Nivelles_-_JPG.jpg/330px-0_Gertrude_de_Nivelles_-_JPG.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/ff/0_Gertrude_de_Nivelles_-_JPG.jpg/440px-0_Gertrude_de_Nivelles_-_JPG.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1160" data-file-height="2264" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:0_Gertrude_de_Nivelles_-_JPG.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Nivelles" title="Nivelles">Nivelles</a> (Belgium), the Saint Gertrude of Nivelles statue</div></div></div>
<p>Gertrude is portrayed as leading a devout life until her death.It is  possible that after taking the veil in ca. 640, she never left the monastery cloister, thus escaping politics and local affairs.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Gertrude is described as "exhausted by a life of charity, fasting and prayer" at the end of her short life.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDonnay-Rocmans_in_translation,_p.35_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDonnay-Rocmans_in_translation,_p.35-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> The Cambridge Medieval History says that "because of too much abstinence and keeping of vigils... her body was sorrily exhausted with serious illness."<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gertrude's <i>Vita</i> describes her, after relinquishing her role as abbess, spending her time praying intensely and secretly wearing a hair shirt. According to her biographer, Gertrude felt the time of her death approaching and asked a pilgrim from the Fosses monastery when she would die. This pilgrim is commonly believed to be Ultan, Foillan's brother. Fouracre and Gerberding dispute that Ultan was Abbot of Fosses, but there is some speculation.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Ultan prophesied that Gertrude would die on 17 March, the very next day, and also the feast day of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Patrick" title="Saint Patrick">Saint Patrick</a>. Furthermore, Ultan prophesied that "she may pass joyously because blessed Bishop Patrick with the chosen angels of God... are prepared to receive her." True to the prophecy, Gertrude died the next day after praying all night and taking communion. Shortly after her death, the monk Rinchinus as well as the author of the <i>Vita</i> noticed a pleasant odor in cell with her body.<sup id="cite_ref-vita_3-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vita-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Just before her death in 659, Gertrude instructed the nuns at Nivelles to bury her in an old veil left behind by a traveling pilgrim and Gertrude's own hair shirt. She died in poverty, 17 March 659, at the age, we are told, of thirty-three years.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDonnay-Rocmans_in_translation,_p.35_30-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDonnay-Rocmans_in_translation,_p.35-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gertrude's choice of burial clothing is a pattern in medieval <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> as an expression of humility and piety. Her death and the image of her weak and humble figure is, in fact, a critical point in her biographer's narrative. Her monastery also benefitted from this portrayal because the haircloth and veil in which Gertrude was interred became relics. Bonnie Effros contends that identification with tombs like Gertrude's signaled higher privilege and prestige within the church. Tombs covered with cloths often functioned as altars for those who had access to them. At Nivelles, her relics were only publicly displayed for feast days, Easter, and other holy days.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:2017_Kruisherenkerk,_zuidelijke_zijkapel,_St-Gertrudisschildering_08.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/2017_Kruisherenkerk%2C_zuidelijke_zijkapel%2C_St-Gertrudisschildering_08.jpg/220px-2017_Kruisherenkerk%2C_zuidelijke_zijkapel%2C_St-Gertrudisschildering_08.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="141" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/2017_Kruisherenkerk%2C_zuidelijke_zijkapel%2C_St-Gertrudisschildering_08.jpg/330px-2017_Kruisherenkerk%2C_zuidelijke_zijkapel%2C_St-Gertrudisschildering_08.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/2017_Kruisherenkerk%2C_zuidelijke_zijkapel%2C_St-Gertrudisschildering_08.jpg/440px-2017_Kruisherenkerk%2C_zuidelijke_zijkapel%2C_St-Gertrudisschildering_08.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2800" data-file-height="1794" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:2017_Kruisherenkerk,_zuidelijke_zijkapel,_St-Gertrudisschildering_08.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Saint Gertrude saving a house on fire, detail of a mural in the <a href="/wiki/Crosier_Monastery,_Maastricht" title="Crosier Monastery, Maastricht">Crosier Monastery, Maastricht</a></div></div></div>
<p>Gertrude is the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the City of <a href="/wiki/Nivelles" title="Nivelles">Nivelles</a>, The towns of <a href="/wiki/Geertruidenberg" title="Geertruidenberg">Geertruidenberg</a>, <a href="/wiki/Breda" title="Breda">Breda</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Bergen-op-Zoom" class="mw-redirect" title="Bergen-op-Zoom">Bergen-op-Zoom</a> in <a href="/wiki/North_Brabant" title="North Brabant">North Brabant</a>, also are under her patronage.<sup id="cite_ref-ott_19-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ott-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Gertrude was also the patron saint of the <a href="/wiki/Canons_Regular_of_the_Order_of_the_Holy_Cross" title="Canons Regular of the Order of the Holy Cross">Order of the Holy Cross</a> (Crosiers or Crutched Friars). In the <a href="/wiki/Crosier_Monastery,_Maastricht#Monastery_church" title="Crosier Monastery, Maastricht">Crosier Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Maastricht" title="Maastricht">Maastricht</a>, the Netherlands, a large mural from the 16th century depicts eight scenes from her life and legend.
</p><p>The legend of Gertrude's vision of the ocean voyage led her to be as well the patron saint of travelers. In memory of this event, medieval travelers drank a so-called "Sinte Geerts Minne" or "Gertrudenminne" before setting out on their journey. Her attention to the care of her garden led her assistance to be invoked by gardeners, and also against rats and mental illness.<sup id="cite_ref-ott_19-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ott-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Le Tour Sainte-Gertrude is a traditional procession around Nivelles. The abbesses and the canons used to regularly make a long journey outside the walls of the abbey in emulation of Gertrude, to meet the farmers, the poor and the sick. Many of the pilgrims participate in costume, as they accompany a cart bearing a reliquary containing Gertrude's relics. In May 2004, the Saint Gertrude Tour was proclaimed "Oral and Intangible Heritage Masterpiece of the French Community."<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> The hundred years-old secondary school "Collège Sainte-Gertrude de Nivelles" founded by the <a href="/wiki/D%C3%A9sir%C3%A9-Joseph_Mercier" title="Désiré-Joseph Mercier">Cardinal Désiré-Joseph Mercier</a> in the city owes its name to the saint.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Gertrude_of_Nivelles_in_Literature">Gertrude of Nivelles in Literature</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: Gertrude of Nivelles in Literature">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Vita_Sanctae_Geretrudis_and_the_Additamentum_Nivialense_de_Fuilano"><i>Vita Sanctae Geretrudis</i> and the <i>Additamentum Nivialense de Fuilano</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: Vita Sanctae Geretrudis and the Additamentum Nivialense de Fuilano">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <i>Vita</i> was originally thought to have been written in the eleventh century, but this was later disproven with the discovery of a version dating from the eighth century. Bruno Krush argues that the work is written around the same time that the events it describes take place, and there is wide agreement that it was written before 670, and after 663. The time range is determined using a combination of Latin style, references by contemporary works, the accuracy of the events (indicating a close proximity to their occurrence), and references in the text to known events. The <i>Vita</i> is one of just a few sources dating from seventh century France, and one of only three from Austrasia (all of which deal with Gertrude). This makes the <i>Vita</i> very important as a source for Charlemagne's ancestry as well as placing the "Cradle of the Carolingians" in the middle <a href="/wiki/Meuse" title="Meuse">Meuse</a> in Brabant as opposed to <a href="/wiki/Moselle" title="Moselle">Moselle</a> in Luxembourg, where <a href="/wiki/Pepin_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Pepin II">Pepin II</a> and <a href="/wiki/Plectrude" title="Plectrude">Plectrude</a> had large tracts of land.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The author of <i>Vita</i> writes as a first-hand witness to the events he describes. Although it is perfectly plausible that he could have been a monk or nun, and there some debate on this topic.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Based on his reference to himself "with another brother," the author is most likely male.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPeyroux,_p.39_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPeyroux,_p.39-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Vita</i> was originally written for Abbot Agnes, who succeeded Wulfetrud upon her death.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Source_integrity">Source integrity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=16" title="Edit section: Source integrity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>As indicated by Charlemagne's inclusion of Arnulf of Metz in his family tree (in a work by <a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Deacon" title="Paul the Deacon">Paul the Deacon</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Lombards" title="Lombards">Lombard</a>), there were incentives to being associated with saints in Carolingian times. Fouracre and Gerberding argue that there were large incentives to being associated with saints in the seventh century as well, casting doubt on the genealogy presented in many sources. However, these scholars argue that the close temporal relationship of the three Austrasian sources to the life of Gertrude as well as the monastic audience of the works make them more than likely credible.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to Catherine Peyroux, who believes that because author is writing very near Gertrude's lifetime, account must at least be "essentially plausible to Gertrude's contemporaries."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPeyroux,_p.39_34-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPeyroux,_p.39-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Relationship_with_Arnulf_of_Metz">Relationship with Arnulf of Metz</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=17" title="Edit section: Relationship with Arnulf of Metz">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gertrude's relationship with Arnulf of Metz is a persistent source of confusion for scholars and students alike. Numerous sources point to a relationship between Gertrude and Arnulf,<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> while others believe this relationship is invented.<sup id="cite_ref-fouracre_8-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fouracre-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> In particular, the debate focuses on Arnulf's relationship with Ansegisel, the husband of Begga, Gertrude's sister. Sources that include Arnulf in the Pippinid family state that Arnulf is that father of Ansegisel. Sources making the opposite claim do not.
</p><p>Ian Wood recommends focusing only on the four earliest sources for this information, as later sources are based on these few documents.  He starts with the continuations of the chronicles of Fredegar, which do not mention this connection, and are based on an earlier work. He says that "since Childebrand himself was the half-brother of <a href="/wiki/Charles_Martel" title="Charles Martel">Charles Martel</a>, it is not surprising that the Fredegar continuator add the information contained in the Liber Historiae Francorum material largely concerned with Austrasia and Frisia" in 751.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> However, he adds no information regarding Arnulf at this time. The Liber is one of the earliest works detailing the history of this period and makes no mention of the relationship between Arnulf and Ansegisel.
</p><p>Moving to a later source, Wood shows how the Annales Mettenses Priores radically change the picture (from the Liber, the earliest source for the late seventh century, written in 727). The Annales allude to the power held by previous members of the family, especially by Pippin I. They also allude to Pippin I's relationship to Arnulf, Bishop of Metz, although they do not specify the nature of that relationship. In addition they talk with great admiration of Pepin II's grandmother, Itta, and his Aunt, Gertrude. From the start, therefore, the Annales Mettenses Priores announce their intention of turning the history of the seventh and eighth centuries into a history of the Pippinids, or the Carolingians they were to become."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.258_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.258-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> As a result of this shift, Wood argues that "For the period up until 714, therefore, Annales Mettenses Priores produce a substantially different account of events from that offered by the Liber Historiae Francorum, making Pepin the center of attention, and conferring on him complete power from the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Tertry" title="Battle of Tertry">Battle of Tertry</a> onwards."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.258_39-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.258-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>This change in focus, while not invalid per se, certainly is problematic, because the Annales were written long after the time period they describe. This is especially important, notes Wood, because "as a reading of history the so-called Metz Prior Annals have been extremely influential, providing the most popular interpretation of the late Merovingian period. Nevertheless, they show the Pippinids and Merovingian history as the Carolingians wished to see them."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.258_39-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.258-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> Despite this different focus, even the Metz Annales do not state that Arnulf is Ansegisel's father, saying only that he is a great ally of Pippin.
</p><p>Wood believes that the shift in focus of the Metz Prior Annals is deliberate, citing the need to glorify the sanctity of the newly powerful Pippinids. "The other asset which the family was to develop, its sanctity, was beginning to be realized only in the last decades of the seventh century. Although Arnulf of Metz is thought to have been Pepin II's grandfather, the evidence for this is not early, and even the Annales Mettenses Priores were uncertain about the nature of the relationship between Arnulf and the Pippinids."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> According to Wood, this link comes first from <a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Deacon" title="Paul the Deacon">Paul the Deacon</a> (Gesta episcoporum Mettensium) and is suspect, as Paul was not familiar with the events he was writing about and had limited access to reference materials.
</p><p>Of the other early sources that might establish a link between Ansegisel and Arnulf, all that is left is the <i>Vita Arnulfi</i>, or "Life of Arnulf." However, according to Wood, it is "not clear that the <i>Vita Arnulfi</i>... was written in the seventh century."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> It is possible that this work was a forgery, created later to sanctify the Carolingian line. This argument is not without base, because after Gertrude died in 659, "her sanctity was unquestionably promoted by the family in the late seventh century" beginning with her '<i>Vita</i> in 670.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood,_p.259-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Recent_popular_culture">Recent popular culture</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gertrude_of_Nivelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=18" title="Edit section: Recent popular culture">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The assignment of Gertrude as patron of <a href="/wiki/Cat" title="Cat">cats</a> and the designation of the cat as one of her <a href="/wiki/Saint_symbolism" title="Saint symbolism">attributes</a> seems to date from the 1980s. It is not mentioned at all in Madou's extensive historical survey from 1975. A more superficial association of Gertrude with the cat as a mouse hunter goes further back. Her veneration as protector against rats and mice dates from the early 15th century during the <a href="/wiki/Black_Plague" class="mw-redirect" title="Black Plague">Black Plague</a> and spread from Southwestern Germany to the Netherlands and <a href="/wiki/Catalonia" title="Catalonia">Catalonia</a>. Some 20th-century <a href="/wiki/Folkloristics" class="mw-redirect" title="Folkloristics">folkloristics</a> research conflated her with the Germanic goddess <a href="/wiki/Frigg#Archaeological_record" title="Frigg">Frigg</a>, who may have been depicted riding a cat.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> The authoritative <i><a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handw%C3%B6rterbuch_des_deutschen_Aberglaubens" class="extiw" title="de:Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens">Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens</a></i> (published in multiple volumes, 1927–1942) does not verify the connection to cats. The first major English-language publication presenting her as patron of cats is a 1981 catalogue of the <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art" title="Metropolitan Museum of Art">Metropolitan Museum of Art</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
