<p><b>Pelagia</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Πελαγία</span></span>; <abbr title="died">d.</abbr>&#160;early 4th century), distinguished as <b>Pelagia of Tarsus</b> and <b>Pelagia the Martyr</b> (<span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Πελαγία ἡ Μάρτυς</span></span>, <i>Pelagía ē Mártys</i>), was a <a href="/wiki/Christian_legend" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian legend">legendary</a> <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyr" title="Christian martyr">martyr</a> who lived in <a href="/wiki/Tarsus_(city)" class="mw-redirect" title="Tarsus (city)">Tarsus</a> in <a href="/wiki/Cilicia" title="Cilicia">Cilicia</a> (southeastern <a href="/wiki/Asia_Minor" class="mw-redirect" title="Asia Minor">Asia Minor</a>) during the reign of <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman</a> <a href="/wiki/List_of_Roman_emperors" title="List of Roman emperors">emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  Originally, her feast day was celebrated on <b>October 8</b>, in common with SS Pelagia the Virgin &amp; Pelagia the Harlot, both of Antioch and one or both of whom her story is probably modeled after. <sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKirsch1911_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKirsch1911-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><span class="anchor" id="Life"></span><span class="anchor" id="Biography"></span>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legend">Legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagia_of_Tarsus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to tradition, Diocletian's son, the heir to the throne, fell in love with her and wanted to marry Pelagia. She replied that she could not, because she had sworn to preserve her virginity <a href="/wiki/Mystical_marriage" class="mw-redirect" title="Mystical marriage">and was wedded to Christ</a>. In his sorrow, Diocletian's son committed <a href="/wiki/Suicide" title="Suicide">suicide</a>. Pelagia was sent to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> by her pagan mother, where Diocletian asked her to become his wife. She refused, calling the emperor insane. She was then <a href="/wiki/Burned_at_the_stake" class="mw-redirect" title="Burned at the stake">burnt to death</a>, and her flesh melted and smells of <a href="/wiki/Myrrh" title="Myrrh">myrrh</a> pervaded throughout Rome. By some versions, she was burned within a <a href="/wiki/Brazen_bull" title="Brazen bull">brazen bull</a>. The story concludes by relating that the pagans sent four lions to surround her bones, but instead they protected her bones from vultures and crows until a Christian bishop could recover them.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pelagia_of_Tarsus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There is little historical base to the story, as Diocletian had a daughter Valeria but no sons—a fact of considerable importance to the history of his reign. However, he did carry out <a href="/wiki/Diocletianic_Persecution" title="Diocletianic Persecution">the last intensive persecution of Christians in Roman history</a>, many of whose victims were indeed burned alive. Thus, Pelagia might have been an actually martyr of that time, even if under different circumstances than those recounted in the later story.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2015)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> More probably, commemorations of the <a href="/wiki/Pelagia_the_Virgin" title="Pelagia the Virgin">historical martyr Pelagia</a> at Tarsus were probably embellished and given a local setting at some point.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKirsch1911_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKirsch1911-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Constantine_the_Great" title="Constantine the Great">Constantine the Great</a> built a church on the reputed site of her remains.
</p>
