<p><b>Sophronius</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Σωφρόνιος</span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Arabic_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Arabic language">Arabic</a>: <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">صفرونيوس</span>; c. 560 – March 11, 638), called <b>Sophronius the Sophist</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> was the <a href="/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem" title="Greek Orthodox Patriarch of Jerusalem">Patriarch of Jerusalem</a> from 634 until his death.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Orthodox">Eastern Orthodox</a> and <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic</a> Churches. Before rising to the primacy of the <a href="/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Patriarchate_of_Jerusalem" title="Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Jerusalem">see of Jerusalem</a>, he was a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> and <a href="/wiki/Theologian" class="mw-redirect" title="Theologian">theologian</a> who was the chief protagonist for orthodox teaching in the doctrinal controversy on the essential nature of <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">Jesus</a> and his volitional acts.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Travels">Travels</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sophronius_of_Jerusalem&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Travels">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Sophronius was born in <a href="/wiki/Damascus" title="Damascus">Damascus</a> around 560.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He has been claimed to be of <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Greeks" title="Byzantine Greeks">Byzantine Greek</a><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> or <a href="/wiki/Assyrian_people" title="Assyrian people">Syriac</a> descent.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources" title="Wikipedia:Citing sources"><span title="This citation requires a reference to the specific page or range of pages in which the material appears. (March 2021)">page&#160;needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> A teacher of <a href="/wiki/Rhetoric" title="Rhetoric">rhetoric</a>, Sophronius became an <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a> in <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a> about 580 and then entered the <a href="/wiki/Monastery_of_St._Theodosius" class="mw-redirect" title="Monastery of St. Theodosius">monastery of St. Theodosius</a><sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> near <a href="/wiki/Bethlehem" title="Bethlehem">Bethlehem</a>. Traveling to monastic centres in <a href="/wiki/Asia_Minor" class="mw-redirect" title="Asia Minor">Asia Minor</a>, Egypt, and <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, he accompanied the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine</a> chronicler <a href="/wiki/John_Moschus" title="John Moschus">St. John Moschus</a>, who dedicated to him his celebrated tract on the religious life, <i><a href="/wiki/Spiritual_Meadow" title="Spiritual Meadow">Spiritual Meadow</a></i> (and whose feast day in the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Rite" title="Byzantine Rite">Byzantine Rite</a>, 11 March&#160;&#91;<a href="/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates" title="Old Style and New Style dates">O.S.</a> 24 March&#93;, is shared with Sophronius'<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>). On the death of Moschus in Rome in 619, Sophronius accompanied the body back to <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a> for monastic burial. He traveled to <a href="/wiki/Alexandria" title="Alexandria">Alexandria</a>, Egypt, and to <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> in the year 633 to persuade the respective patriarchs to renounce <a href="/wiki/Monoenergism" title="Monoenergism">Monoenergism</a>, a heterodox teaching that espoused a single, divine energy in Christ to the exclusion of a human capacity for choice. Except for his synodal letter for the <a href="/wiki/Third_Council_of_Constantinople" title="Third Council of Constantinople">Third Council of Constantinople</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Sophronius' extensive writings on this question are all lost.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (March 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Elected_Patriarch">Elected Patriarch</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sophronius_of_Jerusalem&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Elected Patriarch">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Although unsuccessful in his mission to condemn Monoenergism, Sophronius was elected patriarch of Jerusalem in 634. Soon after his enthronement he forwarded his noted synodical letter to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Honorius_I" title="Pope Honorius I">Pope Honorius I</a> and to the Eastern patriarchs, explaining the orthodox belief in the two natures, human and divine, of Christ, as opposed to Monoenergism, which he viewed as a subtle form of heretical <a href="/wiki/Monophysitism" title="Monophysitism">Monophysitism</a> (which posited a single divine nature for Christ).<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Moreover, he composed a <a href="/wiki/Florilegium" title="Florilegium">Florilegium</a> (“Anthology”) of some 600 texts from the <a href="/wiki/Church_Fathers" title="Church Fathers">Early Church Fathers</a> in favour of the Christian tenet of <a href="/wiki/Dyothelitism" title="Dyothelitism">Dyothelitism</a> (positing both human and divine wills in Christ). This document also is lost.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (March 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Teachings">Teachings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sophronius_of_Jerusalem&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Teachings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In his <a href="/wiki/Christmas" title="Christmas">Christmas</a> sermon of 634, Sophronius was more concerned with keeping the clergy in line with the <a href="/wiki/Chalcedonian" class="mw-redirect" title="Chalcedonian">Chalcedonian</a> view of God, giving only the most conventional of warnings of the <a href="/wiki/Saracen" title="Saracen">Saracen</a> advance on <a href="/wiki/Palestine_(region)" title="Palestine (region)">Palestine</a>, commenting that the Saracens already controlled Bethlehem. Sophronius, who viewed the Saracen control of Palestine as "unwitting representatives of God's inevitable chastisement of weak and wavering Christians",<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> died soon after the fall of Jerusalem to the <a href="/wiki/Caliph" class="mw-redirect" title="Caliph">caliph</a> <a href="/wiki/Umar_ibn_al-Khattab" class="mw-redirect" title="Umar ibn al-Khattab">Umar I</a> in 637, but not before he had negotiated the recognition of civil and religious liberty for Christians in exchange for tribute - an agreement known as <a href="/wiki/The_Umariyya_Covenant" class="mw-redirect" title="The Umariyya Covenant">Umari Treaty</a>. The caliph himself came to Jerusalem, and met with the patriarch at the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre" title="Church of the Holy Sepulchre">Church of the Holy Sepulchre</a>. Sophronius invited Umar to pray there, but Umar declined, fearing to endanger the Church's status as a Christian temple.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Beside <a href="/wiki/Polemics" class="mw-redirect" title="Polemics">polemics</a>, Sophronius' writings included an encomium on the Alexandrian martyrs <a href="/wiki/Cyrus_and_John" title="Cyrus and John">Cyrus and John</a> in gratitude for an extraordinary cure of his failing vision. He also wrote 23 <a href="/wiki/Anacreontic" class="mw-redirect" title="Anacreontic">Anacreontic</a> (classical metre) poems on such themes as the <a href="/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(636%E2%80%93637)" title="Siege of Jerusalem (636–637)">Muslim siege of Jerusalem</a> and on various <a href="/wiki/Liturgy" title="Liturgy">liturgical</a> celebrations.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> His <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://web.archive.org/web/20080512054133/http://www.monachos.net/library/Sophronius_of_Jerusalem,_Anacreontica_19_and_20"><i>Anacreontica</i> 19 and 20</a> seem to be an expression of the longing desire he had of the Holy City, possibly when he was absent from Jerusalem during one of his many journeys. The order of the two poems has to be inverted to establish a correct sequence of the diverse subjects. Arranged in this way, the two poems describe a complete circuit throughout the most important sanctuaries of Jerusalem at the end of the 6th century, described as the golden age of Christianity in the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Land" title="Holy Land">Holy Land</a>. Themes of Anacreonticon 20 include the gates of Jerusalem (or Solyma), the Anastasis, the <a href="/wiki/Golgotha" class="mw-redirect" title="Golgotha">Rock of the Cross</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre" title="Church of the Holy Sepulchre">Constantinian Basilica</a>, <a href="/wiki/Mount_Zion" title="Mount Zion">Mount Sion</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Praetorium" title="Praetorium">Praetorium</a>, <a href="/wiki/Pool_of_Siloam" title="Pool of Siloam">St. Mary at the Probatica</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Gethsemane" title="Gethsemane">Gethsemane</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Mount_of_Olives" title="Mount of Olives">Mount of Olives</a>, Bethany, and Bethlehem come next in Anacreonticon 19.
Sophronius also wrote down the Life of <a href="/wiki/Mary_of_Egypt" title="Mary of Egypt">St. Mary of Egypt</a>, which is read on the fifth Thursday of Lent in the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Rite" title="Byzantine Rite">Byzantine Rite</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<p>According to the Muslim treaty <a href="/wiki/Pact_of_Omar" class="mw-redirect" title="Pact of Omar">Pact of Omar</a>, caliph <a href="/wiki/Omar_ibn_al-Khattab" class="mw-redirect" title="Omar ibn al-Khattab">Omar ibn al-Khattab</a> came to Jerusalem in 637 after the conquest of Jerusalem and toured the city with Sophronius who greeted him on a <a href="/wiki/Lectica" class="mw-redirect" title="Lectica">lectica</a> while Omar lead a camel with his servant riding it. During the negotiation treaty for the surrender of Jerusalem, Omar proposed for all Christian businesses, assets and Churches to be retained and the Arab army would not plunder the city, which was not the normal custom at the time. Omar also requested for the Jewish population of Jerusalem to be reinstated, having previously been purged by the Roman Christian army, by finding 80 Jewish families and resettling them in the city. During the tour of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre" title="Church of the Holy Sepulchre">Church of the Holy Sepulchre</a>, the time for prayer came, and despite Sophronius's offer to Omar to pray inside the Church, Omar chose to pray outside. According to Islamic tradition, the caliph's reason for declining to pray there was because in the future Muslims might say that great Omar prayed here resulting in them wanting to build a mosque there. So appreciating the caliph's intelligence he gave the keys of the church to him. Unable to refuse it the caliph gave it to a family of Muslims from <a href="/wiki/Medina" title="Medina">Medina</a> and asked them to open the church and close it; the keys of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre" title="Church of the Holy Sepulchre">Church of the Holy Sepulchre</a> still remain with the Muslim family.
</p>
