<p><b>Paulinus of Nola</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="&#39;p&#39; in &#39;pie&#39;">p</span><span title="/ɔː/: &#39;au&#39; in &#39;fraud&#39;">ɔː</span><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;l&#39; in &#39;lie&#39;">l</span><span title="/aɪ/: &#39;i&#39; in &#39;tide&#39;">aɪ</span><span title="&#39;n&#39; in &#39;nigh&#39;">n</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;s&#39; in &#39;sigh&#39;">s</span></span>/</a></span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Paulinus Nolanus</i>; also <a href="/wiki/Anglicisation" title="Anglicisation">anglicized</a> as <b>Pauline of Nola</b>;<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;354</span> – 22 June 431) born <b>Pontius Meropius Anicius Paulinus</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-Loffler_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Loffler-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> was a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman</a> <a href="/wiki/Roman_poetry" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman poetry">poet</a>, writer, and <a href="/wiki/Roman_senate" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman senate">senator</a> who attained the ranks of <a href="/wiki/Suffect_consul" class="mw-redirect" title="Suffect consul">suffect consul</a> (<span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;377</span>) and <a href="/wiki/Roman_governor" title="Roman governor">governor</a> of <a href="/wiki/Campania" title="Campania">Campania</a> (<span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;380</span>&#160;– c.<span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;381</span>) but – following the assassination of the <a href="/wiki/List_of_Roman_emperors" title="List of Roman emperors">emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Gratian" title="Gratian">Gratian</a> and under the influence of his <a href="/wiki/Hispania" title="Hispania">Hispanic</a> wife <a href="/wiki/Therasia_of_Nola" title="Therasia of Nola">Therasia of Nola</a> — abandoned his career, was <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptized</a> as a <a href="/wiki/Christianity_in_the_Roman_Empire" class="mw-redirect" title="Christianity in the Roman Empire">Christian</a>, and probably after Therasia's death became <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Nola" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Nola">bishop of Nola</a> in Campania. While there, he wrote poems in honor of his predecessor <a href="/wiki/Felix_of_Nola" title="Felix of Nola">Saint&#160;Felix</a> and corresponded with other Christian leaders throughout the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">empire</a>. He is credited with the introduction of <a href="/wiki/Altar_bell" title="Altar bell">bells</a> to <a href="/wiki/Christian_liturgy" title="Christian liturgy">Christian worship</a> and helped resolve the disputed election of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_I" title="Pope Boniface I">Pope Boniface&#160;I</a>.
</p><p>His renunciation of his wealth and station in favor of an <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a> and philanthropic life was held up as an example by many of his contemporaries—including <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine</a>, <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a>, <a href="/wiki/Martin_of_Tours" title="Martin of Tours">Martin</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Ambrose" title="Ambrose">Ambrose</a>—and he was subsequently venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Christian_saint" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian saint">saint</a>. His <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> became a focus of <a href="/wiki/Christian_pilgrimage" title="Christian pilgrimage">pilgrimage</a>, but were removed from Nola sometime between the 11th and 20th centuries. His <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_the_saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Calendar of the saints">feast day</a> is observed on 22 June in both the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic</a> and <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Churches</a>. In <a href="/wiki/Nola" title="Nola">Nola</a>, the entire week around his feast day is celebrated as the <a href="/wiki/Festival_of_the_Lilies" class="mw-redirect" title="Festival of the Lilies">Festival of the Lilies</a>.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paulinus_of_Nola&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_Paulinus_of_Nola._Line_engraving._Wellcome_V0032823.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Saint_Paulinus_of_Nola._Line_engraving._Wellcome_V0032823.jpg/220px-Saint_Paulinus_of_Nola._Line_engraving._Wellcome_V0032823.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="340" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Saint_Paulinus_of_Nola._Line_engraving._Wellcome_V0032823.jpg/330px-Saint_Paulinus_of_Nola._Line_engraving._Wellcome_V0032823.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Saint_Paulinus_of_Nola._Line_engraving._Wellcome_V0032823.jpg/440px-Saint_Paulinus_of_Nola._Line_engraving._Wellcome_V0032823.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2309" data-file-height="3567" /></a><figcaption>Line engraving of Saint Paulinus of Nola</figcaption></figure>
<p>Pontius Meropius Paulinus was born <span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;352</span> at <a href="/wiki/Bordeaux" title="Bordeaux">Bordeaux</a>, in southwestern France. He was from a notable <a href="/wiki/Roman_senate" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman senate">senatorial</a> family with estates in the <a href="/wiki/Aquitaine" title="Aquitaine">Aquitaine</a> province of France, northern Iberia, and southern Italy. Paulinus was a kinsman of <a href="/wiki/Melania_the_Elder" title="Melania the Elder">Melania the Elder</a>. He was educated in Bordeaux, where his teacher, the poet <a href="/wiki/Ausonius" title="Ausonius">Ausonius</a>, also became his friend. At some time during his boyhood he made a visit to the shrine of St Felix at Nola near Naples.<sup id="cite_ref-Loffler_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Loffler-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His normal career as a young member of the senatorial class did not last long. In 375, the Emperor <a href="/wiki/Gratian" title="Gratian">Gratian</a> succeeded his father <a href="/wiki/Valentinian_I" title="Valentinian I">Valentinian</a>. Gratian made Paulinus <a href="/wiki/Suffect_consul" class="mw-redirect" title="Suffect consul">suffect consul</a> at Rome <span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;377</span>, and appointed him governor of the southern Italian province of <a href="/wiki/Campania" title="Campania">Campania</a> <span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;380</span>. Paulinus noted the Campanians' devotion to Saint <a href="/wiki/Felix_of_Nola" title="Felix of Nola">Felix of Nola</a> and built a road for pilgrims, as well as a hospice for the poor near the local shrine.<sup id="cite_ref-cna_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cna-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 383 Gratian was assassinated at <a href="/wiki/Lyon" title="Lyon">Lyon</a>, France, and Paulinus went to Milan to attend the school of Ambrose.<sup id="cite_ref-Benedict_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Benedict-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Around 384 he returned to Bordeaux. There he married <a href="/wiki/Therasia_of_Nola" title="Therasia of Nola">Therasia</a>, a Christian noblewoman from Barcelona.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Paulinus was threatened with the charge of having murdered his brother.<sup id="cite_ref-Loffler_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Loffler-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> It is possible that an attempt was made to accuse him in order to confiscate his property.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> He was baptized by Bishop Delphinus of Bordeaux. He and his wife traveled to Iberia about 390. When they lost their only child eight days after birth they decided to withdraw from the world, and live a secluded religious life.<sup id="cite_ref-Loffler_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Loffler-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Paulinus was close to both <a href="/wiki/Pelagius" title="Pelagius">Pelagius</a> and to the <a href="/wiki/Pelagian" class="mw-redirect" title="Pelagian">Pelagian</a> writer <a href="/wiki/Julian_of_Eclanum" title="Julian of Eclanum">Julian of Eclanum</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:StatuadiSanPaolinodaNola.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/StatuadiSanPaolinodaNola.JPG/220px-StatuadiSanPaolinodaNola.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/StatuadiSanPaolinodaNola.JPG/330px-StatuadiSanPaolinodaNola.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/StatuadiSanPaolinodaNola.JPG/440px-StatuadiSanPaolinodaNola.JPG 2x" data-file-width="2736" data-file-height="3648" /></a><figcaption>Statue of St. Paulinus in <a href="/wiki/Nola" title="Nola">Nola</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>In 393 or 394, after some resistance from Paulinus, he was ordained a <a href="/wiki/Presbyter" title="Presbyter">presbyter</a> on Christmas Day by <a href="/wiki/Lampius" title="Lampius">Lampius</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Barcelona" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Barcelona">Bishop of Barcelona</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Bardenhewer_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bardenhewer-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> (This was similar to what had happened with <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</a>, who had been ordained against his protestations in the year 391 at the behest of a crowd cooperating with Bishop Valerius in the north African city of <a href="/wiki/Hippo_Regius" title="Hippo Regius">Hippo Regius</a>.) However, there is some debate as to whether the ordination was canonical, since Paulinus received ordination "at a leap" (<i><a href="/wiki/Per_saltum" title="Per saltum">per saltum</a></i>), without receiving minor orders first.<sup id="cite_ref-Loffler_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Loffler-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Paulinus refused to remain in <a href="/wiki/Barcelona" title="Barcelona">Barcelona</a>, and in late spring of 395 he and his wife moved from Iberia to <a href="/wiki/Nola" title="Nola">Nola</a> in Campania where he remained until his death. Paulinus credited his conversion to Saint Felix, who was buried in Nola, and each year would write a poem in honor of him. 
</p><p>The great building works undertaken by Paulinus in 402-403 were the crowning glory and ornament of the renovated Nola. He restored and improved the ancient basilica erected in the martyr's honour. He and <a href="/wiki/Therasia_of_Nola" title="Therasia of Nola">Therasia</a> also rebuilt a church commemorating Saint Felix, of great size and richly decorated, a monument of Christian art, with magnificent porticoes and fountains, for which a copious supply of water was brought from nearby <a href="/wiki/Avella" title="Avella">Avella</a>. Great crowds of pilgrims flocked to the martyr's tomb.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>In January 406 following the peace after the defeat of <a href="/wiki/Radagaisus" title="Radagaisus">Radagaisus</a>, Paulinus invited a circle of guests including 
<a href="/wiki/Melania_the_Younger" title="Melania the Younger">Melania the Younger</a> and her husband and mother (Albina) and many other christians such as the Bishop of Beneventum, and where Melania wished to stay with all her household, though she left before 408.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>During these years Paulinus engaged in considerable epistolary dialogue with <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> among others about monastic topics. "Paulinus decided to invest his money for the poor and the church rather than rejecting it completely, which stands in contrast to other more severe contemporary views such as Jerome's".<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Therasia died some time between 408 and 410, and shortly afterwards Paulinus received episcopal ordination.<sup id="cite_ref-cna_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cna-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Around 410, Paulinus was chosen Bishop of Nola, where he served for twenty years. Like a growing number of aristocrats in the late 4th and early 5th centuries who were entering the clergy rather than taking up the more usual administrative careers in the imperial service, Paulinus spent a great deal of his money on his chosen church, city and <a href="/wiki/Christian_liturgy" title="Christian liturgy">ritual</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-dingdong_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dingdong-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Paulinus died at Nola on 22 June 431.<sup id="cite_ref-cna_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cna-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The following year the presbyter Uranus wrote his "On the Death of Paulinus" (<i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">De Obitu Paulini</i></span></i>), an account of the death and character of Paulinus.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Influence">Influence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paulinus_of_Nola&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Influence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Bas-relief_Saint_Paulinus_Torregrotta.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/Bas-relief_Saint_Paulinus_Torregrotta.JPG/220px-Bas-relief_Saint_Paulinus_Torregrotta.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/Bas-relief_Saint_Paulinus_Torregrotta.JPG/330px-Bas-relief_Saint_Paulinus_Torregrotta.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/Bas-relief_Saint_Paulinus_Torregrotta.JPG/440px-Bas-relief_Saint_Paulinus_Torregrotta.JPG 2x" data-file-width="3456" data-file-height="4608" /></a><figcaption>Bas-relief of Saint Paulinus in <a href="/wiki/Torregrotta" title="Torregrotta">Torregrotta</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>As <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Nola" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Nola">bishop of Nola</a>, Paulinus is traditionally credited with the introduction of the use of <a href="/wiki/Altar_bell" title="Altar bell">bells</a> in church services. One form of medieval handbell was known as the <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">nola</i></span></i> and medieval steeple bells were known as <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">campana</i></span>s</i> from this supposed origin.<sup id="cite_ref-dingdong_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dingdong-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> However, Dr. Adolf Buse, professor at the Seminary of Cologne, showed that the use of bells in churches, an invention credited to Paulinus by tradition, is not due to him, nor even to the town of Nola.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Already during his governorship Paulinus had developed a fondness for the 3rd-century martyr, <a href="/wiki/Felix_of_Nola" title="Felix of Nola">Felix of Nola</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Bardenhewer_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bardenhewer-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Felix was a minor saint of local importance and patronage whose tomb had been built within the local necropolis at <a href="/wiki/Cimitile" title="Cimitile">Cimitile</a>, just outside the town of <a href="/wiki/Nola" title="Nola">Nola</a>. As governor, Paulinus had widened the road to Cimitile and built a residence for travelers; it was at this site that Paulinus and <a href="/wiki/Therasia_of_Nola" title="Therasia of Nola">Therasia</a> took up residence.  Nearby were a number of small chapels and at least one old basilica.  Paulinus rebuilt the complex, constructing a brand new basilica to Felix and gathering to him a small monastic community.  Paulinus wrote an annual hymn (<i>natalicium</i>) in honor of Saint Felix for the feast day when processions of pilgrims were at their peak. In these hymns we can understand the personal relationship Paulinus felt between himself and Felix, his advocate in heaven.  His poetry shares with much of the work of the early 5th century an ornateness of style that classicists of the 18th and 19th centuries found cloying and dismissed as decadent, though Paulinus' poems were highly regarded at the time and used as educational models.
</p><p>Many of Paulinus's letters to his contemporaries, including Ausonius and <a href="/wiki/Sulpicius_Severus" title="Sulpicius Severus">Sulpicius Severus</a> in southern <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a>, <a href="/wiki/Victricius_of_Rouen" class="mw-redirect" title="Victricius of Rouen">Victricius of Rouen</a> in northern Gaul, and Augustine in Africa, are preserved. Four letters from Paulinus to Augustine survive, and eight from Augustine to Paulinus. In one, Augustine invites Paulinus to visit Africa. As a publishing technique at that time, Augustine often sent copies of his works to Paulinus, to be copied and circulated in Italy.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
Paulinus may have been indirectly responsible for Augustine's <i>Confessions</i>:  Paulinus wrote to <a href="/wiki/Alypius_of_Thagaste" title="Alypius of Thagaste">Alypius</a>, Bishop of <a href="/wiki/Tagaste" class="mw-redirect" title="Tagaste">Thagaste</a> and a close friend of <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine</a>, asking about his conversion and taking up of the <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a> life.  Alypius's autobiographical response does not survive; Augustine's ostensible answer to that query is the <i>Confessions</i>. Paulinus also wrote five letters to Delphinus and six to <a href="/wiki/Amandus_of_Bordeaux" title="Amandus of Bordeaux">Amandus of Bordeaux</a>.
</p><p>"Paulinus' surviving letters and poems, many devoted to the feast day of Felix, reveal his attitudes and values, illuminate his social and spiritual relationships, preserve vivid traces of the literary and aesthetic evolution of Latin literature under the influence of Christian ideas, and document the emergence of the late antique cult of the saints."<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>We know about his buildings in honor of Saint Felix from literary and archaeological evidence, especially from his long letter to Sulpicius Severus describing the arrangement of the building and its decoration.  He includes a detailed description of the <a href="/wiki/Apse" title="Apse">apse</a> <a href="/wiki/Mosaic" title="Mosaic">mosaic</a> over the main altar and gives the text for a long inscription he had written to be put on the wall under the image.  By explaining how he intended the visitors to understand the image over the altar, Paulinus provided rare insight into the intentions of a patron of art in the later Empire. He explained his project in a Poem dedicated to another great catechist, St <a href="/wiki/Nicetas_of_Remesiana" title="Nicetas of Remesiana">Nicetas of Remesiana</a>, as he accompanied him on a visit to his basilicas: "I now want you to contemplate the paintings that unfold in a long series on the walls of the painted porticos.&#160;... It seemed to us useful to portray sacred themes in painting throughout the house of Felix, in the hope that when the peasants see the painted figure, these images will awaken interest in their astonished minds."<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In later life Paulinus, by then a highly respected church authority, participated in multiple church synods investigating various ecclesiastical controversies of the time, including <a href="/wiki/Pelagianism" title="Pelagianism">Pelagianism</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legend">Legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paulinus_of_Nola&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Gregory_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Gregory the Great">Gregory the Great</a> recounts a popular story that alleges that when the Vandals raided Campania, a poor widow came to Paulinus for help when her only son had been carried off by the son-in-law of the Vandal king. Having exhausted his resources in ransoming other captives, Paulinus said, "Such as I have I give thee", and went to Africa to exchange places with the widow's son. There Paulinus was accepted in place of the widow's son, and employed as gardener. After a time the king found out that his son-in-law's slave was the great Bishop of Nola. He at once set him free, granting him also the freedom of all the captive townsmen of Nola.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a>, "the historical truth of this episode is disputed, but the figure of a Bishop with a great heart who knew how to make himself close to his people in the sorrowful trials of the barbarian invasions lives on."<sup id="cite_ref-Benedict_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Benedict-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Relics">Relics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paulinus_of_Nola&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Relics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Giglio_2011_Franklin_Sq.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cf/Giglio_2011_Franklin_Sq.JPG/220px-Giglio_2011_Franklin_Sq.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="275" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cf/Giglio_2011_Franklin_Sq.JPG/330px-Giglio_2011_Franklin_Sq.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cf/Giglio_2011_Franklin_Sq.JPG/440px-Giglio_2011_Franklin_Sq.JPG 2x" data-file-width="576" data-file-height="720" /></a><figcaption>Giglio in Franklin Square, New York City, 2011</figcaption></figure>
<p>About 800 Prince <a href="/wiki/Grimoald_III_of_Benevento" title="Grimoald III of Benevento">Grimoald III of Benevento</a> removed Paulinus's bones as <a href="/wiki/Relics" class="mw-redirect" title="Relics">relics</a>.
</p><p>From the 11th century they rested at the church of Saint Adalbert, now Saint Bartholomew, on the <a href="/wiki/Tiber_Island" title="Tiber Island">island in the Tiber</a> in Rome. In 1908 <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_X" title="Pope Pius X">Pope Pius X</a> permitted them to be <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relics)" class="mw-redirect" title="Translation (relics)">translated</a> to the new cathedral at Nola, where they were reinterred on 15 May 1909.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The bones are now found in the small <a href="/wiki/Sicily" title="Sicily">Sicilian</a> city of <a href="/wiki/Sutera" title="Sutera">Sutera</a>, where they dedicate a feast day, and conduct a procession for the saint at Easter each year.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Modern_devotion_to_Saint_Paulinus">Modern devotion to Saint Paulinus</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Paulinus_of_Nola&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Modern devotion to Saint Paulinus">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The people of modern-day Nola and the surrounding regions remain devoted to Saint Paulinus.  His feast day is celebrated annually in Nola during <i>"La Festa dei Gigli"</i> (the Feast of the Lilies), in which Gigli and several large statues in honor of the saint, placed on towers, are carried upon the shoulders of the faithful around the city. In the United States the descendants of Italian immigrants from <a href="/wiki/Nola" title="Nola">Nola</a> and <a href="/wiki/Brusciano" title="Brusciano">Brusciano</a> continue the tradition in <a href="/wiki/Williamsburg,_Brooklyn#Feast_of_St._Paulinus_and_Our_Lady_of_Mount_Carmel" title="Williamsburg, Brooklyn">Brooklyn</a>. This proud tradition is also kept alive in East Harlem, held on Giglio Way by the Giglio Society of East Harlem and on Long Island in West Hempstead with the Sons of San Paulino di Nola.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Paulinus is also venerated in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, where his feast day commemorated on <a href="/wiki/January_23_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics)#Pre-Schism_Western_saints" title="January 23 (Eastern Orthodox liturgics)">23 January</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
