<p><b>Peter Canisius</b> <span class="noexcerpt nowraplinks" style="font-size:85%; font-weight:normal;"><a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">SJ</a></span> (<a href="/wiki/Dutch_language" title="Dutch language">Dutch</a>: <i lang="nl">Pieter Kanis</i>; 8 May 1521 – 21 December 1597) was a Dutch <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a> <a href="/wiki/Catholic_priest" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic priest">Catholic priest</a>. He became known for his strong support for the Catholic faith during the <a href="/wiki/Protestant_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant Reformation">Protestant Reformation</a> in <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a>, <a href="/wiki/Austria" title="Austria">Austria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bohemia" title="Bohemia">Bohemia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Moravia" title="Moravia">Moravia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Switzerland" title="Switzerland">Switzerland</a> and the <a href="/wiki/British_Isles" title="British Isles">British Isles</a>. The restoration of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> in Germany after the <a href="/wiki/Protestant_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant Reformation">Protestant Reformation</a> is largely attributed to the work there of the Society of Jesus, which he led. He is <a href="/wiki/Veneration" title="Veneration">venerated</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and as a <a href="/wiki/Doctor_of_the_Church" title="Doctor of the Church">Doctor of the Church</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Canisius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He was born in 1521 in <a href="/wiki/Nijmegen" title="Nijmegen">Nijmegen</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Guelders" class="mw-redirect" title="Guelders">Duchy of Guelders</a>, which, until 1549, was part of the <a href="/wiki/Habsburg_Netherlands" title="Habsburg Netherlands">Habsburg Netherlands</a> within the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire" title="Holy Roman Empire">Holy Roman Empire</a> and is now the <a href="/wiki/Netherlands" title="Netherlands">Netherlands</a>. His father was a wealthy <a href="/wiki/Burgermeister" class="mw-redirect" title="Burgermeister">burgermeister</a>, Jacob Kanis. His mother, Ægidia van Houweningen, died shortly after Peter's birth. He was sent to study at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Cologne" title="University of Cologne">University of Cologne</a>, where he earned a <a href="/wiki/Master%27s_degree" title="Master&#39;s degree">master's degree</a> in 1540, at the age of 19.<sup id="cite_ref-Braunsberger_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Braunsberger-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While there, he met <a href="/wiki/Peter_Faber" title="Peter Faber">Peter Faber</a>, one of the founders of the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">Society of Jesus</a>. Through him, Canisius became the first Dutchman to join the newly founded <a href="/wiki/Society_of_Jesus" class="mw-redirect" title="Society of Jesus">Society of Jesus</a> in 1543. Through his <a href="/wiki/Preaching" class="mw-redirect" title="Preaching">preaching</a> and writings, Peter Canisius became one of the most influential Catholics of his time. He supervised the founding and maintenance of the first <a href="/wiki/German_language" title="German language">German-speaking</a> Jesuit colleges, often with little resources at hand. At the same time he preached in the city and vicinity, and debated and taught in the university.<sup id="cite_ref-Braunsberger_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Braunsberger-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Due to his frequent travels between the colleges, a tedious and dangerous occupation at the time, he became known as the Second Apostle of Germany.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>

<p>Canisius exerted a strong influence on the <a href="/wiki/Ferdinand_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Ferdinand I, Holy Roman Emperor">Emperor Ferdinand I</a>. The king's eldest son (later <a href="/wiki/Maximilian_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Maximilian II, Holy Roman Emperor">Maximilian II</a>) appointed Phauser, a married priest, to the office of court preacher. Canisius warned Ferdinand I, verbally and in writing, and opposed Phauser in public disputations. Maximilian was obliged to dismiss Phauser and, on this account, the rest of his life he harboured a grudge against Canisius.<sup id="cite_ref-Braunsberger_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Braunsberger-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1547 he attended several sessions of the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Trent" title="Council of Trent">Council of Trent</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Canisius was an influential teacher and preacher, especially through his "German <a href="/wiki/Catechism" title="Catechism">Catechism</a>", a book which defined the basic principles of Catholicism in the German language and made them more accessible to readers in German-speaking countries. He was offered the post of <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Vienna" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Vienna">Bishop of Vienna</a> in 1554, but declined in order to continue his traveling and teachings. He did, however, serve as administrator of the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Vienna" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Vienna">Diocese of Vienna</a> for one year, until a new bishop was appointed for it.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>He moved to Germany, where he was one of the main Catholic theologians at the <a href="/wiki/Colloquy_of_Worms_(1557)" title="Colloquy of Worms (1557)">Colloquy of Worms</a> in 1557, and later served as the main <a href="/wiki/Preacher" title="Preacher">preacher</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Cathedral_of_Augsburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Cathedral of Augsburg">Cathedral of Augsburg</a> from 1559 to 1568, where he strongly witnessed to his faith on three or four occasions each week. Canisius was renowned as a popular preacher.<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> In 1562 he founded what was to become the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Innsbruck" title="University of Innsbruck">University of Innsbruck</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>
In <i>Christ The King – Lord of History</i> by Anne W. Carroll, it states:</p><blockquote><p>Protestantism had made much headway in Germany because many intellectuals had adopted it, making Catholicism appear to be the religion of the ignorant. By his debates, his writing and his teachings, Peter showed that Catholicism was thoroughly rational, that the Protestant arguments were not convincing.</p><p>By his efforts, Peter won <a href="/wiki/Bavaria" title="Bavaria">Bavaria</a> (southern Germany) and the <a href="/wiki/Rhineland" title="Rhineland">Rhineland</a> (central Germany) back to the Catholic Church. He also won converts in <a href="/wiki/Austria" title="Austria">Austria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hungary" title="Hungary">Hungary</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bohemia" title="Bohemia">Bohemia</a> and <a href="/wiki/Poland" title="Poland">Poland</a>. Poland had become largely Protestant, but thanks to the efforts of Peter and other Jesuits, it returned to the Church and is still Catholic today despite Communist persecution.</p></blockquote>
<p>By the time he left Germany, the Society of Jesus in Germany had evolved from a small band of priests into a powerful tool of the <a href="/wiki/Counter-Reformation" title="Counter-Reformation">Counter-Reformation</a>. Canisius spent the last twenty years of his life in <a href="/wiki/Fribourg" title="Fribourg">Fribourg</a>, where he founded the Jesuit <a href="https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coll%C3%A8ge_Saint-Michel_(Fribourg)" class="extiw" title="fr:Collège Saint-Michel (Fribourg)">Collège Saint-Michel</a>, which trained generations of young men for careers and future university studies.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> During this period, Canisius's work "was translated into almost every language of Europe";<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> for example, his <i>Summa Doctrinae Christianae</i> (1554) was translated into Scots by Adam King and published in 1588.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>In 1591, at the age of 70, Canisius suffered a <a href="/wiki/Stroke" title="Stroke">stroke</a> which left him partially paralyzed, but he continued to preach and write with the aid of a secretary until his death in Fribourg.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He was initially buried at the Church of St Nicholas. His remains were later transferred to the church of the Jesuit College, which he had founded and where he had spent the last year of his life, and interred in front of the main altar of the church; the room he occupied during those last months is now a chapel open for the veneration of the faithful.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Pastoral_strategy">Pastoral strategy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Canisius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Pastoral strategy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2><p>
Canisius lived during the height of the <a href="/wiki/Protestant_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant Reformation">Protestant Reformation</a> and dedicated much of his work to the clarification of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Faith</a> in light of the emergence of the new <a href="/wiki/Protestantism" title="Protestantism">Protestant</a> doctrines. His lasting contribution is his three catechisms, which he published in Latin and German, which became widespread and popular in Catholic regions.<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> In his fight with German Protestantism, he requested much more flexibility from Rome, arguing:<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style></p><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>If you treat them right, the Germans will give you everything. Many err in matters of faith, but without arrogance. They err the German way, mostly honest, a bit simple-minded, but very open for everything Lutheran. An honest explanation of the faith would be much more effective than a polemical attack against reformers.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>He rejected attacks against <a href="/wiki/John_Calvin" title="John Calvin">John Calvin</a> and <a href="/wiki/Melanchthon" class="mw-redirect" title="Melanchthon">Melanchthon</a>: "With words like these, we don't cure patients, we make them incurable."<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mariology_of_Canisius">Mariology of Canisius</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Canisius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Mariology of Canisius">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Mariology_of_the_saints" title="Mariology of the saints">Mariology of the saints</a></div>
<p>Canisius taught that, while there are many roads leading to Jesus Christ, for him the <a href="/wiki/Veneration" title="Veneration">veneration</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Blessed Virgin Mary">Blessed Virgin Mary</a> is the best.<sup id="cite_ref-Stegmüller,_1052_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Stegmüller,_1052-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> His sermons and letters document a clear preoccupation with Marian veneration.<sup id="cite_ref-Stegmüller,_1052_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Stegmüller,_1052-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Under the heading "prayer" he explains the <i><a href="/wiki/Ave_Maria" class="mw-redirect" title="Ave Maria">Ave Maria</a></i> (Hail Mary) as the basis for Catholic Marian piety.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Less known are his Marian books, in which he published prayers and contemplative texts. To the <a href="/wiki/Hail_Mary" title="Hail Mary">Hail Mary</a> he added the sentence: <i>Holy Mary, Mother of God, pray for us sinners.</i><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Eleven years later it was included in the <a href="/wiki/Catechism_of_the_Council_of_Trent" class="mw-redirect" title="Catechism of the Council of Trent">Catechism of the Council of Trent</a> of 1566.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Canisius published an applied Mariology for preachers, in which Mary is described in tender and warm words.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> He actively promoted the <a href="/wiki/Sodality_of_Our_Lady" title="Sodality of Our Lady">sodalities of our Lady</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Rosary" title="Rosary">rosary</a> associations.
</p><p>Theologically, Canisius defended Catholic Mariology in his 1577 book, <i>De Maria Virgine Incomparabili et Dei Genitrice Sacrosancta Libri Quinque</i>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> The book was ordered by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_V" title="Pope Pius V">Pope Pius V</a> to present a factual presentation of the Catholic Marian teachings in the Bible, the early Christians, the Church Fathers and contemporary theology. Canisius explains and documents Church teachings through the ages regarding the person and character of Mary, her virtues and youth.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> He traces historical documents about the perpetual virginity of Mary, and her freedom from sin.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> He explains the dogma of "Mother of God" with numerous quotations from the fathers after the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Ephesus" title="Council of Ephesus">Council of Ephesus</a>. He shows that Church teaching has not changed.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> He answers the <i><a href="/wiki/Sola_scriptura" title="Sola scriptura">sola scriptura</a></i> arguments of Protestants by analyzing the biblical basis for mariology.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Book five explains the Catholic view of the assumption as living faith for centuries, supported by most prominent Church writers.
</p><p>From today's perspective, Canisius clearly erred in some of his sources, but, because of his factual analysis of original sources, it is considered as representing one of the best theological achievements in the 16th century.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Canisius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Canisius was <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_IX" title="Pope Pius IX">Pope Pius IX</a> in 1864, and later <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> and declared a <a href="/wiki/Doctor_of_the_Church" title="Doctor of the Church">Doctor of the Church</a> on 21 May 1925 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XI" title="Pope Pius XI">Pope Pius XI</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> was included in the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> in 1926, for celebration on 27 April. In the <a href="/wiki/Liturgy" title="Liturgy">liturgical</a> reform of 1969, it was moved to 21 December, the anniversary of his death, the normal day for celebrating a saint's entry into heaven (although it is still kept by the Society of Jesus on 27 April).
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Peter_Canisius&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Relieken_canisius.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Relieken_canisius.jpg/200px-Relieken_canisius.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="150" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Relieken_canisius.jpg/300px-Relieken_canisius.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Relieken_canisius.jpg/400px-Relieken_canisius.jpg 2x" data-file-width="560" data-file-height="420" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Relieken_canisius.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Relics associated with St Peter Canisius</div></div></div>
<p>In recognition there of Canisius' early work in the establishment of Jesuit education, there are multiple educational institutions named for him. Among them is the Canisius College for seminarians in <a href="/wiki/Vienna" title="Vienna">Vienna</a>, Austria, the first institution named for him, as well as Canisius College, a Jesuit secondary school in his hometown of Nijmegen and the <a href="/wiki/Alma_mater" title="Alma mater">alma mater</a> of <a href="/wiki/Peter_Hans_Kolvenbach" title="Peter Hans Kolvenbach">Peter Hans Kolvenbach</a>, a recent <a href="/wiki/Superior_General_of_the_Society_of_Jesus" title="Superior General of the Society of Jesus">Superior General of the Society of Jesus</a>. Another <a href="/wiki/Canisius_College" title="Canisius College">Canisius College</a>, a university, and <a href="/wiki/Canisius_High_School" title="Canisius High School">Canisius High School</a>, a secondary school, are located in <a href="/wiki/Buffalo,_New_York" title="Buffalo, New York">Buffalo, New York</a>. Furthermore, a Jesuit-run Canisius Kolleg can be found in Berlin, Germany. There are also two secondary schools named after Canisius, <a href="/wiki/Kolese_Kanisius" class="mw-redirect" title="Kolese Kanisius">Kolese Kanisius</a> (<i>Collegium Canisianum</i> or <i>Canisius College</i>), in <a href="/wiki/Jakarta,_Indonesia" class="mw-redirect" title="Jakarta, Indonesia">Jakarta, Indonesia</a>, and Canisianum Roman Catholic HS in the Omusati Region of Namibia, Africa.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In addition, there is a primary school: Basisschool Petrus Canisius in Puth in <a href="/wiki/Limburg_(Netherlands)" title="Limburg (Netherlands)">Limburg</a>, Netherlands. In 1850 the Canisius Hospital was established on the corner of the Houtmarkt and the Pauwelstraat in Nijmegen. In 1974 it merged with Wilhelmina Hospital located at the Weg door Jonkerbos in Nijmegen, to become Canisius-Wilhemina Hospital.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>The <i>Apologetisch Vereniging St. Petrus Canisius</i> (St. Peter Canisius Association for <a href="/wiki/Apologetics" title="Apologetics">Apologetics</a>) was founded in the Netherlands in 1904 to defend the Catholic Church against <a href="/wiki/Socialism" title="Socialism">socialism</a> and <a href="/wiki/Liberalism" title="Liberalism">liberalism</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>From the middle of the nineteenth century on German churchmen, including <a href="/wiki/Michael_von_Faulhaber" title="Michael von Faulhaber">Michael Cardinal von Faulhaber</a> (1869–1952), considered Canisius as a new "Apostle of Germany", a successor of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Boniface" title="Saint Boniface">Saint Boniface</a>, for his importance for German Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
