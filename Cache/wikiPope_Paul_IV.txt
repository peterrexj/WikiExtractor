<p><b>Pope Paul IV</b>, born <b>Gian Pietro Carafa</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Paulus IV</i>; <a href="/wiki/Italian_language" title="Italian language">Italian</a>: <i lang="it">Paolo IV</i>; 28 June 1476&#160;– 18 August 1559) was head of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> and ruler of the <a href="/wiki/Papal_States" title="Papal States">Papal States</a> from 23 May 1555 to his death, in August 1559.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  While serving as papal nuncio in <a href="/wiki/Spain" title="Spain">Spain</a>, he developed an anti-Spanish outlook that later coloured his papacy. In response to an invasion of part of the Papal States by Spain during his papacy, he called for a French military intervention. After a defeat of the French and with Spanish troops at the edge of Rome, the Papacy and Spain reached a compromise: French and Spanish forces left the Papal States and the Pope thereafter adopted a neutral stance between France and Spain.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Carafa was appointed bishop of Chieti, but resigned in 1524 in order to found with <a href="/wiki/Saint_Cajetan" title="Saint Cajetan">St. Cajetan</a> the Congregation of Clerics Regular (<a href="/wiki/Theatines" title="Theatines">Theatines</a>). Recalled to Rome, and made <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Naples" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Naples">Archbishop of Naples</a>, he worked to re-organize the <a href="/wiki/Inquisitorial_system#History" title="Inquisitorial system">Inquisitorial system</a> in response to the emerging <a href="/wiki/Protestant" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant">Protestant</a> movement in Europe, any dialogue with which he opposed (the inquisition itself had been first instituted by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Innocent_III" title="Pope Innocent III">Pope Innocent III</a> who first regulated inquisitional procedure in the 13th century). Carafa was elected pope in 1555 through the influence of Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Alessandro_Farnese_(cardinal)" title="Alessandro Farnese (cardinal)">Alessandro Farnese</a> in the face of opposition from Emperor Charles V. His papacy was characterized by strong nationalism in reaction to the influence of <a href="/wiki/Philip_II_of_Spain" title="Philip II of Spain">Philip II of Spain</a> and the Habsburgs. The appointment of <a href="/wiki/Carlo_Carafa" title="Carlo Carafa">Carlo Carafa</a> as <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_Nephew" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinal Nephew">Cardinal Nephew</a> damaged the papacy further, and scandals forced Paul to remove him from office. He curbed some clerical abuses in Rome, but his methods were seen as harsh. He would introduce the first modern <a href="/wiki/Index_Librorum_Prohibitorum" title="Index Librorum Prohibitorum">Index Librorum Prohibitorum</a> or "Index of Prohibited Books" banning works he saw as in error. In spite of his advanced age, he was a tireless worker and issued new decrees and regulations daily, unrelenting in his determination to keep Protestants and recently immigrated <a href="/wiki/Marrano" title="Marrano">Marranos</a> from gaining influence in the Papal States. He had some hundred of the Marranos of Ancona thrown into prison; 50 were sentenced by the tribunal of the Inquisition and 25 of these were burned at the stake. Paul IV issued the <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">Papal bull</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Cum_nimis_absurdum" title="Cum nimis absurdum">Cum nimis absurdum</a></i>, which confined Jews in Rome to the neighbourhood <i>claustro degli Ebrei</i> ("enclosure of the Hebrews"), later known as the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Ghetto" title="Roman Ghetto">Roman Ghetto</a>.  He died highly unpopular, to the point that his family rushed his burial to make sure his body would not be desecrated by a popular uprising.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Paul_IV&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gian Pietro Carafa was born in <a href="/wiki/Capriglia_Irpina" title="Capriglia Irpina">Capriglia Irpina</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Avellino" title="Avellino">Avellino</a>, into the prominent <a href="/wiki/House_of_Carafa" title="House of Carafa">Carafa</a> family of <a href="/wiki/Naples" title="Naples">Naples</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> His father Giovanni Antonio Carafa died in <a href="/wiki/West_Flanders" title="West Flanders">West Flanders</a> in 1516 and his mother Vittoria Camponeschi was the daughter of Pietro Lalle Camponeschi, 5th Conte di <a href="/wiki/Montorio_nei_Frentani" title="Montorio nei Frentani">Montorio</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Naples" title="Naples">Neapolitan</a> nobleman, and <a href="/wiki/Don_(honorific)" title="Don (honorific)">Dona</a> Maria de Noronha, a <a href="/wiki/Portuguese_people" title="Portuguese people">Portuguese</a> noblewoman of the House of <a href="/wiki/Pereira_(surname)" title="Pereira (surname)">Pereira</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (February 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Church_career">Church career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Paul_IV&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Church career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop">Bishop</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Paul_IV&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Bishop">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>He was mentored by Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Oliviero_Carafa" title="Oliviero Carafa">Oliviero Carafa</a>, his relative, who resigned the <a href="/wiki/See_of_Chieti" class="mw-redirect" title="See of Chieti">see of Chieti</a> (Latin <i>Theate</i>) in his favour.  Under the direction of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_X" title="Pope Leo X">Pope Leo X</a>, he was ambassador to England and then papal <a href="/wiki/Nuncio" title="Nuncio">nuncio</a> in Spain, where he conceived a violent detestation of Spanish rule that affected the policies of his later papacy.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1524, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_VII" title="Pope Clement VII">Pope Clement VII</a> allowed Carafa to resign his <a href="/wiki/Benefice" title="Benefice">benefices</a> and join the <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a> and newly founded Congregation of Clerks Regular, popularly called the <a href="/wiki/Theatines" title="Theatines">Theatines</a>, after Carafa's <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_see" title="Episcopal see">see</a> of <i>Theate.</i>  Following the <a href="/wiki/Sack_of_Rome_(1527)" title="Sack of Rome (1527)">sack of Rome</a> in 1527, the order moved to <a href="/wiki/Venice" title="Venice">Venice</a>.  But Carafa was recalled to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> by the reform-minded <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_III" title="Pope Paul III">Pope Paul III</a> (1534–49), to sit on a committee of reform of the papal court, an appointment that forecasted an end to a <a href="/wiki/Humanism" title="Humanism">humanist</a> papacy and a revival of <a href="/wiki/Scholasticism" title="Scholasticism">scholasticism</a>, as Carafa was a disciple of <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas" title="Thomas Aquinas">Thomas Aquinas</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Cardinal">Cardinal</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Paul_IV&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Cardinal">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In December 1536 he was made <a href="/wiki/Cardinal_(Catholic_Church)" title="Cardinal (Catholic Church)">Cardinal-Priest</a> of <i><a href="/wiki/S._Pancrazio" class="mw-redirect" title="S. Pancrazio">S. Pancrazio</a></i> and then <a href="/wiki/Archbishop" title="Archbishop">Archbishop</a> of <a href="/wiki/Naples" title="Naples">Naples</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Diet_of_Regensburg_(1541)" title="Diet of Regensburg (1541)">Regensburg Colloquy</a> in 1541 failed to achieve any measure of reconciliation between Catholics and Protestants in Europe, but instead saw a number of prominent Italians defect to the Protestant camp. In response, Carafa was able to persuade Pope Paul III to set up a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Inquisition" title="Roman Inquisition">Roman Inquisition</a>, modelled on the <a href="/wiki/Spanish_Inquisition" title="Spanish Inquisition">Spanish Inquisition</a> with himself as one of the Inquisitors-General. The <a href="/wiki/Papal_Bull" class="mw-redirect" title="Papal Bull">Papal Bull</a> was promulgated in 1542 and Carafa vowed, "Even if my own father were a heretic, I would gather the wood to burn him".<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Election_as_pope">Election as pope</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Paul_IV&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Election as pope">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/May_1555_papal_conclave" title="May 1555 papal conclave">May 1555 papal conclave</a></div>
<p>He was a surprise choice as pope to succeed <a href="/wiki/Pope_Marcellus_II" title="Pope Marcellus II">Pope Marcellus II</a> (1555); his severe and unbending character combined with his advanced age and Italian patriotism meant under normal circumstances he would have declined the honor. He accepted apparently because <a href="/wiki/Emperor_Charles_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Emperor Charles V">Emperor Charles V</a> was opposed to his accession.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Carafa, elected on 23 May 1555, took the name of "Paul IV" in honor of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_III" title="Pope Paul III">Pope Paul III</a> who named him as a cardinal. He was <a href="/wiki/Papal_coronation" title="Papal coronation">crowned</a> as pope on 26 May 1555 by the <a href="/wiki/Protodeacon" title="Protodeacon">protodeacon</a>. He formally took possession of the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint_John_Lateran" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica of Saint John Lateran">Basilica of Saint John Lateran</a> on 28 October 1555.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Papacy">Papacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Paul_IV&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Papacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>As pope, Paul IV's nationalism was a driving force; he used the office to preserve some liberties in the face of fourfold foreign occupation. Like <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_III" title="Pope Paul III">Pope Paul III</a>, he was an enemy of the <a href="/wiki/Colonna_family" title="Colonna family">Colonna family</a>. His treatment of <a href="/wiki/Giovanna_d%27Aragona" title="Giovanna d&#39;Aragona">Giovanna d'Aragona</a>, who had married into that family, drew further negative comment from Venice because she had long been a patron of artists and writers.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Paul IV was displeased at the French signing a five-year truce with Spain in February 1556 (in the midst of the <a href="/wiki/Italian_War_of_1551%E2%80%931559" title="Italian War of 1551–1559">Italian War of 1551–1559</a>) and urged King Henry II to join the Papal States in an invasion of Spanish Naples. On 1 September 1556, King Philip II responded by preemptively invading the Papal States with 12,000 men under the <a href="/wiki/Fernando_%C3%81lvarez_de_Toledo,_3rd_Duke_of_Alba" title="Fernando Álvarez de Toledo, 3rd Duke of Alba">Duke of Alba</a>. French forces approaching from the north were defeated and forced to withdraw at Civitella in August 1557.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The papal armies were left exposed and were defeated, with Spanish troops arriving at the edge of Rome. Out of fear of another sack of Rome, Paul IV agreed to the Duke of Alba's demand for the Papal States to declare neutrality by signing the Peace of Cave-Palestrina on 12 September 1557. <a href="/wiki/Emperor_Charles_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Emperor Charles V">Emperor Charles V</a> criticized the peace agreement as being overly generous to the Pope.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As <a href="/wiki/Cardinal-nephew" title="Cardinal-nephew">cardinal-nephew</a>, <a href="/wiki/Carlo_Carafa" title="Carlo Carafa">Carlo Carafa</a> became his uncle's chief political adviser. Having accepted a pension from the French, Cardinal Carafa worked to secure a French alliance.<sup id="cite_ref-John_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-John-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Carlo's older brother <a href="/wiki/Giovanni_Carafa,_Duke_of_Paliano" title="Giovanni Carafa, Duke of Paliano">Giovanni</a> was made commander of the papal forces and <a href="/wiki/Duke_of_Paliano" class="mw-redirect" title="Duke of Paliano">Duke of Paliano</a> after the pro-Spanish <a href="/wiki/Colonna_family" title="Colonna family">Colonna</a> were deprived of that town in 1556. Another nephew, <a href="/wiki/Antonio_Carafa_(cardinal)" title="Antonio Carafa (cardinal)">Antonio</a>, was given command of the papal guard and made Marquis of Montebello. Their conduct became notorious in Rome. However, at the conclusion of the disastrous war with <a href="/wiki/Philip_II_of_Spain" title="Philip II of Spain">Philip II of Spain</a> in the Italian War, and after many scandals, Paul IV publicly disgraced his nephews and banished them from Rome in 1559.<sup id="cite_ref-John_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-John-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>With the <a href="/wiki/Protestant_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant Reformation">Protestant Reformation</a>, the papacy required all Roman Catholic rulers to consider <a href="/wiki/Protestant" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant">Protestant</a> rulers as <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heretics</a>, thus making their realms illegitimate.  At the time of Paul's election, Queen <a href="/wiki/Mary_I_of_England" title="Mary I of England">Mary I of England</a> was two years into her reign, and was rolling back the <a href="/wiki/English_Reformation" title="English Reformation">English Reformation</a> that had occurred under her half-brother Edward VI.  Paul IV issued a <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">papal bull</a> in 1555, <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Ilius, per quem Reges regnant</i></span></i>, removing all Church measures against the English government, and further recognising Mary and her husband Philip as <a href="/wiki/Monarchy_of_Ireland" title="Monarchy of Ireland">King and Queen of Ireland</a>, rather than merely being "lord".<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>  Despite the bull, his relations with England were not positive.  Paul IV had known Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Reginald_Pole" title="Reginald Pole">Reginald Pole</a> while Pole was living in Italy and the two had been members of the <a href="/wiki/Spirituali" title="Spirituali">spirituali</a> together.  Pole was the leader of Mary's efforts, but Paul IV seems to have hated Pole and become convinced he was a crypto-Protestant.  Combined with hostility towards Spain and thus Mary's husband, Paul IV refused to allow any English bishops to be appointed, and began inquisitorial discipline proceedings against Pole, leading to the "farcical" situation that by 1558, the most serious opponent of English Catholicism was the Pope himself.<sup id="cite_ref-ryrie_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ryrie-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>  He also angered people in England by insisting on the restitution of property confiscated during the <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries" title="Dissolution of the monasteries">dissolution of the monasteries</a>.  After Mary's death, he rejected the succession of <a href="/wiki/Elizabeth_I_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Elizabeth I of England">Elizabeth I of England</a> to the Crown.<sup id="cite_ref-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Catholic_Encyclopaedia2-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Paul IV was violently opposed to the liberal Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Giovanni_Morone" title="Giovanni Morone">Giovanni Morone</a>, whom he strongly suspected of being a hidden Protestant, so much that he had him imprisoned. In order to prevent Morone from succeeding him and imposing what he believed to be his Protestant beliefs on the Church, Pope Paul IV codified the Catholic Law excluding heretics and non-Catholics from receiving or legitimately becoming pope, in the bull <i><a href="/wiki/Cum_ex_apostolatus_officio" title="Cum ex apostolatus officio">Cum ex apostolatus officio</a></i>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Paul IV was rigidly orthodox, austere in life, and authoritarian in manner. He affirmed the Catholic doctrine of <i><a href="/wiki/Extra_ecclesiam_nulla_salus" class="mw-redirect" title="Extra ecclesiam nulla salus">extra ecclesiam nulla salus</a></i> ('outside the Church there is no salvation'), and used the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Office" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Office">Holy Office</a> to suppress the <a href="/wiki/Spirituali" title="Spirituali">Spirituali</a>, a Catholic group deemed heretical. The strengthening of the Inquisition continued under Paul IV, and few could consider themselves safe by virtue of position in his drive to reform the Church; even cardinals he disliked could be imprisoned.<sup id="cite_ref-DurantW_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DurantW-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> He appointed inquisitor Michele Ghislieri, the future <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_V" title="Pope Pius V">Pope Pius V</a>, to the position of Supreme Inquisitor despite the fact as Inquisitor of <a href="/wiki/Como" title="Como">Como</a>, Ghislieri's persecutions had inspired a citywide rebellion, forcing him to flee in fear for his life.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:VicoloCapocciutoInGhettoByRoeslerFranz.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/VicoloCapocciutoInGhettoByRoeslerFranz.jpg/220px-VicoloCapocciutoInGhettoByRoeslerFranz.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="158" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/VicoloCapocciutoInGhettoByRoeslerFranz.jpg/330px-VicoloCapocciutoInGhettoByRoeslerFranz.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/VicoloCapocciutoInGhettoByRoeslerFranz.jpg/440px-VicoloCapocciutoInGhettoByRoeslerFranz.jpg 2x" data-file-width="487" data-file-height="349" /></a><figcaption>Vicolo Capocciuto, Roman Ghetto by Franz Roesler c.1880</figcaption></figure>
<p>On 17 July 1555, Paul IV issued one of the most infamous papal bulls in Church history.  The <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">bull</a>, <i><a href="/wiki/Cum_nimis_absurdum" title="Cum nimis absurdum">Cum nimis absurdum</a></i>, ordered the creation of a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Ghetto" title="Roman Ghetto">Jewish ghetto in Rome</a>.  The pope set its borders near the Rione Sant'Angelo, an area where large numbers of Jews already resided, and ordered it walled off from the rest of the city. A single gate, locked every day at sundown, was the only means of reaching the rest of the city. The Jews themselves were forced to pay all design and construction costs related to the project, which came to a total of roughly 300 <i>scudi</i>. The bull restricted Jews in other ways as well. They were forbidden to have more than one <a href="/wiki/Synagogue" title="Synagogue">synagogue</a> per city—leading, in Rome alone, to the destruction of seven "excess" places of worship.  All Jews were forced to wear distinctive yellow <a href="/wiki/Jewish_hat" title="Jewish hat">hats</a>, especially outside the ghetto, and they were forbidden to trade in everything but food and secondhand clothes.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>  Christians of all ages were encouraged to treat the Jews as second-class citizens; for a Jew to defy a Christian in any way was to invite severe punishment, often at the hands of a mob.  By the end of Paul IV's five-year reign, the number of Roman Jews had dropped by half.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>  Yet his anti-Jewish legacy endured for over 300 years: the ghetto he established ceased to exist only with the dissolution of the <a href="/wiki/Papal_States" title="Papal States">Papal States</a> in 1870.  Its walls were torn down in 1888.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/Leopold_von_Ranke" title="Leopold von Ranke">Leopold von Ranke</a>, a rigid austerity and an earnest zeal for the restoration of primitive habits became the dominant tendency of his papacy. Monks who had left their monasteries were expelled from the city and from the Papal States. He would no longer tolerate the practice by which one man had been allowed to enjoy the revenues of an office while delegating its duties to another.<sup id="cite_ref-Wines_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Wines-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>All begging was forbidden. Even the collection of alms for Masses, which had previously been made by the clergy, was discontinued. A medal was struck representing Christ driving the money changers from the Temple. Paul IV put in place a reform of the papal administration designed to stamp out trafficking of principal positions in the Curia.<sup id="cite_ref-John_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-John-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> All secular offices, from the highest to the lowest, were assigned to others based on merit. Important economies were made, and taxes were proportionately remitted. Paul IV established a chest, of which only he held the key, for the purpose of receiving all complaints that anyone desired to make.<sup id="cite_ref-Wines_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Wines-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During his papacy, censorship reached new heights.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDeming2012&#91;httpsbooksgooglecombooksidlXGEXih4yLYCpgPA36_p.&amp;nbsp;36&#93;_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeming2012[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidlXGEXih4yLYCpgPA36_p.&amp;nbsp;36]-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Among his first acts as pope was to cut off <a href="/wiki/Michelangelo" title="Michelangelo">Michelangelo</a>'s pension, and he ordered the nudes of <i>The Last Judgment</i> in the <a href="/wiki/Sistine_Chapel" title="Sistine Chapel">Sistine Chapel</a> be painted more modestly (a request that Michelangelo ignored) (the beginning of the Vatican's <a href="/wiki/Fig_leaf" title="Fig leaf">Fig leaf</a> campaign). Paul IV also introduced the <a href="/wiki/Index_Librorum_Prohibitorum" title="Index Librorum Prohibitorum">Index Librorum Prohibitorum</a> or "Index of Prohibited Books" to <a href="/wiki/Venice" title="Venice">Venice</a>, then an independent and prosperous trading state, in order to crack down on the growing threat of Protestantism. Under his authority, all books written by Protestants were banned, together with Italian and German translations of the Latin Bible.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the Papal States, a <a href="/wiki/Marrano" title="Marrano">Marrano</a> presence was noticeable. In Rome and, even more so, the seaport of Ancona, they thrived under benevolent popes Clement VII (1523–34), Paul III (1534–49), and Julius III (1550–55). They even received a guarantee that if accused of <a href="/wiki/Apostasy" title="Apostasy">apostasy</a> they would be subject only to papal authority. But Paul IV (1555–59), the voice of the Counter-Reformation, dealt them an irreparable blow when he withdrew the protections previously given and initiated a campaign against them. As a result of this, 25 were burned at the stake in the spring of 1556.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Consistories">Consistories</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Paul_IV&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Consistories">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Cardinals_created_by_Paul_IV" title="Cardinals created by Paul IV">Cardinals created by Paul IV</a></div>
<p>Throughout his pontificate, Paul IV named 46 cardinals in four consistories, including his future successor <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_V" title="Pope Pius V">Michele Ghislieri</a> (the future Pope Pius V). According to Robert Maryks, the pope decided to nominate the Jesuit priest <a href="/wiki/Diego_Laynez" title="Diego Laynez">Diego Laínez</a> to the cardinalate. However, Father Alfonso Salmerón warned <a href="/wiki/Saint_Ignatius_of_Loyola" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Ignatius of Loyola">Saint Ignatius of Loyola</a> of this, as did Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Otto_Truchsess_von_Waldburg" title="Otto Truchsess von Waldburg">Otto Truchsess von Waldburg</a>. In response, Father Pedro de Ribadeneyra repeated what the saint had said to him: "If our Lord does not lay down his hand, we will have Master Laínez a cardinal, but I certify to you, if it were, that it be with so much noise that the world would understand how the Society accepts these things".<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Paul_IV&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Paul IV's health began to break down in May 1559. He rallied in July, holding public audiences and attending meetings of the Inquisition. But he engaged in fasting, and the heat of the summer wore him down again. He was bedridden, and on 17 August it became clear he would not live. Cardinals and other officials gathered at his bedside on 18 August, where Paul IV asked them to elect a "righteous and holy" successor and to retain the Inquisition as "the very basis" of the Catholic Church's power. By 2 or 3 pm, he was close to death, and died at 5 pm.<sup id="cite_ref-Levant718_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Levant718-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The people of Rome did not forget what they had suffered because of the war he had brought on the State. Crowds of people gathered at the <a href="/wiki/Campidoglio" class="mw-redirect" title="Campidoglio">Piazza del Campidoglio</a> and began rioting even before Paul IV died.<sup id="cite_ref-Stow41_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Stow41-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> His statue, erected before the Campidoglio just months before, had a yellow hat placed on it (similar to the yellow hat Paul IV had forced Jews to wear in public). After a mock trial, the statue was decapitated.<sup id="cite_ref-Stow41_21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Stow41-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> It was then thrown into the <a href="/wiki/Tiber" title="Tiber">Tiber</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Levant719_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Levant719-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The crowd broke into the three city jails and freed more than 400 prisoners, then broke into the offices of the Inquisition at the Palazzo dell' Inquisizone near to the <a href="/wiki/San_Rocco,_Rome" title="San Rocco, Rome">Church of San Rocco</a>. They murdered the Inquisitor, Tommaso Scotti, and freed 72 prisoners. One of those released was Dominican <a href="/wiki/John_Craig_(minister)" class="mw-redirect" title="John Craig (minister)">John Craig</a>, who later was a colleague of <a href="/wiki/John_Knox" title="John Knox">John Knox</a>. The people ransacked the palace, and then set it afire (destroying the Inquisition's records).<sup id="cite_ref-Levant718_20-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Levant718-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> That same day, or the next day (records are unclear), the crowd attacked the Church of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_sopra_Minerva" title="Santa Maria sopra Minerva">Santa Maria sopra Minerva</a>. The intercession of some local nobility dissuaded them from burning it and killing all those within.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> On the third day of rioting, the crowd removed the Carafa family coat of arms from all churches, monuments, and other buildings in the city.<sup id="cite_ref-Levant719_22-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Levant719-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The crowd dedicated to him the following <a href="/wiki/Pasquino" title="Pasquino">pasquinata</a>:<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<dl><dd><i>Carafa hated by the devil and the sky</i></dd>
<dd><i>is buried here with his rotting corpse,</i></dd>
<dd><i>Erebus has taken the spirit;</i></dd>
<dd><i>he hated peace on earth, our faith he contested.</i></dd>
<dd><i>he ruined the church and the people, men and sky offended;</i></dd>
<dd><i>treacherous friend, suppliant with the army which was fatal to him.</i></dd>
<dd><i>You want to know more? Pope was him and that is enough.</i></dd>
<dd></dd></dl>
<p>Such hostile views have not mellowed much with time; modern historians tend to view his papacy as an especially poor one. His policies stemmed from personal prejudices—against Spain, for example, or the Jews—rather than any overarching political or religious goals. In a time of precarious balance between Catholic and Protestant, his adversarial nature did little to slow the latter's spread across northern Europe.  His anti-Spanish feelings alienated the Habsburgs, arguably the most powerful Catholic rulers in Europe, and his ascetic personal beliefs left him out of touch with the artistic and intellectual movements of his era (he often spoke of whitewashing the <a href="/wiki/Sistine_Chapel_ceiling" title="Sistine Chapel ceiling">Sistine ceiling</a>).  Such a reactionary attitude alienated clergy and laity alike: historian John Julius Norwich calls him "the worst pope of the 16th century."<sup id="cite_ref-:0_14-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Four or five hours after his death, Paul IV's body was taken to the <a href="/wiki/Cappella_Paolina" title="Cappella Paolina">Cappella Paolina</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Apostolic_Palace" title="Apostolic Palace">Apostolic Palace</a>. It <a href="/wiki/Lying_in_repose" title="Lying in repose">lay in repose</a>, and a choir sang the <a href="/wiki/Office_of_the_Dead" title="Office of the Dead">Office of the Dead</a> on the morning of 19 August. Cardinals and many others then paid homage to Paul IV ("kissed the feet of the pope"). The <a href="/wiki/Canon_(priest)" class="mw-redirect" title="Canon (priest)">canons</a> of <a href="/wiki/St._Peter%27s_Basilica" title="St. Peter&#39;s Basilica">St. Peter's Basilica</a> refused to take his body into the basilica unless they were paid the customary money and gifts. Instead, the canons sang the usual <a href="/wiki/Canonical_hours" title="Canonical hours">office</a> in the Cappella del Santissimo Sacramento (Chapel of the Blessed Sacrament). Paul IV's body was taken to the <a href="/wiki/Sistine_Chapel" title="Sistine Chapel">Sistine Chapel</a> in the Apostolic Palace at 6 pm.<sup id="cite_ref-Levant719_22-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Levant719-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Paul IV's nephew, <a href="/wiki/Cardinal-nephew" title="Cardinal-nephew">Cardinal-nephew</a> <a href="/wiki/Carlo_Carafa" title="Carlo Carafa">Carlo Carafa</a>, arrived in Rome late on 19 August. Worried that the rioters might break in and desecrate the pope's corpse, at 10 pm Cardinal Carafa had Pope Paul IV buried without ceremony next to the Cappella del Volto Santo (Chapel of the Holy Face) in St. Peter's. His remains stayed there until October 1566, when his successor as pope, Pius V, had them transferred to Santa Maria sopra Minerva. In the chapel founded by Paul IV's uncle and mentor, Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Oliviero_Carafa" title="Oliviero Carafa">Oliviero Carafa</a>, a tomb was created by <a href="/wiki/Pirro_Ligorio" title="Pirro Ligorio">Pirro Ligorio</a> and Paul IV's remains were placed therein.<sup id="cite_ref-Levant719_22-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Levant719-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_fiction">In fiction</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Paul_IV&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: In fiction">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Paul IV's title in the <a href="/wiki/Prophecy_of_St._Malachy" class="mw-redirect" title="Prophecy of St. Malachy">Prophecy of St. Malachy</a> is "Of the Faith of Peter".<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As Paul IV, Carafa appears as a character in John Webster's Jacobean revenge drama <i><a href="/wiki/The_White_Devil" title="The White Devil">The White Devil</a></i> (1612).<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the novel <i><a href="/wiki/Q_(novel)" title="Q (novel)">Q</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/Luther_Blissett_(nom_de_plume)" class="mw-redirect" title="Luther Blissett (nom de plume)">Luther Blissett</a>, while not appearing himself, Gian Pietro Carafa is mentioned repeatedly as the cardinal whose spy and <i>agent provocateur</i>, Qoelet, causes many of the disasters to befall Protestants during the Reformation and the Roman Church's response in the 16th century.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Alison MacLeod's 1968 historical novel "The Hireling" depicts Cardinal Carafa befriending the English Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Reginald_Pole" title="Reginald Pole">Reginald Pole</a> during Pole's long exile in Italy, their later falling out, and Pole's feelings of betrayal after Carafa, once elevated to the Papacy, charges him with heresy at the very time when Pole was striving to return England to the Catholic fold.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Pope Paul IV is a major villain in <a href="/wiki/Sholem_Asch" title="Sholem Asch">Sholem Asch</a>'s 1921 historical novel <i>The Witch of Castile</i> (Yiddish: <i>Di Kishufmakherin fun Kastilien</i>, Hebrew: Ha'Machshepha Mi'Castilia המכשפה מקשיטליה). The book's depiction of a young Sephardi Jewish woman in Rome being falsely accused of witchcraft and being burned at the stake, dying as a Jewish martyr, is placed in the context of Paul IV's actual persecution of the Jews.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (May 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
