</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1066479718">.mw-parser-output .infobox-subbox{padding:0;border:none;margin:-3px;width:auto;min-width:100%;font-size:100%;clear:none;float:none;background-color:transparent}.mw-parser-output .infobox-3cols-child{margin:auto}.mw-parser-output .infobox .navbar{font-size:100%}body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-header,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-subheader,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-above,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-title,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-image,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-full-data,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-below{text-align:center}</style><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div class="honorific-prefix" style="display:inline;font-size: 77%; font-weight: normal;">Saint</div><br /><div style="display:inline;" class="fn">Eustadiola</div></th></tr><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-header" style="background-color:gold;">Nun</th></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Born</th><td class="infobox-data">594<br /><a href="/wiki/Bourges" title="Bourges">Bourges, France</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Died</th><td class="infobox-data">684 (aged&#160;89&#8211;90)</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Venerated&#160;in</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a><br /><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Feast</a></th><td class="infobox-data">8 June</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p><b>Eustadiola</b> (594–684) was a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>, <a href="/wiki/Widow" title="Widow">widow</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a>. She was born to wealthy and politically powerful parents in <a href="/wiki/Bourges" title="Bourges">Bourges, France</a>. She married due to pressure from her family, but became a widow at a young age, which gave her the financial and social independence to live what <i>Sainted Women of the Dark Ages</i> centuries later called a "semiretired religious life".<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-106_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-106-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> She gave away her wealth to the poor, founded churches, monasteries, and convents, and used her wealth and influence to expand and decorate the buildings. Eustadiola was abbess of the convent she founded in Bourges, and lived as an ascetic for 70 years. Many miracles and healings were attributed to her. Her feast day is celebrated on 8 June.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eustadiola&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Eustadiola was born in 594 to the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Senate" title="Roman Senate">senatorial</a> nobility, to wealthy and politically powerful parents,<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-107_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-107-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> in <a href="/wiki/Bourges" title="Bourges">Bourges, France</a>. She received an education and "became wise in religious ways"<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-107_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-107-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> at a young age. Her parents urged her to marry to give them heirs, which she did, and had one son, Tetradius.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> She was widowed at a young age,<sup id="cite_ref-antiochen_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-antiochen-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> which gave her the financial and social independence to live a "semiretired religious life"<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-106_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-106-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> after her husband died, without any formal vows or submission to outside <a href="/wiki/Monasticism" title="Monasticism">monastic rules</a>, like many wealthy widows of the time.<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-106_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-106-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The little that is known about Eustadiola is because her biography is documented in the early 8th-century <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> of her <a href="/wiki/Patronage" title="Patronage">patron</a>, contemporary, and fellow saint, Bishop <a href="/wiki/Sulpitius_the_Pious" title="Sulpitius the Pious">Sulpicius the Pious</a>, who helped her resist her family's pressure to remarry.<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-107_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-107-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-95_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-95-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Like many wealthy widows of the time, Eustadiola gave away her wealth to the poor, and dedicated the houses she owned as <a href="/wiki/Basilica" title="Basilica">basilicas</a> in honor of the <a href="/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus" title="Mary, mother of Jesus">Virgin Mary</a> and the 3rd-century martyr, <a href="/wiki/Eugenia_of_Rome" title="Eugenia of Rome">Saint Eugenia of Rome</a>. She decorated the churches with gold and silver vessels, gems and other jewels, objects used during <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">masses</a> and ceremonies, books, and <a href="/wiki/Turret_(architecture)" title="Turret (architecture)">turrets</a>. She made, with her own hands and assisted by her servants, embroidered <a href="/wiki/Vestment" title="Vestment">vestments</a>, <a href="/wiki/Altar_cloth" title="Altar cloth">altar cloths</a> with gold fringes, and wall hangings. She built a <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> and a <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a>, where she and other women, inspired by her example, lived in community as nuns, but without formal recognition. Eustadiola was abbess of the convent she founded, Moyen-Montiers at Bourges, and lived as an ascetic for 70 years, caring for the poor and supporting widows and orphans. She did not live a strictly cloistered life because of her daily trips to the <a href="/wiki/Basilica" title="Basilica">basilica</a> of <a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle" title="Paul the Apostle">Saint Paul</a> in Bourges, which she helped build.<sup id="cite_ref-antiochen_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-antiochen-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-109110_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-109110-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Like many convents and monasteries of the period, her communities accepted both wealthy and poor women and its residents' ranks and position in society were unimportant.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As historian Jo Ann McNamara said, "She was full of faith, ablaze with charity, affable of speech, amicable of aspect, endowed with prudence, famed for temperance, firm with internal fortitude, steady with just censures, great of spirit, robust with patience and gentle in humility".<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Many cures and healings were attributed to Eustadiola's prayers. For example, blind people received sight from the water in which she washed her hands and face. Her prayers and the prayers of her fellow nuns ended a drought; she encouraged the sisters at the basilica of St. Paul to pray for rain, which were answered almost immediately; before they were able to return home to the convent, they were drenched by the downpour.<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-109110_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-109110-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> According to McNamara, "such proof of divine favor" helped protect Eustadiola's wealth from those who would prevent her from using it to support her communities and charitable works, and provided her with "prestige as a mediator in settling local quarrels".<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-95_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-95-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Eustadiola died in 684, at the age of 90, and was buried at St. Paul's Basilica. The bishop of Bournes, who attended her funeral, reported that "he had never seen such enormous grief at the death of a religious at church or even at the end of anyone in royal power".<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Her feast day is celebrated on 8 June.<sup id="cite_ref-antiochen_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-antiochen-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eustadiola&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Little is known about Eustadiola; McNamara calls her one of "many forgotten individuals who pursued roads to sanctity not often commemorated by official historians".<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-107_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-107-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The kind of informal, unsupervised <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a> she practiced was disapproved of since the time of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I" title="Pope Gregory I">Pope Gregory I</a>, but it was still widespread in <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a> in the 7th century.<sup id="cite_ref-mcnamara-107_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcnamara-107-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> According to writer Jane T. Schulenburg, Eustadiola, like many abbesses and saints of her day, was praised for enlarging her churches and monastic buildings, for her role in obtaining relics for them, and for decorating and enriching their appearance.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Many miracles, including healings from blindness and lameness, occurred at her tomb and through her intercessions, both before her death and afterwards. McNamara reports that two people were healed of blindness after applying a mixture of dust from her tomb and oil from the lamp before her sepulcher to their eyes and another when she was anointed with the lamp oil at Eustadiola's sepulcher.<sup id="cite_ref-antiochen_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-antiochen-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> McNamara reported that so many people were healed, it was difficult to list them all.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
