<p><b>Charles Watson-Wentworth, 2nd Marquess of Rockingham</b>, <span class="noexcerpt nowraplinks" style="font-size:100%; font-weight:normal;"><a href="/wiki/Knight_of_the_Order_of_the_Garter" class="mw-redirect" title="Knight of the Order of the Garter">KG</a><span style="font-size:14px;">&#44;</span>&#32;<a href="/wiki/Privy_Council_of_the_United_Kingdom" class="mw-redirect" title="Privy Council of the United Kingdom">PC</a><span style="font-size:14px;">&#44;</span>&#32;<a href="/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society" title="Fellow of the Royal Society">FRS</a></span> (13 May 1730 – 1 July 1782; styled <b>The Hon. Charles Watson-Wentworth</b> before 1733, <b>Viscount Higham</b> between 1733 and 1746, <b>Earl of Malton</b> between 1746 and 1750 and <b>The Marquess of Rockingham</b> in 1750) was a British <a href="/wiki/Whigs_(British_political_party)" title="Whigs (British political party)">Whig</a> statesman and <a href="/wiki/Magnate" title="Magnate">magnate</a>, most notable for his two terms as <a href="/wiki/Prime_minister_of_Great_Britain" class="mw-redirect" title="Prime minister of Great Britain">prime minister of Great Britain</a>. He became the patron of many Whigs, known as the <a href="/wiki/Rockingham_Whigs" title="Rockingham Whigs">Rockingham Whigs</a>, and served as a leading Whig grandee. He served in only two high offices during his lifetime (prime minister and <a href="/wiki/Leader_of_the_House_of_Lords" title="Leader of the House of Lords">Leader of the House of Lords</a>) but was nonetheless very influential during his one and a half years of service.
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r886046785">.mw-parser-output .toclimit-2 .toclevel-1 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-3 .toclevel-2 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-4 .toclevel-3 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-5 .toclevel-4 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-6 .toclevel-5 ul,.mw-parser-output .toclimit-7 .toclevel-6 ul{display:none}</style><div class="toclimit-3"><meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" /></div>
<h2><span id="Early_life:_1730.E2.80.931751"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life:_1730–1751">Early life: 1730–1751</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_Watson-Wentworth,_2nd_Marquess_of_Rockingham&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life: 1730–1751">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>A descendant of the <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Wentworth,_1st_Earl_of_Strafford" title="Thomas Wentworth, 1st Earl of Strafford">1st Earl of Strafford</a>, Lord Rockingham was brought up at the family home of <a href="/wiki/Wentworth_Woodhouse" title="Wentworth Woodhouse">Wentworth Woodhouse</a> near <a href="/wiki/Rotherham" title="Rotherham">Rotherham</a><sup id="cite_ref-York1911_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-York1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> in <a href="/wiki/Yorkshire" title="Yorkshire">Yorkshire</a>. He was educated at <a href="/wiki/Westminster_School" title="Westminster School">Westminster School</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-dnb_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dnb-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> During the <a href="/wiki/Jacobite_rising_of_1745" title="Jacobite rising of 1745">Jacobite rising of 1745</a> <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Watson-Wentworth,_1st_Marquess_of_Rockingham" title="Thomas Watson-Wentworth, 1st Marquess of Rockingham">Rockingham's father</a> made him a colonel and organised volunteers to defend the country against the "<a href="/wiki/Charles_Edward_Stuart" title="Charles Edward Stuart">Young Pretender</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 3">&#58;&#8202;3&#8202;</span></sup> Rockingham's sister Mary wrote to him from London, saying the King "did not doubt but that you was as good a colonel as he has in his army" and his other sister Charlotte wrote that "you have gained immortal honour and I have every day the satisfaction of hearing twenty handsome things said of the Blues and their Collonel".<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 3">&#58;&#8202;3&#8202;</span></sup> The march of the Jacobite army into northern England caused the Wentworth household to flee to <a href="/wiki/Doncaster" title="Doncaster">Doncaster</a> and Rockingham rode from Wentworth to <a href="/wiki/Carlisle" title="Carlisle">Carlisle</a> to join the <a href="/wiki/Prince_William_Augustus,_Duke_of_Cumberland" class="mw-redirect" title="Prince William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland">Duke of Cumberland</a> in pursuit of the "Young Pretender". Rockingham did this without parental consent and Cumberland wrote to Rockingham's father, saying that his "zeal on this occasion shows the same principles fix't that you yourself have given such strong proofs of".<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 3">&#58;&#8202;3&#8202;</span></sup> Rockingham wrote to his father that Cumberland "blamed me for my disobedience, yet as I came with a design of saving my King and country...it greatly palliated my offence".<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 3">&#58;&#8202;3&#8202;</span></sup> Rockingham's mother wrote to his father: "Though I hope you won't tell it him, never any thing met with such general applause, in short he is the hero of these times, and his Majesty talks of this young Subject, in such terms, as must please you to hear...in the Drawing Room<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> no two people talk together, but he makes part of the discourse".<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 4">&#58;&#8202;4&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>In April 1746 Rockingham's father was made a <a href="/wiki/Marquess" title="Marquess">marquess</a> (remaining the only <a href="/wiki/Marquess" title="Marquess">marquess</a> in the British peerage for quite some time) and Rockingham himself assumed the courtesy title of Earl of Malton. These honours came about due to the patronage of <a href="/wiki/Henry_Pelham" title="Henry Pelham">Henry Pelham</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 4">&#58;&#8202;4&#8202;</span></sup> At this time Rockingham was travelling across Europe under the tutorship of George Quarme, as his father had decided against sending him to Cambridge.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 5–9">&#58;&#8202;5–9&#8202;</span></sup><sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> During his stay in Rome, Rockingham noted that amongst Englishmen Whigs outnumbered Jacobites four-to-one and there were "no Persons of rank about the Pretender" and that "the vile spirit of Jacobitism" was greatly declining.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 8">&#58;&#8202;8&#8202;</span></sup> When in <a href="/wiki/Herrenhausen" class="mw-redirect" title="Herrenhausen">Herrenhausen</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hanover" title="Hanover">Hanover</a> Rockingham met George II and made an impression: the King told Rockingham's uncle Henry Finch that he had never seen a finer or a more promising youth.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 9">&#58;&#8202;9&#8202;</span></sup> In September 1750, two months before his father's death, he was raised to the Peerage of Ireland in his own right as Baron Malton and Earl Malton.
</p>
<h2><span id="Early_political_career:_1751.E2.80.931765"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_political_career:_1751–1765">Early political career: 1751–1765</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_Watson-Wentworth,_2nd_Marquess_of_Rockingham&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early political career: 1751–1765">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Member_of_Parliament">Member of Parliament</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_Watson-Wentworth,_2nd_Marquess_of_Rockingham&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Member of Parliament">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Young_Marquess_of_Rockingham.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Young_Marquess_of_Rockingham.jpg/170px-Young_Marquess_of_Rockingham.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="234" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Young_Marquess_of_Rockingham.jpg/255px-Young_Marquess_of_Rockingham.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Young_Marquess_of_Rockingham.jpg/340px-Young_Marquess_of_Rockingham.jpg 2x" data-file-width="581" data-file-height="800" /></a><figcaption>A young Rockingham</figcaption></figure>
<p>On 13 May 1751 (his 21st birthday), Rockingham inherited his father's estates. The rents from the land in Yorkshire, Northamptonshire and Ireland gave him an annual income of £20,000 (equivalent to £3,300,000&#32;in 2021).<sup id="cite_ref-inflation-UK_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-inflation-UK-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He also controlled both of the borough parliamentary seats of <a href="/wiki/Malton_(UK_Parliament_constituency)" title="Malton (UK Parliament constituency)">Malton</a> and one seat for the single-member borough of <a href="/wiki/Higham_Ferrers_(UK_Parliament_constituency)" title="Higham Ferrers (UK Parliament constituency)">Higham Ferrers</a> (Northants), along with twenty-three livings and five chaplaincies in the church.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 10">&#58;&#8202;10&#8202;</span></sup> In July he was appointed Lord Lieutenant and <i><a href="/wiki/Custos_rotulorum" title="Custos rotulorum">custos rotulorum</a></i> of the West Riding in Yorkshire, Lord Lieutenant of York city, and <i>custos rotulorum</i> of York city and county. In 1751–52 Rockingham joined <a href="/wiki/White%27s" title="White&#39;s">White's</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Jockey_Club" title="Jockey Club">Jockey Club</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Royal_Society" title="Royal Society">Royal Society</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 10">&#58;&#8202;10&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>Rockingham's maiden speech was on 17 March 1752 in support of the bill which disposed of Scottish lands confiscated in the aftermath of the Jacobite rising of 1745. He wanted the lands cultivated by people "employed in husbandry &amp; handicrafts" who repudiated "plunder, rapine &amp; rebellion". He said "the highlanders have remained in their ancient state, prolific, bold, idle, &amp; consequently hives of rebellion". He compared his favoured policy with the policy which his ancestor Lord Strafford had used in Ireland. Rockingham's speech was not well received, with <a href="/wiki/Horace_Walpole" title="Horace Walpole">Horace Walpole</a> criticising him for venturing into "a debate so much above his force".<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 11">&#58;&#8202;11&#8202;</span></sup> Rockingham's uncle <a href="/wiki/William_Murray,_1st_Earl_of_Mansfield" title="William Murray, 1st Earl of Mansfield">William Murray</a>, the Solicitor-General, believed him to be poorly educated, so he employed Quarme as Rockingham's tutor again. Rockingham was for four months to study <a href="/wiki/Demosthenes" title="Demosthenes">Demosthenes</a> for oratory, and to learn the histories of the Assyrian, Persian, Greek and Roman empires along with modern history. Murray wanted Rockingham to take after Sir <a href="/wiki/Walter_Raleigh" title="Walter Raleigh">Walter Raleigh</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 11">&#58;&#8202;11&#8202;</span></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Lord_of_the_Bedchamber">Lord of the Bedchamber</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_Watson-Wentworth,_2nd_Marquess_of_Rockingham&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Lord of the Bedchamber">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 1752, Rockingham was appointed <a href="/wiki/Gentleman_of_the_Bedchamber" class="mw-redirect" title="Gentleman of the Bedchamber">Lord of the Bedchamber</a> to George II and married Mary Bright.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In 1753 the Rockingham Club was formed, containing the first Rockingham Whigs. Rockingham hired <a href="/wiki/James_Stuart_(1713%E2%80%931788)" class="mw-redirect" title="James Stuart (1713–1788)">James Stuart</a>, of whom he was a patron,<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> to paint portraits of <a href="/wiki/William_III_of_England" title="William III of England">William III</a> and George II for the club rooms. The club held monthly meetings and a list written in June 1754 showed it had 133 members.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 20">&#58;&#8202;20&#8202;</span></sup> In 1755 the King appointed him to the honorary office of Vice Admiral of the North.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 21">&#58;&#8202;21&#8202;</span></sup> During a French <a href="/wiki/Great_Britain_in_the_Seven_Years%27_War" title="Great Britain in the Seven Years&#39; War">invasion scare</a> in 1756 Rockingham raised a volunteer militia at his own expense and when rioting broke out against Army enlistments Rockingham restored order without the use of military force in Sheffield. The <a href="/wiki/Secretary_at_War" title="Secretary at War">Secretary at War</a>, <a href="/wiki/William_Barrington,_2nd_Viscount_Barrington" title="William Barrington, 2nd Viscount Barrington">Lord Barrington</a>, wrote to him: "You are the only instance of a Lord lieutenant's exerting the civil authority upon these occasions".<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 21">&#58;&#8202;21&#8202;</span></sup> Rockingham asked in 1760 to be made a knight of the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_the_Garter" title="Order of the Garter">Order of the Garter</a> and the King consented.
</p><p>In 1760, George II died, and his grandson ascended the throne as <a href="/wiki/George_III" title="George III">George III</a>. Rockingham was allied to the <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Pelham-Holles,_1st_Duke_of_Newcastle" title="Thomas Pelham-Holles, 1st Duke of Newcastle">Duke of Newcastle</a> and his supporters, whilst the new King had a favourite in <a href="/wiki/John_Stuart,_3rd_Earl_of_Bute" title="John Stuart, 3rd Earl of Bute">Lord Bute</a>. Rockingham believed that Bute and his supporters wanted to take "the whole Administration &amp; Government of this country into their hands" and wanted Newcastle to resign now before he would be inevitably disposed of. Rockingham believed that the revolution in British politics since George III's accession was harmful to the country, since it removed the Whigs from their ascendancy which had settled the constitution and secured the House of Hanover on the British throne. Rockingham wrote to Newcastle:
</p>
<blockquote><p>...without flattery to your Grace, I must look and ever shall upon you and your connections as the solid foundations on which every good which has happened to this country since the [Glorious] Revolution, have been erected. ... What a medley of government is probably soon to take place &amp; when it does what an alarm will ensue!<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 37">&#58;&#8202;37&#8202;</span></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Rockingham resigned as Lord of the Bedchamber on 3 November 1762 in protest at the King's policies and other Whigs associated with the Duke of Newcastle did the same.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 43–44">&#58;&#8202;43–44&#8202;</span></sup> The next month the King removed Rockingham from the office of Lord Lieutenant of the West Riding, Lord Lieutenant of the city and county of York, as custos rotulorum of the North and West Riding, as custos rotulorum of the city and county of York and as Vice Admiral of <a href="/wiki/York" title="York">York</a> and county.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 45">&#58;&#8202;45&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>Over the next several years, Rockingham gradually became the leader of those of Newcastle's supporters who were unwilling to reconcile themselves to the premierships of Bute and his successor, <a href="/wiki/George_Grenville" title="George Grenville">George Grenville</a>.
</p>
<h2><span id="Prime_Minister:_1765.E2.80.931766"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Prime_Minister:_1765–1766">Prime Minister: 1765–1766<span class="anchor" id="First_premiership"></span></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_Watson-Wentworth,_2nd_Marquess_of_Rockingham&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Prime Minister: 1765–1766">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/First_Rockingham_ministry" title="First Rockingham ministry">First Rockingham ministry</a></div>
<p>The king's dislike, as well as Grenville's general lack of parliamentary support, led to his dismissal in 1765, and, following negotiations conducted through the medium of the king's uncle, the <a href="/wiki/Prince_William_Augustus,_Duke_of_Cumberland" class="mw-redirect" title="Prince William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland">Duke of Cumberland</a>, Lord Rockingham was appointed Prime Minister.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Rockingham recovered the honours of which he had been deprived in 1762. Rockingham appointed his allies <a href="/wiki/Henry_Seymour_Conway" title="Henry Seymour Conway">Henry Seymour Conway</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Augustus_FitzRoy,_3rd_Duke_of_Grafton" title="Augustus FitzRoy, 3rd Duke of Grafton">Duke of Grafton</a> as <a href="/wiki/Secretary_of_State_(United_Kingdom)" title="Secretary of State (United Kingdom)">secretaries of state</a>. Also at this time, <a href="/wiki/Edmund_Burke" title="Edmund Burke">Edmund Burke</a>, the Irish statesman and philosopher, became his private secretary and would remain a lifelong friend, political ally and advisor until Rockingham's death from influenza in 1782.
</p><p>Rockingham's administration was dominated by the American issue. Rockingham wished for repeal of the <a href="/wiki/Stamp_Act_1765" title="Stamp Act 1765">Stamp Act 1765</a> and won a Commons vote on the repeal resolution by 275 to 167 in 1766.<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 113">&#58;&#8202;113&#8202;</span></sup> However Rockingham also passed the <a href="/wiki/Declaratory_Act" title="Declaratory Act">Declaratory Act</a>, which asserted that the British Parliament had the right to legislate for the American colonies in all cases whatsoever.
</p><p>However, internal dissent within the cabinet led to his resignation and the appointment of <a href="/wiki/William_Pitt,_1st_Earl_of_Chatham" title="William Pitt, 1st Earl of Chatham">Lord Chatham</a> as Prime Minister (the Duke of Grafton was appointed <a href="/wiki/First_Lord_of_the_Treasury" title="First Lord of the Treasury">First Lord of the Treasury</a>, one of the few cases in which those two offices were separate).
</p>
<h2><span id="Opposition:_1766.E2.80.931782"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Opposition:_1766–1782">Opposition: 1766–1782</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_Watson-Wentworth,_2nd_Marquess_of_Rockingham&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Opposition: 1766–1782">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Charles_Watson-Wentworth,_Second_Marquis_of_Rockingham.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/Charles_Watson-Wentworth%2C_Second_Marquis_of_Rockingham.jpg/170px-Charles_Watson-Wentworth%2C_Second_Marquis_of_Rockingham.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="277" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/Charles_Watson-Wentworth%2C_Second_Marquis_of_Rockingham.jpg/255px-Charles_Watson-Wentworth%2C_Second_Marquis_of_Rockingham.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/Charles_Watson-Wentworth%2C_Second_Marquis_of_Rockingham.jpg/340px-Charles_Watson-Wentworth%2C_Second_Marquis_of_Rockingham.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1379" data-file-height="2250" /></a><figcaption>Lord Rockingham painted by <a href="/wiki/Joshua_Reynolds" title="Joshua Reynolds">Joshua Reynolds</a> in 1768</figcaption></figure>
<p>Rockingham spent the next sixteen years in opposition. He was a keen supporter of constitutional rights for colonists.
</p><p>Rockingham wrote to <a href="/wiki/Edmund_Burke" title="Edmund Burke">Edmund Burke</a> on 14 February 1771: "I fear indeed the future struggles of the people in defence of their Constitutional Rights will grow weaker and weaker. It is much too probable that the power and influence of the Crown will increase rapidly. We live at the period when for the first time since the Revolution, the power and influence of the Crown is held out, as the main and chief and only support of Government. If we...do not exert now, we may accelerate the abject state to which the Constitution may be reduced".<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> On 24 May 1771 Benjamin Franklin arrived from the Rectory of Thornhill, where he had stayed with the Rev. John Michell, vicar to Rockingham's kinsman, fellow leading politician and keen advocate of colonists' rights Sir George Savile.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Rockingham wrote to <a href="/wiki/Augustus_Keppel,_1st_Viscount_Keppel" title="Augustus Keppel, 1st Viscount Keppel">Augustus Keppel</a> on 3 November 1779, saying that he believed the war against America could not be won, that the government was corrupt but not unpopular, and that the longer this continued the greater the danger to the liberties and the constitution of Britain: "Perhaps a total change of <i>men</i> and <i>measures</i>, &amp; <i>system</i> in the Government: of this country might have effect on the councils of some foreign countries...who might think that it was no longer a Court system to combat, but that the whole nation wd; united &amp; make the utmost efforts".<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Rockingham was recruited to hunt down the <a href="/wiki/Cragg_Vale_Coiners" title="Cragg Vale Coiners">Cragg Vale Coiners</a>. He had thirty Coiners arrested by Christmas Day 1769.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Prime_Minister:_1782">Prime Minister: 1782<span class="anchor" id="Second_premiership"></span></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_Watson-Wentworth,_2nd_Marquess_of_Rockingham&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Prime Minister: 1782">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Second_Rockingham_ministry" title="Second Rockingham ministry">Second Rockingham ministry</a></div>
<p>In 1782 he was appointed Prime Minister for a second time (with <a href="/wiki/Charles_James_Fox" title="Charles James Fox">Charles James Fox</a> and <a href="/wiki/William_Petty,_2nd_Earl_of_Shelburne" title="William Petty, 2nd Earl of Shelburne">Lord Shelburne</a> as Secretaries of State) and, upon taking office, pushed for an acknowledgement of the independence of the United States, initiating an end to British involvement in the <a href="/wiki/American_War_of_Independence" class="mw-redirect" title="American War of Independence">American War of Independence</a>.
</p><p>Due to rising unemployment, in this second premiership, Rockingham's administration saw the passage of <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Gilbert_(politician)" title="Thomas Gilbert (politician)">Gilbert</a>'s Act, the <a href="/wiki/Relief_of_the_Poor_Act_1782" title="Relief of the Poor Act 1782">Relief of the Poor Act 1782</a>, after 17 years of opposing Thomas Gilbert's ideas, this saw the creation of <a href="/wiki/Poor_law_unions" class="mw-redirect" title="Poor law unions">unions</a> of <a href="/wiki/Civil_parishes_in_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Civil parishes in England">civil parishes</a>, later officially called <i>unions under Gilbert's Act</i>, to provide outdoor relief and set up <a href="/wiki/Workhouse" title="Workhouse">workhouses</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Paul_Langford" title="Paul Langford">Paul Langford</a> has asserted that the Rockingham administration "represented a landmark in constitutional history. The ministerial changes of 1782 involved a more extensive upheaval among office-holders than any since 1714, virtually replacing one administration with another drawn from opposition".<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Rockingham's second term was short-lived, for Lord Rockingham died fourteen weeks later at the beginning of July from an <a href="/wiki/Influenza" title="Influenza">influenza</a> epidemic. He was replaced as Prime Minister by <a href="/wiki/William_Petty,_2nd_Earl_of_Shelburne" title="William Petty, 2nd Earl of Shelburne">Lord Shelburne</a>, who was more reluctant to accept the total independence of America and proposed a form of Dominion status, but by April 1783 he succeeded in securing peace with America and this feat remains his legacy.<sup id="cite_ref-www.number10.gov.uk_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-www.number10.gov.uk-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Rockingham was buried in the Strafford family vault in <a href="/wiki/York_Minster" title="York Minster">York Minster</a> in Yorkshire.<sup id="cite_ref-odnb_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-odnb-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_Watson-Wentworth,_2nd_Marquess_of_Rockingham&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Rockingham's estates, but not his marquessate, passed to his nephew <a href="/wiki/William_Fitzwilliam,_4th_Earl_Fitzwilliam" title="William Fitzwilliam, 4th Earl Fitzwilliam">William Fitzwilliam, 4th Earl Fitzwilliam</a>. Burke wrote to Fitzwilliam on 3 July 1782: "You are Lord Rockingham in every thing. ... I have no doubt that you will take it in good part, that his old friends, who were attached to him by every tie of affection, and of principle, and among others myself, should look to you, and should not think it an act of forwardness and intrusion to offer you their services".<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 383">&#58;&#8202;383&#8202;</span></sup> On 7 July 150 supporters of Rockingham met at Fitzwilliam's house and decided to withdraw support for Lord Shelburne's administration. The old Rockingham party fragmented, with Fox and the Duke of Portland leading a coalition of Whigs. The Whig party further split over the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>, with Burke writing to Fitzwilliam on 4 January 1797: "As to our old friends, they are so many individuals, not a jot more separated from your Lordship, than they are from one another. There is no mutual affection, communication, or concert between them".<sup id="cite_ref-hoff_3-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hoff-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page: 385">&#58;&#8202;385&#8202;</span></sup>
</p><p>The Whig historian <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Babington_Macaulay,_1st_Baron_Macaulay" class="mw-redirect" title="Thomas Babington Macaulay, 1st Baron Macaulay">Thomas Babington Macaulay</a> was an admirer of Rockingham and his Whig faction:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>They were men worthy to have charged by the side of <a href="/wiki/John_Hampden" title="John Hampden">Hampden</a> at <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Chalgrove" class="mw-redirect" title="Battle of Chalgrove">Chalgrove</a>, or to have exchanged the last embrace with <a href="/wiki/William_Russell,_Lord_Russell" title="William Russell, Lord Russell">Russell</a> on the scaffold in Lincoln's Inn Fields. They carried into politics the same high principles of virtue which regulated their private dealings, nor would they stoop to promote even the noblest and most salutary ends by means which honour and probity condemn. Such men were Lord John Cavendish, Sir George Savile, and others whom we hold in honour as the second founders of the Whig party, as the restorers of its pristine health and energy after half a century of degeneracy. The chief of this respectable band was the Marquess of Rockingham, a man of splendid fortune, excellent sense, and stainless character. He was indeed nervous to such a degree that, to the very close of his life, he never rose without great reluctance and embarrassment to address the House of Lords. But, though not a great orator, he had in a high degree some of the qualities of a statesman. He chose his friends well; and he had, in an extraordinary degree, the art of attaching them to him by ties of the most honourable kind. The cheerful fidelity with which they adhered to him through many years of almost hopeless opposition was less admirable than the disinterestedness and delicacy which they showed when he rose to power.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>

<dl><dt>Crest</dt>
<dd>A Griffin passant wings elevated Argent beaked forelegged and ducally-gorged Or</dd>

<dd>Quarterly, 1st and 4th, Argent on a Chevron engrailed Azure between three Martlets Sable as many Crescents Or (Watson); 2nd and 3rd, Sable a Chevron between three Leopards' Faces Or (Wentworth)</dd>

<dd>On <i>On the dexter side</i> a Griffin Argent beaked and forelegged Gules collared vairé Ermine and Azure and <i>on the sinister side</i> a Lion Or collared vairé Ermine and Gules</dd>
