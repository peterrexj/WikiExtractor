					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Story in Christian folklore and the Qur'an</div>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">For the Feeder EP, see <a href="/wiki/Seven_Sleepers_(EP)" title="Seven Sleepers (EP)">Seven Sleepers (EP)</a>.</div>

<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:7sleepersmedievalmanuscript.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/11/7sleepersmedievalmanuscript.jpg/220px-7sleepersmedievalmanuscript.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="157" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/11/7sleepersmedievalmanuscript.jpg/330px-7sleepersmedievalmanuscript.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/11/7sleepersmedievalmanuscript.jpg/440px-7sleepersmedievalmanuscript.jpg 2x" data-file-width="471" data-file-height="336" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:7sleepersmedievalmanuscript.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Decius" title="Decius">Decius</a> orders the <a href="/wiki/Immurement" title="Immurement">walling in</a> of the Seven sleepers<sup id="cite_ref-Fortescue_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fortescue-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> From a 14th-century manuscript.</div></div></div>
<p>In the <a href="/wiki/Islam" title="Islam">Islamic</a> and <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> traditions, the <b>Seven Sleepers</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">επτά κοιμώμενοι</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek" title="Romanization of Greek">romanized</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Greek-language romanization"><i lang="el-Latn">hepta koimōmenoi</i></span>,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Septem dormientes</i>), otherwise known as the <b>Sleepers of Ephesus</b> and <b>Companions of the Cave</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-Archer_2016_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Archer_2016-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> is a <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">medieval</a> <a href="/wiki/Legend" title="Legend">legend</a> about a group of youths who hid inside a cave<sup id="cite_ref-madaincavelocation_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-madaincavelocation-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> outside the city of <a href="/wiki/Ephesus" title="Ephesus">Ephesus</a> (modern-day <a href="/wiki/Sel%C3%A7uk" title="Selçuk">Selçuk</a>, <a href="/wiki/Turkey" title="Turkey">Turkey</a>) around AD 250 to escape <a href="/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians_in_the_Roman_Empire" title="Persecution of Christians in the Roman Empire">one of the Roman persecutions of Christians</a> and emerged some 300 years later. Another version of the story appears in the <a href="/wiki/Quran" title="Quran">Quran</a> (<a href="/wiki/Q18:9%E2%80%9326" class="mw-redirect" title="Q18:9–26">18:9–26</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-Archer_2016_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Archer_2016-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> It was also translated into <a href="/wiki/New_Persian" title="New Persian">Persian</a>, <a href="/wiki/Kyrgyz_language" title="Kyrgyz language">Kyrgyz</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Tatar_language" title="Tatar language">Tatar</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-WW_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WW-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The earliest version of this story comes from the Syriac bishop <a href="/wiki/Jacob_of_Serugh" title="Jacob of Serugh">Jacob of Serugh</a> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;450</span>–521), which is itself derived from an earlier Greek source, now lost.<sup id="cite_ref-van-der-horst-2011_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-van-der-horst-2011-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> An outline of this tale appears in the writings of <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Tours" title="Gregory of Tours">Gregory of Tours</a> (538–594) and in <i>History of the <a href="/wiki/Lombards" title="Lombards">Lombards</a></i> of <a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Deacon" title="Paul the Deacon">Paul the Deacon</a> (720–799).<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The best-known Western version of the story appears in <a href="/wiki/Jacobus_de_Voragine" title="Jacobus de Voragine">Jacobus de Voragine</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Golden_Legend" title="Golden Legend">Golden Legend</a></i> (1259–1266).  See <a href="/wiki/Bibliotheca_Hagiographica_Orientalis" title="Bibliotheca Hagiographica Orientalis">BHO</a> (Pueri septem) ##1012-1022;<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Bibliotheca_Hagiographica_Graeca" title="Bibliotheca Hagiographica Graeca">BHG</a> (Pueri VII) ##1593–1599;<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Bibliotheca_Hagiographica_Latina" title="Bibliotheca Hagiographica Latina">BHL</a> Dormientes (Septem) Ephesi ##2313–2319.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Accounts are found in at least nine medieval languages and preserved in over 200 manuscripts, mainly dating to between the 9th and 13th centuries. These include 104 <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> manuscripts, 40 <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>, 33 <a href="/wiki/Arabic" title="Arabic">Arabic</a>, 17 <a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a>, six <a href="/wiki/Ethiopic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ethiopic">Ethiopic</a>, five <a href="/wiki/Coptic_language" title="Coptic language">Coptic</a>, two <a href="/wiki/Armenian_language" title="Armenian language">Armenian</a>, one <a href="/wiki/Middle_Irish" title="Middle Irish">Middle Irish</a>, and one <a href="/wiki/Old_English" title="Old English">Old English</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-BG_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-BG-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> It was also translated into <a href="/wiki/Sogdian_language" title="Sogdian language">Sogdian</a>. In the 13th century, the poet <a href="/wiki/Chardri" title="Chardri">Chardri</a> composed an <a href="/wiki/Old_French" title="Old French">Old French</a> version.
</p><p>The <i><a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a></i> mentions the Seven Sleepers of <a href="/wiki/Ephesus" title="Ephesus">Ephesus</a> under the date of 27 July (June according to Vatican II calendar).<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_calendar" title="Byzantine calendar">Byzantine calendar</a> commemorates them with feasts on 4 August and 22 October. The ninth-century Irish calendar <span title="Old Irish (to 900)-language text"><i lang="sga"><a href="/wiki/F%C3%A9lire_%C3%93engusso" class="mw-redirect" title="Félire Óengusso">Félire Óengusso</a></i></span> commemorates the Seven Sleepers on 7 August.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Syriac Orthodox">Syriac Orthodox</a> calendars gives various dates: 21 April, 2 August, 13 August, 23 October and 24 October.<sup id="cite_ref-WW_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WW-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span id="Number.2C_duration_and_names"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Number,_duration_and_names">Number, duration and names</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Number, duration and names">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Early versions do not all agree on or even specify the number of sleepers. The Jews and the <a href="/wiki/Christian_community_of_Najran" title="Christian community of Najran">Christians of Najran</a> believed in only three brothers; the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_East" title="Church of the East">East Syriac</a>, five.<sup id="cite_ref-BG_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-BG-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Most Syriac accounts have eight, including a nameless watcher which God sets over the sleepers.<sup id="cite_ref-WW_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WW-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> However, in Islam their specific number is mentioned. <a href="/wiki/Al-Kahf#Summary" title="Al-Kahf">Qur'an 18:22</a> discusses the disputes regarding their numbers. The verse says:
</p>
<blockquote><p>Some will say, "They were three, their dog was the fourth," while others will say, "They were five, their dog was the sixth," only guessing blindly. And others will say, "They were seven and their dog was the eighth." Say, O Prophet, "My Lord knows best their exact number. Only a few people know as well." So do not argue about them except with sure knowledge, nor consult any of those who debate about them.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>This verse rejects the first two claims as "guessing at the unseen". But it leaves the third claim of the number of the sleepers being seven and their dog being their eighth unchallenged.
</p><p>Regarding "only a few people know", <a href="/wiki/Ibn_Abbas" title="Ibn Abbas">Abdullah Ibn Abbas</a>, one of the companions of <a href="/wiki/Muhammad" title="Muhammad">Muhammad</a>, is reported to say in an authentic narration reported in the <a href="/wiki/Tafsir" title="Tafsir">tafseer</a> of the verse in <a href="/wiki/Tafsir_al-Tabari#:~:text=&#39;Collection_of_Statements_on_the,Tabari_(838–923)." title="Tafsir al-Tabari">Tafseer At Tabari</a>, "I'm from those a few whose exception Allah has made. And their number is seven."
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="https://ar.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D8%AD%D8%AF%D9%8A%D8%AB_%D8%B5%D8%AD%D9%8A%D8%AD" class="extiw" title="ar:حديث صحيح">ar:حديث_صحيح</a></div>
<p>The number of years the sleepers slept also varies between accounts. The highest number, given by Gregory of Tours, was 373 years. Some accounts have 372. <a href="/wiki/Jacobus_de_Voragine" title="Jacobus de Voragine">Jacobus de Voragine</a> calculated it at 196 (from the year 252 until 448).<sup id="cite_ref-BG_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-BG-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Other calculations suggest 195.<sup id="cite_ref-WW_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WW-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Islamic accounts, including the Qur'an, give a sleep of 309 years. These are presumably <a href="/wiki/Lunar_year" class="mw-redirect" title="Lunar year">lunar years</a>, which would make it 300 <a href="/wiki/Solar_year" class="mw-redirect" title="Solar year">solar years</a>. <a href="/wiki/Al-Kahf#Summary" title="Al-Kahf">Qur'an 18:25</a> says, "And they remained in their cave for three hundred years and exceeded by nine."<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bartłomiej Grysa lists at least seven different sets of names for the sleepers:<sup id="cite_ref-BG_11-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-BG-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<ul><li>Maximian, Martinian, Dionisius, John, Constantine, Malchus, Serapion</li>
<li>Maximilian, Martinian, Dionisius, John, Constantine, Malkhus, Serapion, Anthony</li>
<li>Maximilian, Martinian, Dionisius, John, Constantine, Yamblikh (Iamblichus), Anthony</li>
<li>Makṯimilīnā (Maksimilīnā, Maḥsimilīnā), Marnūš (Marṭūs), Kafašṭaṭyūš (Ksōṭōnos), Yamlīḫā (Yamnīḫ), Mišlīnā, Saḏnūš, Dabranūš (Bīrōnos), Samōnos, Buṭōnos, Qālos (according to aṭ-Ṭabarī and <a href="/wiki/Ad-Dam%C4%ABr%C4%AB" class="mw-redirect" title="Ad-Damīrī">ad-Damīrī</a>)</li>
<li>Achillides, Probatus, Stephanus, Sambatus, Quiriacus, Diogenus, Diomedes (according to Gregory of Tours)</li>
<li>Ikilios, Fruqtis, Istifanos, Sebastos, Qiryaqos, Dionisios (according to <a href="/wiki/Michael_the_Syrian" title="Michael the Syrian">Michael the Syrian</a>)</li>
<li>Aršellītīs, Probatios, Sabbastios, Stafanos, Kīriakos, Diōmetios, Avhenios (according to the Coptic version)</li></ul>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Origins">Origins</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Origins">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Whether the original account was written in Syriac or Greek was a matter of debate, but today a Greek original is generally accepted.<sup id="cite_ref-BG_11-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-BG-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-WW_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WW-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The pilgrim account <i><a href="/wiki/De_situ_terrae_sanctae" title="De situ terrae sanctae">De situ terrae sanctae</a></i>, written between 518 and 531, records the existence of a church dedicated to the sleepers in Ephesus.<sup id="cite_ref-WW_5-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WW-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The story appeared in several Syriac sources before Gregory of Tours's lifetime. The earliest Syriac manuscript copy is in MS Saint-Petersburg No. 4, which dates to the fifth century.<sup id="cite_ref-WW_5-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WW-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> It was retold by <a href="/wiki/Symeon_the_Metaphrast" title="Symeon the Metaphrast">Symeon the Metaphrast</a>. The Seven Sleepers form the subject of a homily in verse by the <a href="/wiki/Edessa" title="Edessa">Edessan</a> poet-theologian <a href="/wiki/Jacob_of_Serugh" title="Jacob of Serugh">Jacob of Serugh</a> (died 521),<sup id="cite_ref-Sabine_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Sabine-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> which was published in the <i><a href="/wiki/Acta_Sanctorum" title="Acta Sanctorum">Acta Sanctorum</a></i>. Another sixth-century version, in a Syrian manuscript in the <a href="/wiki/British_Museum" title="British Museum">British Museum</a> (<i>Cat. Syr. Mss</i>, p.&#160;1090), gives eight sleepers.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Account_in_the_Quran">Account in the Quran</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Account in the Quran">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Al-Kahf" title="Al-Kahf">Al-Kahf</a></div>
<p>The story of the Companions of the Cave (<a href="/wiki/Arabic_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Arabic language">Arabic</a>: <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">أصحاب الکهف</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic" title="Romanization of Arabic">romanized</a>:&#160;</small><span title="Arabic-language romanization"><i lang="ar-Latn">'aṣḥāb al-kahf</i></span>) is referred to in <a href="/wiki/Quran_18:9-26" class="mw-redirect" title="Quran 18:9-26">Quran 18:9-26</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Archer_2016_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Archer_2016-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The precise number of the sleepers is not stated. The Quran furthermore points to the fact that people, shortly after the incident emerged, started to make "idle guesses" as to how many people were in the cave. To this the Quran asserts that: "My Sustainer knows best how many they were".<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Similarly, regarding the exact period of time the people stayed in the cave, the Quran, after asserting the guesswork of the people that "they remained in the cave for 300 years and nine added", resolves that "God knows best how long they remained [there]." According to the 25th verse of Al-Kahf, the Companions of the Cave have slept for 300 years in the solar calendar and slept 309 in the lunar calendar since the lunar calendar is 11 days shorter than the solar, which explains the inclusion of the additional nine years.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> The Quran says the sleepers included a dog, who sat at the entrance of the cave (verse 18).<sup id="cite_ref-Archer_2016_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Archer_2016-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Christian_story">Christian story</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Christian story">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Story">Story</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Story">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Seven_sleepers.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/Seven_sleepers.jpg/220px-Seven_sleepers.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="157" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/92/Seven_sleepers.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="300" data-file-height="214" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Seven_sleepers.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>A 19th&#160;century German votive painting of the Seven Sleepers. The writing says <i>Bittet für uns Ihr hl. sieben Schläfer</i> (<i>Pray for us, Holy Seven Sleepers</i>).</div></div></div>
<p>The story says that during the <a href="/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians" title="Persecution of Christians">persecutions</a> by the Roman emperor <a href="/wiki/Decius" title="Decius">Decius</a>, around AD&#160;250, seven young men were accused of following <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a>. They were given some time to recant their faith, but they refused to bow to Roman idols. Instead they chose to give their worldly goods to the poor and retire to a mountain cave to pray, where they fell asleep. The Emperor, seeing that their attitude towards <a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">paganism</a> had not improved, ordered the mouth of the cave to be sealed.<sup id="cite_ref-Fortescue_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fortescue-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Siebenschlaefer1.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Siebenschlaefer1.JPG/220px-Siebenschlaefer1.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Siebenschlaefer1.JPG/330px-Siebenschlaefer1.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Siebenschlaefer1.JPG/440px-Siebenschlaefer1.JPG 2x" data-file-width="4032" data-file-height="2688" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Siebenschlaefer1.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Headstone" title="Headstone">Headstones</a> in the <a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siebenschl%C3%A4ferkirche_(Rotthof)" class="extiw" title="de:Siebenschläferkirche (Rotthof)">Siebenschläferkirche (Rotthof), Germany</a></div></div></div>
<p>Decius died in 251, and many years passed during which Christianity went from being persecuted to being the <a href="/wiki/State_church_of_the_Roman_Empire" class="mw-redirect" title="State church of the Roman Empire">state religion of the Roman Empire</a>. At some later time—usually given as during the reign of <a href="/wiki/Theodosius_II" title="Theodosius II">Theodosius II</a> (408–450)—in AD 447 when heated discussions were taking place between various schools of Christianity about the resurrection of the body in the day of judgement and life after death, a landowner decided to open up the sealed mouth of the cave, thinking to use it as a cattle pen. He opened it and found the sleepers inside. They awoke, imagining that they had slept but one day, and sent one of their number to Ephesus to buy food, with instructions to be careful.<sup id="cite_ref-Sabine_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Sabine-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Upon arriving in the city, this person was astounded to find buildings with crosses attached; the townspeople for their part were astounded to find a man trying to spend old coins from the reign of Decius. The bishop was summoned to interview the sleepers; they told him their miracle story, and died praising God.<sup id="cite_ref-Fortescue_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fortescue-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The various lives of the Seven Sleepers in Greek are listed and in other non-Latin languages at <a href="/wiki/Bibliotheca_Hagiographica_Orientalis" title="Bibliotheca Hagiographica Orientalis"><i>BHO</i></a>.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Dissemination">Dissemination</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Dissemination">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The story rapidly attained a wide diffusion throughout Christendom. It was popularized in the West by Gregory of Tours, in his late 6th-century collection of miracles, <i>De gloria martyrum</i> (<i>Glory of the Martyrs)</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-Sabine_18-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Sabine-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Gregory claimed to have gotten the story from "a certain Syrian interpreter" (<i>Syro quidam interpretante</i>), but this could refer to either a Syriac- or Greek-speaker from the Levant.<sup id="cite_ref-WW_5-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WW-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> During the period of the <a href="/wiki/Crusades" title="Crusades">Crusades</a>, bones from the sepulchres near Ephesus, identified as relics of the Seven Sleepers, were transported to <a href="/wiki/Marseille" title="Marseille">Marseille</a>, France, in a large stone coffin, which remained a trophy of the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_St_Victor,_Marseille" title="Abbey of St Victor, Marseille">Abbey of St Victor, Marseille</a>.
</p><p>The Seven Sleepers were included in the <i><a href="/wiki/Golden_Legend" title="Golden Legend">Golden Legend</a></i> compilation, the most popular book of the later Middle Ages, which fixed a precise date for their resurrection, AD&#160;478, in the reign of Theodosius.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Caves_of_the_Seven_Sleepers">Caves of the Seven Sleepers</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Caves of the Seven Sleepers">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Several sites<sup id="cite_ref-madaincavelocation_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-madaincavelocation-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> are attributed as the "Cave of the Seven Sleepers", but none have been archaeologically proven to be the actual site. As the earliest versions of the legend spread from <a href="/wiki/Ephesus" title="Ephesus">Ephesus</a>, an early Christian <a href="/wiki/Catacomb" class="mw-redirect" title="Catacomb">catacomb</a> came to be associated with it, attracting scores of pilgrims. On the slopes of Mount Pion (Mount Coelian) near Ephesus (near modern <a href="/wiki/Sel%C3%A7uk" title="Selçuk">Selçuk</a> in Turkey), the <a href="/wiki/Grotto" title="Grotto">grotto</a> of the Seven Sleepers with ruins of the religious site built over it was excavated in 1926–1928.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 394">&#58;&#8202;394&#8202;</span></sup> The excavation brought to light several hundred graves dated to the 5th and 6th&#160;centuries. Inscriptions dedicated to the Seven Sleepers were found on the walls and in the graves. This grotto is still shown to tourists.
</p><p>Other possible sites of the cave of the Seven Sleepers are in <a href="/wiki/Af%C5%9Fin" title="Afşin">Afşin</a> and <a href="/wiki/Tarsus,_Mersin" title="Tarsus, Mersin">Tarsus</a>, Turkey. Afşin is near the antique <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman</a> city of <a href="/wiki/Arabissus" title="Arabissus">Arabissus</a>, to which the <a href="/wiki/East_Roman" class="mw-redirect" title="East Roman">East Roman</a> Emperor <a href="/wiki/Justinian" class="mw-redirect" title="Justinian">Justinian</a> paid a visit. The Emperor brought marble niches as gifts from Western Turkey for the site, which are preserved inside the <a href="/wiki/Eshab-%C4%B1_Kehf_Kulliye" title="Eshab-ı Kehf Kulliye">Eshab-ı Kehf Kulliye</a> mosque to this day. The site was a <a href="/wiki/Hittites" title="Hittites">Hittite</a> temple, used as a Roman temple and later as a church in Roman and <a href="/wiki/Byzantine" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine">Byzantine</a> times. The <a href="/wiki/Seljuks" class="mw-redirect" title="Seljuks">Seljuks</a> continued to use the place of worship as a church and a mosque. It was turned into a mosque over time with the conversion of the local population to Islam.
</p><p>There is a cave near <a href="/wiki/Amman" title="Amman">Amman</a>, Jordan, also known as the cave of seven sleepers, which has eight smaller sealed tombs present inside and a ventilation duct coming out of the cave.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Modern_literature">Modern literature</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Modern literature">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_modern">Early modern</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Early modern">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>

<p>The account had become proverbial in 16th&#160;century Protestant culture. The poet <a href="/wiki/John_Donne" title="John Donne">John Donne</a> could ask,
</p>
<div class="poem">
<p>&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;I wonder, by my troth, what thou and I<br />
&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;Did, till we loved? Were we not weaned till then?<br />
&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;But sucked on country pleasures, childishly?<br />
&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;&#160;Or snorted we in the Seven Sleepers' den?—John Donne, "<a href="/wiki/The_Good-Morrow" title="The Good-Morrow">The Good-Morrow</a>".
</p>
</div>
<p>In <a href="/wiki/John_Heywood" title="John Heywood">John Heywood</a>'s <i>Play called the Four PP</i> (1530s), the Pardoner, a Renaissance update of the protagonist in <a href="/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer" title="Geoffrey Chaucer">Chaucer</a>'s "<a href="/wiki/The_Pardoner%27s_Tale" title="The Pardoner&#39;s Tale">The Pardoner's Tale</a>", offers his companions the opportunity to kiss "a slipper / Of one of the Seven Sleepers", but the relic is presented as absurdly as the Pardoner's other offerings, which include "the great-toe of the Trinity" and "a buttock-bone of Pentecost."<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Little is heard of the Seven Sleepers during <a href="/wiki/The_Enlightenment" class="mw-redirect" title="The Enlightenment">the Enlightenment</a>, but the account revived with the coming of <a href="/wiki/Romanticism" title="Romanticism">Romanticism</a>. The <i>Golden Legend</i> may have been the source for retellings of the Seven Sleepers in <a href="/wiki/Thomas_de_Quincey" class="mw-redirect" title="Thomas de Quincey">Thomas de Quincey</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Confessions_of_an_English_Opium-Eater" title="Confessions of an English Opium-Eater">Confessions of an English Opium-Eater</a></i>, in a poem by <a href="/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe" title="Johann Wolfgang von Goethe">Goethe</a>, <a href="/wiki/Washington_Irving" title="Washington Irving">Washington Irving</a>'s "<a href="/wiki/Rip_van_Winkle" class="mw-redirect" title="Rip van Winkle">Rip van Winkle</a>", <a href="/wiki/H._G._Wells" title="H. G. Wells">H. G. Wells</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/The_Sleeper_Awakes" title="The Sleeper Awakes">The Sleeper Awakes</a></i>. It also might have an influence on the motif of the "<a href="/wiki/King_asleep_in_mountain" title="King asleep in mountain">King asleep in mountain</a>". <a href="/wiki/Mark_Twain" title="Mark Twain">Mark Twain</a> did a burlesque of the story of the Seven Sleepers in Chapter&#160;13 of Volume&#160;2 of <i>The Innocents Abroad</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Contemporary">Contemporary</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seven_Sleepers&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Contemporary">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Serbian writer <a href="/wiki/Danilo_Ki%C5%A1" title="Danilo Kiš">Danilo Kiš</a> retells the story of the Seven Sleepers in a short story, "The Legend of the Sleepers" from his book <i>The Encyclopedia of the Dead</i>.
</p><p>Italian author <a href="/wiki/Andrea_Camilleri" title="Andrea Camilleri">Andrea Camilleri</a> incorporates the story in his novel <i><a href="/wiki/The_Terracotta_Dog" title="The Terracotta Dog">The Terracotta Dog</a></i>, in which the protagonist is led to a cave containing the titular watchdog (as described in the <i>Qur'an</i> and called "Kytmyr" in Sicilian folk-lore) and the saucer of silver coins with which one of the sleepers is to buy "pure food" from the bazaar in Ephesus (<i>Qur'an</i> 18.19). The Seven Sleepers are symbolically replaced by lovers <a href="/w/index.php?title=Lisetta_Moscato&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Lisetta Moscato (page does not exist)">Lisetta Moscato</a> and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Mario_Cunich&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Mario Cunich (page does not exist)">Mario Cunich</a>, who were killed in their nuptial bed by an assassin hired by Lisseta's incestuous father and later laid to rest in a cave in the Sicilian countryside.
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Susan_Cooper" title="Susan Cooper">Susan Cooper</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/The_Dark_Is_Rising" title="The Dark Is Rising">The Dark Is Rising</a></i> series, Will Stanton awakens the Seven Sleepers in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Grey_King" title="The Grey King">The Grey King</a></i>, and in <i><a href="/wiki/Silver_on_the_Tree" title="Silver on the Tree">Silver on the Tree</a></i> they ride in the last battle against the Dark.
</p><p>The <i>Seven Sleepers</i> series by <a href="/wiki/Gilbert_Morris" title="Gilbert Morris">Gilbert Morris</a> takes a modern approach to the story, in which seven teenagers must be awakened to fight evil in a post-nuclear-apocalypse world.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/John_Buchan" title="John Buchan">John Buchan</a> refers to the Seven Sleepers in <i><a href="/wiki/The_Three_Hostages" title="The Three Hostages">The Three Hostages</a></i>, where <a href="/wiki/Richard_Hannay" title="Richard Hannay">Richard Hannay</a> surmises that his wife Mary, who is a sound sleeper, is descended from one of the seven who has married one of the <a href="/wiki/Parable_of_the_Ten_Virgins" title="Parable of the Ten Virgins">Foolish Virgins</a>.
</p><p>Several languages have idioms related to the Seven Sleepers, including:
</p>
<ul><li><a href="/wiki/Hungarian_language" title="Hungarian language">Hungarian</a>: <i>hétalvó</i>, literally a "seven-sleeper", or "one who sleeps for an entire week", is a colloquial reference to a person who oversleeps or who is typically drowsy.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup><sup class="reference nowrap"><span title="Page / location: 8">&#58;&#8202;8&#8202;</span></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Irish_language" title="Irish language">Irish</a>: "Na seacht gcodlatáin" refers to hibernating animals.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Norwegian_language" title="Norwegian language">Norwegian</a>: a late riser may be referred to as a <i>syvsover</i> ("seven sleeper")<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Swedish_language" title="Swedish language">Swedish</a>: a late riser may be referred to as a <i>sjusovare</i> ("seven sleeper").<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Welsh_language" title="Welsh language">Welsh</a>: a late riser may be referred to as a <i>saith cysgadur</i> ("seven sleeper") – as in the 1885 novel <i><a href="/wiki/Rhys_Lewis_(novel)" title="Rhys Lewis (novel)">Rhys Lewis</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/Daniel_Owen" title="Daniel Owen">Daniel Owen</a>, where the protagonist is referred to as such in chapter 37, p.&#160;294 (Hughes a'i Fab, <a href="/wiki/Caerdydd" class="mw-redirect" title="Caerdydd">Caerdydd</a>, 1948).</li></ul>
