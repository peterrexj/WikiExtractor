<p><b>Cuthbert</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a>&#58; <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">Cūþbeorht</i></span>, <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Cuthbertus</i>;<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> died 26 October 760) was a medieval Anglo-Saxon <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a> in England. Prior to his elevation to Canterbury, he was abbot of a monastic house, and perhaps may have been <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Hereford" title="Bishop of Hereford">Bishop of Hereford</a> also, but evidence for his holding Hereford mainly dates from after the <a href="/wiki/Norman_Conquest_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Norman Conquest of England">Norman Conquest of England</a> in 1066. While Archbishop, he held church councils and built a new church in Canterbury. It was during Cuthbert's archbishopric that the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_York" title="Diocese of York">Diocese of York</a> was raised to an archbishopric. Cuthbert died in 760 and was later regarded as a saint.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_Hereford">Early life and Hereford</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert_of_Canterbury&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life and Hereford">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Of noble birth,<sup id="cite_ref-Hindley106_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hindley106-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Cuthbert is first recorded as the <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> of <a href="/wiki/Lyminge_Abbey" title="Lyminge Abbey">Lyminge Abbey</a>, from where he was elevated to the <a href="/wiki/See_of_Hereford" class="mw-redirect" title="See of Hereford">see of Hereford</a> in 736.<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook217_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook217-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The identification of the Cuthbert who was Bishop of Hereford with the Cuthbert who became archbishop, however, comes from <a href="/wiki/Florence_of_Worcester" title="Florence of Worcester">Florence of Worcester</a> and other post-<a href="/wiki/Norman_conquest_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Norman conquest of England">Conquest</a> sources. The contemporary record in the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> says that Cuthbert was <a href="/wiki/Consecrated" class="mw-redirect" title="Consecrated">consecrated</a> archbishop, where if he had been Bishop of Hereford, he would have been <a href="/wiki/Translation_(ecclesiastical)" title="Translation (ecclesiastical)">translated</a>. No consecration is needed when a bishop is translated from one see to another. Given the nature of the sources, the identification of the bishop of Hereford with the archbishop of Canterbury, while likely, must not be regarded as proven.<sup id="cite_ref-Brooks80_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks80-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>If Cuthbert was at Hereford, he served in that capacity for four years before his elevation to the <a href="/wiki/See_of_Canterbury" class="mw-redirect" title="See of Canterbury">See of Canterbury</a> in 740.<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook214_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook214-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He is credited with the composition of an epitaph for the tomb of his three predecessors at Hereford. The cathedral church of the see may not even have been located at Hereford by Cuthbert's time.<sup id="cite_ref-DNBPutta_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNBPutta-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Kings31_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kings31-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Whoever Cuthbert was prior to his election to Canterbury, he probably owed his selection as archbishop to the influence of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelbald_of_Mercia" title="Æthelbald of Mercia">Æthelbald, King of Mercia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby113_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby113-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> A number of Mercians were appointed to Canterbury during the 730s and 740s, which suggests that Mercian authority was expanding into Kent.<sup id="cite_ref-Kingship24_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kingship24-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Canterbury">Canterbury</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert_of_Canterbury&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Canterbury">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Cuthbert was the recipient of a long letter from <a href="/wiki/Boniface" class="mw-redirect" title="Boniface">Boniface</a> who complained about the lax morals of the clergy in the British Isles,<sup id="cite_ref-Hindley142_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hindley142-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> and too much drinking of alcohol by the Anglo-Saxon bishops.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby52_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby52-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Cuthbert also sent letters to <a href="/wiki/Lullus" title="Lullus">Lull</a> who was <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Mainz" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Mainz">Archbishop of Mainz</a> and a native of England.<sup id="cite_ref-ASE174_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ASE174-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> During Cuthbert's time as archbishop he no longer claimed authority over all of Britain, like his predecessor Theodore. Pope <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_III" title="Pope Gregory III">Gregory III</a> in 735 had sent a <a href="/wiki/Pallium" title="Pallium">pallium</a> to the bishop of York, raising the see of York to the status of an archbishopric. As a sign of the enhanced status of York, Cuthbert only consecrated bishops south of the <a href="/wiki/Humber" title="Humber">Humber</a> and his synods were attended only by bishops from the south of England.<sup id="cite_ref-Brooks80_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks80-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cuthbert presided over the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Clovesho" class="mw-redirect" title="Council of Clovesho">Council of Clovesho</a> in 747 along with Æthelbald of Mercia.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby116_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby116-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> This gathering mandated that all clergy should explain the basic tenets of Christianity to the laity,<sup id="cite_ref-Hindley106_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hindley106-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> as well as legislating on clerical dress, control of monasteries, and the behavior of the clergy. It also mandated that each diocese hold a synod to proclaim the decisions of the council.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby116_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby116-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Cuthbert sent his <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a> Cynebert to Pope Gregory III after the council with a report on the council and its resolutions. This action may have been taken in response to Boniface's complaints about Cuthbert and Æthelbald to the papacy.<sup id="cite_ref-Hindley106_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hindley106-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The actions of the council were also gathered into a collection at Cuthbert's command.<sup id="cite_ref-Blair111_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Blair111-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After the council, Cuthbert continued to correspond with Boniface up until Boniface's martyrdom in 754, and then sent condolences to Boniface's successor. Cuthbert held a second synod in 758, but nothing is known of any enactments it made. He also built the church of St. John the Baptist in <a href="/wiki/Canterbury" title="Canterbury">Canterbury</a>, which was destroyed by fire in 1067. He was buried in his new church.<sup id="cite_ref-DNB_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-DNB-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> The new church was located on the west side of the cathedral, and was used as a <a href="/wiki/Baptistery" title="Baptistery">baptistery</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Blair202_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Blair202-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Brooks39_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks39-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> The church also became a burial site for many of the archbishops, and later was used for trials by ordeal. There is no explicit contemporary reference that states that these uses were intended by Cuthbert, but the fact that the church was dedicated to St. John the Baptist argues strongly that Cuthbert at least intended the new building as a baptistery.<sup id="cite_ref-Brooks51_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks51-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The burial practices of the archbishops did change after Cuthbert, but it is not clear whether this was intended by Cuthbert, as a Post-Conquest Canterbury <a href="/wiki/Cartulary" title="Cartulary">cartulary</a> has it, or due to other reasons, unconnected with Cuthbert. Although <a href="/wiki/Sonia_Chadwick_Hawkes" title="Sonia Chadwick Hawkes">Sonia Hawkes</a> argues that the change in burial customs, which extended over most of Britain, resulted from Cuthbert's mandating burial in church yards, instead of outside the city limits as had been the custom previously. However, the main evidence for this theory is a 16th-century tradition at Canterbury and the archaeological evidence of a change in burial patterns. Although a change did occur, the archaeological evidence does not give a reason why this change happened, and given the late date of the Canterbury tradition, the theory cannot be considered proven.<sup id="cite_ref-Brooks80_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Brooks80-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_legacy">Death and legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cuthbert_of_Canterbury&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Death and legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Cuthbert died on 26 October 760,<sup id="cite_ref-Handbook214_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Handbook214-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> and was later considered a saint with a feast day of 26 October.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> He was buried in his church of St. John, and was the first Archbishop of Canterbury that was not buried in <a href="/wiki/St_Augustine%27s_Abbey" title="St Augustine&#39;s Abbey">St Augustine's Abbey</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Intro150_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Intro150-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> His letters to the <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_mission" title="Anglo-Saxon mission">Anglo-Saxon missionaries</a> on the European continent show him to have been highly educated.<sup id="cite_ref-Intro144_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Intro144-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
