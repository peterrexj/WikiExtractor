<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">For the author of "Verses on the Battle that was Fought at Fontenoy", see <a href="/wiki/Angelbert" title="Angelbert">Angelbert</a>.</div>

<p><b>Angilbert</b> (<span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;760</span>&#160;– 18 February 814)<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECE1913_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECE1913-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> was a noble <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frankish</a> poet who was educated under <a href="/wiki/Alcuin" title="Alcuin">Alcuin</a> and served <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a> as a secretary, diplomat, and son-in-law. He is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Pre-Congregation" class="mw-redirect" title="Pre-Congregation">pre-Congregation</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and is still honored on the day of his death, 18 February.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Angilbert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:BN_lat._13159,_fol._43r.jpeg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/e/ed/BN_lat._13159%2C_fol._43r.jpeg/220px-BN_lat._13159%2C_fol._43r.jpeg" decoding="async" width="220" height="353" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/e/ed/BN_lat._13159%2C_fol._43r.jpeg/330px-BN_lat._13159%2C_fol._43r.jpeg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/e/ed/BN_lat._13159%2C_fol._43r.jpeg/440px-BN_lat._13159%2C_fol._43r.jpeg 2x" data-file-width="951" data-file-height="1526" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:BN_lat._13159,_fol._43r.jpeg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>A page from the <a href="/wiki/Psalter_of_Charlemagne" title="Psalter of Charlemagne">Psalter of Charlemagne</a>, copied between 795 and 800, and probably given by Charlemagne to Angilbert when the king visited Saint-Riquier for Easter 800.<sup id="cite_ref-MH2001a_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MH2001a-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup></div></div></div>
<p>Angilbert seems to have been brought up at the court of Charlemagne at the palace school in Aquae Grani (<a href="/wiki/Aachen" title="Aachen">Aachen</a>). He was educated there as the pupil and then friend of the great English scholar Alcuin. When Charlemagne sent his young son <a href="/wiki/Pepin_of_Italy" title="Pepin of Italy">Pepin</a> to Italy as King of the <a href="/wiki/Lombards" title="Lombards">Lombards</a> Angilbert went along as <i>primicerius palatii,</i> a high administrator of the satellite court.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECE1913_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECE1913-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> As the friend and adviser of Pepin, he assisted for a while in the government of Italy. Angilbert delivered the document on <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm" title="Byzantine Iconoclasm">Iconoclasm</a> from the Frankish Synod of Frankfurt to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Adrian_I" title="Pope Adrian I">Pope Adrian I</a>, and was later sent on three important embassies to the pope, in 792, 794, and 796.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1911_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> At one time, he served an officer of the maritime provinces.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECITD1998_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECITD1998-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He accompanied Charlemagne to Rome in 800<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1878_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1878-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> and was one of the <a href="/wiki/Testament_of_Charlemagne" title="Testament of Charlemagne">witnesses to his will in 811</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1911_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There are various traditions concerning Angilbert's relationship with <a href="/wiki/Bertha,_daughter_of_Charlemagne" title="Bertha, daughter of Charlemagne">Bertha, daughter of Charlemagne</a>. One holds that they were married,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1878_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1878-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> another that they were not.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECITD1998_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECITD1998-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> They had, however, at least two sons and one daughter, one of whom, <a href="/wiki/Nithard" title="Nithard">Nithard</a>, became a notable figure in the mid-9th century,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1911_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and the daughter Bertha, went on to marry Helgaud II, count of Ponthieu. Control of marriage and the meanings of legitimacy were hotly contested in the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>.  Bertha and Angilbert are an example of how resistance to the idea of a sacramental marriage could coincide with holding church offices. On the other hand, some historians have speculated that Charlemagne opposed formal marriages for his daughters out of concern for political rivalries from their potential husbands; none of Charlemagne's daughters were married, despite political offers of arranged marriages.
</p><p>In 790, Angilbert retired to the abbey of Centulum, the "Monastery of St Richarius" (<span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Sancti Richarii monasterium</i></span>) at present-day <a href="/wiki/Saint-Riquier" title="Saint-Riquier">Saint-Riquier</a> in <a href="/wiki/Picardy" title="Picardy">Picardy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1878_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1878-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Elected <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> in 794,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1878_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1878-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> he rebuilt the monastery and endowed it with a library of 200 volumes.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECE1913_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECE1913-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> It was not uncommon for the <a href="/wiki/Merovingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingian">Merovingian</a>, <a href="/wiki/Carolingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Carolingian">Carolingian</a>, or later kings to make laymen abbots of monasteries; the layman would often use the income of the monastery as his own and leave the monks a bare minimum for the necessary expenses of the foundation. Angilbert, in contrast, spent a great deal rebuilding Saint-Riquier; when he completed it, Charlemagne spent Easter of the year 800 there. In keeping with Carolingian policies, Angilbert established a school at Saint-Riquier to educate the local boys.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEBE2003_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEBE2003-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Poetry">Poetry</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Angilbert&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Poetry">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Angilbert's Latin poems reveal the culture and tastes of a man of the world, enjoying the closest intimacy with the imperial family.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1911_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Charlemagne and the other men at court were known by affectionate and jesting nicknames. Charlemagne was referred to as "David", a reference to the Biblical king <a href="/wiki/David" title="David">David</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWilmot-Buxton192293_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilmot-Buxton192293-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Angilbert was nicknamed "<a href="/wiki/Homer" title="Homer">Homer</a>" because he wrote poetry,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECITD1998_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECITD1998-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and was the probable author of an <a href="/wiki/Epic_poetry" title="Epic poetry">epic</a>, of which the fragment which has been preserved describes the life at the palace and the meeting between Charlemagne and Leo III. It is a mosaic from <a href="/wiki/Virgil" title="Virgil">Virgil</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ovid" title="Ovid">Ovid</a>, <a href="/wiki/Lucan" title="Lucan">Lucan</a> and <a href="/wiki/Venantius_Fortunatus" title="Venantius Fortunatus">Venantius Fortunatus</a>, composed in the manner of Einhard's use of Suetonius.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1911_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Of the shorter poems, besides the greeting to Pippin on his return from the campaign against the <a href="/wiki/Pannonian_Avars" title="Pannonian Avars">Avars</a> (796), an epistle to David (i.e., Charlemagne) incidentally reveals a delightful picture of the poet living with his children in a house surrounded by pleasant gardens near the emperor's palace. The reference to Bertha, however, is distant and respectful, her name occurring merely on the list of princesses to whom he sends his salutation.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1911_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The poem <i><a href="/wiki/De_conversione_Saxonum" class="mw-redirect" title="De conversione Saxonum">De conversione Saxonum</a></i> has been attributed to Angilbert.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERabe199554_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERabe199554-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Angilbert's poems were published by <a href="/wiki/Ernst_D%C3%BCmmler" title="Ernst Dümmler">Ernst Dümmler</a> in the <i><a href="/wiki/Monumenta_Germaniae_Historica" title="Monumenta Germaniae Historica">Monumenta Germaniae Historica</a></i>. For criticisms of this edition, see <a href="/wiki/Ludwig_Traube_(palaeographer)" title="Ludwig Traube (palaeographer)">Ludwig Traube</a> in Max Roediger's <i>Schriften für germanische Philologie</i> (1888).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEB1911_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
