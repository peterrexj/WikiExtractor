<p><b>Fra Angelico</b>, OP (born <b>Guido di Pietro</b>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1395</span><sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>&#160;&#8211;&#32;18 February 1455) was a <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Preachers" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of Preachers">Dominican</a> <a href="/wiki/Friar" title="Friar">friar</a> and <a href="/wiki/Italians" title="Italians">Italian</a> <a href="/wiki/List_of_Italian_painters" title="List of Italian painters">painter</a> of the Early <a href="/wiki/Italian_Renaissance" title="Italian Renaissance">Renaissance</a>, described by <a href="/wiki/Giorgio_Vasari" title="Giorgio Vasari">Giorgio Vasari</a> in his <i><a href="/wiki/Lives_of_the_Artists" class="mw-redirect" title="Lives of the Artists">Lives of the Artists</a></i> as having "a rare and perfect talent".<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He earned his reputation primarily for the series of frescoes he made for his own friary, San Marco, in <a href="/wiki/Florence" title="Florence">Florence</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> then worked in Rome and other cities. All his known work is of religious subjects.
</p><p>He was known to contemporaries as <b>Fra Giovanni da Fiesole</b> (Brother John of <a href="/wiki/Fiesole" title="Fiesole">Fiesole</a>) and <b>Fra Giovanni Angelico</b> (Angelic Brother John). In modern Italian he is called <i><b>Beato Angelico</b></i> (Blessed Angelic One);<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> the common English name Fra Angelico means the "Angelic friar".
</p><p>In 1982, <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> proclaimed him <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">"blessed"</a><sup id="cite_ref-blessed_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-blessed-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> in recognition of the holiness of his life, thereby making the title of "Blessed" official. <a href="/wiki/Fiesole" title="Fiesole">Fiesole</a> is sometimes misinterpreted as being part of his formal name, but it was merely the name of the town where he took his vows as a <a href="/wiki/Dominican_friar" class="mw-redirect" title="Dominican friar">Dominican friar</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERossetti19116_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERossetti19116-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> and was used by contemporaries to distinguish him from others who were also known as Fra Giovanni. He is listed in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a><sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> as <i>Beatus Ioannes Faesulanus, cognomento Angelicus</i>—"Blessed John of Fiesole, surnamed 'the Angelic' ".
</p><p>Vasari wrote of Fra Angelico that "it is impossible to bestow too much praise on this holy father, who was so humble and modest in all that he did and said and whose pictures were painted with such facility and piety."<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span id="Early_life.2C_1395.E2.80.931436"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life,_1395–1436">Early life, 1395–1436</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early life, 1395–1436">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Fra Angelico was born Guido di Pietro in the hamlet of <a href="/wiki/Vicchio_di_Mugello" class="mw-redirect" title="Vicchio di Mugello">Rupecanina</a><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> in the <a href="/wiki/Tuscany" title="Tuscany">Tuscan</a> area of <a href="/wiki/Mugello_region" class="mw-redirect" title="Mugello region">Mugello</a> near <a href="/wiki/Fiesole" title="Fiesole">Fiesole</a> towards the end of the 14th century. Nothing is known of his parents. He was baptized Guido. As a child, he was probably known after the Italian fashion as Guidolino ("Little Guido"). The earliest recorded document concerning Fra Angelico dates from 17 October 1417, when he joined a religious confraternity or guild at the <a href="/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Mount_Carmel" title="Our Lady of Mount Carmel">Carmine</a> Church, still under the name of Guido di Pietro.  This record reveals that he was already a painter, a fact that is subsequently confirmed by two records of payment to Guido di Pietro in January and February 1418 for work done in the church of Santo Stefano del Ponte.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  The first record of Angelico as a friar dates from 1423, when he is first referred to as Fra Giovanni (Friar John), following the custom of those entering one of the older <a href="/wiki/Religious_order_(Catholic)" title="Religious order (Catholic)">religious orders</a> of taking a new name.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>  He was a member of the local community at Fiesole, not far from Florence, of the <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican Order</a>, one of the medieval Orders belonging to a category known as <a href="/wiki/Mendicant_Orders" class="mw-redirect" title="Mendicant Orders">mendicant Orders</a> because they generally lived not from the income of estates but from begging or donations. <a href="/wiki/Fra" class="mw-redirect" title="Fra">Fra</a>, a contraction of <i>frater</i> (Latin for 'brother'), is a conventional title for a mendicant friar.
</p><p>According to Vasari, Fra Angelico initially received training as an <a href="/wiki/Illuminated_manuscripts" class="mw-redirect" title="Illuminated manuscripts">illuminator</a>, possibly working with his older brother <a href="/wiki/Benedetto_da_Fiesole" title="Benedetto da Fiesole">Benedetto</a> who was also a Dominican and an illuminator. The former Dominican convent of <a href="/wiki/San_Marco,_Florence" title="San Marco, Florence">San Marco</a> in Florence, now a state museum, holds several manuscripts that are thought to be entirely or partly by his hand.<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The painter <a href="/wiki/Lorenzo_Monaco" title="Lorenzo Monaco">Lorenzo Monaco</a> may have contributed to his art training, and the influence of the <a href="/wiki/Sienese_school" class="mw-redirect" title="Sienese school">Sienese school</a> is discernible in his work. He also trained with master Varricho in Milan<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERossetti19116–7_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERossetti19116–7-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> He had several important charges in the <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convents</a> he lived in, but this did not limit his art, which very soon became famous.  According to <a href="/wiki/Vasari" class="mw-redirect" title="Vasari">Vasari</a>, the first paintings of this artist were an <a href="/wiki/Altarpiece" title="Altarpiece">altarpiece</a> and a painted screen for the <a href="/wiki/Carthusians" title="Carthusians">Charterhouse (Carthusian monastery)</a> of <a href="/wiki/Florence_Charterhouse" title="Florence Charterhouse">Florence</a>; none such exist there now.<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>From 1408 to 1418, Fra Angelico was at the <a href="/wiki/San_Domenico,_Cortona" title="San Domenico, Cortona">Dominican friary of Cortona</a>, where he painted frescoes, now mostly destroyed, in the Dominican Church and may have been assistant to <a href="/wiki/Gherardo_Starnina" title="Gherardo Starnina">Gherardo Starnina</a> or a follower of his.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Between 1418 and 1436 he was at the <a href="/wiki/Convent_of_San_Domenico,_Fiesole" title="Convent of San Domenico, Fiesole">convent of Fiesole</a>, where he also executed a number of frescoes for the church and the <a href="/wiki/Fiesole_Altarpiece" title="Fiesole Altarpiece">Fiesole Altarpiece</a>, which was deteriorated but has since been restored. A predella of the altarpiece remains intact and is conserved in the <a href="/wiki/National_Gallery,_London" class="mw-redirect" title="National Gallery, London">National Gallery, London</a>, and is a great example of Fra Angelico's ability. It shows Christ in Glory surrounded by more than 250 figures, including beatified Dominicans. In this period he painted some of his masterpieces including a version of  <i><a href="/wiki/Madonna_of_Humility_(Fra_Angelico)" title="Madonna of Humility (Fra Angelico)">The Madonna of Humility</a></i>, well preserved and property of the <a href="/wiki/Thyssen-Bornemisza_Museum" title="Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum">Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum</a> but on loan to the <a href="/wiki/Museu_Nacional_d%27Art_de_Catalunya" title="Museu Nacional d&#39;Art de Catalunya">MNAC</a> of Barcelona, an <a href="/wiki/Annunciation_(Fra_Angelico,_Madrid)" title="Annunciation (Fra Angelico, Madrid)"><i>Annunciation</i></a> along with a <i><a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/the-virgin-with-the-pomegranate/61b654df-1de2-483f-88bb-404f37747d4a">Madonna of the Pomegranate</a>,</i> at the <a href="/wiki/Museo_del_Prado" title="Museo del Prado">Prado Museum</a>.
</p>
<h3><span id="San_Marco.2C_Florence.2C_1436.E2.80.931445"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="San_Marco,_Florence,_1436–1445">San Marco, Florence, 1436–1445</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: San Marco, Florence, 1436–1445">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:ANGELICO,_Fra_Annunciation,_1437-46_(2236990916).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/ANGELICO%2C_Fra_Annunciation%2C_1437-46_%282236990916%29.jpg/280px-ANGELICO%2C_Fra_Annunciation%2C_1437-46_%282236990916%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="280" height="194" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/ANGELICO%2C_Fra_Annunciation%2C_1437-46_%282236990916%29.jpg/420px-ANGELICO%2C_Fra_Annunciation%2C_1437-46_%282236990916%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/ANGELICO%2C_Fra_Annunciation%2C_1437-46_%282236990916%29.jpg/560px-ANGELICO%2C_Fra_Annunciation%2C_1437-46_%282236990916%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1008" data-file-height="700" /></a><figcaption><i><a href="/wiki/Annunciation_(Fra_Angelico,_San_Marco)" title="Annunciation (Fra Angelico, San Marco)">Annunciation</a></i>, <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1440–1445</span></figcaption></figure>
<p>In 1436, Fra Angelico was one of a number of the friars from Fiesole who moved to the newly built <a href="/wiki/San_Marco,_Florence" title="San Marco, Florence">convent or friary of San Marco</a> in Florence. This was an important move which put him in the centre of artistic activity of the region and brought about the patronage of <a href="/wiki/Cosimo_de%27_Medici" title="Cosimo de&#39; Medici">Cosimo de' Medici</a>, one of the wealthiest and most powerful members of the city's governing authority (or "Signoria") and founder of the dynasty that would dominate Florentine politics for much of the Renaissance. Cosimo had a <a href="/wiki/Cosimo_de%27_Medici%27s_cell" title="Cosimo de&#39; Medici&#39;s cell">cell reserved for himself</a> at the friary in order that he might retreat from the world. 
It was, according to Vasari, at Cosimo's urging that Fra Angelico set about the task of decorating the convent, including the magnificent fresco of the Chapter House, the often-reproduced <a href="/wiki/Annunciation_(Fra_Angelico,_San_Marco)" title="Annunciation (Fra Angelico, San Marco)">Annunciation</a> at the top of the stairs leading to the cells, the Maesta (or Coronation of the Madonna) with Saints (cell 9) and the many other devotional frescoes, of smaller format but remarkable luminous quality, depicting aspects of the Life of Christ that adorn the walls of each cell.<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1439 Fra Angelico completed one of his most famous works, the <i><a href="/wiki/San_Marco_Altarpiece" title="San Marco Altarpiece">San Marco Altarpiece</a></i> at Florence. The result was unusual for its time. Images of the enthroned Madonna and Child surrounded by saints were common, but they usually depicted a setting that was clearly heaven-like, in which saints and angels hovered about as divine presences rather than people. But in this instance, the saints stand squarely within the space, grouped in a natural way as if they were able to converse about the shared experience of witnessing the Virgin in glory. Paintings such as this, known as <a href="/wiki/Sacred_Conversation" class="mw-redirect" title="Sacred Conversation">Sacred Conversations</a>, were to become the major commissions of <a href="/wiki/Giovanni_Bellini" title="Giovanni Bellini">Giovanni Bellini</a>, <a href="/wiki/Perugino" class="mw-redirect" title="Perugino">Perugino</a> and <a href="/wiki/Raphael" title="Raphael">Raphael</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Hartt_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hartt-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Fra_Angelico_-_San_Marco_Altarpiece_-_WGA00509_02.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/90/Fra_Angelico_-_San_Marco_Altarpiece_-_WGA00509_02.jpg/220px-Fra_Angelico_-_San_Marco_Altarpiece_-_WGA00509_02.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="211" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/90/Fra_Angelico_-_San_Marco_Altarpiece_-_WGA00509_02.jpg/330px-Fra_Angelico_-_San_Marco_Altarpiece_-_WGA00509_02.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/90/Fra_Angelico_-_San_Marco_Altarpiece_-_WGA00509_02.jpg/440px-Fra_Angelico_-_San_Marco_Altarpiece_-_WGA00509_02.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1145" data-file-height="1100" /></a><figcaption><i><a href="/wiki/San_Marco_Altarpiece" title="San Marco Altarpiece">San Marco Altarpiece</a></i></figcaption></figure>
<h3><span id="The_Vatican.2C_1445.E2.80.931455"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Vatican,_1445–1455">The Vatican, 1445–1455</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: The Vatican, 1445–1455">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Fra_Angelico_012.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Fra_Angelico_012.jpg/180px-Fra_Angelico_012.jpg" decoding="async" width="180" height="244" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Fra_Angelico_012.jpg/270px-Fra_Angelico_012.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Fra_Angelico_012.jpg/360px-Fra_Angelico_012.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1256" data-file-height="1704" /></a><figcaption><i>The Crucified Christ</i> (detail)</figcaption></figure>
<p>In 1445 <a href="/wiki/Pope_Eugene_IV" title="Pope Eugene IV">Pope Eugene IV</a> summoned him to Rome to paint the frescoes of the Chapel of the <a href="/wiki/Sacraments_of_the_Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Sacraments of the Roman Catholic Church">Holy Sacrament</a> at <a href="/wiki/St_Peter%27s_Basilica" class="mw-redirect" title="St Peter&#39;s Basilica">St Peter's</a>, later demolished by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_III" title="Pope Paul III">Pope Paul III</a>. Vasari claims that at this time Fra Angelico was offered the <a href="/wiki/Archbishopric_of_Florence" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishopric of Florence">Archbishopric of Florence</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Nicholas_V" title="Pope Nicholas V">Pope Nicholas V</a>, and that he refused it, recommending another friar for the position. The story seems possible and even likely. However, if Vasari's date is correct, then the pope must have been <a href="/wiki/Pope_Eugene_IV" title="Pope Eugene IV">Eugene IV</a> and not <a href="/wiki/Pope_Nicholas_V" title="Pope Nicholas V">Nicholas</a>, who was elected Pope only on 6 March 1447. Moreover, the archbishop in 1446–1459 was the Dominican <a href="/wiki/Antoninus_of_Florence" title="Antoninus of Florence">Antoninus of Florence</a> (Antonio Pierozzi), canonized by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Adrian_VI" title="Pope Adrian VI">Pope Adrian VI</a> in 1523. In 1447 Fra Angelico was in <a href="/wiki/Orvieto" title="Orvieto">Orvieto</a> with his pupil, <a href="/wiki/Benozzo_Gozzoli" title="Benozzo Gozzoli">Benozzo Gozzoli</a>, executing works for the <a href="/wiki/Duomo_di_Orvieto" class="mw-redirect" title="Duomo di Orvieto">Cathedral</a>. Among his other pupils were <a href="/wiki/Zanobi_Strozzi" title="Zanobi Strozzi">Zanobi Strozzi</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>From 1447 to 1449 Fra Angelico was back at the Vatican, designing the frescoes for the <a href="/wiki/Niccoline_Chapel" title="Niccoline Chapel">Niccoline Chapel</a> for Nicholas V. The scenes from the lives of the two martyred <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacons</a> of the Early Christian Church, <a href="/wiki/St._Stephen" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Stephen">St. Stephen</a> and <a href="/wiki/St._Lawrence" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Lawrence">St. Lawrence</a> may have been executed wholly or in part by assistants. The small chapel, with its brightly frescoed walls and gold leaf decorations gives the impression of a jewel box.  From 1449 until 1452, Fra Angelico returned to his old convent of Fiesole, where he was the Prior.<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-WMR_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WMR-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_beatification">Death and beatification</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Death and beatification">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Fra_Angelico,_Fra_Filippo_Lippi,_The_Adoration_of_the_Magi.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Fra_Angelico%2C_Fra_Filippo_Lippi%2C_The_Adoration_of_the_Magi.jpg/300px-Fra_Angelico%2C_Fra_Filippo_Lippi%2C_The_Adoration_of_the_Magi.jpg" decoding="async" width="300" height="300" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Fra_Angelico%2C_Fra_Filippo_Lippi%2C_The_Adoration_of_the_Magi.jpg/450px-Fra_Angelico%2C_Fra_Filippo_Lippi%2C_The_Adoration_of_the_Magi.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Fra_Angelico%2C_Fra_Filippo_Lippi%2C_The_Adoration_of_the_Magi.jpg/600px-Fra_Angelico%2C_Fra_Filippo_Lippi%2C_The_Adoration_of_the_Magi.jpg 2x" data-file-width="11480" data-file-height="11480" /></a><figcaption>The <i><a href="/wiki/Adoration_of_the_Magi_(Fra_Angelico_and_Filippo_Lippi)" title="Adoration of the Magi (Fra Angelico and Filippo Lippi)">Adoration of the Magi</a></i> is a <a href="/wiki/Tondo_(art)" title="Tondo (art)">tondo</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Adoration_of_the_Magi" title="Adoration of the Magi">Adoration of the Magi</a>. It is credited to Fra Angelico and <a href="/wiki/Filippo_Lippi" title="Filippo Lippi">Filippo Lippi</a> and dates to c. 1440/1460.</figcaption></figure>
<p>In 1455, Fra Angelico died while staying at a Dominican convent in Rome, perhaps on an order to work on Pope Nicholas' chapel. He was buried in the church of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_sopra_Minerva" title="Santa Maria sopra Minerva">Santa Maria sopra Minerva</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-WMR_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WMR-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> 
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>When singing my praise, don't liken my talents to those of Apelles. <br /> Say, rather, that, in the name of Christ, I gave all I had to the poor.<br />
</p><p>The deeds that count on Earth are not the ones that count in Heaven.<br />
</p><p>
I, Giovanni, am the flower of Tuscany.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite>Translation of epitaph<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup></cite></div></blockquote>
<p>The English writer and critic <a href="/wiki/William_Michael_Rossetti" title="William Michael Rossetti">William Michael Rossetti</a> wrote of the friar:
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>From various accounts of Fra Angelico's life, it is possible to gain some sense of why he was deserving of canonization. He led the devout and ascetic life of a Dominican friar, and never rose above that rank; he followed the dictates of the order in caring for the poor; he was always good-humored. All of his many paintings were of divine subjects, and it seems that he never altered or retouched them, perhaps from a religious conviction that, because his paintings were divinely inspired, they should retain their original form. He was wont to say that he who illustrates the acts of Christ should be with Christ. It is averred that he never handled a brush without fervent prayer and he wept when he painted a Crucifixion. <a href="/wiki/The_Last_Judgment_(Angelico)" class="mw-redirect" title="The Last Judgment (Angelico)">The Last Judgment</a> and the Annunciation were two of the subjects he most frequently treated.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERossetti19117_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERossetti19117-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-WMR_15-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WMR-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote><p>
Pope John Paul II beatified Fra Angelico on 3 October 1982, and in 1984 declared him patron of Catholic artists.<sup id="cite_ref-blessed_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-blessed-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"></p><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>Angelico was reported to say "He who does Christ's work must stay with Christ always". This motto earned him the epithet "Blessed Angelico", because of the perfect integrity of his life and the almost divine beauty of the images he painted, to a superlative extent those of the Blessed Virgin Mary.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite><a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a></cite></div></blockquote>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Evaluation">Evaluation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Evaluation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Fra_Angelico_009.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/Fra_Angelico_009.jpg/388px-Fra_Angelico_009.jpg" decoding="async" width="388" height="190" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/Fra_Angelico_009.jpg/582px-Fra_Angelico_009.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/Fra_Angelico_009.jpg/776px-Fra_Angelico_009.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4096" data-file-height="2008" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/San_Marco,_Florence" title="San Marco, Florence">San Marco, Florence</a>,<i><a href="/wiki/The_Last_Judgment_(Angelico)" class="mw-redirect" title="The Last Judgment (Angelico)">The Day of Judgement</a></i>, upper panel of an altarpiece. It shows the precision, detail and colour required in a commissioned work</figcaption></figure>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Angelico,_tebaide_01.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Angelico%2C_tebaide_01.jpg/388px-Angelico%2C_tebaide_01.jpg" decoding="async" width="388" height="134" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Angelico%2C_tebaide_01.jpg/582px-Angelico%2C_tebaide_01.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Angelico%2C_tebaide_01.jpg/776px-Angelico%2C_tebaide_01.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1508" data-file-height="520" /></a><figcaption>A <a href="/wiki/Thebaide" class="mw-redirect" title="Thebaide">Thebaide</a>, showing the activities in the lives of the saints, 1420</figcaption></figure>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Background">Background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Fra Angelico was working at a time when the style of painting was in a state of change. This process of change had begun a hundred years previous with the works of <a href="/wiki/Giotto" title="Giotto">Giotto</a> and several of his contemporaries, notably <a href="/wiki/Giusto_de%27_Menabuoi" title="Giusto de&#39; Menabuoi">Giusto de' Menabuoi</a>, both of whom had created their major works in <a href="/wiki/Padua,_Italy" class="mw-redirect" title="Padua, Italy">Padua</a>, although Giotto was trained in <a href="/wiki/Florence" title="Florence">Florence</a> by the great Gothic artist, <a href="/wiki/Cimabue" title="Cimabue">Cimabue</a>, and painted a fresco cycle of St Francis in the Bardi Chapel in the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_di_Santa_Croce" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica di Santa Croce">Basilica di Santa Croce</a>. Giotto had many enthusiastic followers, who imitated his style in <a href="/wiki/Fresco" title="Fresco">fresco</a>, some of them, notably the <a href="/wiki/Pietro_Lorenzetti" title="Pietro Lorenzetti">Lorenzetti</a>, achieving great success.<sup id="cite_ref-Hartt_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hartt-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage">Patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Patronage" title="Patronage">patrons</a> of these artists were most often monastic establishments or wealthy families endowing a church. Because the paintings often had devotional purpose, the clients tended to be conservative. Frequently, it would seem, the wealthier the client, the more conservative the painting. There was a very good reason for this. The paintings that were commissioned made a statement about the patron. Thus the more <a href="/wiki/Gold_leaf" title="Gold leaf">gold leaf</a> it displayed, the more it spoke to the patron's glory. The other valuable commodities in the paint-box were <a href="/wiki/Lapis_lazuli" title="Lapis lazuli">lapis lazuli</a> and <a href="/wiki/Vermilion" title="Vermilion">vermilion</a>. Paint made from these colours did not lend itself to a tonal treatment. The azure blue made of powdered lapis lazuli went on flat, the depth and brilliance of colour being, like the gold leaf, a sign of the patron's ability to provide well. For these reasons, <a href="/wiki/Altarpiece" title="Altarpiece">altarpieces</a> are often much more conservatively painted than frescoes, which were often of almost life-sized figures and relied upon a stage-set quality rather than lavish display in order to achieve effect.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Contemporaries">Contemporaries</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Contemporaries">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Fra Angelico was the contemporary of <a href="/wiki/Gentile_da_Fabriano" title="Gentile da Fabriano">Gentile da Fabriano</a>. Gentile's altarpiece of the <i><a href="/wiki/Adoration_of_the_Magi_(Gentile_da_Fabriano)" title="Adoration of the Magi (Gentile da Fabriano)">Adoration of the Magi</a></i>, 1423, in the <a href="/wiki/Uffizi" title="Uffizi">Uffizi</a> is regarded as one of the greatest works of the style known as <a href="/wiki/International_Gothic" title="International Gothic">International Gothic</a>. At the time it was painted, another young artist, known as <a href="/wiki/Masaccio" title="Masaccio">Masaccio</a>, was working on the frescoes for the <a href="/wiki/Brancacci_Chapel" title="Brancacci Chapel">Brancacci Chapel</a> at the church of the Carmine. Masaccio had fully grasped the implications of the art of <a href="/wiki/Giotto" title="Giotto">Giotto</a>. Few painters in Florence saw his sturdy, lifelike and emotional figures and were not affected by them. His work partner was an older painter, <a href="/wiki/Masolino" class="mw-redirect" title="Masolino">Masolino</a>, of the same generation as Fra Angelico. Masaccio died at 27, leaving the work unfinished.<sup id="cite_ref-Hartt_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hartt-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Altarpieces">Altarpieces</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Altarpieces">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The works of Fra Angelico reveal elements that are both conservatively <a href="/wiki/Gothic_art" title="Gothic art">Gothic</a> and progressively <a href="/wiki/Renaissance" title="Renaissance">Renaissance</a>. In the altarpiece of the <a href="/wiki/Coronation_of_the_Virgin" title="Coronation of the Virgin">Coronation of the Virgin</a>, painted for the Florentine church of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_Novella" title="Santa Maria Novella">Santa Maria Novella</a>, are all the elements that a very expensive altarpiece of the 14th century was expected to provide; a precisely tooled <a href="/wiki/Gold_ground" title="Gold ground">gold ground</a>, much azure, and much vermilion. The workmanship of the gilded haloes and gold-edged robes is exquisite and all very Gothic. What makes this a Renaissance painting, as against Gentile da Fabriano's masterpiece, is the solidity, three-dimensionality and naturalism of the figures and the realistic way in which their garments hang or drape around them. Even though it is clouds these figures stand upon, and not the earth, they do so with weight.<sup id="cite_ref-Hartt_13-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hartt-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Fra_Angelico_042_adjusted.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Fra_Angelico_042_adjusted.jpg/220px-Fra_Angelico_042_adjusted.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="261" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Fra_Angelico_042_adjusted.jpg/330px-Fra_Angelico_042_adjusted.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Fra_Angelico_042_adjusted.jpg/440px-Fra_Angelico_042_adjusted.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2536" data-file-height="3013" /></a><figcaption><i>The <a href="/wiki/Transfiguration_of_Jesus" title="Transfiguration of Jesus">Transfiguration</a></i> shows the directness, simplicity and restrained palette typical of these frescoes.  Located in a monk's cell at the Convent San' Marco, its apparent purpose is to encourage private devotion.</figcaption></figure>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Frescoes">Frescoes</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Frescoes">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The series of <a href="/wiki/Frescoes" class="mw-redirect" title="Frescoes">frescoes</a> that Fra Angelico painted for the Dominican friars at San Marcos realise the advancements made by Masaccio and carry them further. Away from the constraints of wealthy clients and the limitations of panel painting, Fra Angelico was able to express his deep reverence for his God and his knowledge and love of humanity. The meditational frescoes in the cells of the convent have a quieting quality about them. They are humble works in simple colours. There is more mauvish pink than there is red, and the brilliant and expensive blue is almost totally lacking. In its place is dull green and the black and white of Dominican robes. There is nothing lavish, nothing to distract from the spiritual experiences of the humble people who are depicted within the frescoes. Each one has the effect of bringing an incident of the life of Christ into the presence of the viewer. They are like windows into a parallel world. These frescoes remain a powerful witness to the piety of the man who created them.<sup id="cite_ref-Hartt_13-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hartt-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
Vasari relates that <a href="/wiki/Cosimo_de%27_Medici" title="Cosimo de&#39; Medici">Cosimo de' Medici</a> seeing these works, inspired Fra Angelico to create a large <a href="/wiki/Crucifixion" title="Crucifixion">Crucifixion</a> scene with many saints for the <a href="/wiki/Chapter_House" class="mw-redirect" title="Chapter House">Chapter House</a>. As with the other frescoes, the wealthy patronage did not influence the Friar's artistic expression with displays of wealth.<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Masaccio ventured into perspective with his creation of a realistically painted niche at <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_Novella" title="Santa Maria Novella">Santa Maria Novella</a>. Subsequently, Fra Angelico demonstrated an understanding of <a href="/wiki/Linear_perspective" class="mw-redirect" title="Linear perspective">linear perspective</a> particularly in his Annunciation paintings set inside the sort of arcades that <a href="/wiki/Michelozzo" title="Michelozzo">Michelozzo</a> and <a href="/wiki/Filippo_Brunelleschi" title="Filippo Brunelleschi">Brunelleschi</a> created at San' Marco's and the square in front of it.<sup id="cite_ref-Hartt_13-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hartt-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Lives_of_the_Saints">Lives of the Saints</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Lives of the Saints">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Angelico,_niccolina_12.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d1/Angelico%2C_niccolina_12.jpg/250px-Angelico%2C_niccolina_12.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="189" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d1/Angelico%2C_niccolina_12.jpg/375px-Angelico%2C_niccolina_12.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d1/Angelico%2C_niccolina_12.jpg/500px-Angelico%2C_niccolina_12.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1031" data-file-height="780" /></a><figcaption><i><a href="/wiki/Saint_Lawrence" title="Saint Lawrence">Saint Lawrence</a> distributing alms</i> (1447), in the Vatican, incorporates the expensive pigments, gold leaf and elaborate design typical of Vatican commissions.</figcaption></figure>
<p>When Fra Angelico and his assistants went to the <a href="/wiki/Vatican_City" title="Vatican City">Vatican</a> to decorate the chapel of Pope Nicholas, the artist was again confronted with the need to please the very wealthiest of clients. In consequence, walking into the small chapel is like stepping into a jewel box. The walls are decked with the brilliance of colour and gold that one sees in the most lavish creations of the Gothic painter <a href="/wiki/Simone_Martini" title="Simone Martini">Simone Martini</a> at the Lower Church of <a href="/wiki/St_Francis_of_Assisi" class="mw-redirect" title="St Francis of Assisi">St Francis of Assisi</a>, a hundred years earlier. Yet Fra Angelico has succeeded in creating designs which continue to reveal his own preoccupation with humanity, with humility and with piety. The figures, in their lavish gilded robes, have the sweetness and gentleness for which his works are famous. According to Vasari:
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>In their bearing and expression, the saints painted by Fra Angelico come nearer to the truth than the figures done by any other artist.<sup id="cite_ref-Vasari_2-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Vasari-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>It is probable that much of the actual painting was done by his assistants to his design. Both Benozzo Gozzoli and Gentile da Fabriano were highly accomplished painters. Benozzo took his art further towards the fully developed Renaissance style with his expressive and lifelike portraits in his masterpiece depicting the <a href="/wiki/Magi_Chapel#Gozzoli&#39;s_Journey_of_the_Magi" title="Magi Chapel">Journey of the Magi</a>, painted in the <a href="/wiki/Medici" class="mw-redirect" title="Medici">Medici</a>'s private chapel at their <a href="/wiki/Palazzo" class="mw-redirect" title="Palazzo">palazzo</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Blessing_Redeemer_(1423);_Fra_Angelico1.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/15/Blessing_Redeemer_%281423%29%3B_Fra_Angelico1.jpg/220px-Blessing_Redeemer_%281423%29%3B_Fra_Angelico1.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="277" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/15/Blessing_Redeemer_%281423%29%3B_Fra_Angelico1.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="316" data-file-height="398" /></a><figcaption><i>Blessing Redeemer</i> (1423)</figcaption></figure>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Artistic_legacy">Artistic legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Artistic legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Through Fra Angelico's pupil Benozzo Gozzoli's careful portraiture and technical expertise in the art of fresco we see a link to <a href="/wiki/Domenico_Ghirlandaio" title="Domenico Ghirlandaio">Domenico Ghirlandaio</a>, who in turn painted extensive schemes for the wealthy patrons of Florence, and through Ghirlandaio to his pupil <a href="/wiki/Michelangelo" title="Michelangelo">Michelangelo</a> and the High Renaissance.
</p><p>Apart from the lineal connection, superficially there may seem little to link the humble priest with his sweetly pretty <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Madonnas</a> and timeless <a href="/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Crucifixion of Christ">Crucifixions</a> to the dynamic expressions of Michelangelo's larger-than-life creations. But both these artists received their most important commissions from the wealthiest and most powerful of all patrons, the Vatican.
</p><p>When Michelangelo took up the Sistine Chapel commission, he was working within a space that had already been extensively decorated by other artists. Around the walls the <i>Life of Christ</i> and <i>Life of Moses</i> were depicted by a range of artists including his teacher <a href="/wiki/Domenico_Ghirlandaio" title="Domenico Ghirlandaio">Ghirlandaio</a>, <a href="/wiki/Raphael" title="Raphael">Raphael</a>'s teacher <a href="/wiki/Perugino" class="mw-redirect" title="Perugino">Perugino</a> and <a href="/wiki/Botticelli" class="mw-redirect" title="Botticelli">Botticelli</a>. They were works of large scale and exactly the sort of lavish treatment to be expected in a Vatican commission, vying with each other in the complexity of design, number of figures, elaboration of detail and skilful use of gold leaf. Above these works stood a row of painted Popes in brilliant brocades and gold tiaras. None of these splendours have any place in the work which Michelangelo created. Michelangelo, when asked by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Julius_II" title="Pope Julius II">Pope Julius II</a> to ornament the robes of the Apostles in the usual way, responded that they were very poor men.<sup id="cite_ref-Hartt_13-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hartt-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Within the cells of San'Marco, Fra Angelico had demonstrated that painterly skill and the artist's personal interpretation were sufficient to create memorable works of art, without the expensive trappings of blue and gold. In the use of the unadorned fresco technique, the clear bright pastel colours, the careful arrangement of a few significant figures and the skillful use of expression, motion and gesture, Michelangelo showed himself to be the artistic descendant of Fra Angelico. Frederick Hartt describes Fra Angelico as "prophetic of the mysticism" of painters such as <a href="/wiki/Rembrandt" title="Rembrandt">Rembrandt</a>, <a href="/wiki/El_Greco" title="El Greco">El Greco</a> and <a href="/wiki/Zurbar%C3%A1n" class="mw-redirect" title="Zurbarán">Zurbarán</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Hartt_13-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hartt-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h3><span id="San_Marco.2C_Florence.2C_1436.E2.80.931445_2"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="San_Marco,_Florence,_1436–1445_2">San Marco, Florence, 1436–1445</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=16" title="Edit section: San Marco, Florence, 1436–1445">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<ul><li>Altarpiece for chancel – <i>Virgin with <a href="/wiki/Saints_Cosmas_and_Damian" title="Saints Cosmas and Damian">Saints Cosmas and Damian</a>, attended by Saints Dominic, Peter, Francis, Mark, John Evangelist and Stephen</i>. Cosmas and Damian were patrons of the Medici. The altarpiece was commissioned in 1438 by Cosimo de' Medici. It was removed and disassembled during the renovation of the convent church in the seventeenth century. Two of the nine <a href="/wiki/Predella" title="Predella">predella</a> panels remain at the convent; seven are in Washington, Munich, Dublin and Paris. Unexpectedly, in 2006 the last two missing panels, Dominican saints from the side panels, turned up in the estate of a modest collector in Oxfordshire, who had bought them in California in the 1960s.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>

<p>Each cell is decorated with a fresco which matches in size and shape of the single round-headed window beside it. The frescoes are apparently for contemplative purposes. They have a pale, serene, unearthly beauty. Many of Fra Angelico's finest and most reproduced works are among them. There are, particularly in the inner row of cells, some of the less inspiring quality and of the more repetitive subject, perhaps completed by assistants.<sup id="cite_ref-Hartt_13-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hartt-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Many pictures include Dominican saints as witnesses of the scene each in one of the nine traditional prayer postures depicted in <a href="/wiki/De_Modo_Orandi" title="De Modo Orandi">De Modo Orandi</a>. The friar using the cell could place himself in the scene.

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Discovery_of_lost_works">Discovery of lost works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fra_Angelico&amp;action=edit&amp;section=18" title="Edit section: Discovery of lost works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Worldwide press coverage reported in November 2006 that two missing masterpieces by Fra Angelico had turned up, having hung in the spare room of the late Jean Preston, in her terrace house in <a href="/wiki/Oxford" title="Oxford">Oxford</a>, England. Her father had bought them for £100 each in the 1960s then bequeathed them to her when he died.<sup id="cite_ref-Morris_20April_2007_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Morris_20April_2007-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Preston, an expert medievalist, recognised them as being high-quality Florentine renaissance, but did not realize that they were works by Fra Angelico until they were identified in 2005 by Michael Liversidge of Bristol University.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>  There was almost no demand at all for medieval art during the 1960s and no dealers showed any interest, so Preston's father bought them almost as an afterthought along with some manuscripts. The paintings are two of eight side panels of a large altarpiece painted in 1439 for Fra Angelico's monastery at San Marco, which was later split up by <a href="/wiki/Napoleon" title="Napoleon">Napoleon</a>'s army. While the centre section is still at the monastery, the other six small panels are in German and US museums. These two panels were presumed lost forever. The Italian Government had hoped to purchase them but they were outbid at auction on 20 April 2007 by a private collector for £1.7M.<sup id="cite_ref-Morris_20April_2007_24-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Morris_20April_2007-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Both panels are now restored and exhibited in the San Marco Museum in Florence.
</p>
