<p><b>Eadberht</b> (died 19 or 20 August 768) was <a href="/wiki/King_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="King of Northumbria">king of Northumbria</a> from 737 or 738 to 758. He was the brother of <a href="/wiki/Ecgbert,_Archbishop_of_York" class="mw-redirect" title="Ecgbert, Archbishop of York">Ecgbert, Archbishop of York</a>. His reign is seen as a return to the imperial ambitions of seventh-century <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbria</a> and may represent a period of economic prosperity. He faced internal opposition from rival dynasties and at least two actual or potential rivals were killed during his reign. In 758 he abdicated in favour of his son <a href="/wiki/Oswulf_of_Northumbria" title="Oswulf of Northumbria">Oswulf</a> and became a monk at <a href="/wiki/York" title="York">York</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Origins">Origins</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eadberht_of_Northumbria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Origins">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Eadberht became ruler of Northumbria following the second abdication of his cousin <a href="/wiki/Ceolwulf_of_Northumbria" title="Ceolwulf of Northumbria">Ceolwulf</a>, who entered the monastery at <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a>. Unlike Ceolwulf's first abdication, which clearly involved force, his second, in favour of Eadberht, may have been voluntary.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Silver_sceatta_of_Eadberht_(YORYM_2000_2283)_obverse.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Silver_sceatta_of_Eadberht_%28YORYM_2000_2283%29_obverse.jpg/220px-Silver_sceatta_of_Eadberht_%28YORYM_2000_2283%29_obverse.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="227" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Silver_sceatta_of_Eadberht_%28YORYM_2000_2283%29_obverse.jpg/330px-Silver_sceatta_of_Eadberht_%28YORYM_2000_2283%29_obverse.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Silver_sceatta_of_Eadberht_%28YORYM_2000_2283%29_obverse.jpg/440px-Silver_sceatta_of_Eadberht_%28YORYM_2000_2283%29_obverse.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2204" data-file-height="2273" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Silver_sceatta_of_Eadberht_(YORYM_2000_2283)_obverse.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Silver sceatta of Eadberht</div></div></div>
<p>Eadberht son of Eata was a descendant of <a href="/wiki/Ida_of_Bernicia" title="Ida of Bernicia">Ida of Bernicia</a> through either his son Ocga (<a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a> and <a href="/wiki/Anglian_collection" title="Anglian collection">Anglian collection</a>) or Eadric (<i><a href="/wiki/Historia_Brittonum" title="Historia Brittonum">Historia Brittonum</a></i>).  The genealogy gives Eadberht's father Eata the <a href="/wiki/Cognomen" title="Cognomen">cognomen</a> <i>Glin Mawr</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Northumbria">Northumbria</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eadberht_of_Northumbria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Northumbria">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Eadberht appears to have faced opposition from rival families throughout his reign. Eardwine, probably the son of King <a href="/wiki/Eadwulf_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Eadwulf of Northumbria">Eadwulf</a>, and grandfather of the future king <a href="/wiki/Eardwulf_of_Northumbria" title="Eardwulf of Northumbria">Eardwulf</a>, was killed in 740.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In 750 Offa, son of King <a href="/wiki/Aldfrith_of_Northumbria" title="Aldfrith of Northumbria">Aldfrith</a> was taken from the sanctuary of Lindisfarne and put to death after a siege, while Bishop <a href="/wiki/Cynewulf_of_Lindisfarne" title="Cynewulf of Lindisfarne">Cynewulf of Lindisfarne</a>, who had presumably supported Offa, was deposed and detained in <a href="/wiki/York" title="York">York</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> The importance of religious foundations in Northumbrian political struggles and family feuds is apparent. Eardwine's family is associated with <a href="/wiki/Ripon" title="Ripon">Ripon</a>, Offa and Ceolwulf with Lindisfarne, and <a href="/wiki/Hexham" title="Hexham">Hexham</a> appears to have supported kings and noblemen opposed by the Lindisfarne community.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Eadberht, however, as brother of the Archbishop of York, enjoyed the support of the greatest Northumbrian prelate.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Eadberht's reign saw major reforms to the Northumbrian coinage, and some coins name King Eadberht and Archbishop Ecgberht. Kirby concludes that "the indications are that Eadberht was bringing new prosperity to his kingdom."<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby,_p._150_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby,_p._150-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> A letter sent by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_I" title="Pope Paul I">Pope Paul I</a> to Eadberht and Ecgberht, ordering them to return lands taken from Abbot Fothred, and given to his brother Moll, presumed to be the same person as the later king <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelwald_Moll" class="mw-redirect" title="Æthelwald Moll">Æthelwald Moll</a>, suggests that Eadberht's reign saw attempts at reclaiming some of the vast lands which had been granted to the church in earlier reigns.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Neighbours">Neighbours</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eadberht_of_Northumbria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Neighbours">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Kirby suggests that "a revival of seventh-century northern imperial ambitions had evidently occurred among the Northumbrians at the court of Eadberht".<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby,_p._150_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby,_p._150-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The first record of Eadberht's efforts to recreate this dominion appear in 740, the year of Earnwine's death. A war between the Picts and the Northumbrians is reported, during which <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelbald_of_Mercia" title="Æthelbald of Mercia">Æthelbald</a>, King of <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercia</a>, took advantage of the absence of Eadberht to ravage his lands. The reason for the war is unclear, but Woolf suggests that it was related to the killing of Earnwine. Earnwine's father had been an exile in the north after his defeat in the civil war of 705–706, and it may be that the Pictish king <a href="/wiki/%C3%93engus_I_of_the_Picts" class="mw-redirect" title="Óengus I of the Picts">Óengus</a>, or Æthelbald, or both, had tried to place him on the Northumbrian throne.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 750, Eadberht conquered the plain of <a href="/wiki/Kyle,_Ayrshire" title="Kyle, Ayrshire">Kyle</a> and in 756, he campaigned alongside King Óengus against the <a href="/wiki/Britons_(historical)" class="mw-redirect" title="Britons (historical)">Britons</a> of <a href="/wiki/Alt_Clut" class="mw-redirect" title="Alt Clut">Alt Clut</a>. The campaign is reported as follows: 
</p>
<dl><dd>In the year of the Lord's incarnation 756, king Eadberht in the eighteenth year of his reign, and Unust, king of Picts led armies to the town of <a href="/wiki/Dumbarton" title="Dumbarton">Dumbarton</a>. And hence the Britons accepted terms there, on the first day of the month of August. But on the tenth day of the same month perished almost the whole army which he led from Ouania to Niwanbirig.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup></dd></dl>
<p>That Ouania is <a href="/wiki/Govan" title="Govan">Govan</a> is now reasonably certain,<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> but the location of Newanbirig is less so. Although there are many Newburghs, it is Newburgh-on-Tyne near <a href="/wiki/Hexham" title="Hexham">Hexham</a> that has been the preferred location.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby,_p._150_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby,_p._150-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> An alternative interpretation of the events of 756 has been advanced: it identifies Newanbirig with <a href="/wiki/Newborough,_Staffordshire" title="Newborough, Staffordshire">Newborough</a> by <a href="/wiki/Lichfield" title="Lichfield">Lichfield</a> in the kingdom of Mercia. A defeat here for Eadberht and Óengus by Æthelbald's Mercians would correspond with the claim in the Saint Andrews foundation legends that a king named Óengus son of Fergus founded the church there as a thanksgiving to <a href="/wiki/Saint_Andrew" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Andrew">Saint Andrew</a> for saving him after a defeat in Mercia.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Abdication">Abdication</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eadberht_of_Northumbria&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Abdication">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Eadberht abdicated in 758, entering the monastery attached to the cathedral of York. His death there in 768 is recorded in <a href="/wiki/Symeon_of_Durham" title="Symeon of Durham">Symeon of Durham</a>'s chronicle.<sup id="cite_ref-Kirby,_p._150_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kirby,_p._150-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Symeon's <i>History of the Church of Durham</i> records that Eadberht was buried in the porch of the cathedral, alongside his brother Ecgberht, who had died in 766.
</p><p>His son <a href="/wiki/Oswulf_of_Northumbria" title="Oswulf of Northumbria">Oswulf</a> succeeded him, but was murdered within the year.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> However, his daughter Osgifu's husband <a href="/wiki/Alhred_of_Northumbria" title="Alhred of Northumbria">Alhred</a> became king, and Eadberht's descendants, such as Oswulf's son <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lfwald_I_of_Northumbria" title="Ælfwald I of Northumbria">Ælfwald</a> and Osgifu's son <a href="/wiki/Osred_II_of_Northumbria" title="Osred II of Northumbria">Osred</a> contested for the Northumbrian throne until the end of the century. Eadberht's last known descendant is Osgifu's son Saint <a href="/wiki/Alcmund_of_Derby" class="mw-redirect" title="Alcmund of Derby">Alhmund</a>, murdered in 800 on the orders of King <a href="/wiki/Eardwulf_of_Northumbria" title="Eardwulf of Northumbria">Eardwulf</a>, and reputed a <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
