<p><b>Catherine of Bologna</b> [Caterina de' Vigri] (8 September 1413 – 9 March 1463)<sup id="cite_ref-Dunbar_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dunbar-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> was an Italian <a href="/wiki/Poor_Clares" title="Poor Clares">Poor Clare</a>, writer, teacher, mystic, artist, and saint. The <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of artists and against temptations, Catherine de' Vigri was venerated for nearly three centuries in her native <a href="/wiki/Bologna" title="Bologna">Bologna</a> before being formally <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> in 1712 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_XI" title="Pope Clement XI">Pope Clement XI</a>. Her <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is 9 March.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Catherine_of_Bologna&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Catherine came from an upper-class family, the daughter of Benvenuta Mammolini of Bologna and Giovanni Vigri, a Ferrarese notary who worked for <a href="/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_III_d%27Este,_Marquis_of_Ferrara" title="Niccolò III d&#39;Este, Marquis of Ferrara">Niccolò III d'Este, Marquis of Ferrara</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Dunbar_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dunbar-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> She was raised at Niccolo III's court as a lady-in-waiting to his wife <a href="/wiki/Parisina_Malatesta" title="Parisina Malatesta">Parisina Malatesta</a> (d. 1425) and became lifelong friends with his natural daughter Margherita d'Este (d. 1478).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArthur200593–122_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArthur200593–122-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> During this time, she received some education in reading, writing, music, playing the viola, and had access to illuminated manuscripts in the d'Este Court library.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArthur201871–76_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArthur201871–76-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1426, after Niccolo III's execution of Parisina d'Este for infidelity, Catherine left court and joined a lay community of <a href="/wiki/Beguines_and_Beghards" title="Beguines and Beghards">beguines</a> living a semi-religious life and following the Augustinian rule. The women were divided over whether instead to adhere to the Franciscan rule, which eventually happened.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> In 1431 the beguine house was converted into the Observant Poor Clare convent of Corpus Domini, which grew from 12 women in 1431 to 144 women by the end of the century.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Catherine lived at Corpus Domini, Ferrara most of her life from 1431 to 1456, serving as Mistress of Novices. She was a model of piety and reported experiencing miracles and several visions of Christ, the Virgin Mary, Thomas Becket, and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Joseph" title="Saint Joseph">Joseph</a>, as well as future events, such as the fall of Constantinople in 1453. She wrote a number of religious treatises, lauds, sermons, and copied and illustrated her own breviary (see below).
</p><p>In 1455 the Franciscans and the governors of Bologna requested that she become <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> of a new convent, which was to be established under the name of Corpus Domini in Bologna. She left Ferrara in July 1456 with 12 sisters to start the new community and remained abbess there until her death on 9 March 1463. Catherine was buried in the convent graveyard, but after eighteen days, a sweet smell emanated from the grave and the <a href="/wiki/Incorrupt" class="mw-redirect" title="Incorrupt">incorrupt</a> body was <a href="/wiki/Exhumation" class="mw-redirect" title="Exhumation">exhumed</a>. It was eventually relocated to a chapel where it remains on display, dressed in her religious habit, seated upright behind glass. A contemporary Poor Clare, Sister Illuminata Bembo, wrote her biography in 1469.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBembo2001_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBembo2001-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> A strong local Bolognese cult of Caterina Vigri developed and she became a Beata in the 1520s but was not canonized until 1712.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Literary_works">Literary works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Catherine_of_Bologna&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Literary works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Caterina_-_Sette_armi_spirituali,_circa_1475_-_2367343.tif" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/Caterina_-_Sette_armi_spirituali%2C_circa_1475_-_2367343.tif/lossy-page1-220px-Caterina_-_Sette_armi_spirituali%2C_circa_1475_-_2367343.tif.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="289" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/Caterina_-_Sette_armi_spirituali%2C_circa_1475_-_2367343.tif/lossy-page1-330px-Caterina_-_Sette_armi_spirituali%2C_circa_1475_-_2367343.tif.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/Caterina_-_Sette_armi_spirituali%2C_circa_1475_-_2367343.tif/lossy-page1-440px-Caterina_-_Sette_armi_spirituali%2C_circa_1475_-_2367343.tif.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2031" data-file-height="2670" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Caterina_-_Sette_armi_spirituali,_circa_1475_-_2367343.tif" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Sette armi spirituali</i>, 1475</div></div></div>
<p>Catherine's best-known text is <i>Seven Spiritual Weapons Necessary for Spiritual Warfare</i><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> which she appears to have first written in 1438 and then rewritten and augmented between 1450 and 1456. Although she probably taught similar ideas, she kept the written version hidden until she neared death and then handed it to her confessor with instructions to send a copy to the Poor Clares at Ferrara. Part of this book describes at length her visions both of God and of <a href="/wiki/Satan" title="Satan">Satan</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The treatise was circulated in manuscript form through a network of Poor Clare convents. The <i>Sette Armi Spirituali</i> became an important part of the campaign for her canonization. It was first printed in 1475, and went through 21 later editions in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, including being translated into Latin, French, Portuguese, English, Spanish, and German. It, therefore, played an important role in the dissemination of late medieval vernacular mysticism in the early modern period. In addition, she wrote lauds, short religious treatises, and letters,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEServenti2000_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEServenti2000-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> as well as a 5000-line Latin poem called the <i>Rosarium Metricum</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> the <i>I Dodici Giardini</i> and <i>I Sermoni</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> These were discovered around 2000 and described by Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Giacomo_Biffi" title="Giacomo Biffi">Giacomo Biffi</a>: as "now revealed in their surprising beauty. We can ascertain that she was not undeserving of her renown as a highly cultivated person. We are now in a position to meditate on a veritable monument of theology which, after the <i>Treatise on the Seven Spiritual Weapons</i>, is made up of distinct and autonomous parts: <i>The Twelve Gardens</i>, a mystical work of her youth, <i>Rosarium</i>, a Latin poem on the life of Jesus, and <i>The Sermons</i>, copies of Catherine's words to her religious sisters."  Saint Catherine of Bologna had good education in drawing, writing, reading and language.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Artistic_works">Artistic works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Catherine_of_Bologna&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Artistic works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:172px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Catherinebolognaart.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Catherinebolognaart.jpg/170px-Catherinebolognaart.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="258" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Catherinebolognaart.jpg/255px-Catherinebolognaart.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/27/Catherinebolognaart.jpg 2x" data-file-width="300" data-file-height="455" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Catherinebolognaart.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Madonna_(art)" title="Madonna (art)">Madonna</a> of the Peach, by St. Catherine of Bologna</div></div></div>
<p>Catherine represents the rare phenomenon of a 15th-century nun–an artist whose artworks are preserved in her personal breviary.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFortunatiLeonardi2004_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFortunatiLeonardi2004-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> She meditated while she copied the scriptural text, adding about 1000 prayer rubrics, and drew initials with bust-portraits of saints, paying special attention to images of Clare and Francis.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArthur2004177–192_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArthur2004177–192-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Besides multiple images of Christ and the infant swaddled Christ Child, she depicted other saints, including Thomas Becket, Jerome, Paul, Anthony of Padua, Mary Magdalene, and Catherine of Alexandria. Her self-taught style incorporated motifs from needlework and devotional prints. Some saints' images, interwoven with text and rubrics, display an idiosyncratic, inventive iconography also found in German nuns' artworks (nönnenarbeiten).<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> The breviary and its images surely served a didactic function within the convent community.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Other panel paintings and manuscripts attributed to her include the <i>Madonna and Child</i> (nicknamed the <i>Madonna del Pomo</i>) in the Cappella Della Santa, a possible portrait or self-portrait in the autograph copy of the <i>Sette Armi Spirituali</i>, a Redeemer, and another Madonna and Child in her chapel.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Recently one scholar has tried to question certain attributions.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A drawing of a Man of Sorrows or Resurrected Christ found in a miscellany of lauds (Ms. 35 no.4, Archivio Generale Arcivescovile, Bologna) has also been attributed to her. Catherine is significant as a woman artist who articulated an aesthetic philosophy. She explained that although it took precious time, the purpose of her religious art was "to increase devotion for herself and others".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEArthur201886–118_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEArthur201886–118-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
