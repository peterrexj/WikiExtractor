<p><b>Nikolaj Velimirović</b> (<a href="/wiki/Serbian_Cyrillic_alphabet" title="Serbian Cyrillic alphabet">Serbian Cyrillic</a>: Николај Велимировић; 4 January 1881 &#x5b;<a href="/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates" title="Old Style and New Style dates">O.S.</a> 23 December 1880&#x5d;&#160;– 18 March&#160;&#91;<a href="/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates" title="Old Style and New Style dates">O.S.</a> 5 March&#93;&#160;1956) was bishop of the eparchies of <a href="/wiki/Ohrid" title="Ohrid">Ohrid</a> and <a href="/wiki/Eparchy_of_%C5%BDi%C4%8Da" title="Eparchy of Žiča">Žiča</a> (1920–1956) in the <a href="/wiki/Serbian_Orthodox_Church" title="Serbian Orthodox Church">Serbian Orthodox Church</a>. An influential theological writer and a highly gifted orator, he was often referred to as the new <a href="/wiki/John_Chrysostom" title="John Chrysostom">John Chrysostom</a><sup id="cite_ref-orthodoxinfo_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-orthodoxinfo-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and historian Slobodan G. Markovich calls him "one of the most influential bishops of the Serbian Orthodox Church in the twentieth century".<sup id="cite_ref-Markovich_2017_143_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Markovich_2017_143-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As a young man, he came close to dying of <a href="/wiki/Dysentery" title="Dysentery">dysentery</a> and decided that he would dedicate his life to God if he survived. He lived and was <a href="/wiki/Tonsure" title="Tonsure">tonsured</a> as a monk under the name <i>Nikolaj</i> in 1909. He was ordained into the clergy, and quickly became an important leader and spokesperson for the Serbian Orthodox Church, especially in its relations with the West. When <a href="/wiki/Nazi_Germany" title="Nazi Germany">Nazi Germany</a> <a href="/wiki/Invasion_of_Yugoslavia" title="Invasion of Yugoslavia">occupied</a> <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia" title="Kingdom of Yugoslavia">Yugoslavia</a> in <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">World War II</a>, Velimirović was imprisoned and eventually taken to <a href="/wiki/Dachau_concentration_camp" title="Dachau concentration camp">Dachau concentration camp</a>.
</p><p>After being liberated by the <a href="/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II" title="Allies of World War II">Allies</a> at the end of the war, he chose not to return to Yugoslavia (which became a <a href="/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia" title="Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia">Socialist republic</a> after the war). He moved to the <a href="/wiki/United_States" title="United States">United States</a> in 1946, where he remained until his death in 1956. He strongly supported the unity of all <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox churches</a> and established particularly good relationships with the <a href="/wiki/Anglican" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican">Anglican</a> and <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 24 May 2003, he was canonized as a saint by the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Synod_of_the_Serbian_Orthodox_Church" title="Holy Synod of the Serbian Orthodox Church">Holy Synod of the Serbian Orthodox Church</a> as Saint Nikolaj of Ohrid and Žiča (Свети Николај Охридски и Жички) though he is often<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> referred to as Saint Nikolaj of Serbia (Свети Николај Српски).
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Childhood">Childhood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Childhood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>He was born as Nikola Velimirović in the small village of <a href="/wiki/Leli%C4%87" title="Lelić">Lelić</a>, <a href="/wiki/Valjevo" title="Valjevo">Valjevo</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Principality_of_Serbia" title="Principality of Serbia">Principality of Serbia</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> on the day of the feast of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Naum" title="Saint Naum">Saint Naum</a> of Ohrid, whose monastery would later be his episcopal see. He was the first of nine children born to Dragomir and Katarina Velimirović (née Filipović), pious farmers. Being very weak, he was baptised soon after his birth in the <a href="/wiki/%C4%86elije_monastery" class="mw-redirect" title="Ćelije monastery">Ćelije monastery</a>, where his relics are now resting. He was given the name Nikola because <a href="/wiki/Saint_Nicholas" title="Saint Nicholas">Saint Nicholas</a> was the family's patron saint. The first lessons about God, Jesus Christ, the lives of the saints and the holy days of the Church year were provided to him by his mother, who also regularly took him to the Ćelije monastery for prayer and <a href="/wiki/Holy_Communion" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Communion">Holy Communion</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-cohp_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cohp-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Education">Education</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Education">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_as_a_student.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ce/St._Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_as_a_student.jpg/200px-St._Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_as_a_student.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="200" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ce/St._Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_as_a_student.jpg/300px-St._Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_as_a_student.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ce/St._Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_as_a_student.jpg/400px-St._Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_as_a_student.jpg 2x" data-file-width="719" data-file-height="720" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_as_a_student.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Velimirović as a student</div></div></div>
<p>His formal education also began in the Ćelije monastery and continued in Valjevo. He applied for admission into the Military Academy, but was refused because he didn't pass the physical exam. He was admitted to the Seminary of Saint Sava in <a href="/wiki/Belgrade" title="Belgrade">Belgrade</a>, where, apart from the standard subjects, he explored a significant number of writings of both Eastern and Western authors, such as Shakespeare, Voltaire, Nietzsche, Marx, Pushkin, Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, and others.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> He graduated in 1905.<sup id="cite_ref-cohp_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cohp-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Nikola had been chosen to become a professor in the Seminary of Saint Sava, but it was decided that he needed to pursue further Eastern Orthodox studies before becoming a teacher. As an outstanding student, he was chosen to continue his studies in Russia and Western Europe. He had a gift for languages and soon possessed a good knowledge of Russian, French and German. He attended the Theological Academy in St. Petersburg and then he went to <a href="/wiki/Switzerland" title="Switzerland">Switzerland</a> and obtained his doctorate of divinity from the Old Catholic Theological Faculty at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Berne" class="mw-redirect" title="University of Berne">University of Berne</a> with <a href="/wiki/Magna_cum_laude" class="mw-redirect" title="Magna cum laude">magna cum laude</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-cohp_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cohp-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Markovich_2017_143_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Markovich_2017_143-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He received his doctorate in Theology in 1908, with the dissertation entitled <i>Faith in the Resurrection of Christ as the Foundation of the Dogmas of the Apostolic Church</i>. This original work was written in German and published in Switzerland in 1910, and later translated into Serbian. The dissertation for his <a href="/wiki/Doctorate" title="Doctorate">doctor's degree</a> in philosophy was prepared at Oxford and defended in Geneva, in French. The title was <i>Berkeley's Philosophy</i>.
</p><p>His stay in Britain left an impact on his views and education, which is seen from the fact that he quotes or mentions <a href="/wiki/Charles_Dickens" title="Charles Dickens">Charles Dickens</a>, <a href="/wiki/Lord_Byron" title="Lord Byron">Lord Byron</a>, <a href="/wiki/John_Milton" title="John Milton">John Milton</a>, <a href="/wiki/Charles_Darwin" title="Charles Darwin">Charles Darwin</a>, <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle" title="Thomas Carlyle">Thomas Carlyle</a>, <a href="/wiki/Shakespeare" class="mw-redirect" title="Shakespeare">Shakespeare</a> and <a href="/wiki/George_Berkeley" title="George Berkeley">George Berkeley</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Markovich_2017_143_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Markovich_2017_143-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastic_life">Monastic life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Monastic life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In the autumn of 1909, Nikola returned home and became seriously ill with <a href="/wiki/Dysentery" title="Dysentery">dysentery</a>. He decided that if he recovered he would become a monk and devote his life to God. At the end of 1909 his health got better and he was tonsured a monk, receiving the name <b>Nikolaj</b>.<sup id="cite_ref-OCA_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-OCA-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  He was soon ordained a <a href="/wiki/Hieromonk" title="Hieromonk">hieromonk</a> and then elevated to the rank of <a href="/wiki/Archimandrite" title="Archimandrite">Archimandrite</a>. In 1910 he was entrusted with a mission to Great Britain in order to gain the co-operation of the Church of England in educating the young students who had been evacuated when the Austrian, German and Bulgarian forces threatened to overwhelm the country.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Missions_during_World_War_I">Missions during World War I</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Missions during World War I">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>

<p>In his lifetime, Father Nikolaj visited the <a href="/wiki/United_States" title="United States">USA</a> four times. He visited Britain in 1910. He studied English and was capable of addressing an audience and making a strong impression on the listeners. Shortly after the outbreak of World War I this contributed to his appointment by the Serbian government to a mission in the United States. In 1915, as an unknown Serbian monk, he toured most of the major U.S. cities, where he held numerous lectures, fighting for the union of the Serbs and South Slavic peoples. This mission gained ground: America sent over 20,000 volunteers to Europe, most of whom later fought on the <a href="/wiki/Salonika_front" class="mw-redirect" title="Salonika front">Salonika front</a>. During Velimirović's US-campaign occurred the great retreat of the Serbian Army through the mountains of <a href="/wiki/Albania" title="Albania">Albania</a>. He embarked home in 1916; as his country was now in enemy hands, he went to Britain instead. Not only did he fulfill his mission, but he was also awarded a <a href="/wiki/Doctor_of_Divinity" title="Doctor of Divinity">Doctorate of Divinity</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Honoris_causa" class="mw-redirect" title="Honoris causa">honoris causa</a></i> from the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Cambridge" title="University of Cambridge">University of Cambridge</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-bank_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bank-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He gave a series of notable lectures at <a href="/wiki/St._Margaret%27s,_Westminster" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Margaret&#39;s, Westminster">St. Margaret's, Westminster</a>, and preached in <a href="/wiki/St._Paul%27s_Cathedral" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Paul&#39;s Cathedral">St. Paul's Cathedral</a>, making him the first Eastern Orthodox Christian to preach at St Paul's.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Professor at the Faculty of Orthodox Theology Bogdan Lubardić has identified three phases in the development of Velimirovich's ideas: the pre-Ohrid phase (1902–1919), the Ohrid phase (1920–1936), and the post-Ohrid phase (1936–1956).<sup id="cite_ref-Markovich_2017_143_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Markovich_2017_143-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop">Bishop</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Bishop">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:237px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Vladika_Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_crop.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/Vladika_Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_crop.jpg/235px-Vladika_Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_crop.jpg" decoding="async" width="235" height="160" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/Vladika_Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_crop.jpg/353px-Vladika_Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_crop.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/Vladika_Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_crop.jpg/470px-Vladika_Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_crop.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4170" data-file-height="2844" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Vladika_Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87_crop.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Bishop Nikolaj Velimirović in <a href="/wiki/Ohrid" title="Ohrid">Ohrid</a> with the company of Princess Ljubica in 1928</div></div></div>
<p>In 1919, <a href="/wiki/Archimandrite" title="Archimandrite">Archimandrite</a> Nikolaj was consecrated <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_%C5%BDi%C4%8Da" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Žiča">Bishop of Žiča</a><sup id="cite_ref-bank_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bank-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> but did not remain long in that diocese, being asked to take over the office of Bishop in the Eparchy of Ohrid (1920-1931) and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Eparchy_of_Ohrid_and_Bitola&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Eparchy of Ohrid and Bitola (page does not exist)">Eparchy of Ohrid and Bitola</a> (1931-1936) in southern parts of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia" title="Kingdom of Yugoslavia">Kingdom of Yugoslavia</a>. In 1920, for the third time, he journeyed again to the United States, this time on a mission to organize the Serbian Orthodox Diocese of North America.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> He made another trip to the United States in 1927.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_1930s">The 1930s</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: The 1930s">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>German Chancellor <a href="/wiki/Adolf_Hitler" title="Adolf Hitler">Adolf Hitler</a> awarded him with a civil medal in 1935 for his contribution in 1926 in renovation of the WWI German military cemetery in <a href="/wiki/Bitola" title="Bitola">Bitola</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200532_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200532-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> In 1936, he finally resumed his original office of Bishop in the <a href="/wiki/Eparchy_of_%C5%BDi%C4%8Da" title="Eparchy of Žiča">Eparchy of Žiča</a>, returning to the <a href="/wiki/Monastery_of_%C5%BDi%C4%8Da" class="mw-redirect" title="Monastery of Žiča">Monastery of Žiča</a> not far distant from <a href="/wiki/Valjevo" title="Valjevo">Valjevo</a> and Lelić, where he was born. According to Jovan Byford, Velimirović's philosophy in 1939 got racist overtone typical of the time, as he considered the Serbs to be of <i>"Aryan race"</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200530,171_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200530,171-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Detention_and_imprisonment_in_World_War_II">Detention and imprisonment in World War II</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Detention and imprisonment in World War II">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1097763485"/><table class="box-More_citations_needed_section plainlinks metadata ambox ambox-content ambox-Refimprove" role="presentation"><tbody><tr><td class="mbox-image"><div class="mbox-image-div"><a href="/wiki/File:Question_book-new.svg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/50px-Question_book-new.svg.png" decoding="async" width="50" height="39" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/75px-Question_book-new.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/100px-Question_book-new.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="512" data-file-height="399" /></a></div></td><td class="mbox-text"><div class="mbox-text-span">This section <b>needs additional citations for <a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability" title="Wikipedia:Verifiability">verification</a></b>.<span class="hide-when-compact"> Please help <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit">improve this article</a> by <a href="/wiki/Help:Referencing_for_beginners" title="Help:Referencing for beginners">adding citations to reliable sources</a>. Unsourced material may be challenged and removed.</span>  <span class="date-container"><i>(<span class="date">May 2016</span>)</i></span><span class="hide-when-compact"><i> (<small><a href="/wiki/Help:Maintenance_template_removal" title="Help:Maintenance template removal">Learn how and when to remove this template message</a></small>)</i></span></div></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>During <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">World War II</a>, in 1941, as soon as the German forces occupied Yugoslavia, Bishop Nikolaj was arrested by the <a href="/wiki/Nazis" class="mw-redirect" title="Nazis">Nazis</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">Monastery</a> of <a href="/wiki/%C5%BDi%C4%8Da" title="Žiča">Žiča</a>, after which he was confined in the Monastery of <a href="/wiki/Ljubostinja" title="Ljubostinja">Ljubostinja</a>. Later he was transferred to the Monastery of <a href="/wiki/Vojlovica_Monastery" title="Vojlovica Monastery">Vojlovica</a> (near <a href="/wiki/Pan%C4%8Devo" title="Pančevo">Pančevo</a>) in which he was confined together with the <a href="/wiki/Serbian_Patriarch_Gavrilo_V" class="mw-redirect" title="Serbian Patriarch Gavrilo V">Serbian Patriarch Gavrilo V</a> until the end of 1944. On 15 September 1944, both Patriarch Gavrilo V (Dožić) and Bishop Nikolaj were sent to <a href="/wiki/Dachau_concentration_camp" title="Dachau concentration camp">Dachau concentration camp</a>, which was at that time the main <a href="/wiki/Priest_Barracks_of_Dachau_Concentration_Camp" title="Priest Barracks of Dachau Concentration Camp">concentration camp for clerics</a> arrested by the Nazis. Both Velimirović and Dožić were held as <i>special prisoners</i> (<i>Ehrenhäftlinge</i>) imprisoned in the so-called <i>Ehrenbunker</i> (or <i>Prominentenbunker</i>) separated from the work camp area, together with high-ranking <a href="/wiki/Nazi" class="mw-redirect" title="Nazi">Nazi</a> enemy officers and other prominent prisoners whose arrests had been dictated by Hitler directly.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In August 1943 German general <a href="/wiki/Hermann_Neubacher" title="Hermann Neubacher">Hermann Neubacher</a> became special emissary of the German Foreign Office for Southeastern Europe. From 11 September 1943, he was also made responsible for Albania. In December 1944 as part of a settlement of Neubacher with <a href="/wiki/Milan_Nedi%C4%87" title="Milan Nedić">Milan Nedić</a> and <a href="/wiki/Dimitrije_Ljoti%C4%87" title="Dimitrije Ljotić">Dimitrije Ljotić</a> Germans were release Velimirović and Dožić who were transferred from Dachau to <a href="/wiki/Slovenia" title="Slovenia">Slovenia</a>, as the <a href="/wiki/Nazis" class="mw-redirect" title="Nazis">Nazis</a> attempted to make use of Patriarch Gavrilo's and Nikolaj's authority among the Serbs in order to gain allies in the anti-Communist movements.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200535_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200535-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Contrary to claims of torture and abuse at the camp, Patriarch Dožić testified himself that both he and Velimirović were treated normally.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> During his stay in Slovenia, Velimirović blessed volunteers of Dimitrije Ljotić and other collaborators and war criminals such as <a href="/wiki/Dobroslav_Jev%C4%91evi%C4%87" title="Dobroslav Jevđević">Dobroslav Jevđević</a> and <a href="/wiki/Mom%C4%8Dilo_%C4%90uji%C4%87" title="Momčilo Đujić">Momčilo Đujić</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200535–36_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200535–36-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> In the final years of World War II in the book <i>"Reči srpskom narodu kroz tamnički prozor"</i> he says they the Jews condemned and killed Christ "suffocated by the stinking spirit of Satan", and further he writes  that "Jews proved to be worse opponents of God than the pagan Pilate", "Devil teaches them so, their father", "the Devil taught them how to rebel against the Son of God, Jesus Christ."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200530–31_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200530–31-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Later, Velimirović and Patriarch Gavrilo (Dožić) were moved to <a href="/wiki/Austria" title="Austria">Austria</a>, and were finally liberated by the US <a href="/wiki/36th_Infantry_Division_(United_States)" title="36th Infantry Division (United States)">36th Infantry Division</a> in <a href="/wiki/Tyrol_(state)" title="Tyrol (state)">Tyrol</a> in 1945.<sup id="cite_ref-cohp_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cohp-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Immigration_and_Last_Years">Immigration and Last Years</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Immigration and Last Years">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>After the war he never returned to the <a href="/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia" title="Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia">Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia</a>, but after spending some time in Europe, he finally immigrated as a <a href="/wiki/Refugee" title="Refugee">refugee</a> to the United States in 1946. There, in spite of his health problems, he continued his missionary work, for which he is considered <i>An Apostle and Missionary of the New Continent</i> (quote by Fr. <a href="/wiki/Alexander_Schmemann" title="Alexander Schmemann">Alexander Schmemann</a>), and has also been enlisted as <i>an American Saint</i><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> and included on the icons and frescoes <i>All American Saints</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His books were banned by the Yugoslav communist government in 1947.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He taught at several <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox</a> <a href="/wiki/Seminary" title="Seminary">seminaries</a> such as St. Sava's Seminary (<a href="/wiki/Libertyville,_Illinois" title="Libertyville, Illinois">Libertyville, Illinois</a>), <a href="/wiki/Saint_Tikhon%27s_Orthodox_Theological_Seminary" title="Saint Tikhon&#39;s Orthodox Theological Seminary">Saint Tikhon's Orthodox Theological Seminary</a> and Monastery (<a href="/wiki/South_Canaan,_Pennsylvania" class="mw-redirect" title="South Canaan, Pennsylvania">South Canaan, Pennsylvania</a>), and <a href="/wiki/St._Vladimir%27s_Orthodox_Theological_Seminary" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Vladimir&#39;s Orthodox Theological Seminary">St. Vladimir's Orthodox Theological Seminary</a> (now in <a href="/wiki/Crestwood,_New_York" class="mw-redirect" title="Crestwood, New York">Crestwood, New York</a>).
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Literary_criticism">Literary criticism</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Literary criticism">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Amfilohije_Radovi%C4%87" class="mw-redirect" title="Amfilohije Radović">Amfilohije Radović</a> points out that part of his success lies in his high education and ability to write well and his understanding of European culture.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Literary critic <a href="/wiki/Milan_Bogdanovi%C4%87" title="Milan Bogdanović">Milan Bogdanović</a> claims that everything Velimirović wrote after his <a href="/wiki/Ohrid" title="Ohrid">Ohrid</a> years did nothing more than paraphrase Eastern Orthodox canon and dogma. Bogdanović views him as a conservative who glorified the Church and its ceremonies as an institution.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> Others say he brought little novelty into Eastern Orthodox thought.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> This, however, is explained by true Orthodox thought, because, as Saint <a href="/wiki/John_of_Damascus" title="John of Damascus">John of Damascus</a> writes, "It is for that reason that I say (teach) nothing of what is mine. I briefly express the thoughts and words passed down by Godly and wise men."<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Posthumous">Posthumous</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Posthumous">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Ljubomir_Simonovi%C4%87_-_Vladika_Nikolaj.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d7/Ljubomir_Simonovi%C4%87_-_Vladika_Nikolaj.jpg/200px-Ljubomir_Simonovi%C4%87_-_Vladika_Nikolaj.jpg" decoding="async" width="200" height="272" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d7/Ljubomir_Simonovi%C4%87_-_Vladika_Nikolaj.jpg/300px-Ljubomir_Simonovi%C4%87_-_Vladika_Nikolaj.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d7/Ljubomir_Simonovi%C4%87_-_Vladika_Nikolaj.jpg/400px-Ljubomir_Simonovi%C4%87_-_Vladika_Nikolaj.jpg 2x" data-file-width="800" data-file-height="1088" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Ljubomir_Simonovi%C4%87_-_Vladika_Nikolaj.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Painting of bishop Nikolaj by Ljubomir Simonović</div></div></div>
<p>Velimirović died on 18 March 1956, while in prayer at the foot of his bed before the Liturgy, at the Russian Orthodox Monastery of St. Tikhon in <a href="/wiki/South_Canaan_Township,_Wayne_County,_Pennsylvania" title="South Canaan Township, Wayne County, Pennsylvania">South Canaan Township, Wayne County, Pennsylvania</a>. He was buried near the tomb of poet <a href="/wiki/Jovan_Du%C4%8Di%C4%87" title="Jovan Dučić">Jovan Dučić</a> at the Monastery of St. Sava at <a href="/wiki/Libertyville,_Illinois" title="Libertyville, Illinois">Libertyville, Illinois</a>. After the fall of communism, his remains were ultimately re-buried in his home town of <a href="/wiki/Leli%C4%87" title="Lelić">Lelić</a> on 12 May 1991, next to his parents and his nephew, Bishop Jovan Velimirović. On 19 May 2003, the Holy Assembly of Bishops of the <a href="/wiki/Serbian_Orthodox_Church" title="Serbian Orthodox Church">Serbian Orthodox Church</a> recognized Bishop Nikolaj (Velimirović) of Ohrid and Žiča as a saint and decided to include him into the calendar of saints of Holy Orthodox Church (5 and 18 March).<sup id="cite_ref-ROCOR_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ROCOR-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-crkveni_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-crkveni-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-OCA_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-OCA-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Views">Views</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Views">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Allegations_of_antisemitism">Allegations of antisemitism</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Allegations of antisemitism">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Several of Nikolaj Velimirović's writings and public speeches have been identified by historians as containing <a href="/wiki/Antisemitism" title="Antisemitism">antisemitic</a> rhetoric and/or <a href="/wiki/Hate_speech" title="Hate speech">hate speech</a> directed at <a href="/wiki/Jews" title="Jews">Jews</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Notably, the first written record of Velimirović's antisemitic beliefs comes over a decade before the start of <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">World War II</a> and his stay at the <a href="/wiki/Dachau_concentration_camp" title="Dachau concentration camp">Dachau concentration camp</a>. In fact, the first chronicled case of Velimirović expressing antisemitic beliefs dates back to a 1927 sermon delivered in the United States.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200843_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200843-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>From the 1927 sermon titled "A Story about the Wolf and the Lamb," Velimirović's proclamations are summarized by social psychologist Jovan Byford:
</p>
<blockquote><p>In his take on the well known Christian parable about the wolf and the lamb, Velimirović referred to "Jewish leaders in Jerusalem" at the time of the crucifixion as "wolves," whose thirst for blood of the Lamb of God was motivated by their "god-hating nationalism."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200843_32-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200843-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Byford's summary of Velimirović's "A Story about the Wolf and the Lamb" is significant for it serves as evidence that Velimirović may have utilized biblical undertones and Christian parable as a means of 'validating' his antisemitic statements to his followers. An interview that Jovan Byford conducted with Mladen Obradović (leader of Serbian far-right political organization <a href="/wiki/Obraz_(organization)" title="Obraz (organization)">Obraz</a>) may suggest that this explanation still persists among some members of <a href="/wiki/Serbian_Orthodox_Church" title="Serbian Orthodox Church">Serbian Orthodox</a> society. A contemporary defender of Velimirović's reputation and <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> status, Obradović defends the antisemitic writings of Velimimirović by stating that Velimirović's words only echo what had been written in early Christian texts:<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford2008175_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford2008175-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<blockquote><p>You have the very words of the Lord Jesus Christ when he says to the Pharisees that they are a "brood of vipers" or that their father is the Devil; Bishop Nikolaj merely quotes the Gospels.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford2008175_33-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford2008175-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>In a speech delivered in 1936 at the Žica Monastery in Serbia, Velimirović spoke out against what he perceived to be a Jewish threat to Christianity in front of a distinguished audience that included Yugoslavian Prime Minister <a href="/wiki/Milan_Stojadinovi%C4%87" title="Milan Stojadinović">Milan Stojadinović</a>. Velimirović used specific lines of this speech to accuse Jews of leading a secretive, coordinated effort against Christianity and "faith in real God".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200843–44_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200843–44-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Velimirović's writing in <i>Words to the Serbian People Through the Prison Window</i> is generally seen as the strongest evidence of the <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> Bishop holding antisemitic beliefs. Notably, many proponents of Velimirović's ideology suggest that the work is not definitive evidence of the Bishop's true ideology and beliefs about Jews and Judaism because they claim that it was written under duress during his time at <a href="/wiki/Dachau_concentration_camp" title="Dachau concentration camp">Dachau</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200877_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200877-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> The excerpts from Velimirović's <i>Words to the Serbian People Through the Prison Window</i> that attract the most attention from scholars studying antisemitism are quoted in Jovan Byford's <i>Denial and Repression of Antisemitism: Post-Communist Remembrance of the Serbian Bishop Nikolaj Velimirović</i> as follows:
</p>
<blockquote><p>The Devil teaches them [Jews]; the Devil taught them how to stand against the son of God, Jesus Christ. The Devil taught them through the centuries how to fight against the sons of Christ, against the children of light, against the followers of the Gospel and eternal life [Christians]. [...]<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200843–44_34-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200843–44-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<blockquote><p>Europe knows nothing other than what Jews serve up as knowledge. It believes nothing other than what Jews order it to believe. It knows the value of nothing until Jews impose their own measure of values […] all modern ideas including democracy, and strikes, and socialism, and atheism, and religious tolerance, and pacifism, and global revolution, and capitalism, and communism are the inventions of Jews, or rather their father, the Devil.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200845_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200845-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Additionally, Jovan Byford identifies the antisemitic ideology of Velimirović in the work, <i>Indian Letters</i> in which the figure of a Jewish woman portrays Satan.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200845_36-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200845-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Notably, this example of Velimirović's antisemitic portrayal is again linked to <a href="/wiki/Conspiracy_theory" title="Conspiracy theory">conspiracy</a> as Velimirović describes the woman as standing for, "all destructive and secret associations plotting against Christianity, religion, and the state."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200845_36-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200845-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Despite accusations of antisemitism, it is recorded that Velimirović protected a Jewish family by facilitating their escape from Nazi-occupied Serbia. Ela Trifunović (Ela Nayhaus), wrote to the Serbian Orthodox Church in 2001, claiming that she had spent 18 months hiding in the Ljubostinja monastery to which she was smuggled by Velimirović, guarded and later helped move on with false papers.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> Historians inclined to side with the view that Velimirović's writings prove that he held antisemitic beliefs note that this one incidence of the Bishop saving a Jewish family is commonly exaggerated by pro-Velimirović groups as evidence of his universal kindness and selflessness against the several confirmed antisemitic writings tied to Velimirović.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford2008162–163_38-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford2008162–163-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Views_on_Germans_and_Hitler">Views on Germans and Hitler</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: Views on Germans and Hitler">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Adolf Hitler decorated Nikolaj Velimirović in 1935 for his contributions to the restoration of a German military cemetery in Bitola in 1926.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200847_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200847-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> Some claim that the order was returned in protest at German aggression in 1941.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In a treatise on St. Sava in 1935, he supported Hitler's treatment of the German national church<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> and is quoted as saying:
</p>
<blockquote><p>However, a due respect is to be to the current German Leader, who being a simple craftsman and a man from the people, realized that nationalism without faith is an anomaly, a cold and insecure mechanism. And so, in the XX century, he came to the idea of Saint Sava, and as a layman undertook among his people that most important work, befitting a saint, a genius and a hero. And for us that work has been accomplished by Saint Sava, the first among the saints, the first among the geniuses and the first among the heroes in our history. He accomplished it perfectly, he accomplished it without fight and without blood, and he accomplished it not yesterday or the day before, but 700 years ago.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>In spite of accusations of collaboration leveled during Communist times, some of Velimirović's actions and writings were directed against the Germans who got suspicious of him when he supported the coup in April 1941.<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> They suspected him of collaborating with the Chetniks and formally arrested him and kept him first in Ljubostinja Monastery in 1941 and then in 1944 in Dachau concentration camp.
</p><p>In Dachau, he was imprisoned in <i>Ehrenbunker</i>, together with other clergy and high-ranking Nazi enemy officers, and was allowed to wear his own religious clothes, having access to the officer's canteen. It is claimed that he was never tortured and had access to officers' medical services. Contrary to the reports that Velimirović was liberated when the Americans' 36th Division reached Dachau, both he and Patriarch Dožić were actually released in November 1944, having spent three months in the camp. They travelled to Slovenia, from where Velimirovic continued first to Austria then to United States.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEByford200855_44-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEByford200855-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Views_on_Ljoti.C4.87"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Views_on_Ljotić">Views on Ljotić</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Nikolaj_Velimirovi%C4%87&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: Views on Ljotić">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Velimirović had a high opinion of <a href="/wiki/Dimitrije_Ljoti%C4%87" title="Dimitrije Ljotić">Dimitrije Ljotić</a>, a Serbian fascist politician and German collaborationist.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In an interview given in the United States in 1953, Velimirović claimed that he was the spiritual gray eminence behind the nationalist and collaborating extreme-right <i><a href="/wiki/ZBOR" class="mw-redirect" title="ZBOR">ZBOR</a></i> organization.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> The military arm of that organization (SDK - Srpski Dobrovoljački Korpus - <i><a href="/wiki/Serbian_Volunteer_Corps_(World_War_II)" title="Serbian Volunteer Corps (World War II)">Serbian Volunteer Corps</a></i>) was fighting against both <a href="/wiki/Yugoslav_Partisans" title="Yugoslav Partisans">Partisans</a> and <a href="/wiki/Chetniks" title="Chetniks">Chetniks</a> in World War II and was responsible for numerous civilian executions in Serbia of both Serbs and other nationals (Jews, Roma, etc.) When the leader of <i>ZBOR</i>, Dimitrije Ljotić, was arrested in 1940 by the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia" title="Kingdom of Yugoslavia">Yugoslav</a> government, Velimirović protested in a letter to the PM, <a href="/wiki/Dragi%C5%A1a_Cvetkovi%C4%87" title="Dragiša Cvetković">Dragiša Cvetković</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup> Velimirović attended Ljotić's funeral in 1945 and spoke very positively of him even though it was already known that Ljotić was collaborating with the Germans. He spoke of Ljotić as an "ideologue of Serbian nationalism".<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
