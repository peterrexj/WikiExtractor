<p><b>Fulbert of Chartres</b> (<a href="/wiki/French_language" title="French language">French</a>: <i lang="fr">Fulbert de Chartres</i>; 952–970&#8211;10 April 1028) was the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Chartres" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Chartres">Bishop of Chartres</a> from 1006 to 1028 and a teacher at the <a href="/wiki/School_of_Chartres" title="School of Chartres">Cathedral school</a> there. Fulbert was a pupil of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Sylvester_II" title="Pope Sylvester II">Gerbert of Aurillac</a>, who would later become Pope Sylvester II. He was responsible for the advancement of the <a href="/wiki/Nativity_of_Mary" title="Nativity of Mary">Nativity of the Virgin</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> on September 8 and for one of the many reconstructions of the <a href="/wiki/Chartres_Cathedral" title="Chartres Cathedral">Chartres Cathedral</a>. Most of the available information about him is found in the letters he wrote from 1004–1028 to both secular and religious figures of the day.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fulbert_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There is no conclusive evidence as to the exact date or location of Fulbert's birth, the sources vary in listing dates from 952 to 970.<sup id="cite_ref-M5-Bxvi_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-M5-Bxvi-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> As to his place of birth, most sources place it in northern <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>, possibly <a href="/wiki/Picardy" title="Picardy">Picardy</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-M5-Bxvi_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-M5-Bxvi-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> although some say northern <a href="/wiki/Italy" title="Italy">Italy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Wellman,_p._136_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Wellman,_p._136-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The sources do agree, however, that he was of humble birth.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>  Information from several sources places him at the <a href="/wiki/Cathedral_school" title="Cathedral school">cathedral school</a> in <a href="/wiki/Rheims" class="mw-redirect" title="Rheims">Rheims</a> in the 980s,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> where one of his fellow students was the future King <a href="/wiki/Robert_II_of_France" title="Robert II of France">Robert II</a> (the Pious) of <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> In the early to mid-990s Fulbert arrived at and began his involvement with the cathedral school there. His position is variously described as schoolmaster or assistant.<sup id="cite_ref-Bxvii-W136_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bxvii-W136-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> He also assumed some minor ecclesiastical roles in the cathedral, but he was not a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Bxvii-W136_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bxvii-W136-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>  In 1004 he became a deacon, and in 1006 he was appointed Bishop of Chartres,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> a position he maintained until his death on 10 April 1028 or 1029, again the sources vary, but the majority seem to settle on 1028.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> There is some dispute over Fulbert's <a href="/wiki/Sainthood" class="mw-redirect" title="Sainthood">sainthood</a>, which arises from his contemporaries describing him as having a "saintly nature", a description which continued to be used by others after his death. Fulbert was never officially <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> by the Church, but permission was given by <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> for the <a href="/wiki/Diocese" title="Diocese">dioceses</a> of <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Chartres" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Chartres">Chartres</a> and <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Poitiers" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Poitiers">Poitiers</a> to celebrate his life on 10 April.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His feast is included in <a href="/wiki/Martyrologium_Romanum" class="mw-redirect" title="Martyrologium Romanum">Martyrologium Romanum</a> (2004) on 10 April<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fulbert_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Letters">Letters</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fulbert_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Letters">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Of the writings that can be verifiably attributed to Fulbert, the bulk consists of his letters.  His most famous letter was to Duke <a href="/wiki/William_V_of_Aquitaine" class="mw-redirect" title="William V of Aquitaine">William V of Aquitaine</a> on the duties of <a href="/wiki/Feudal_lord" class="mw-redirect" title="Feudal lord">feudal lord</a> and <a href="/wiki/Vassal" title="Vassal">vassal</a>. He also wrote to fellow churchmen on a variety of <a href="/wiki/Liturgical" class="mw-redirect" title="Liturgical">liturgical</a> issues including the appointment of bishops, <a href="/wiki/Excommunication" title="Excommunication">excommunication</a>, and obedience. His letters also include correspondence about mundane issues of everyday life such as thanking people for medicine and setting up meetings.  These letters provide insight into a variety of issues in the late tenth and early eleventh century <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Poems">Poems</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fulbert_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Poems">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Fulbert wrote approximately 24 poems which have sometimes been described as humorous, such as his poem about the <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> in the desert,<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> or lovely, as when describing his “Ode to the Nightingale”.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Hymns">Hymns</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fulbert_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Hymns">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Most of Fulbert's <a href="/wiki/Hymns" class="mw-redirect" title="Hymns">hymns</a> were written to glorify the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> He also wrote “Chorus Novae Jerusalem”  (<a href="/wiki/Ye_Choirs_of_New_Jerusalem" title="Ye Choirs of New Jerusalem">Ye Choirs of New Jerusalem</a>), to be sung at <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Easter</a> services.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Sermons">Sermons</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fulbert_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Sermons">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Fulbert's most famous <a href="/wiki/Sermon" title="Sermon">sermon</a> is “ Approbate Consuetudinis”, in which he provides information regarding the importance of the celebration of the “Feast of Mary’s Nativity”.<sup id="cite_ref-Fassler,_p._406_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fassler,_p._406-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Theological_contributions">Theological contributions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fulbert_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Theological contributions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Like the recent <a href="/wiki/Millennium" title="Millennium">millennium</a> change, the one during Fulbert's lifetime also created a fear of the end of the world. The veneration of the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin Mary</a> was already established in the Church, and Fulbert used this to teach her importance.  The results were twofold, it helped to ease people's fears and greatly expanded the Marian Cult and Chartres's position in it. <a href="/wiki/Chartres" title="Chartres">Chartres</a> was already involved due to its being the holder of a <a href="/wiki/Sacred_relic" class="mw-redirect" title="Sacred relic">sacred relic</a> of Mary's, the “Sancta Camisia”, (Holy Tunic), which has been variously described as being worn by Mary during the <a href="/wiki/Annunciation" title="Annunciation">Annunciation</a><sup id="cite_ref-Wellman,_p._136_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Wellman,_p._136-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> or during the <a href="/wiki/Birth_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Birth of Christ">birth of Christ</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> This tunic was already the subject of a <a href="/wiki/Miracle" title="Miracle">miracle</a> because of its use by an earlier bishop of Chartres, Gauscelinus, in 911 to ward off the invading <a href="/wiki/Normans" title="Normans">Normans</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Fulbert expanded on the theme of miracles involving Mary, especially those cases where she had interceded between sinners and God. In this way people could pray for Mary's <a href="/wiki/Intercession" title="Intercession">intercession</a> with God on their behalf in the perceived coming apocalypse.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Fulbert himself was involved in one of these miracles; when he was gravely ill Mary had healed him with a drop of milk because of his devotion to her. This also served to give Mary the image of not only the <a href="/wiki/Mother_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Mother of Christ">mother of Christ</a>, but for all who believed in her, their mother too.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> All of this led to Fulbert's ultimate goal of promoting a special feast day to celebrate Mary's Nativity.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>To gain popular support for this feast, Fulbert wrote his famous <a href="/wiki/Sermon" title="Sermon">sermon</a> “Approbate Consuetudinis” in which he relates Mary's miracles. He also brings in the evidence of Mary's family lineage, which the Bible traces back to <a href="/wiki/David" title="David">King David</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Fassler,_p._406_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fassler,_p._406-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> In his sermon Fulbert used the symbolism of the “Stirps Jesse” (<a href="/wiki/Tree_of_Jesse" title="Tree of Jesse">Tree of Jesse</a>) to help explain Mary's familial relationship to the great men of the past and how it was determined, as described in <a href="/wiki/Scripture" class="mw-redirect" title="Scripture">Scripture</a>, that she would be the one to whom Christ would be born.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> This again served to enhance her importance to the world and convince people of the need to celebrate her birth. This sermon led to a number of <a href="/wiki/Liturgical" class="mw-redirect" title="Liturgical">liturgical</a> changes throughout the next few centuries in <a href="/wiki/Europe" title="Europe">Europe</a>. The sermon itself, or variations of it, and the <a href="/wiki/Chants" class="mw-redirect" title="Chants">chants</a> associated with it, became part of the service for the feast day of Mary's Nativity on Sept. 8.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> By promoting the Feast day of Mary's Nativity, Fulbert was able to advance the importance of Mary and therefore the cult of her veneration grew. This in turn enhanced the importance of the <a href="/wiki/Cathedral_of_Chartres" class="mw-redirect" title="Cathedral of Chartres">Cathedral of Chartres</a> as a centre for Marian devotion, and also gave people a spiritual symbol to turn to in times of need at the turn of the <a href="/wiki/Millennium" title="Millennium">millennium</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Ecclesiastical_reform">Ecclesiastical reform</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fulbert_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Ecclesiastical reform">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>During his time in <a href="/wiki/Chartres" title="Chartres">Chartres</a> Fulbert played an important role in the development and spread of the ideas that led to the <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_Reform" title="Gregorian Reform">Gregorian church reforms</a> of the eleventh century under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_VII" title="Pope Gregory VII">Pope Gregory VII</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ziezulewicz,_p._401_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ziezulewicz,_p._401-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> These reforms concerned the division between the powers of church and state, especially in the appointment of new <a href="/wiki/Abbots" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbots">abbots</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishops</a>. In the eleventh century the secular rulers had a habit of appointing whomever they wanted to fill vacant church positions. Fulbert and some of his students, such as Abbot <a href="/w/index.php?title=Albert_of_Marmoutier&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Albert of Marmoutier (page does not exist)">Albert of Marmoutier</a>, routinely wrote that it was up to the clergy and the citizens of the diocese involved to elect a replacement,<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> the authority being found in the rulings of the <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Nicaea" title="First Council of Nicaea">First Council of Nicaea</a> (325) and the Council of <a href="/wiki/Antioch" title="Antioch">Antioch</a> (264-272).<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>  These reforms also stated that the Church, not the state, was responsible for disciplining the clergy. The issues of <a href="/wiki/Simony" title="Simony">simony</a> (the buying of church offices) and immoral clerics were also addressed by Fulbert.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Although the reforms were issued by Gregory VII, some of its ideas came from Fulbert, whose writings were disseminated through his students.<sup id="cite_ref-Ziezulewicz,_p._401_26-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ziezulewicz,_p._401-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Architectural_contributions">Architectural contributions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Fulbert_of_Chartres&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Architectural contributions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After <a href="/wiki/Chartres_Cathedral" title="Chartres Cathedral">Chartres Cathedral</a> burned in 1020, Fulbert devoted his energies to raising funds for its rebuilding, which was completed in 1037, nine years after his death.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> In 1194 the cathedral was again almost completely destroyed by fire; only the <a href="/wiki/Crypt" title="Crypt">crypt</a>, some of the west <a href="/wiki/Facade" class="mw-redirect" title="Facade">facade</a> and two towers remained.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> The crypt has been incorporated into all subsequent reconstructions.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> The construction of the <a href="/wiki/Gothic_architecture" title="Gothic architecture">Gothic-style</a> cathedral that stands to-day began afterward. It is in this cathedral that we see Fulbert's influences that resulted from his promotion of the Feast day of Mary's Nativity and the cult of the Virgin Mary. The sculptures around the three <a href="/wiki/Portal_(architecture)" title="Portal (architecture)">portals</a> depict the life of Mary, who is the central figure in the Royal Portal.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>  One of the cathedral's <a href="/wiki/Stained_glass_windows" class="mw-redirect" title="Stained glass windows">stained glass windows</a> depicts the <a href="/wiki/Tree_of_Jesse" title="Tree of Jesse">Tree of Jesse</a>, which traces <a href="/wiki/Mary_(mother_of_Jesus)" class="mw-redirect" title="Mary (mother of Jesus)">Mary's</a> family and the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Family" title="Holy Family">Holy Family</a>, again a reference to Fulbert's teachings in regards to the Feast of Mary's Nativity.
</p>
