<p><b>Juliana of Liège</b> (also called <b>Juliana of Mount-Cornillon</b>), (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 1192 or 1193 &#8211; 5 April 1258) was a medieval <a href="/wiki/Norbertine" class="mw-redirect" title="Norbertine">Norbertine</a> <a href="/wiki/Canoness_regular" class="mw-redirect" title="Canoness regular">canoness regular</a> and <a href="/wiki/Mysticism" title="Mysticism">mystic</a> in what is now <a href="/wiki/Belgium" title="Belgium">Belgium</a>. Traditional scholarly sources have long recognized her as the promoter of the <a href="/wiki/Feast_of_Corpus_Christi" title="Feast of Corpus Christi">Feast of Corpus Christi</a>, first celebrated in Liège in 1246, and later adopted for the Catholic Church in 1264.  More recent scholarship includes manuscript analysis of the initial version of the <a href="/wiki/Liturgy_of_the_Hours" title="Liturgy of the Hours">Office</a>, as found in <i>The Hague, National Library of the Netherlands</i> (KB 70.E.4) and a close reading of her Latin <i><a href="/wiki/Biography" title="Biography">vita</a></i>, a <a href="/wiki/Textual_criticism" title="Textual criticism">critical edition</a> of which was published in <a href="/wiki/French_language" title="French language">French</a> by the Belgian scholar and current (2023) bishop of Liège, <a href="/wiki/Jean-Pierre_Delville" title="Jean-Pierre Delville">Jean-Pierre Delville</a>.
</p><p>Newer scholarly work notes the many references to her musical and <a href="/wiki/Liturgy" title="Liturgy">liturgical</a> performances. Modern women scholars recognize Juliana as the "author" of the initial version of the Latin <a href="/wiki/Liturgy_of_the_Hours" title="Liturgy of the Hours">Office</a>, <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.begijnhofbreda.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Antifoon-Animarum-cibus.m4a"><i>Animarum cibus</i></a>, which takes its title from the beginning of its first <a href="/wiki/Antiphon" title="Antiphon">antiphon</a>.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Juliana_of_Li%C3%A8ge&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Juliana and her twin sister Agnes were born in the village of <a href="/wiki/Fl%C3%A9ron" title="Fléron">Retinnes</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Prince-Bishopric_of_Li%C3%A8ge" title="Prince-Bishopric of Liège">Prince-Bishopric of Liège</a>. They were <a href="/wiki/Orphan" title="Orphan">orphaned</a> at age five and placed in a newly founded <a href="/wiki/Hospice" title="Hospice">hospice</a> at Mont-Cornillon, right outside of <a href="/wiki/Li%C3%A8ge" title="Liège">Liège</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Canonry" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonry">canonry</a> seems to have been established on the model of a <a href="/wiki/Double_monastery" title="Double monastery">double monastery</a>, with both canons and canonesses, each living in their own wing of the monastery. The two girls were initially placed on a small farm next to the canonry. Juliana, after entering the Order at the age of 13,<sup id="cite_ref-vocations_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vocations-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> worked for many years in its <a href="/wiki/Leprosy" title="Leprosy">leprosarium</a>. Agnes seems to have died young, as there is no further mention of her in the archives.
</p><p>From her early youth, Juliana had great veneration for the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a> (as did many of the women of Liège) and longed for a special <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> in its honor. When Juliana was 16 she had her first vision which recurred subsequently several times. Her vision presented the moon in its full splendour, crossed diametrically by a dark stripe. In time she came to understand that the moon symbolized the life of the church on earth, the opaque line, on the other hand, represented the absence of a liturgical feast in honor of Christ's Body and Blood.<sup id="cite_ref-Benedict_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Benedict-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Not having any way to bring about such a feast, she kept her thoughts to herself, except for sharing them with an <a href="/wiki/Anchorite" title="Anchorite">anchoress</a>, Eve of Liège, who lived in a cell adjacent to the Basilica of St. Martin, and a few other trusted sisters in her monastery. Her vision is illustrated on the <a href="/wiki/Historiated_initial" title="Historiated initial">historiated initial</a> letter of her <i>vita</i> as it appears in <i>Paris, Bibliothèque de l'Arsenal</i> (MS 945, fol. 2).
</p><p>Around 1225, she was elected <a href="/wiki/Prioress" class="mw-redirect" title="Prioress">prioress</a> of the double canonry and told her visions to her <a href="/wiki/Confessor" title="Confessor">confessor</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Canon John of Lausanne (a <a href="/wiki/Secular_canon" class="mw-redirect" title="Secular canon">secular canon</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Chapter_(religion)" title="Chapter (religion)">collegiate chapter</a> at Saint Martin Basilica). Canon John had many contacts among the distinguished French theologians and Dominican professors who had gathered in Liège.  These included <a href="/wiki/Robert_de_Thorete" class="mw-redirect" title="Robert de Thorete">Robert de Thorete</a>, the Bishop of Liège, <a href="/wiki/Hugh_of_Saint-Cher" title="Hugh of Saint-Cher">Hugh of Saint-Cher</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican</a> <a href="/wiki/Provincial_superior" title="Provincial superior">Prior Provincial</a> for France, and Jacques Pantaleon of Troyes, <a href="/wiki/Archdeacon" title="Archdeacon">Archdeacon</a> of Liège, who later became <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Verdun" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Verdun">Diocese of Verdun</a>, then <a href="/wiki/Latin_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Patriarch of Jerusalem">Latin Patriarch of Jerusalem</a>, and finally pope, ruling under the name <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_IV" title="Pope Urban IV">Pope Urban IV</a>. Canon John reportedly relayed Juliana's vision to these distinguished religious leaders. These theologians agreed unanimously that there was nothing in the devotion of the feast contrary to the Catholic faith and endorsed instituting it.
</p><p>Upon receiving <a href="/wiki/Approbation_(Catholic_canon_law)" title="Approbation (Catholic canon law)">approbation</a> from local religious authorities, Juliana set to work with Canon John, who was still a young man, and together they composed the initial version of the office, <i>Animarum cibus</i>.  This early office can be found in the composite manuscript, <i>The Hague, National Library of the Netherlands</i> (KB 70.E.4). In 1246, Bishop Robert instituted the first feast of Christ's Body and Blood for his own diocese. He died later that same year, however, and never saw it completed, though the feast was celebrated by the canons of St. Martin.
</p><p>Juliana's life was filled with tumult, largely as a consequence of the religious and political controversies rampant in Liège: an emerging urban <a href="/wiki/Middle_class" title="Middle class">middle class</a> demanding new rights, political rivalries between the <a href="/wiki/Guelphs" class="mw-redirect" title="Guelphs">Guelphs</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Ghibellines" class="mw-redirect" title="Ghibellines">Ghibellines</a>, and internecine quarrels among the lower nobility in <a href="/wiki/Flanders" title="Flanders">Flanders</a>. These conflicts created a context ripe for this type of movement.  When Juliana became prioress of the canonry, she re-instated strict Augustinian rules.  In 1240, the canonry and adjacent leprosarium came under the supervision of a man named Roger, a vicious man who had gained the position through simony and intrigue. He immediately disliked both Juliana and her reproaches, and incited the citizenry against her,<sup id="cite_ref-mershman_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mershman-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> accusing her of diverting and stealing the hospital's funds.  She fled to the <a href="/wiki/Anchorite#Medieval_times" title="Anchorite">anchorhold</a> of her friend, Dame Eve, and was then received into Canon John's house, adjacent to the basilica. With the help of <a href="/wiki/Robert_of_Thourotte" title="Robert of Thourotte">Robert of Thourotte</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Li%C3%A8ge" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Liège">Bishop of Liège</a>, Juliana was vindicated and restored to her former position in the canonry. Roger was deposed. In 1247, however, upon the death of Bishop Robert, Roger once again regained control of Mont Cornillon under the new bishop, Henry de Gueldre, and Juliana was again driven out.  These events in Juliana's biography, to a certain extent, point to the larger historical backdrop of rivalry over the vacated bishopric, amplified by the <a href="/wiki/Excommunication" title="Excommunication">excommunication</a> of <a href="/wiki/Frederick_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor">Frederick II</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Innocent_IV" title="Pope Innocent IV">Pope Innocent IV</a>.
</p><p>Thereafter Juliana found refuge in the <a href="/wiki/Cistercian" class="mw-redirect" title="Cistercian">Cistercian</a> monasteries at Robermont, Val-Benoit, and Val-Notre-Dame, and then among the poor <a href="/wiki/Beguines" class="mw-redirect" title="Beguines">Beguines</a>.  Aided by Abbess Imene, who was the sister of Archbishop Conrad of Cologne, Juliana took up residence at the Cistercian Abbey of Salzinnes, and finally <a href="/wiki/Fosses-la-Ville" title="Fosses-la-Ville">Fosses-la-Ville</a>, in the <a href="/wiki/County_of_Namur" title="County of Namur">County of Namur</a>, where she lived in seclusion until her death. On her deathbed she asked for her confessor, John of Lausanne, supposedly to reveal to him long hidden secrets. But neither he nor any of her friends from Liege arrived.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Upon her death, based on her wishes, her friend, the Cistercian <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> Gobert d'Aspremont, moved her body to <a href="/wiki/Villers_Abbey" title="Villers Abbey">Villers Abbey</a>. On the following Sunday her remains were moved to the section of the cemetery reserved for saints. Although her cult developed immediately, it did not receive official recognition until 1869 under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_IX" title="Pope Pius IX">Pope Pius IX</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Juliana_of_Li%C3%A8ge&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1261, the Archdeacon Pantaleon was elected Pope, and took the name <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_IV" title="Pope Urban IV">Pope Urban IV</a>. In 1264, Urban IV instituted the Solemnity of Corpus Christi on the Thursday after Pentecost as a feast for the entire Latin Church, by the papal bull <i>Transiturus de hoc mundo</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-Benedict_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Benedict-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He commissioned his chief <a href="/wiki/Theology" title="Theology">theologian</a>, <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas" title="Thomas Aquinas">Thomas Aquinas</a>, to compose an office for the Feast of Corpus Christi. Thomas composed two versions, with considerable textual overlap: <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Sapiencia edificavit</i></span> (no Latin misspelling here) and then <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Sacerdos in eternum</i></span>. The inter-textual study of these Offices has been the topic of considerable research, with most scholars concluding that they represent "draft" and final versions of the work of Thomas. Pope Urban recorded the initial celebration in letters to the various clergy, but also sent a copy to Juliana's friend, <a href="/wiki/Dame_(disambiguation)" class="mw-disambig" title="Dame (disambiguation)">Dame</a> the recluse Eve of Saint Martin at St. Martin Basilica.  She is thought by contemporary scholars to have composed the initial version of Juliana's <i>vita</i> in French and thus stands, alongside Juliana, as among the first women authors of medieval Europe.
</p><p>The first formal theological statement of the doctrine of transubstantiation can be found in the homily by Thomas in the <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Sacerdos in eternum</i></span> version as found in BNF 1143 (a musical manuscript devoted entirely to the office).  Scholars have also noted the movement from doctrinal to biblical versions in the Office texts.  And, stylistically, Juliana's version sets known texts to new music; the Aquinas version rearranges biblical quotations to known chants, thus creating <a href="/wiki/Contrafactum" title="Contrafactum">contrafacta</a>. In 1264 <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_IV" title="Pope Urban IV">Pope Urban IV</a> issued the <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">papal bull</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Transiturus" title="Transiturus">Transiturus</a></i> in which the <a href="/wiki/Feast_of_Corpus_Christi" title="Feast of Corpus Christi">Feast of Corpus Christi</a>, i.e., the feast of the <a href="/wiki/Body_of_Christ" title="Body of Christ">Body of Christ</a> was declared a feast throughout the entire <a href="/wiki/Latin_liturgical_rites" title="Latin liturgical rites">Latin Rite</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> This was the very first papally sanctioned universal feast in the history of the Latin Rite.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The feast is traditionally celebrated on the Thursday after Trinity Sunday, but in the liturgical reforms of 1969, under <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI" title="Pope Paul VI">Pope Paul VI</a>, the bishops of every nation have the option to transfer it to the following Sunday.
</p><p>Juliana was <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> in 1869 by Pope Pius IX and further celebrated by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a>, who wrote a letter mentioning her on the 750th anniversary of the Feast of Corpus Christi. Her feast day is the 6 April.<sup id="cite_ref-vocations_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vocations-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
