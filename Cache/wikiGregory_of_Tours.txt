<p><b>Gregory of Tours</b> (born <b>Georgius Florentius</b>; 30 November <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> 538 – 17 November 594 AD) was a <a href="/wiki/Gallo-Roman_culture" title="Gallo-Roman culture">Gallo-Roman</a> historian and <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Tours">Bishop of Tours</a> during the <a href="/wiki/Merovingian_dynasty" title="Merovingian dynasty">Merovingian period</a><sup id="cite_ref-cathen_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathen-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and is known as the "father of French history."<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was a <a href="/wiki/Prelate" title="Prelate">prelate</a> in the Merovingian kingdom, encompassing <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a>'s historic region.
</p><p>Gregory's most notable work is the <i>Decem Libri Historiarum</i> (Ten Books of Histories), also known as the <i>Historia Francorum</i> (History of the Franks). <i>Decem Libri Historiarum</i> is considered a <a href="/wiki/Primary_source" title="Primary source">primary source</a> for the study of Merovingian history and chronicles the accounts of the <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Franks</a> during the period. Gregory is also known for documenting accounts of religious figures, notably that of <a href="/wiki/Martin_of_Tours" title="Martin of Tours">Martin of Tours</a>. It is alleged that the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint_Martin,_Tours" title="Basilica of Saint Martin, Tours">Tomb of St. Martin</a> became a <a href="/wiki/Christian_pilgrimage" title="Christian pilgrimage">Christian pilgrimage</a> site in the 6th century due in part to Gregory's writings.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gregory was born in <a href="/wiki/Clermont-Ferrand" title="Clermont-Ferrand">Clermont</a>, in the <a href="/wiki/Auvergne_(province)" class="mw-redirect" title="Auvergne (province)">Auvergne</a> region of central Gaul.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He was born into the upper stratum of Gallo-Roman society as the son of Florentius, <a href="/wiki/Roman_Senate#Senate_in_the_West" title="Roman Senate">Senator</a> of Clermont, by his wife Armentaria II, niece of <a href="/wiki/Nicetius_of_Lyon" title="Nicetius of Lyon">Bishop Nicetius of Lyon</a> and granddaughter of both Florentinus, Senator of Geneva, and Saint Gregory of Langres. Gregory had several noted bishops and saints as close relatives (his family effectively monopolised the Bishoprics of Tours, Lyon, and Langres at the time of his birth), and, according to Gregory, he was connected to thirteen of the eighteen bishops of Tours preceding him by ties of kinship. Gregory's paternal grandmother, Leocadia, descended from Vettius Epagathus, the illustrious martyr of Lyon.
</p><p>His father died while Gregory was young and his widowed mother moved to <a href="/wiki/Burgundy_(region)" class="mw-redirect" title="Burgundy (region)">Burgundy</a> where she had property. Gregory went to live with his paternal uncle <a href="/wiki/Gal_I_(Bishop_of_Clermont)" title="Gal I (Bishop of Clermont)">St. Gallus, Bishop of Clermont</a>, under whom, and his successor St. Avitus, Gregory had his education. Gregory also received the clerical <a href="/wiki/Tonsure" title="Tonsure">tonsure</a> from Gallus. Having contracted a serious illness, he made a visit of devotion to the tomb of St. Martin at Tours. Upon his recovery, he began to pursue a clerical career and was ordained deacon by Avitus. Upon the death of <a href="/wiki/Eufronius" title="Eufronius">St. Euphronius</a>, he was chosen as bishop by the clergy and people, who had been charmed with his piety, learning, and humility. Their deputies overtook him at the court of <a href="/wiki/Sigebert_I" title="Sigebert I">King Sigebert of Austrasia</a>, and being compelled to acquiesce, though much against his will, Gregory was consecrated by Giles, Bishop of Rheims, on 22 August 573, at the age of thirty-four.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He spent most of his career at Tours, although he assisted at the council of Paris in 577.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The rough world he lived in was on the cusp of the dying world of Antiquity and the new culture of early <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">medieval</a> Europe. Gregory lived also on the border between the Frankish culture of the Merovingians to the north and the Gallo-Roman culture of the south of Gaul.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Division_of_Gaul_-_511.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/Division_of_Gaul_-_511.jpg/220px-Division_of_Gaul_-_511.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="201" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/Division_of_Gaul_-_511.jpg/330px-Division_of_Gaul_-_511.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/Division_of_Gaul_-_511.jpg/440px-Division_of_Gaul_-_511.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1241" data-file-height="1136" /></a><figcaption>Realms of <a href="/wiki/Merovingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingian">Merovingian</a> Gaul at the death of <a href="/wiki/Clovis_I" title="Clovis I">Clovis</a> (511 AD).</figcaption></figure>
<p>At Tours, Gregory could not have been better placed to hear everything and meet every one of influence in Merovingian culture. Tours lay on the watery highway of the navigable <a href="/wiki/Loire" title="Loire">Loire</a>. Five Roman roads radiated from Tours, which lay on the main thoroughfare between the Frankish north and Aquitania, with Spain beyond. At Tours the Frankish influences of the north and the Gallo-Roman influences of the south had their chief contact (see map). As the center for the popular cult of St Martin, Tours was a pilgrimage site, hospital, and a political sanctuary to which important leaders fled during periods of violence and turmoil in Merovingian politics.
</p><p>Gregory struggled through personal relations with four Frankish kings, <a href="/wiki/Sigebert_I" title="Sigebert I">Sigebert I</a>, <a href="/wiki/Chilperic_I" title="Chilperic I">Chilperic I</a>, <a href="/wiki/Guntram" title="Guntram">Guntram</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Childebert_II" title="Childebert II">Childebert II</a>, and he personally knew most of the leading Franks.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gregory wrote in <a href="/wiki/Late_Latin" title="Late Latin">Late Latin</a>, which frequently departed from <a href="/wiki/Classical_Latin" title="Classical Latin">classical</a> usage in both syntax and spelling, though with relatively few changes in inflection.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Gr%C3%A9goire_de_Tours,_Histoire_des_Francs,_livres_1_%C3%A0_6,_page_de_frontispice.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/37/Gr%C3%A9goire_de_Tours%2C_Histoire_des_Francs%2C_livres_1_%C3%A0_6%2C_page_de_frontispice.jpg/180px-Gr%C3%A9goire_de_Tours%2C_Histoire_des_Francs%2C_livres_1_%C3%A0_6%2C_page_de_frontispice.jpg" decoding="async" width="180" height="229" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/37/Gr%C3%A9goire_de_Tours%2C_Histoire_des_Francs%2C_livres_1_%C3%A0_6%2C_page_de_frontispice.jpg/270px-Gr%C3%A9goire_de_Tours%2C_Histoire_des_Francs%2C_livres_1_%C3%A0_6%2C_page_de_frontispice.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/37/Gr%C3%A9goire_de_Tours%2C_Histoire_des_Francs%2C_livres_1_%C3%A0_6%2C_page_de_frontispice.jpg/360px-Gr%C3%A9goire_de_Tours%2C_Histoire_des_Francs%2C_livres_1_%C3%A0_6%2C_page_de_frontispice.jpg 2x" data-file-width="472" data-file-height="600" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Book_frontispiece" title="Book frontispiece">Frontispiece</a> of <i>Historia Francorum</i>.</figcaption></figure>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="History_of_the_Franks"><i>History of the Franks</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: History of the Franks">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Summary">Summary</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Summary">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Gregory of Tours' history is a dense work, with numerous narratives and characters. It contains Christian tales of miracles, descriptions of omens and natural events, stories of Christian martyrs, dialogues of church debates, discussions of the lives of holy men, nobility, and eccentric peasants, frequent Bible verses and references, and explorations of the complex international relations between numerous tribes and nations including the <a href="/wiki/Lombards" title="Lombards">Lombards</a>, <a href="/wiki/Visigoths" title="Visigoths">Visigoths</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ostrogoths" title="Ostrogoths">Ostrogoths</a> and <a href="/wiki/Huns" title="Huns">Huns</a>, not to mention Gregory's personal biography and interpretation of events.
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_1">Book 1</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Book 1">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Book One begins with a pronouncement by the author that he is a Frankish Catholic clergyman who follows the Nicene Creed and abhors heresy like those of the "wicked" <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arian</a> sect among other heresies. The narrative history begins with a brief epitome of the Biblical <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old Testament</a>, <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a>, and the subsequent spread of the Christian Religion into Gaul. Next Gregory covers the history of Christianity in Gaul and some of the major events in Roman-Gallo relations. It ends with the death of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Martin_of_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Martin of Tours">Saint Martin of Tours</a> in 397.
</p>
<h4><span id="Book_2_.28397-511.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_2_(397-511)">Book 2 (397-511)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Book 2 (397-511)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Book Two covers the beginnings of the Merovingian dynasty, including King <a href="/wiki/Clovis_I" title="Clovis I">Clovis I</a>'s conversion to Christianity by his wife <a href="/wiki/Clotilde" title="Clotilde">Clotilde</a> and ending with his death in 511, after his conquest of large tracts of land in modern-day France.
</p>
<h4><span id="Book_3_.28511-548.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_3_(511-548)">Book 3 (511-548)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Book 3 (511-548)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Book Three follows the four sons of King Clovis who equally divide his realms after his death in 511. These four kings, <a href="/wiki/Theuderic_I" title="Theuderic I">Theuderic I</a>, <a href="/wiki/Chlothar_I" title="Chlothar I">Clothar I</a>, <a href="/wiki/Childebert_I" title="Childebert I">Childebert I</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Chlodomer" title="Chlodomer">Chlodomer</a>, quarrel and fight for supremacy of the Frankish realm. Despite their disputes, they occasionally work together against an outside threat, such as their attack of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_the_Burgundians" title="Kingdom of the Burgundians">Burgundians</a> in 523. Eventually Clothar becomes the most powerful King in the Frankish realm. After the death of King Theoderic I in 534, Book Three ends with the death of his son and successor King <a href="/wiki/Theudebert_I" title="Theudebert I">Theudebert I</a> in 548. After 548, the kingdom is inherited by <a href="/wiki/Theudebald" title="Theudebald">Theudebald</a> until his death in 555.
</p>
<h4><span id="Book_4_.28548-575.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_4_(548-575)">Book 4 (548-575)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Book 4 (548-575)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Book Four continues from when the two remaining sons of Clovis die: King Childebert in 558 and King Clothar in 561. The last years of his life see the entire realm of the Franks ruled by Clothar. At the time of his demise in 561 (like Clovis before him), the Kingdom is divided equally between four sons of Clothar; <a href="/wiki/Charibert_I" title="Charibert I">King Charibert I</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sigebert_I" title="Sigebert I">King Sigibert I</a>, <a href="/wiki/Guntram" title="Guntram">King Guntram</a> and <a href="/wiki/Chilperic_I" title="Chilperic I">King Chilperic I</a> and their quarrel for control of the entire kingdom. A truce between them is maintained until after the death of King Charibert I in 567. Clothar's remaining sons fight for the supremacy of the kingdom, with King Sigibert showing the strongest military force. Book Four ends with the killing of King Sigbert in 575, leaving King Chilperic as the dominant king. Gregory of Tours blames <a href="/wiki/Fredegund" title="Fredegund">Fredegund</a>, the wife of King Chilperic, for this assassination. Fredegund has long held a grudge against King Sigibert and his wife <a href="/wiki/Brunhilda_of_Austrasia" title="Brunhilda of Austrasia">Brunhilda</a>.
</p>
<h4><span id="Book_5_.28575-581.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_5_(575-581)">Book 5 (575-581)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Book 5 (575-581)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Book Five begins the part where the author has much personal knowledge about the events in the Frankish Kingdom. This book and the ones after, are considerably longer and more detailed than the previous, whilst covering a shorter amount of time. This book also contains Gregory's impressions of ecclesiastical issues he witnessed and had some bearing on. It describes a possible debate that Gregory had with a rival Arian church leader. Moreover, book 5 also introduces <a href="/wiki/Childebert_II" title="Childebert II">Childebert II</a>, the son of recently slain King Sigibert, and of the still living Brunhilda. Childebert is taken along with Brunhilda under the protection of King Gunthram, brother and sometime rival of King Chilperic.
</p>
<h4><span id="Book_6_.28581-584.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_6_(581-584)">Book 6 (581-584)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Book 6 (581-584)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>In Book Six, the young Childebert betrays his alliance with his adoptive uncle King Gunthram, who had protected Childebert and his mother after his father Sigibert's death. Now Childebert forms an alliance with his uncle, King Chilperic, who had often been an enemy of King Sigibert. Later, King Chilperic is murdered under mysterious circumstances in 584.
</p>
<h4><span id="Book_7_.28584.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_7_(584)">Book 7 (584)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Book 7 (584)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>In Book Seven, Fredegund assumes regency for her young son <a href="/wiki/Chlothar_II" title="Chlothar II">Clothar II</a>. In the future he will be king of all Franks until his death in 629, beyond Gregory's narrative which ends in roughly 593. Fredegund and her son are under the protection of King Gunthram. She remains in power until her death in 597. Also in this book is the rebellion of <a href="/wiki/Gundoald" title="Gundoald">Gundovald</a> and its failure. Gundovald claimed to be a lost illegitimate son of dead King Chlothar I. Many of the Frankish nobles and the Byzantine emperor <a href="/wiki/Maurice_(emperor)" title="Maurice (emperor)">Maurice</a> gave some support to this rebellion; however, it is swiftly crushed by King Guntram.
</p>
<h4><span id="Book_8_.28585.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_8_(585)">Book 8 (585)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Book 8 (585)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>"Many evil things were done at this time", as Gregory writes in Book VIII. It begins with the travels of King Guntram to Paris and Orleans and describes numerous confrontations between the king and some bishops. Meanwhile, king Guntram becomes ill and fears for his life. Gregory comments that the king's illness is a just punishment since he is planning to send a great number of bishops into exile. Fredegund gives two poisoned daggers to two clerics and sends them away with the order to assassinate King Childebert and Brunehild. However, the two clerics are arrested by Childebert, tortured and executed. Meanwhile, Fredegund is also behind the assassination of Bishop Praetextus of Rouen while he is praying in his church. King Guntram orders his army to march against Arian <a href="/wiki/Septimania" title="Septimania">Septimania</a> and Spain without success and blames his army commanders for having allowed atrocities and random destruction.
</p>
<h4><span id="Book_9_.28586-587.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_9_(586-587)">Book 9 (586-587)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Book 9 (586-587)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>In Book Nine, the <a href="/wiki/Treaty_of_Andelot" title="Treaty of Andelot">Treaty of Andelot</a> is signed in 587 between King Guntram, Brunhilda, and King Childebert II. The treaty is a close pact of alliance, wherein Childebert is formally adopted as Guntram's heir. Brunhilda also formally allies with Guntram and comes under his protection.
</p>
<h4><span id="Book_10_.28587-591.29"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Book_10_(587-591)">Book 10 (587-591)</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: Book 10 (587-591)">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The last book is set around 589. Basina, the daughter of King Chilperic I and Clotilda, daughter of King Charibert leads a brief revolt from a nunnery. The 18 Bishops of Tours are named and described. It ends with a summary of Gregory's previous written works.
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Analysis">Analysis</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: Analysis">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/List_of_Frankish_kings" title="List of Frankish kings">List of Frankish kings</a></div>
<p>The <i>Historia Francorum</i> is made up of ten books. Books I to IV initially recount the world's history from the Creation (as was traditional);<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> but move quickly on to the <a href="/wiki/Christianization_of_Gaul" class="mw-redirect" title="Christianization of Gaul">Christianization of Gaul</a>, the life and times of <a href="/wiki/Martin_of_Tours" title="Martin of Tours">Saint Martin of Tours</a>, the conversion of the Franks and the conquest of Gaul under <a href="/wiki/Clovis_I" title="Clovis I">Clovis</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and the more detailed history of the <a href="/wiki/List_of_Frankish_kings" title="List of Frankish kings">Frankish kings</a> down to the death of <a href="/wiki/Sigebert_I" title="Sigebert I">Sigebert I</a> in 575. At this date Gregory had been bishop of Tours for two years.
</p><p>With his fifth book, Gregory embarks (with some relief) on contemporary history, opening "Here, I am glad to say, begins Book V".<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> This, the second part of his history, books V and VI, closes with <a href="/wiki/Chilperic_I" title="Chilperic I">Chilperic I</a>'s death in 584.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2019)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> During the years that Chilperic held Tours, relations between him and Gregory were tense. After hearing rumours that the Bishop of Tours had slandered his wife, <a href="/wiki/Fredegund" title="Fredegund">Fredegund</a>, Chilperic had Gregory arrested and tried for treason – a charge which threatened both Gregory's bishopric and his life.<sup id="cite_ref-cathen_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathen-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The most eloquent passage in the <i>Historia</i> is the closing chapter of book VI, in which Chilperic's character is summed up unsympathetically through the use of an invective: <a href="/wiki/Herod_Antipas" title="Herod Antipas">Herod</a> and <a href="/wiki/Nero" title="Nero">Nero</a> are among the comparisons employed.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The third part, comprising books VII to X, takes his increasingly personal account to the year 591, and concludes with a plea for further chroniclers to preserve his work in entirety (as indeed would be done).<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> An <a href="/wiki/Epilogue" title="Epilogue">epilogue</a> was written in 594, the year of Gregory's death.
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Problems_of_interpretation">Problems of interpretation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=16" title="Edit section: Problems of interpretation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Readers of the <i>Historia Francorum</i> must decide whether this is a royal history or whether Gregory was writing to please his patrons. It is likely that one royal Frankish house is more generously treated than others. Gregory was also a Catholic bishop, and his writing reveals views typical of someone in his position. His views on perceived dangers of <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a>, still strong among the <a href="/wiki/Visigoths" title="Visigoths">Visigoths</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> led him to preface the <i>Historia</i> with a detailed expression of his orthodoxy on the nature of Christ. In addition, his ridiculing of <a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">pagans</a> and <a href="/wiki/Judaism" title="Judaism">Jews</a> reflected how his works were used to spread the <a href="/wiki/Christian_faith" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian faith">Christian faith</a>. For example, in book 2, chapters 28–31, he describes the pagans as incestuous and weak and then describes the process by which newly converted King Clovis leads a much better life than that of a pagan and is healed of all the conundrums he experienced as a pagan.
</p><p>Gregory's education was the standard <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> one of <a href="/wiki/Late_Antiquity" class="mw-redirect" title="Late Antiquity">Late Antiquity</a>, focusing on <a href="/wiki/Virgil" title="Virgil">Virgil</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Aeneid" title="Aeneid">Aeneid</a></i> and <a href="/wiki/Martianus_Capella" title="Martianus Capella">Martianus Capella</a>'s <i>Liber de Nuptiis Mercurii et Philologiae</i>, but also other key texts such as <a href="/wiki/Orosius" title="Orosius">Orosius</a>'s <i>Chronicles</i>, which his <i>Historia</i> continues, and <a href="/wiki/Sallust" title="Sallust">Sallust</a>; he refers to all these works in his own. His education, as was typical for the time, did not extend to a broad acquaintance with the pagan classics,<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> but rather progressed to mastery of the <a href="/wiki/Vulgate_Bible" class="mw-redirect" title="Vulgate Bible">Vulgate Bible</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> It is said that he constantly complained about his use of grammar.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> He did not understand how to correctly write masculine and feminine phrases, reflecting either a lack of ability or changes in the Latin language. Though he had read <a href="/wiki/Virgil" title="Virgil">Virgil</a>, considered the greatest Latin stylist, he cautions that "We ought not to relate their lying fables, lest we fall under sentence of eternal death." By contrast, he seems to have thoroughly studied the lengthy and complex Vulgate Bible, as well as numerous religious works and historical treatises, which he frequently quotes, particularly in the earlier books of the <i>Historia</i>.
</p><p>The main impression that historians once retained from the <i>Historia</i> focused on Gregory's anecdotes about violence;<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> until recently, historians tended to conclude that Merovingian Gaul was a chaotic, brutal mess. Recent scholarship has refuted that view. Through more careful readings, scholars have concluded that Gregory's underlying purpose was to highlight the vanity of secular life and contrast it with the miracles of the Saints.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Though Gregory conveys political and other messages through the <i>Historia</i>, and these are studied very closely, historians now generally agree that this contrast is the central and ever-present narrative device.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (March 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Chilp%C3%A9ric_Ier_et_%C3%A9v%C3%AAques.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/Chilp%C3%A9ric_Ier_et_%C3%A9v%C3%AAques.jpg/260px-Chilp%C3%A9ric_Ier_et_%C3%A9v%C3%AAques.jpg" decoding="async" width="260" height="260" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/Chilp%C3%A9ric_Ier_et_%C3%A9v%C3%AAques.jpg/390px-Chilp%C3%A9ric_Ier_et_%C3%A9v%C3%AAques.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/Chilp%C3%A9ric_Ier_et_%C3%A9v%C3%AAques.jpg/520px-Chilp%C3%A9ric_Ier_et_%C3%A9v%C3%AAques.jpg 2x" data-file-width="986" data-file-height="986" /></a><figcaption>St Gregory and King <a href="/wiki/Chilperic_I" title="Chilperic I">Chilperic I</a>, from the <i><a href="/wiki/Grandes_Chroniques_de_France" title="Grandes Chroniques de France">Grandes Chroniques de France de Charles V</a></i>, 14th-century illumination.</figcaption></figure>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Hagiographies">Hagiographies</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=17" title="Edit section: Hagiographies">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1097763485"><table class="box-Unreferenced_section plainlinks metadata ambox ambox-content ambox-Unreferenced" role="presentation"><tbody><tr><td class="mbox-image"><div class="mbox-image-div"><span typeof="mw:File"><a href="/wiki/File:Question_book-new.svg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/50px-Question_book-new.svg.png" decoding="async" width="50" height="39" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/75px-Question_book-new.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/100px-Question_book-new.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="512" data-file-height="399" /></a></span></div></td><td class="mbox-text"><div class="mbox-text-span">This section <b>does not <a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources" title="Wikipedia:Citing sources">cite</a> any <a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability" title="Wikipedia:Verifiability">sources</a></b>.<span class="hide-when-compact"> Please help <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit">improve this section</a> by <a href="/wiki/Help:Referencing_for_beginners" title="Help:Referencing for beginners">adding citations to reliable sources</a>. Unsourced material may be challenged and <a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Burden_of_evidence" title="Wikipedia:Verifiability">removed</a>.</span>  <span class="date-container"><i>(<span class="date">August 2019</span>)</i></span><span class="hide-when-compact"><i> (<small><a href="/wiki/Help:Maintenance_template_removal" title="Help:Maintenance template removal">Learn how and when to remove this template message</a></small>)</i></span></div></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>His <i>Life of the Fathers</i> comprises twenty hagiographies of the most prominent men of the preceding generation, taking in a wide range the spiritual community of early medieval Gaul, including lives of bishops, clerics, monks, abbots, holy men and hermits. <a href="/wiki/Illidius" title="Illidius">St. Illidius</a> is praised for his purity of heart, <a href="/wiki/Bracchio" title="Bracchio">St. Brachio</a> the abbot for his discipline and determination in study of the scriptures, <a href="/wiki/Patroclus_of_Bourges" title="Patroclus of Bourges">St Patroclus</a> for his unwavering faith in the face of weakness, and <a href="/wiki/Nicetius_of_Lyon" title="Nicetius of Lyon">St. Nicetius</a>, <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Lyon" title="Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Lyon">bishop of Lyon</a>, for his justice. It is the life of <a href="/wiki/Nicetius_of_Trier" class="mw-redirect" title="Nicetius of Trier">St. Nicetius of Trier</a>, though, which dominates this book; his great authority and sense of episcopal responsibility which is the focus of Gregory's account as his figure, predestined to be great, bestrides the lives of the others. It is told that he felt a weight on his head, but he was unable to see what it was when turning around, though upon smelling its sweet scent he realised that it was the weight of episcopal responsibility. (<i>Life of the Fathers</i>, XVII, 1) He surmounts the others in the glory of his miracles and was chosen by <a href="/wiki/God" title="God">God</a> to have the entire succession of past and future Frankish kings revealed to him.
</p><p>A further aspect of this work to note is the appearance of Gregory himself in certain sections, notably in the life of St. Leobardus. This is for two reasons: Firstly, it creates a distinct link between the temporal and the spiritual worlds, firmly placing the accounts of the lives in a world which is understandable and recognisable, or, seen from the other angle, confirming the presence of miracles in the temporal world.
</p><p>In 587, Gregory began writing the <i>Book of the Glories of the Martyrs</i> (<i>Liber in gloria martyrum</i>), which deals "almost exclusively with the miracles wrought in Gaul by the martyrs of the Roman persecutions."<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> But it also tells the story of one Theodore who made a pilgrimage to India and reported the existence of a large monastery where the body of <a href="/wiki/Thomas_the_Apostle" title="Thomas the Apostle">Thomas the Apostle</a> was first interred and where miracles took place.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Fighting_heresy">Fighting heresy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=18" title="Edit section: Fighting heresy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1097763485"><table class="box-Unreferenced_section plainlinks metadata ambox ambox-content ambox-Unreferenced" role="presentation"><tbody><tr><td class="mbox-image"><div class="mbox-image-div"><span typeof="mw:File"><a href="/wiki/File:Question_book-new.svg" class="mw-file-description"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/50px-Question_book-new.svg.png" decoding="async" width="50" height="39" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/75px-Question_book-new.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/9/99/Question_book-new.svg/100px-Question_book-new.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="512" data-file-height="399" /></a></span></div></td><td class="mbox-text"><div class="mbox-text-span">This section <b>does not <a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources" title="Wikipedia:Citing sources">cite</a> any <a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability" title="Wikipedia:Verifiability">sources</a></b>.<span class="hide-when-compact"> Please help <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit">improve this section</a> by <a href="/wiki/Help:Referencing_for_beginners" title="Help:Referencing for beginners">adding citations to reliable sources</a>. Unsourced material may be challenged and <a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Burden_of_evidence" title="Wikipedia:Verifiability">removed</a>.</span>  <span class="date-container"><i>(<span class="date">August 2019</span>)</i></span><span class="hide-when-compact"><i> (<small><a href="/wiki/Help:Maintenance_template_removal" title="Help:Maintenance template removal">Learn how and when to remove this template message</a></small>)</i></span></div></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Gregory's avowed aim in writing this book was to "fire others with that enthusiasm by which the saints deservedly climbed to heaven", though this was not his sole purpose, and he most surely did not expect his entire audience to show promise of such piety as to witness the power of God flowing through them in the way that it did for the fathers. More immediate concerns were at the forefront of his mind as he sought to create a further layer of religious commitment, not only to the Church at Rome, but also to local churches and cathedrals throughout Gaul. Along with his other books (notably the <i>Glory of the Confessors</i>, the <i>Glory of the Martyrs,</i> and the <i>Life of St. Martin)</i>, meticulous attention is paid to the local as opposed to the universal Christian experience. Within these grandiloquent lives are tales and anecdotes which tie miracles, saints, and their relics to a great diversity of local areas, furnishing his audience with greater knowledge of their local shrine, and providing them with evidence of the work of God in their immediate vicinity, thus greatly expanding their connection with and understanding of their faith. Attacks on heresy also appear throughout his hagiographies; Arianism is taken to be the common face of heresy across Europe, exposed to great ridicule. Often, the scenes which expose the weaknesses of heresy (<i>Glory of the Martyrs</i>, 79, 80) focus on images of fire and burning, whilst the Catholics are proved right by the protection lavished on them by God.
</p><p>This was of great relevance to Gregory himself as he presided over the important see of Tours, where extensive use was made of the cult of St. Martin in establishing the authority of the bishopric with the congregation and in the context of the Frankish church. Gregory's hagiography was an essential component of this. However, this should not be seen as a selfish grab for power on behalf of the bishops who emerge so triumphantly from the <i>Life of the Fathers</i>, but rather as a bid for hegemony of doctrine and control over the practice of worship, which they believed to be in the best interests of their congregation and the wider church.
</p>
<h3><span id="Gregory.27s_Creed"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Gregory's_Creed">Gregory's Creed</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=19" title="Edit section: Gregory&#039;s Creed">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>As an example of Gregory's zeal in his fight against heresy, the <i>Historia Francorum</i> includes a declaration of faith with which Gregory aimed to prove his orthodoxy with respect to the heresies of his time ("so that my reader may have no doubt that I am Catholic for they are.").<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> The confession is in many phrases, each of which refutes a specific Christian heresy. Thus, Gregory's creed presents, in the negative, a virtual litany of heresies:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>I believe, then, in God the Father omnipotent. I believe in <a href="/wiki/Jesus_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesus Christ">Jesus Christ</a> his only Son, our Lord God, born of the Father, not created. [I believe] that he has always been with the Father, not only since time began but before all time. For the Father could not have been so named unless he had a son; and there could be no son without a father. But as for those who say: "There was a time when he was not," [note: A leading belief of Arian Christology.] I reject them with curses, and call men to witness that they are separated from the church. I believe that the word of the Father by which all things were made was Christ. I believe that this word was made flesh and by its suffering the world was redeemed, and I believe that humanity, not deity, was subject to the suffering. I believe that he rose again on the third day, that he freed sinful man, that he ascended to heaven, that he sits on the right hand of the Father, that he will come to judge the living and the dead. I believe that the holy Spirit proceeded from the Father and the Son, that it is not inferior and is not of later origin, but is God, equal and always coeternal with the Father and the Son, consubstantial in its nature, equal in omnipotence, equally eternal in its essence, and that it has never existed apart from the Father and the Son and is not inferior to the Father and the Son. I believe that this holy Trinity exists with separation of persons, and one person is that of the Father, another that of the Son, another that of the Holy Spirit. And in this Trinity confess that there is one Deity, one power, one essence. I believe that the blessed Mary was a virgin after the birth as she was a virgin before. I believe that the soul is immortal but that nevertheless it has no part in deity. And I faithfully believe all things that were established at Nicæa by the three hundred and eighteen bishops. But as to the end of the world I hold beliefs which I learned from our forefathers, that Antichrist will come first. An Antichrist will first propose circumcision, asserting that he is Christ; next he will place his statue in the temple at Jerusalem to be worshiped, just as we read that the Lord said: "You shall see the abomination of desolation standing in the holy place." But the Lord himself declared that that day is hidden from all men, saying; "But of that day and that hour knoweth no one not even the angels in heaven, neither the Son, but the Father alone." Moreover we shall here make answer to the heretics [note: the Arians] who attack us, asserting that the Son is inferior to the Father since he is ignorant of this day. Let them learn then that Son here is the name applied to the Christian people, of whom God says: "I shall be to them a father and they shall be to me for sons." For if he had spoken these words of the only begotten Son he would never have given the angels first place. For he uses these words: "Not even the angels in heaven nor the Son," showing that he spoke these words not of the only-begotten but of the people of adoption. But our end is Christ himself, who will graciously bestow eternal life on us if we turn to him.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Views_on_wine">Views on wine</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=20" title="Edit section: Views on wine">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Gregory's writings make ample references to wine and vineyards. He argued in his writings that wine drinking was defensible when consumed with proper gratitude towards its Creator, but that wine was problematic when consumed solely for pleasure.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=21" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1097763485"><table class="box-Peacock plainlinks metadata ambox ambox-content ambox-peacock" role="presentation"><tbody><tr><td class="mbox-image"><div class="mbox-image-div"><span typeof="mw:File"><span><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/b/b4/Ambox_important.svg/40px-Ambox_important.svg.png" decoding="async" width="40" height="40" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/b/b4/Ambox_important.svg/60px-Ambox_important.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/b/b4/Ambox_important.svg/80px-Ambox_important.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="40" data-file-height="40" /></span></span></div></td><td class="mbox-text"><div class="mbox-text-span">This section contains wording that <b><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Puffery" title="Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch">promotes the subject in a subjective manner</a> without imparting real information</b>.<span class="hide-when-compact"> Please remove or replace such wording and instead of making proclamations about a subject's importance, use facts and attribution to demonstrate that importance.</span>  <span class="date-container"><i>(<span class="date">April 2020</span>)</i></span><span class="hide-when-compact"><i> (<small><a href="/wiki/Help:Maintenance_template_removal" title="Help:Maintenance template removal">Learn how and when to remove this template message</a></small>)</i></span></div></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The <i>Historia Francorum</i> is a central source for early Frankish history, representing the period of transition from late Roman antiquity to early Medieval times in a nascent Europe. It is believed to be the only reliable source of information to describe the emerging military and political power of the Franks in one kingdom.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gregory has often been compared to <a href="/wiki/Herodotus" title="Herodotus">Herodotus</a>;<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> with his detailed interest in, and accounts of, ecclesiastical history and maneuverings, to a bloodier <a href="/wiki/Anthony_Trollope" title="Anthony Trollope">Trollope</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Robert Win came to the conclusion:
</p><p><br />
</p><p>
"There can be no argument that Gregory deliberately structured his narrative to protect himself from any political attacks and that it was the political circumstances around him that governed what he could and could not write.'<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> Gregory's Latin was relatively poor in comparison with earlier centuries when writers were educated at secular Roman grammar and rhetoric schools. He himself is aware of it and apologizes for his poor Latin in his Introduction:</p><blockquote><p>"Ista etenim atque et his similia iugiter intuens dici, pro commemoratione praeteritorum, ut notitiam adtingerint venientum, etsi incultu effatu, nequivi tamen obtegere vel certamena flagitiosorum vel vitam recte viventium; 
</p><p>et praesertim his inlicitus stimulis, quod a nostris fari plerumque miratus sum, quia: 'Philosophantem rethorem intellegunt pauci, loquentem rusticum multi' 
</p><p>('Hearing continually these complaints and others like them I have undertaken to commemorate the past, in order that it may come to the knowledge of the future; and although my speech is rude, I have been unable to be silent as to the struggles between the wicked and the upright; and I have been especially encouraged because, to my surprise, it has often been said by men of our day, that few understand the learned words of the rhetorician but many the rude language of the common people.')
</p><p>
'The 'Historia Francorum' is the only source of that period covering the beginning of the Franks in the decaying Roman Empire from around 397 (the death of Martin of Tours) to 590 (the early reign of king Chlothar II). Gregory's chronology of the Franks is continued with the Fourth Book of Fredegar and its continuations for the events up to 642. Likewise, the fourth Book of Fredegar and its continuations is the only source of any significance for much of the period it covers.</p></blockquote><p>Gregory's hagiographies are also a valuable source of anecdotes and stories which enrich modern understanding of life and belief in Merovingian Gaul. The motivation behind his works was to show readers the importance and strength of Christianity, and this bias should always be remembered. Alongside the most outstanding poet <a href="/wiki/Venantius_Fortunatus" title="Venantius Fortunatus">Venantius Fortunatus</a> in his lifetime, Gregory of Tours is the unique historian from the 6th-century Merovingian world;<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> and his extensive literary output is itself a testimony to the preservation of learning and to the lingering continuity of Gallo-Roman civic culture through the <a href="/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages" title="Early Middle Ages">early Middle Ages</a>.
</p><h2><span class="mw-headline" id="See_also">See also</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=22" title="Edit section: See also">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
