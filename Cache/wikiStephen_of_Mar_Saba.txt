<p><b>Saint Stephen the Sabaite</b> (725 – 796 or 807), also known as <b>Stephen the Hymnographer</b>, was a <a href="/wiki/Christian_monk" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian monk">Christian monk</a> from <a href="/wiki/Julis" title="Julis">Julis</a>, a district of <a href="/wiki/Gaza_City" title="Gaza City">Gaza</a>. He was  a nephew of <a href="/wiki/St._John_of_Damascus" class="mw-redirect" title="St. John of Damascus">St. John of Damascus</a><sup id="cite_ref-LT_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-LT-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and spent a half-century in the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> of <a href="/wiki/Mar_Saba" title="Mar Saba">Mar Saba</a>. He is <a href="/wiki/Venerated" class="mw-redirect" title="Venerated">venerated</a> as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>.
</p><p>Stephen lived the <a href="/wiki/Ascetic" class="mw-redirect" title="Ascetic">ascetic</a> life at the <a href="/wiki/Lavra_of_Saint_Sabas" class="mw-redirect" title="Lavra of Saint Sabas">Lavra of Saint Sabas</a> in <a href="/wiki/Palestine_(region)" title="Palestine (region)">Palestine</a>. 
Stephen was introduced to the monastic life by his uncle, and, at the age of ten, entered the same monastic community as his uncle, St. <a href="/wiki/John_Damascene" class="mw-redirect" title="John Damascene">John Damascene</a>. By his mid-twenties, he felt so drawn to a life of seclusion and <a href="/wiki/Contemplation" title="Contemplation">contemplation</a>, he asked the <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> of the community for permission to live as a <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a>. Due to the great skill in giving <a href="/wiki/Spiritual_direction" title="Spiritual direction">spiritual direction</a> he already showed at that young age, the abbot gave him limited permission. The condition was that he make himself available to others on weekends.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Towards the end of his life, Stephen reported that various cities, <a href="/wiki/Gaza_City" title="Gaza City">Gaza</a> among them, were laid waste to and depopulated by the <a href="/wiki/Saracens" class="mw-redirect" title="Saracens">Saracens</a> (another name for the Muslim <a href="/wiki/Caliphate" title="Caliphate">Caliphate</a> under the rule of the <a href="/wiki/Umayyad" class="mw-redirect" title="Umayyad">Umayyad</a> and <a href="/wiki/Abbasid" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbasid">Abbasid</a> dynasties, referenced in Acta martyrum Sabaitarum, AASS Mart. III, p.&#160;167). On this occasion many monks of St. Sabas met their deaths. 
</p><p>He and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Andrew_the_Blind&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Andrew the Blind (page does not exist)">Andrew the Blind</a> were among the first to compose hymns (<a href="/wiki/Idiomelon" title="Idiomelon">idiomela</a>) in the <a href="/wiki/Triodion" title="Triodion">Triodion</a> (the <a href="/wiki/Liturgical_book" title="Liturgical book">liturgical book</a> used during <a href="/wiki/Great_Lent" title="Great Lent">Great Lent</a>), chanted during the period between the <a href="/wiki/Great_Lent#Publican_and_Pharisee" title="Great Lent">Sunday of the Publican and Pharisee</a> and <a href="/wiki/Palm_Sunday" title="Palm Sunday">Palm Sunday</a>. These idiomela are <a href="/wiki/Stichera" class="mw-redirect" title="Stichera">stichera</a> of which two were written for each weekday of Great Lent. One is chanted at the <a href="/wiki/Aposticha" title="Aposticha">aposticha</a> of <a href="/wiki/Vespers" title="Vespers">Vespers</a> and one at the aposticha of <a href="/wiki/Matins" title="Matins">Matins</a>, each being chanted twice. The idiomela are "exceptionally rich in <a href="/wiki/Doctrine" title="Doctrine">doctrinal</a> content, summing up the whole <a href="/wiki/Theology" title="Theology">theology</a> of the Great Fast".<sup id="cite_ref-LT_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-LT-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The events of the time are recorded in the writings of <a href="/wiki/Leontius_of_Damascus" title="Leontius of Damascus">Leontius of Damascus</a> in his book <i>The Life of St. Stephen the Sabaite</i>.
</p><p>His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is celebrated on <a href="/wiki/October_28_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics)" title="October 28 (Eastern Orthodox liturgics)">October 28</a> on the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_liturgical_calendar" title="Eastern Orthodox liturgical calendar">Eastern Orthodox liturgical calendar</a> (for those Eastern Orthodox Churches which follow the traditional <a href="/wiki/Julian_Calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Julian Calendar">Julian Calendar</a>, October 28 falls on November 10 of the modern <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_Calendar" class="mw-redirect" title="Gregorian Calendar">Gregorian Calendar</a>).
</p><p>In the past, Stephen the Sabaite was sometimes identified with the author known as <a href="/wiki/Stephen_of_Thebes" title="Stephen of Thebes">Stephen of Thebes</a>, but this writer must have lived several centuries earlier.
</p>
