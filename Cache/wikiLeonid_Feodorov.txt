<p><b>Leonid Ivanovich Feodorov</b> (<a href="/wiki/Russian_language" title="Russian language">Russian</a>: <span lang="ru">Леонид Иванович Фёдоров</span>; 4 November 1879 – 7 March 1935) was a <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Studite_Monks" title="Ukrainian Studite Monks">Studite</a> <a href="/wiki/Hieromonk" title="Hieromonk">hieromonk</a> from the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Russian Greek Catholic Church">Russian Greek Catholic Church</a>, the first <a href="/wiki/Exarch" title="Exarch">Exarch</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Catholic_Apostolic_Exarchate_of_Russia" title="Russian Catholic Apostolic Exarchate of Russia">Russian Catholic Apostolic Exarchate of Russia</a>, and a survivor of the <a href="/wiki/Gulag" title="Gulag">Gulag</a> at <a href="/wiki/Solovki_prison_camp" title="Solovki prison camp">Solovki prison camp</a>. He was <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> at <a href="/wiki/Lviv" title="Lviv">Lviv</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> on 27 June 2001.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonid_Feodorov&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Feodorov was born in <a href="/wiki/Saint_Petersburg" title="Saint Petersburg">Saint Petersburg</a>, <a href="/wiki/Russian_Empire" title="Russian Empire">Russia</a>, on 4 November 1879, into a <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Russian Orthodox">Russian Orthodox</a> family.<sup id="cite_ref-Siccardi_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Siccardi-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> His father, Ivan Feodorovich Feodorov, was the son of a former <a href="/wiki/State_serf" title="State serf">State serf</a> from <a href="/wiki/Yaroslavl_Governorate" title="Yaroslavl Governorate">Yaroslavl Governorate</a> and had become the owner of a successful St. Petersburg restaurant whose regular patrons included a young <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Stalin" title="Joseph Stalin">Joseph Stalin</a>. 
</p><p>His mother, Lyuba Feodorov, a woman of <a href="/wiki/Greeks_in_Russia" class="mw-redirect" title="Greeks in Russia">Greek descent</a>, raised him as a single mother after his father's early death. Although she attempted to raise her son as a devout member of the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodox Church</a>, she simultaneously encouraged him to read the popular novelists of the day.
</p><p>He later recalled, "So I began to devour the best known French novelists of the day, <a href="/wiki/%C3%89mile_Zola" title="Émile Zola">Zola</a>, <a href="/wiki/Victor_Hugo" title="Victor Hugo">Hugo</a>, <a href="/wiki/Maupassant" class="mw-redirect" title="Maupassant">Maupassant</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Alexandre_Dumas" title="Alexandre Dumas">Dumas</a>. I became acquainted with the <a href="/wiki/Italian_Renaissance" title="Italian Renaissance">Italian Renaissance</a> and its corrupt literature, <a href="/wiki/Boccaccio" class="mw-redirect" title="Boccaccio">Boccaccio</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ariosto" class="mw-redirect" title="Ariosto">Ariosto</a>. My head came to be like a sewer into which the foulest muck was emptied."<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While attending the Second Imperial <a href="/wiki/Gymnasium_(school)" title="Gymnasium (school)">Gymnasium</a> in St. Petersburg, Feodorov considered himself to be both a <a href="/wiki/Buddhist" class="mw-redirect" title="Buddhist">Buddhist</a> and a <a href="/wiki/Russian_nihilist_movement" title="Russian nihilist movement">Nihilist</a>. He often used to quote the maxim, "War is the daughter of God", and intended to become an officer in the <a href="/wiki/Imperial_Russian_Army" title="Imperial Russian Army">Imperial Russian Army</a>. Under the influence of the Gymnasium's Orthodox chaplain and teacher of religion, Fr. <a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyril_(Smirnov)_of_Kazan&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Cyril (Smirnov) of Kazan (page does not exist)">Konstantin (Smirnov)</a>, Feodorov underwent a religious conversion and instead decided to study for the Orthodox priesthood.
</p><p>After his graduation from the Gymnasium in 1901, he enrolled in the <a href="/wiki/Saint_Petersburg_Theological_Academy" title="Saint Petersburg Theological Academy">Saint Petersburg Theological Academy</a> in order to study for the <a href="/wiki/Priesthood" class="mw-redirect" title="Priesthood">priesthood</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodox Church</a>. During a crisis of Faith, Feodorov came to believe, "through the reading of the Father and of history", that the Roman Catholic Church is the one true church. In response, he began discreetly attending the <a href="/wiki/Tridentine_Mass" title="Tridentine Mass">Tridentine Mass</a> at <a href="/wiki/Church_of_St._Catherine_(Saint_Petersburg)" title="Church of St. Catherine (Saint Petersburg)">St. Catherine's Church</a> on <a href="/wiki/Nevsky_Prospect" title="Nevsky Prospect">Nevsky Prospect</a>. At the Ecclesiastical Academy, his teachers took to calling Feodorov, "our Catholic."<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After much soul-searching, Feodorv decided to leave his studies in the summer of 1902 and travel to Rome in order to openly convert to the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>. In order to interrupt his studies and be granted a passport necessary for foreign travel, Feodorov needed the permission of the Academy Inspector, <a href="/wiki/Archimandrite" title="Archimandrite">Archimandrite</a> <a href="/wiki/Theofan_(Bystrov)" title="Theofan (Bystrov)">Theofan (Bystrov)</a>, who responded, "I know very well why you wish to go to Italy... So be it, and may God keep you."<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Fr. Cyril Korolevsky alleges that Archimandrite Theofan, "was quite convinced of the truth of Catholicism, but like a number of others he could not bring himself to take the definite step."<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> In reality, Archimandrite Theofan remained Orthodox even after the <a href="/wiki/October_Revolution" title="October Revolution">October Revolution</a>, when he become a <a href="/wiki/White_emigre" class="mw-redirect" title="White emigre">refugee</a> from in <a href="/wiki/Bulgaria" title="Bulgaria">Bulgaria</a> and <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>.
</p><p>Leonid Feodorov and his Catholic spiritual mentor, Fr. <a href="/w/index.php?title=Jan_Szyslawski&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Jan Szyslawski (page does not exist)">Jan Szyslawski</a>, travelled to Rome by way of <a href="/wiki/Austria" title="Austria">Austrian</a>-ruled <a href="/wiki/Lviv" title="Lviv">Lviv</a>, where <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop" title="Metropolitan bishop">Metropolitan</a> <a href="/wiki/Andrey_Sheptytsky" title="Andrey Sheptytsky">Andrey Sheptytsky</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church">Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church</a> blessed his mission.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Conversion_and_ordination">Conversion and ordination</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonid_Feodorov&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Conversion and ordination">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On 31 July 1902, Feodorov was formally received into the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> at the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Ges%C3%B9" title="Church of the Gesù">Church of the Gesù</a> in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>. In the aftermath, he began studying at the <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a> seminary at <a href="/wiki/Anagni" title="Anagni">Anagni</a> under the <a href="/wiki/Pseudonym" title="Pseudonym">pseudonym</a> of "Leonidas Pierre," which was meant to keep the Tsar's secret police, or <a href="/wiki/Okhrana" title="Okhrana">Okhrana</a>, off his trail.
</p><p>Although Leonid had originally promised to adopt the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Rite" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Rite">Latin Rite</a>, while studying in the Collegio Leoniano, a <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a> <a href="/wiki/Major_seminary" class="mw-redirect" title="Major seminary">major seminary</a> at <a href="/wiki/Anagni" title="Anagni">Anagni</a>, Leonid came to believe that it was his duty to remain faithful to the liturgy and customs of the Christian East. With the full permission and encouragement of Pope <a href="/wiki/Pius_X" class="mw-redirect" title="Pius X">Pius X</a>, Leonid transferred to the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Byzantine_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Russian Byzantine Catholic Church">Russian Byzantine Catholic Church</a>. As a result of his decision, Leonid was disowned by his former Jesuit mentor and afterwards depended for his finances on Metropolitan Andrei Sheptytsky of <a href="/wiki/Lviv" title="Lviv">Lviv</a>.
</p><p>On March 25, 1911, he received ordination as a Byzantine rite priest by Metropolitan <a href="/wiki/Michael_Mirov" title="Michael Mirov">Michael Mirov</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Bulgarian_Greek_Catholic_Church" title="Bulgarian Greek Catholic Church">Bulgarian Greek Catholic Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Arduino_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Arduino-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> He spent the following years as a <a href="/wiki/Studite_Brethren" class="mw-redirect" title="Studite Brethren">Studite</a> <a href="/wiki/Hieromonk" title="Hieromonk">hieromonk</a> in <a href="/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina" title="Bosnia and Herzegovina">Bosnia</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ukraine" title="Ukraine">Ukraine</a> and was tonsured with monastic name 'Leontiy' on 12 March 1913.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Return_to_Russia">Return to Russia</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonid_Feodorov&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Return to Russia">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On the eve of the <a href="/wiki/First_World_War" class="mw-redirect" title="First World War">First World War</a>, he returned to Saint Petersburg whereupon he was immediately exiled to <a href="/wiki/Tobolsk" title="Tobolsk">Tobolsk</a> in <a href="/wiki/Siberia" title="Siberia">Siberia</a> as a potential threat to the <a href="/wiki/Tsar" title="Tsar">Tsar</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Government" title="Government">government</a> which held Russian Orthodoxy as its <a href="/wiki/State_religion" title="State religion">state religion</a>.
</p><p>After the <a href="/wiki/February_Revolution" title="February Revolution">February Revolution</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Provisional_Government" class="mw-redirect" title="Provisional Government">Provisional Government</a> ordered the release of all political prisoners.<sup id="cite_ref-Siccardi_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Siccardi-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> A three-day <a href="/wiki/Synod" title="Synod">Synod</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Byzantine_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Russian Byzantine Catholic Church">Russian Byzantine Catholic Church</a> opened in <a href="/wiki/Saint_Petersburg" title="Saint Petersburg">Saint Petersburg</a> under the <a href="/wiki/Leadership" title="Leadership">leadership</a> of Metropolitan Andrey, who, at the end of the Synod, named Feodorov <a href="/wiki/Exarch" title="Exarch">Exarch</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Catholic_Apostolic_Exarchate_of_Russia" title="Russian Catholic Apostolic Exarchate of Russia">Russian Catholics</a>. Once Fr. <a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyril_Korolevsky&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Cyril Korolevsky (page does not exist)">Cyril Korolevsky</a> successfully proved to the Holy See that Metropolitan Andrey had simply made use of his rights as defined by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_X" title="Pope Pius X">Pope Pius X</a>, Feodorov's position as Exarch was confirmed by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XV" title="Pope Benedict XV">Pope Benedict XV</a>.
</p><p>He served as abbot in the Church of the Descent of the Holy Spirit in Petrograd, under his leadership the women's order of the Holy Family, the Community of Sisters of the Holy Spirit, and the Society of John Chrysostom were founded. He made presentations, participated in disputes with Orthodox clergy.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Cieplak_Trial">The Cieplak Trial</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonid_Feodorov&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: The Cieplak Trial">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Open persecution of <a href="/wiki/Religion" title="Religion">religion</a> began in 1922. The <a href="/wiki/Clergy" title="Clergy">clergy</a> were forbidden to preach religion to anyone under eighteen years of age. Then, all sacred objects were ordered to be seized for "famine relief" and lay councils called <i>dvatsatkii</i> were installed in each parish by the <a href="/wiki/State_Political_Directorate" title="State Political Directorate">GPU</a> with the intention of making the priest a mere employee. When both the Exarch Leonid and the Latin Rite Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Jan_Cieplak" title="Jan Cieplak">Jan Cieplak</a> refused to permit this, all Catholic parishes were forcibly closed by the State.
</p><p>In the spring of 1923, Exarch Leonid, Archbishop Cieplak, Monsignor <a href="/wiki/Konstanty_Budkiewicz" class="mw-redirect" title="Konstanty Budkiewicz">Konstanty Budkiewicz</a>, and fourteen other Catholic priests and one layman were summoned to <a href="/wiki/Moscow" title="Moscow">Moscow</a> trial before the revolutionary tribunal for <a href="/wiki/Counterrevolution" class="mw-redirect" title="Counterrevolution">counter-revolutionary activities</a>.
</p><p>According to Father Christopher L. Zugger,
</p>
<blockquote><p>The Bolsheviks had already orchestrated several '<a href="/wiki/Show_trial" title="Show trial">show trials</a>.' The <a href="/wiki/Cheka" title="Cheka">Cheka</a> had staged the 'Trial of the St. Petersburg Combat Organization'; its successor, the new <a href="/wiki/GPU" class="mw-redirect" title="GPU">GPU</a>, the '<a href="/wiki/Trial_of_the_Socialist_Revolutionaries" title="Trial of the Socialist Revolutionaries">Trial of the Socialist Revolutionaries</a>.' In these and other such farces, defendants were inevitably sentenced to death or to long prison terms in the north. The Cieplak show trial is a prime example of Bolshevik revolutionary justice at this time. Normal judicial procedures did not restrict revolutionary tribunals at all; in fact, the <a href="/wiki/Prosecutor" title="Prosecutor">prosecutor</a> <a href="/wiki/Nikolai_Krylenko" title="Nikolai Krylenko">N.V. Krylenko</a>, stated that the courts could trample upon the rights of classes other than the <a href="/wiki/Proletariat" title="Proletariat">proletariat</a>. Appeals from the courts went not to a higher court, but to political committees. Western observers found the setting -- the grand ballroom of a former Noblemen's Club, with painted <a href="/wiki/Cherubs" class="mw-redirect" title="Cherubs">cherubs</a> on the ceiling -- singularly inappropriate for such a solemn event. Neither judges nor prosecutors were required to have a legal background, only a proper 'revolutionary' one. That the prominent 'No Smoking' signs were ignored by the judges themselves did not bode well for legalities."<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p><a href="/wiki/New_York_Herald" title="New York Herald">New York Herald</a> correspondent Francis McCullagh, who was present at the trial, later described its fourth day as follows:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>Krylenko, who began to speak at 6:10 PM, was moderate enough at first, but quickly launched into an attack on religion in general and the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> in particular. "The Catholic Church", he declared, "has always exploited the working classes." When he demanded the Archbishop's death, he said, "All the <a href="/wiki/Jesuitical" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuitical">Jesuitical</a> duplicity with which you have defended yourself will not save you from the <a href="/wiki/Death_penalty" class="mw-redirect" title="Death penalty">death penalty</a>. No Pope in the Vatican can save you now." ...As the long oration proceeded, the Red Procurator worked himself into a fury of anti-religious hatred. "Your religion", he yelled, "I spit on it, as I do on all religions, -- on <a href="/wiki/Orthodox_Christianity" class="mw-redirect" title="Orthodox Christianity">Orthodox</a>, <a href="/wiki/Judaism" title="Judaism">Jewish</a>, <a href="/wiki/Islam" title="Islam">Mohammedan</a>, and the rest." "There is no law here but <a href="/wiki/Soviet_Law" class="mw-redirect" title="Soviet Law">Soviet Law</a>," he yelled at another stage, "and by that law you must die."<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Unlike the other defendants, Exarch Leonid insisted on acting as his own attorney, which led to some of the most dramatic moments of the trial. According to Father Zugger,
</p>
<blockquote><p>Dressed in the traditional Russian black <a href="/wiki/Cassock" title="Cassock">cassock</a>, with his long hair a beard often described as 'Christ-like', Feodorov was a man of the <i>narod</i>, of the ordinary Russian people for whom the Revolution had been fought. His presence put the lie to the usual description of Catholicism as 'the Polish religion.' His presentation -- a moving testimony of Russian spirituality and the history of the Church in that country -- evoked the best of Russian <a href="/wiki/Christendom" title="Christendom">Christendom</a>. He pointed out that Greek-Catholics greeted the Revolution with joy, for only then did they have equality. There was no secret organization, they had simply followed Church law. Religious education, the celebration of Mass, and the administration of the Sacraments of marriage and baptism had to be fulfilled. He pointed out that the Church, accused of having neglected the starving, was at that moment feeding 120,000 children daily. Following a scathing rebuttal by Krylenko, Exarch Feodorov rose for his final remarks: "Our hearts are full, not of hatred, but of sadness. You cannot understand us, we are not allowed liberty of conscience. That is the only conclusion we can draw from what we have heard here."<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>With the verdict and sentences already decided upon in advance, Archbishop Cieplak and Monsignor Budkiewicz were both sentenced to death. Exarch Leonid and all the other defendants were sentenced to the term of ten years imprisonment.<sup id="cite_ref-Arduino_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Arduino-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Gulag">The Gulag</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonid_Feodorov&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: The Gulag">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The international uproar which followed the trial gave the Soviet government pause, however. In 1926,<sup id="cite_ref-:0_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> after serving the first three years of his sentence in Moscow's <a href="/wiki/Butyrka_prison" title="Butyrka prison">Butyrka prison</a>, Exarch Leonid was transported to <a href="/wiki/Solovki_prison_camp" title="Solovki prison camp">Solovki prison camp</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Siccardi_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Siccardi-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> located in a former island monastery in the <a href="/wiki/White_Sea" title="White Sea">White Sea</a>.
</p><p>He was a pioneer of ecumenism together with the Orthodox with whom he shared the harsh captivity. In <a href="/wiki/Solovetsky_Monastery" title="Solovetsky Monastery">Solovki</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Tridentine_Mass" title="Tridentine Mass">Tridentine Mass</a> was offered in a <a href="/wiki/Chapel" title="Chapel">chapel</a> which had been restored for the purpose with the permission of the guards. Exarch Leonid would offer the <a href="/wiki/Divine_Liturgy" title="Divine Liturgy">Divine Liturgy</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Byzantine_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Russian Byzantine Catholic Church">Russian Byzantine Catholic Church</a> every other Sunday. When the camp authorities cracked down on this in 1929, the Masses and Divine Liturgies continued in secret.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Release_and_death">Release and death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonid_Feodorov&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Release and death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On 6 August 1929, Exarch Leonid was released to the town of <a href="/wiki/Pinega" title="Pinega">Pinega</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Arkhangelsk_Oblast" title="Arkhangelsk Oblast">Arkhangelsk Oblast</a> and put to work making charcoal. After continuing to teach the <a href="/wiki/Catechism" title="Catechism">Catechism</a> to young boys, he was transferred to the village of Poltava, 15&#160;km from <a href="/wiki/Kotlas" title="Kotlas">Kotlas</a> (not to be confused with the city of <a href="/wiki/Poltava" title="Poltava">Poltava</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ukraine" title="Ukraine">Ukraine</a>), where he completed his sentence in 1932. He chose to reside in <a href="/wiki/Kirov,_Kirov_Oblast" title="Kirov, Kirov Oblast">Kirov, Kirov Oblast</a>,  where, worn out by the rigours of his imprisonment, he died on 7 March 1935.<sup id="cite_ref-Arduino_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Arduino-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Leonid_Feodorov&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On 27 June 2001, Exarch Leonid Feodorov was <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> by Pope John Paul II. He remains deeply venerated among Russian Catholics.
</p><p>Metropolitan <a href="/wiki/Andrey_Sheptytsky" title="Andrey Sheptytsky">Andrey Sheptytsky</a> of the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church said, "We expect that the exarch [Leonid Feodoriv] is on the road to glorification through beatification. Of course, it is much too early to talk about this, but all of us were strongly impressed by his holiness, strengthened by the crown of martyrdom and death; this certainly supports our expectations. On the other hand, as a Russian Catholic, as exarch, as someone who died at the hands of the Bolsheviks, it seems to us that he will be right in the center of attention of the entire Church."<sup id="cite_ref-:0_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
