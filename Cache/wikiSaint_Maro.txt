<p><b>Maron</b>, also called <b>Maroun</b> or <b>Maro</b> (<a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a>: <span lang="syr" dir="rtl">ܡܪܘܢ</span>, <i><span title="Classical Syriac-language romanization"><i lang="syc-Latn">Mārūn</i></span></i>; <a href="/wiki/Arabic_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Arabic language">Arabic</a>: <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">مَارُون</span>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Maron</i>; <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Μάρων</span></span>), was a 4th-century <a href="/wiki/Syrians" title="Syrians">Syrian</a><sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Syriac_Christianity" title="Syriac Christianity">Syriac Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a> <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Taurus_Mountains" title="Taurus Mountains">Taurus Mountains</a> whose followers, after his death, founded a religious Christian movement that became known as the <a href="/wiki/Syriac_Maronite_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Syriac Maronite Church">Syriac Maronite Church</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Full_communion" title="Full communion">full communion</a> with the <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Holy See</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> The religious community which grew from this movement are the modern <a href="/wiki/Maronites" title="Maronites">Maronites</a>.
</p><p>Saint Maron is often portrayed in a black <a href="/wiki/Religious_habit" title="Religious habit">monastic habit</a> with a hanging <a href="/wiki/Stole_(vestment)" title="Stole (vestment)">stole</a>, accompanied by a long <a href="/wiki/Crosier" title="Crosier">crosier</a> staffed by a globe surmounted with a cross. His feast day in the Maronite Church is February 9.<sup id="cite_ref-icsc_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-icsc-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-St.Elias_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-St.Elias-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Maron&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Maron, born in what is now modern Syria, in the middle of the 4th century, was a priest who later became a hermit, retiring to the <a href="/wiki/Taurus_Mountains" title="Taurus Mountains">Taurus Mountains</a> in the region of <a href="/wiki/Cyrrhus" title="Cyrrhus">Cyrrhus</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Antioch" title="Antioch">Antioch</a>. His holiness and miracles attracted many followers, and drew attention throughout the empire. <a href="/wiki/John_Chrysostom" title="John Chrysostom">John Chrysostom</a> wrote to him around AD 405 expressing his great love and respect, and asking Maron to pray for him.<sup id="cite_ref-St.Elias_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-St.Elias-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Maron and Chrysostom are believed to have studied together in the great Christian learning center at <a href="/wiki/Antioch" title="Antioch">Antioch</a>, which at the time was the third largest city in the Roman Empire.<sup id="cite_ref-icsc_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-icsc-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Maron embraced a life of quiet solitude in the mountains north-west of Aleppo.<sup id="cite_ref-Gervers_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Gervers-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> He was known for his simplicity and his extraordinary desire to discover God's presence in all things.<sup id="cite_ref-icsc_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-icsc-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Maron is considered the Father of the spiritual and monastic movement now called the <a href="/wiki/Maronite_Church" title="Maronite Church">Maronite Church</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-St.Elias_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-St.Elias-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastic_spirituality">Monastic spirituality</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Maron&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Monastic spirituality">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Maron's way was deeply monastic with emphasis on the spiritual and ascetic aspects of living. For Maron, all was connected to God and God was connected to all. He did not separate the physical and spiritual world and actually used the physical world to deepen his faith and spiritual experience with God.<sup id="cite_ref-St.Elias_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-St.Elias-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was able to free himself from the physical world by his passion and fervour for prayer and enter into a mystical relationship of love with God.
</p><p>He lived his life in the open air next to a temple he had transformed to a church. He spent his time in prayer and meditation exposed to the forces of nature such as sun, rain, hail and snow. <a href="/wiki/Theodoret" title="Theodoret">Theodoret of Cyrrhus</a> wrote that this was a new type of <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a> that soon enjoyed wide acceptance in Syria and <a href="/wiki/Lebanon" title="Lebanon">Lebanon</a>. His <i>Religious History</i>, written around AD 440, mentions fifteen men and three women who followed this practice, many of them trained or guided by Maron.<sup id="cite_ref-Gervers_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Gervers-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Missionary">Missionary</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Maron&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Missionary">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St._Maron.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/St._Maron.jpg/220px-St._Maron.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="280" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/St._Maron.jpg/330px-St._Maron.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/St._Maron.jpg/440px-St._Maron.jpg 2x" data-file-width="673" data-file-height="858" /></a><figcaption>Russian Orthodox icon of Saint Maron</figcaption></figure>
<p>Saint Maron was a mystic who started this new ascetic-spiritual method that attracted many people in Syria and Lebanon to become his disciples. Accompanying his deeply spiritual and ascetic life, he was a zealous missionary with a passion to spread the message of Christ by preaching it to all he met. He sought not only to cure the physical ailments that people suffered, but had a great quest for nurturing and healing the "lost souls" of both non-Christians and Christians of his time.
</p><p>This missionary work came to fruition when in the mountains of Syria, Saint Maron was able to convert a temple into a Christian church in <a href="/w/index.php?title=Kafr_Nabu&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Kafr Nabu (page does not exist)">Kafr Nabu</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-icsc_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-icsc-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> This was to be the beginning of the conversion to Christianity in Syria which would then influence and spread to Lebanon. After his death in the year 410 in <a href="/w/index.php?title=Kalota,_Syria&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Kalota, Syria (page does not exist)">Kalota</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> his spirit and teachings lived on through his disciples.
</p><p>His burial place is a debated issue. Some Lebanese sources, such as <a href="/wiki/Giuseppe_Simone_Assemani" title="Giuseppe Simone Assemani">Giuseppe Simone Assemani</a> and Maronite bishop Yusef al-Dibs believed he was buried in Arethusa or modern-day <a href="/wiki/Al-Rastan" title="Al-Rastan">al-Rastan</a> along the <a href="/wiki/Orontes_River" title="Orontes River">Orontes River</a> in Syria, while others, like <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a> priest <a href="/wiki/Henri_Lammens" title="Henri Lammens">Henri Lammens</a>, have claimed he is buried in <a href="/wiki/Barad,_Syria" title="Barad, Syria">Brad</a> village to the north of <a href="/wiki/Aleppo" title="Aleppo">Aleppo</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Maronite movement reached Lebanon when Saint Maron's first disciple, <a href="/wiki/Abraham_of_Cyrrhus" title="Abraham of Cyrrhus">Abraham of Cyrrhus</a>, who was called the Apostle of Lebanon, realized that there were many non-Christians in Lebanon and so he set out to convert them to Christianity by introducing them to the way of Saint Maron.<sup id="cite_ref-Gervers_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Gervers-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/William_of_Tyre" title="William of Tyre">William of Tyre</a>, chronicling his arrival in the region of Lebanon during the crusades, writes of the Maronites that they took their name from a certain Maro, whose heresies (described as <a href="/wiki/Monothelitism" title="Monothelitism">monothelitism</a>) they followed for "almost five hundred years", but which they recanted at the time of William's report. Though William of Tyre's indictment of "Maro and his followers" as monothelite heretics has resulted in controversy among scholars, in all probability he was mistakenly referring to a Maro from Edessa instead of the fourth-century St. Maro.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Maronite historians argue that they have always remained in full communion with Rome.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Saint Maron's <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is celebrated on February 9.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Maron&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Saint Maron was known for his gift for healing.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Notable_recognitions">Notable recognitions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Maron&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Notable recognitions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On 23 February 2011, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a> unveiled a statue of Saint Maron on the outer wall of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Peter%27s_Basilica" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Peter&#39;s Basilica">Saint Peter's Basilica</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Vatican</a> and imparted his <a href="/wiki/Apostolic_Blessing" class="mw-redirect" title="Apostolic Blessing">Apostolic Blessing</a>. The 15 feet tall statue was commissioned by the Maronite Church to the Spanish sculptor <a href="/wiki/Marco_Augusto_Due%C3%B1as" title="Marco Augusto Dueñas">Marco Augusto Dueñas</a>. The saint appears in the sculpture holding a miniature, Maronite style church; the sculpture also features an inscription in Syriac reading: <i>The righteous will flourish like a palm tree, they will grow like a <a href="/wiki/Cedars_of_God" title="Cedars of God">cedar of Lebanon</a></i>.  The statue occupied the last available <a href="/wiki/Niche_(architecture)" title="Niche (architecture)">niche</a> in the outer perimeter of Saint Peter's Basilica.<sup id="cite_ref-cath_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cath-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In June 2012, an impressionist painting of Saint Maron, as well as several icons based on images from the 5th-century Syriac <i>Rabboula</i> manuscript including the <a href="/wiki/Crucifixion" title="Crucifixion">Crucifixion</a>, the Marian icon of the "Mother of Light" and the Evangelists, was donated, installed and was solemnly attended by <a href="/wiki/Cardinalate" class="mw-redirect" title="Cardinalate">Cardinal</a> <a href="/wiki/Donald_Wuerl" title="Donald Wuerl">Donald Wuerl</a> at the <a href="/wiki/National_Shrine_of_the_Immaculate_Conception" class="mw-redirect" title="National Shrine of the Immaculate Conception">National Shrine of the Immaculate Conception</a> in <a href="/wiki/Washington_D.C." class="mw-redirect" title="Washington D.C.">Washington D.C.</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> and was formally dedicated on September 23, 2012.
</p>
