<p><b>Denis of Paris</b> was a 3rd-century <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyr" title="Christian martyr">Christian martyr</a> and <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>. According to his <a href="/wiki/Hagiographies" class="mw-redirect" title="Hagiographies">hagiographies</a>, he was <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Paris" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Paris">bishop of Paris</a> (then <a href="/wiki/Lutetia" title="Lutetia">Lutetia</a>) in the third century and, together with his companions Rusticus and Eleutherius, was martyred for his faith by <a href="/wiki/Decapitation" title="Decapitation">decapitation</a>. Some accounts placed this during <a href="/wiki/Domitian" title="Domitian">Domitian</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Domitianic_Persecution" class="mw-redirect" title="Domitianic Persecution">persecution</a> and incorrectly identified St<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>Denis of Paris with the <a href="/wiki/Dionysius_the_Areopagite" title="Dionysius the Areopagite">Areopagite</a> who was converted by <a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle" title="Paul the Apostle">Paul the Apostle</a> and who served as the first <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Athens" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Athens">bishop of Athens</a>. Assuming Denis's historicity, it is now considered more likely that he suffered under the <a href="/wiki/Decian_persecution" title="Decian persecution">persecution</a> of the <a href="/wiki/List_of_Roman_emperors" title="List of Roman emperors">emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Decius" title="Decius">Decius</a> shortly after AD<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>250.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Denis is the most famous <a href="/wiki/Cephalophore" title="Cephalophore">cephalophore</a> in Christian history, with a popular story claiming that the decapitated bishop picked up his head and walked several miles while preaching a sermon on repentance. He is <a href="/wiki/Veneration_of_saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Veneration of saints">venerated</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Catholicism_in_France" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholicism in France">Catholic Church</a> as the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of France and Paris and is accounted one of the <a href="/wiki/Fourteen_Holy_Helpers" title="Fourteen Holy Helpers">Fourteen Holy Helpers</a>. A chapel was raised at the site of his burial by a local Christian woman; it was later expanded into an <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Denis" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of Saint Denis">abbey</a> and <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint_Denis" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica of Saint Denis">basilica</a>, around which grew up the French city of <a href="/wiki/Saint-Denis,_Seine-Saint-Denis" title="Saint-Denis, Seine-Saint-Denis">Saint-Denis</a>, now a suburb of Paris.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Name">Name</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Denis_of_Paris&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Name">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The medieval and modern <a href="/wiki/French_name" title="French name">French</a> <a href="/wiki/Masculine_name" class="mw-redirect" title="Masculine name">masculine</a> <a href="/wiki/Given_name" title="Given name">given name</a> Denis derives from the <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> name <a href="/wiki/Dionysius" title="Dionysius">Dionysius</a> from <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greek" title="Ancient Greek">Greek</a> <a href="/wiki/Dionysios" class="mw-redirect" title="Dionysios">Dionysios</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Denis_of_Paris&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Basilique_Saint-Denis_portail_nord_tympan.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Basilique_Saint-Denis_portail_nord_tympan.jpg/220px-Basilique_Saint-Denis_portail_nord_tympan.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="156" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Basilique_Saint-Denis_portail_nord_tympan.jpg/330px-Basilique_Saint-Denis_portail_nord_tympan.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Basilique_Saint-Denis_portail_nord_tympan.jpg/440px-Basilique_Saint-Denis_portail_nord_tympan.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3653" data-file-height="2593" /></a><figcaption>Beheading of Denis and of his companions, tympanum of the north portal of the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint-Denis" title="Basilica of Saint-Denis">Basilica of Saint-Denis</a></figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Tours" title="Gregory of Tours">Gregory of Tours</a><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> states that Denis was bishop of the <a href="/wiki/Parisii_(France)" class="mw-redirect" title="Parisii (France)">Parisii</a> and was martyred by being beheaded by a sword. The earliest document giving an account of his life and martyrdom, the <i>"Passio SS. Dionysii Rustici et Eleutherii"</i>, dates from c. 600, is mistakenly attributed to the poet <a href="/wiki/Venantius_Fortunatus" title="Venantius Fortunatus">Venantius Fortunatus</a>, and is legendary. Nevertheless, it appears from the <i>Passio</i> that Denis was sent from <a href="/wiki/Roman_Italy" title="Roman Italy">Italia</a> to convert <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a> in the third century, forging a link with the "apostles to the Gauls" reputed to have been sent out with six other missionary bishops under the direction of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Fabian" title="Pope Fabian">Pope Fabian</a>. There Denis was appointed first Bishop of Paris.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The persecutions under <a href="/wiki/Decius" title="Decius">Emperor Decius</a> had all but dissolved the small Christian community at <a href="/wiki/Lutetia" title="Lutetia">Lutetia</a> (Paris).<sup id="cite_ref-cathen_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathen-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Denis, with his inseparable companions Rusticus and Eleutherius, who were martyred with him, settled on the <a href="/wiki/%C3%8Ele_de_la_Cit%C3%A9" title="Île de la Cité">Île de la Cité</a> in the <a href="/wiki/River_Seine" class="mw-redirect" title="River Seine">River Seine</a>. <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Rome" title="Ancient Rome">Roman</a> <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a> lay on the higher ground of the <a href="/wiki/Rive_Gauche" title="Rive Gauche">Left Bank</a>, away from the river.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Martyrdom">Martyrdom</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Denis_of_Paris&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Martyrdom">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Denis and his companions were so effective in converting people that the <a href="/wiki/Pagan" class="mw-redirect" title="Pagan">pagan</a> priests became alarmed over their loss of followers. At their instigation, the Roman Governor arrested the missionaries. After a long imprisonment, Denis and two of his clergy were executed by beheading<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> on the highest hill in Paris (now <a href="/wiki/Montmartre" title="Montmartre">Montmartre</a>), which was likely to have been a <a href="/wiki/Druid" title="Druid">druidic</a> holy place. The martyrdom of Denis and his companions is popularly believed to have given the site its current name, derived from the Latin <i>Mons Martyrum</i> <i>"The Martyrs' Mountain"</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-softheday_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-softheday-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> although the name is possibly derived from <i>Mons Mercurii et Mons Martis</i>, <i>Hill of Mercury and Mars</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-br_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-br-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> After his head was cut off, Denis is said to have picked it up and walked several miles from the summit of the hill, preaching a <a href="/wiki/Sermon" title="Sermon">sermon</a> the entire way, making him one of many <a href="/wiki/Cephalophore" title="Cephalophore">cephalophores</a> in hagiology. Of the many accounts of this martyrdom, this is noted in detail in the <i><a href="/wiki/Golden_Legend" title="Golden Legend">Golden Legend</a></i> and in Butler's <i>Lives Of The Saints</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The site where he stopped preaching and actually died was marked by a small shrine that developed into the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint-Denis" title="Basilica of Saint-Denis">Basilica of Saint-Denis</a>, which became the burial place for the <a href="/wiki/List_of_French_monarchs" title="List of French monarchs">kings of France</a>. Another account has his corpse being thrown into the <a href="/wiki/Seine" title="Seine">Seine</a>, but recovered and buried later that night by his converts.<sup id="cite_ref-patron_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patron-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div style="clear:left;"></div>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Denis_of_Paris&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Musee_du_Moyen_Age_A01.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Musee_du_Moyen_Age_A01.JPG/220px-Musee_du_Moyen_Age_A01.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="391" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Musee_du_Moyen_Age_A01.JPG/330px-Musee_du_Moyen_Age_A01.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Musee_du_Moyen_Age_A01.JPG/440px-Musee_du_Moyen_Age_A01.JPG 2x" data-file-width="570" data-file-height="1014" /></a><figcaption>Late Gothic statue of Saint Denis, limestone, formerly polychromed (<a href="/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_de_Cluny" title="Musée de Cluny">Musée de Cluny</a>)</figcaption></figure>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Nuremberg_chronicles_f_109v_2.png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a2/Nuremberg_chronicles_f_109v_2.png/220px-Nuremberg_chronicles_f_109v_2.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="291" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a2/Nuremberg_chronicles_f_109v_2.png 1.5x" data-file-width="224" data-file-height="296" /></a><figcaption>St Denis in the <i><a href="/wiki/Nuremberg_Chronicle" title="Nuremberg Chronicle">Nuremberg Chronicle</a></i></figcaption></figure>
<p>Veneration of Denis began soon after his death. The bodies of Denis, Eleutherius, and Rusticus were buried on the spot of their martyrdom, where the construction of the <a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/eponym" class="extiw" title="wiktionary:eponym">eponymous</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint_Denis_Basilica" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Denis Basilica">basilica</a> was begun by <a href="/wiki/Genevieve" title="Genevieve">Genevieve</a>, assisted by the people of <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-art_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-art-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Her <i>Vita Sanctae Genovefae</i> attests the presence of a shrine near the present basilica by the close of the fifth century.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Dagobert_I" title="Dagobert I">Dagobert I</a>, great-grandson of <a href="/wiki/Chlothar_I" title="Chlothar I">Chlothar I</a>, had the first Royal Basilica built. The Merovingian tradition was, originally, to bury kings such as <a href="/wiki/Clovis_I" title="Clovis I">Clovis</a> and <a href="/wiki/Clotilde" title="Clotilde">Chlothildis</a> in Paris at Abbey St-Genevieve/Genovefa, as Clovis had ordered its construction in 502 AD. Yet Chilperic I had his own mother, <a href="/wiki/Aregund" title="Aregund">Dowager Queen Aregunda</a>, buried at Saint-Denis. His grandson was clearly following a family tradition. Aregunda's (death about 580 AD) tomb was discovered in 1959, and her burial items can be seen at the <a href="/wiki/Saint-Germain-en-Laye" title="Saint-Germain-en-Laye">Saint-Germain-en-Laye</a> museum. A successor church was erected by <a href="/wiki/Fulrad" title="Fulrad">Fulrad</a>, who became abbot in 749/50 and was closely linked with the accession of the Carolingians to the Merovingian throne.
</p><p>In time, St<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>Denis came to be regarded as the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the <a href="/wiki/French_people" title="French people">French people</a>, with <a href="/wiki/Louis_IX_of_France" title="Louis IX of France">St<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>Louis</a> the patron of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_France" title="Kingdom of France">monarchy</a> and <a href="/wiki/List_of_French_kings" class="mw-redirect" title="List of French kings">royal dynasties</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBaynes1878_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaynes1878-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> <i>Saint Denis</i> or <i>Montjoie! Saint Denis!</i> became the typical <a href="/wiki/War-cry" class="mw-redirect" title="War-cry">war-cry</a> of the <a href="/wiki/French_army" class="mw-redirect" title="French army">French armies</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Oriflamme" title="Oriflamme">oriflamme</a>, which became the standard of France, was the banner consecrated upon his tomb. His veneration spread beyond France when, in 754, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Stephen_II" title="Pope Stephen II">Pope Stephen II</a> brought veneration of Saint Denis to Rome. Soon his <i><a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cultus</a></i> was prevalent throughout Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-art_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-art-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Abbot_Suger" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbot Suger">Abbot Suger</a> removed the relics of Denis, and those associated with Rustique and Eleuthére, from the crypt, to reside under the high altar of the Saint-Denis he rebuilt, 1140–44.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In traditional Catholic practice, Saint Denis is honoured as one of the <a href="/wiki/Fourteen_Holy_Helpers" title="Fourteen Holy Helpers">Fourteen Holy Helpers</a>. Specifically, Denis is invoked against <a href="/wiki/Demonic_possession" class="mw-redirect" title="Demonic possession">diabolical possession</a> and headaches<sup id="cite_ref-cathculture_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathculture-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> and with Geneviève is one of the patron saints of Paris.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Feast">Feast</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Denis_of_Paris&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Feast">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Henri_Bellechose_001.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/Henri_Bellechose_001.jpg/220px-Henri_Bellechose_001.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="168" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/Henri_Bellechose_001.jpg/330px-Henri_Bellechose_001.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/Henri_Bellechose_001.jpg/440px-Henri_Bellechose_001.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4895" data-file-height="3736" /></a><figcaption><i>Last Communion and Martyrdom of Saint Denis</i>, by <a href="/wiki/Henri_Bellechose" title="Henri Bellechose">Henri Bellechose</a>, 1416, which shows the martyrdom of both Denis and his companions</figcaption></figure>
<p>October 9 is celebrated as the feast of Saint Denis and companions, a priest named Rusticus and a <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a>, Eleutherius, who were martyred alongside him and buried with him. The names Rusticus and Eleutherius are non-historical. The feast of Saint Denis was added to the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_Saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Calendar of Saints">Roman Calendar</a> in the year 1568 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_V" title="Pope Pius V">Pope Pius V</a>, although it had been celebrated since at least the year 800.
</p><p>Saint Denis is also <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> with a <a href="/wiki/Commemoration_(Anglicanism)" title="Commemoration (Anglicanism)">commemoration</a><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> in many Anglican Provinces, including the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> and the <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Church_of_Canada" title="Anglican Church of Canada">Anglican Church of Canada</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> on <a href="/wiki/October_9" title="October 9">October 9</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Confusion_with_Dionysius_the_Areopagite">Confusion with Dionysius the Areopagite</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Denis_of_Paris&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Confusion with Dionysius the Areopagite">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Since at least the ninth century, the legends of <a href="/wiki/Dionysius_the_Areopagite" title="Dionysius the Areopagite">Dionysius the Areopagite</a> and Denis of Paris have often been confused. Around 814, <a href="/wiki/Louis_the_Pious" title="Louis the Pious">Louis the Pious</a> brought certain writings attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite to France, and since then it became common among the French legendary writers to argue that Denis of Paris was the same Dionysius who was a famous convert and disciple of <a href="/wiki/Paul_of_Tarsus" class="mw-redirect" title="Paul of Tarsus">Paul of Tarsus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-art_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-art-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The confusion of the personalities of Denis of Paris, Dionysius the Areopagite, and <a href="/wiki/Pseudo-Dionysius_the_Areopagite" title="Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite">pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite</a>, the author of the writings ascribed to Dionysius brought to France by Louis, was initiated through an <i>Areopagitica</i> written in 836 by <a href="/wiki/Hilduin" title="Hilduin">Hilduin</a>, Abbot of Saint-Denis, at the request of <a href="/wiki/Louis_the_Pious" title="Louis the Pious">Louis the Pious</a>. "Hilduin was anxious to promote the dignity of his church, and it is to him that the quite unfounded identification of the patron saint with Dionysius the Areopagite and his consequent connexion with the apostolic age are due."<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Hilduin's attribution had been supported for centuries by the monastic community at <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint-Denis" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of Saint-Denis">Abbey of Saint-Denis</a> and it was one of the origins of their pride. In <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_calamitatum" class="mw-redirect" title="Historia calamitatum">Historia calamitatum</a></i>, <a href="/wiki/Pierre_Abelard" class="mw-redirect" title="Pierre Abelard">Pierre Abelard</a> gives a short account of the strength of this belief and the monastery's harsh opposition to challenges to their claim. Abelard jokingly pointed out a possibility that the founder of the Abbey could have been another Dionysius, who is mentioned as Dionysius of Corinth by <a href="/wiki/Eusebius" title="Eusebius">Eusebius</a>. This irritated the community so much that eventually Abelard left in bitterness. As late as the sixteenth century, scholars might still argue for an Eastern origin of the Basilica of Saint-Denis: one was Godefroi Tillman, in a long preface to a paraphrase of the <i>Letters</i> of the Areopagite, printed in Paris in 1538 by Charlotte Guillard.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Most historiographers agree that this conflated legend is completely erroneous.<sup id="cite_ref-cathen_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cathen-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Depiction_in_art">Depiction in art</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Denis_of_Paris&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Depiction in art">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:StDenis.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b6/StDenis.jpg/220px-StDenis.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="308" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b6/StDenis.jpg/330px-StDenis.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b6/StDenis.jpg/440px-StDenis.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2752" data-file-height="3852" /></a><figcaption>Painting by <a href="/wiki/Jean_Bourdichon" title="Jean Bourdichon">Jean Bourdichon</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>Denis' headless walk has led to his being depicted in art decapitated and dressed as a <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a>, holding his own (often <a href="/wiki/Mitre" title="Mitre">mitred</a>) <a href="/wiki/Cephalophore" title="Cephalophore">head in his hands</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-art_8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-art-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Handling the <a href="/wiki/Halo_(religious_symbol)" class="mw-redirect" title="Halo (religious symbol)">halo</a> in this circumstance poses a unique challenge for the artist. Some put the halo where the head used to be; others have Saint Denis carrying the halo along with the head. Even more problematic than the halo was the issue of how much of his head Denis should be shown carrying.
</p><p>Throughout much of the Middle Ages, the Abbey of St Denis and the <a href="/wiki/Canon_(priest)" class="mw-redirect" title="Canon (priest)">canons</a> of <a href="/wiki/Notre-Dame_de_Paris" title="Notre-Dame de Paris">Notre-Dame Cathedral</a> were in dispute over ownership of the saint's head. The Abbey claimed that they had the entire body, whilst the Cathedral claimed to possess the top of his head which, they claimed, had been severed by the executioner's first blow.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Thus while most depictions of St Denis show him holding his entire head, in others, the patrons have shown their support for the cathedral's claim by depicting him carrying just the crown of his skull, as for example in the mid 13th century window showing the story at <a href="/wiki/Le_Mans_Cathedral" title="Le Mans Cathedral">Le Mans Cathedral</a> (Bay 111).<sup id="cite_ref-SkullCrown_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SkullCrown-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A 1317 illustrated manuscript depicting <i>The Life of Saint Denis</i>, once owned by <a href="/wiki/Philip_V_of_France" title="Philip V of France">King Philip V of France</a>, is preserved in the <a href="/wiki/Biblioth%C3%A8que_Nationale_de_France" class="mw-redirect" title="Bibliothèque Nationale de France">Bibliothèque Nationale</a> in Paris. It was given to the king by his chaplain Gilles, the abbot of Saint-Denis, having been commissioned by Jean de Pontoise, the previous Abbot of Saint-Denis. The manuscript contains seventy-seven miniatures illustrating the life and martyrdom of Saint Denis.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
