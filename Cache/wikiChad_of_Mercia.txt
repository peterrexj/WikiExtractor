<p><b>Chad</b><sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> (died 2 March 672) was a prominent 7th-century <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxon</a> Catholic monk who became <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> of several <a href="/wiki/Monasteries" class="mw-redirect" title="Monasteries">monasteries</a>, <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_York" title="Archbishop of York">Bishop of the Northumbrians</a> and subsequently <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Lichfield" title="Bishop of Lichfield">Bishop of the Mercians and Lindsey People</a>. He was later canonised as a saint.
</p><p>He was the brother of <a href="/wiki/Cedd" title="Cedd">Cedd</a>, also a saint. He features strongly in the work of the Venerable <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> and is credited, together with Cedd, with introducing Christianity to the <a href="/wiki/Mercian_kingdom" class="mw-redirect" title="Mercian kingdom">Mercian kingdom</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Sources">Sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Life_of_St._Chad.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Life_of_St._Chad.JPG/220px-Life_of_St._Chad.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="318" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Life_of_St._Chad.JPG/330px-Life_of_St._Chad.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Life_of_St._Chad.JPG/440px-Life_of_St._Chad.JPG 2x" data-file-width="2489" data-file-height="3599" /></a><figcaption>From a late copy of <i>The old Englisch Homely on the life of St. Chad</i>, <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1200</span>, in the <a href="/wiki/Bodleian_Library" title="Bodleian Library">Bodleian Library</a>, <a href="/wiki/Oxford" title="Oxford">Oxford</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>Most of our knowledge of Chad comes from the writings of <a href="/wiki/The_Venerable_Bede" class="mw-redirect" title="The Venerable Bede">the Venerable Bede</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Bede tells us that he obtained his information about Chad and his brother, Cedd, from the <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monks</a> of <a href="/wiki/Lastingham" title="Lastingham">Lastingham</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> where both were <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbots</a>. Bede also refers to information he received from <a href="/wiki/Trumbert" title="Trumbert">Trumbert</a>, "who tutored me in the <a href="/wiki/Scripture" class="mw-redirect" title="Scripture">Scriptures</a> and who had been educated in the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> by that master",<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-1_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-1-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> i.e. Chad. In other words, Bede considered himself to stand in the spiritual lineage of Chad and had gathered information from at least one who knew him personally.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_education">Early life and education</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early life and education">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Family_links">Family links</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Family links">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Chad was one of four brothers, all active in the <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon England">Anglo-Saxon</a> church. The others were <a href="/wiki/Cedd" title="Cedd">Cedd</a>, <a href="/wiki/Cynibil" title="Cynibil">Cynibil</a> and <a href="/wiki/Caelin" class="mw-redirect" title="Caelin">Caelin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-3_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-3-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Chad seems to have been Cedd's junior, arriving on the political scene about ten years after Cedd. It is reasonable to suppose that Chad and his brothers were drawn from the Northumbrian nobility.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> They certainly had close connections throughout the Northumbrian ruling class. However, the name <i>Chad</i> is actually of <a href="/wiki/British_language_(Celtic)" class="mw-redirect" title="British language (Celtic)">British</a> <a href="/wiki/Celts" title="Celts">Celtic</a>, rather than <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon">Anglo-Saxon</a> origin.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> It is an element found in the personal names of many <a href="/wiki/Wales" title="Wales">Welsh</a> princes and nobles of the period and signifies "battle".
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Education">Education</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Education">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The only major fact that Bede gives about Chad's early life is that he was a student of <a href="/wiki/Aidan_of_Lindisfarne" title="Aidan of Lindisfarne">Aidan</a> at the <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">Celtic</a> <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> at <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-2_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-2-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> In fact, Bede attributes the general pattern of Chad's ministry to the example of Aidan and his own brother, <a href="/wiki/Cedd" title="Cedd">Cedd</a>, who was also a student of St. Aidan.
</p><p>Aidan was a disciple of <a href="/wiki/Columba" title="Columba">Columba</a> and was invited by King <a href="/wiki/Oswald_of_Northumbria" title="Oswald of Northumbria">Oswald of Northumbria</a> to come from <a href="/wiki/Iona" title="Iona">Iona</a> to establish a monastery. Aidan arrived in <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbria</a> in 635 and died in 651. Chad must have studied at Lindisfarne some time between these years.
</p>
<h3><span id="Travels_in_Ireland_and_dating_of_Chad.27s_life"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Travels_in_Ireland_and_dating_of_Chad's_life">Travels in Ireland and dating of Chad's life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Travels in Ireland and dating of Chad&#039;s life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>A number of ecclesiastical settlements were established in 7th century Ireland to accommodate European monks, particularly Anglo-Saxon monks. Around 668, <a href="/wiki/Colm%C3%A1n_of_Lindisfarne" title="Colmán of Lindisfarne">Bishop Colman</a> resigned his see at <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a> and returned to Ireland. Less than three years later he erected <a href="/wiki/Monastery_of_Mayo" title="Monastery of Mayo">an abbey in County Mayo</a> exclusively for the English monks in the village of <a href="/wiki/Mayo,_County_Mayo" title="Mayo, County Mayo">Mayo</a>, subsequently known as <i>Maigh Eo na Saxain</i> ("Mayo of the Saxons").<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Chad traveled to <a href="/wiki/Ireland" title="Ireland">Ireland</a> as a monk,<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-1_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-1-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> before he was ordained a priest. One of his companions was <a href="/wiki/Ecgberht_of_Ripon" title="Ecgberht of Ripon">Ecgberht of Ripon</a>. Egbert was of the Anglian nobility, probably from Northumbria. Bede places them among an influx of English scholars who arrived in Ireland while <a href="/wiki/Finan_of_Lindisfarne" title="Finan of Lindisfarne">Finan</a> and <a href="/wiki/Colm%C3%A1n_of_Lindisfarne" title="Colmán of Lindisfarne">Colmán</a> were bishops at Lindisfarne. This suggests that they left for Ireland some time after Aidan's death in 651. They went to <a href="/wiki/Rath_Melsigi" title="Rath Melsigi">Rath Melsigi</a>, an Anglo-Saxon monastery in <a href="/wiki/County_Carlow" title="County Carlow">County Carlow</a>, for further study. In the controversy over the keeping of Easter, Rath Melsigi accepted the Roman computation.
</p><p>In 664 the twenty-five year old Egbert barely survived a plague that had killed all his other companions.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Chad had by then already left Ireland to help his brother Cedd establish the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Lastingham" title="Lastingham">Laestingaeu</a> in Yorkshire.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> rule was slowly spreading across Western Europe. Chad was trained in an entirely distinct monastic tradition that tended to look back to <a href="/wiki/Martin_of_Tours" title="Martin of Tours">Martin of Tours</a> as an exemplar.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMayr-Harting199197_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMayr-Harting199197-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> The Irish and early Anglo-Saxon monasticism experienced by Chad was peripatetic, stressed <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetic</a> practices and had a strong focus on Biblical <a href="/wiki/Exegesis" title="Exegesis">exegesis</a>, which generated a profound <a href="/wiki/Eschatology" title="Eschatology">eschatological consciousness</a>. Egbert recalled later that he and Chad "followed the monastic life together very strictly&#160;– in prayers and continence, and in meditation on Holy Scripture". Some of the scholars quickly settled in Irish monasteries, while others wandered from one master to another in search of knowledge. Bede says that the Irish monks gladly taught them and fed them, and even let them use their valuable books, without charge. Since books were all produced by hand, with painstaking attention to detail, this was astonishingly generous.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Founding_of_Lastingham">Founding of Lastingham</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Founding of Lastingham">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:LastinghamChurchApsisH1b.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/02/LastinghamChurchApsisH1b.jpg/360px-LastinghamChurchApsisH1b.jpg" decoding="async" width="360" height="203" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/02/LastinghamChurchApsisH1b.jpg/540px-LastinghamChurchApsisH1b.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/02/LastinghamChurchApsisH1b.jpg/720px-LastinghamChurchApsisH1b.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4592" data-file-height="2584" /></a><figcaption>The altar in the crypt of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_St_Mary,_Lastingham" title="Church of St Mary, Lastingham">Church of St Mary, Lastingham</a>, probable site of the early Anglo-Saxon church where Cedd and Chad officiated at Eucharist.</figcaption></figure>
<p>King <a href="/wiki/Oswiu_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Oswiu of Northumbria">Oswiu of Northumbria</a> appointed his nephew, <a href="/wiki/%C5%92thelwald_of_Deira" title="Œthelwald of Deira">Œthelwald</a>, to administer the coastal area of <a href="/wiki/Deira_(kingdom)" class="mw-redirect" title="Deira (kingdom)">Deira</a>. Chad's brother Cælin was chaplain at Œthelwald's court. It was on the initiative of Cælin that Ethelwald donated land for the building of a monastery at <a href="/wiki/Lastingham" title="Lastingham">Lastingham</a> near <a href="/wiki/Pickering,_North_Yorkshire" title="Pickering, North Yorkshire">Pickering</a> in the <a href="/wiki/North_York_Moors" title="North York Moors">North York Moors</a>, close to one of the still-usable <a href="/wiki/Roman_road" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman road">Roman roads</a>. Caelin introduced Ethelwold to Cedd. The monastery became a base for Cedd, who was serving as a missionary bishop in Essex.
</p><p>Bede says that Cedd "fasted strictly in order to cleanse it from the filth of wickedness previously committed there". On the thirtieth day of his forty-day fast, he was called away on urgent business. Cynibil, another of his brothers, took over the fast for the remaining ten days. The incident indicates the brothers ties with Northumbria's ruling dynasty. Laestingaeu was clearly conceived as a base for the family and destined to be under their control for the foreseeable future&#160;– not an unusual arrangement in this period.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMayr-Harting1991253_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMayr-Harting1991253-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Cedd was stricken by the plague, and upon his death in 664, Chad succeeded him as abbot.<sup id="cite_ref-Burton_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Burton-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Abbot_of_Lastingham">Abbot of Lastingham</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Abbot of Lastingham">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Chad's first appearance as an ecclesiastical prelate occurs in 664, shortly after the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Whitby" title="Synod of Whitby">Synod of Whitby</a>, when many Church leaders had been wiped out by the plague&#160;– among them Cedd, who died that year at Lastingham. On the death of his elder brother, Chad succeeded to the position of abbot.<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-3_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-3-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bede tells us of a man called Owin (Owen), who appeared at the door of Lastingham. Owin was a household official of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelthryth" title="Æthelthryth">Æthelthryth</a>, an East Anglian princess who had come to marry <a href="/wiki/Ecgfrith_of_Northumbria" title="Ecgfrith of Northumbria">Ecgfrith</a>, Oswiu's younger son. He decided to renounce the world, and as a sign of this appeared at Lastingham in ragged clothes and carrying an axe. He had come primarily to work manually. He became one of Chad's closest associates.
</p><p>Chad's <a href="/wiki/Eschatology" title="Eschatology">eschatological</a> consciousness and its effect on others is brought to life in a reminiscence attributed to <a href="/wiki/Trumbert" title="Trumbert">Trumbert</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-1_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-1-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> who was one of his students at Lastingham. Chad used to break off reading whenever a gale sprang up and call on God to have pity on humanity. If the storm intensified, he would shut his book altogether and prostrate himself in prayer. During prolonged storms or thunderstorms he would go into the church itself to pray and sing <a href="/wiki/Psalms" title="Psalms">psalms</a> until calm returned. His monks obviously regarded this as an extreme reaction even to English weather and asked him to explain. Chad explained that storms are sent by God to remind humans of the day of judgement and to humble their pride. The typically Celtic Christian involvement with nature was not like the modern <a href="/wiki/Romanticism" title="Romanticism">romantic</a> preoccupation but a determination to read in it the mind of God, particularly in relation to the last things.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMayr-Harting199189_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMayr-Harting199189-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Rise_of_a_dynasty">Rise of a dynasty</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Rise of a dynasty">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In fact, it is possible that he had only recently returned from Ireland when prominence was thrust upon him. However, the growing importance of his family within the Northumbrian state is clear from Bede's account of Cedd's career of the founding of their monastery at <a href="/wiki/Lastingham" title="Lastingham">Lastingham</a> in <a href="/wiki/North_Yorkshire" title="North Yorkshire">North Yorkshire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-3_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-3-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> This concentration of ecclesiastical power and influence within the network of a noble family was probably common in Anglo-Saxon England: an obvious parallel would be the children of King <a href="/wiki/Merewalh" title="Merewalh">Merewalh</a> in Mercia in the following generation.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Cedd" title="Cedd">Cedd</a>, probably the elder brother, had become a very prominent figure in the Church while Chad was in Ireland. Probably as a newly ordained priest, he was sent in 653 by Oswiu on a difficult mission to the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Angles" title="Middle Angles">Middle Angles</a>, at the request of their sub-king Peada, part of a developing pattern of Northumbrian intervention in Mercian affairs. After perhaps a year, he was recalled and sent on a similar mission to the <a href="/wiki/East_Saxons" class="mw-redirect" title="East Saxons">East Saxons</a>, being ordained bishop shortly afterwards. Cedd's position as both a Christian missionary and a royal emissary compelled him to travel often between Essex and Northumbria.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop_of_the_Northumbrians">Bishop of the Northumbrians</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Bishop of the Northumbrians">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Need_for_a_bishop">Need for a bishop</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Need for a bishop">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Bede gives great prominence to the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Whitby" title="Synod of Whitby">Synod of Whitby</a><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> in 663/4, which he shows resolving the main issues of practice in the Northumbrian Church in favour of Roman practice. Cedd is shown acting as the main go-between in the <a href="/wiki/Synod" title="Synod">synod</a> because of his facility with all of the relevant languages. Cedd was not the only prominent churchman to die of plague shortly after the synod. This was one of several outbreaks of the plague; they badly hit the ranks of the Church leadership, with most of the bishops in the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms dead, including the archbishop of Canterbury. Bede tells us that <a href="/wiki/Colman_of_Lindisfarne" class="mw-redirect" title="Colman of Lindisfarne">Colmán</a>, the bishop of the Northumbrians at the time of the Synod, had left for Scotland after the Synod went against him. He was succeeded by <a href="/wiki/Tuda_of_Lindisfarne" title="Tuda of Lindisfarne">Tuda</a>, who lived only a short time after his accession. The tortuous process of replacing him is covered by Bede<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-2_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-2-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> briefly, but in some respects puzzlingly.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Mission_of_Wilfrid">Mission of Wilfrid</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Mission of Wilfrid">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The first choice to replace Tuda was <a href="/wiki/Wilfrid" title="Wilfrid">Wilfrid</a>, a particularly zealous partisan of the Roman cause. Because of the plague, there were not the requisite three bishops available to ordain him, so he had gone to the <a href="/wiki/Frankish_Empire" class="mw-redirect" title="Frankish Empire">Frankish</a> Kingdom of <a href="/wiki/Neustria" title="Neustria">Neustria</a> to seek ordination. This was on the initiative of Alfrid, sub-king of Deira, although presumably Oswiu knew and approved this action at the time. Bede tells us that Alfrid sought a bishop for himself and his own people. This probably means the people of Deira. According to Bede, Tuda had been succeeded as abbot of Lindisfarne by Eata, who had been elevated to the rank of bishop.
</p><p>Wilfrid met with his own teacher and patron, Agilbert, a spokesman for the Roman side at Whitby, who had been made bishop of Paris. Agilbert set in motion the process of ordaining Wilfrid canonically, summoning several bishops to <a href="/wiki/Compi%C3%A8gne" title="Compiègne">Compiègne</a> for the ceremony. Bede tells us that he then lingered abroad for some time after his ordination.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Elevation">Elevation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Elevation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Mercia_in_time_of_Chad.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/2/2f/Mercia_in_time_of_Chad.jpg" decoding="async" width="600" height="470" class="mw-file-element" data-file-width="600" data-file-height="470" /></a><figcaption></figcaption></figure>
<p>Bede implies that Oswiu decided to take further action because Wilfrid was away for longer than expected. It is unclear whether Oswiu changed his mind about Wilfrid, or whether he despaired of his return, or whether he never really intended him to become bishop but used this opportunity to get him out of the country.
</p><p>Chad was invited then to become bishop of the Northumbrians by King Oswiu. Chad is often listed as a <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_York" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of York">Bishop of York</a>. Bede generally uses ethnic, not geographical, designations for Chad and other early <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon">Anglo-Saxon</a> bishops. However at this point, he does also refer to Oswiu's desire that Chad become bishop of the church in <a href="/wiki/York" title="York">York</a>. York later became the diocesan city partly because it had already been designated as such in the earlier Roman-sponsored mission of <a href="/wiki/Paulinus_of_York" title="Paulinus of York">Paulinus</a> to Deira, so it is not clear whether Bede is simply echoing the practice of his own day, or whether Oswiu and Chad were considering a territorial basis and a see for his episcopate. It is quite clear that Oswiu intended Chad to be bishop over the entire Northumbrian people, over-riding the claims of both Wilfrid and Eata.
</p><p>Chad faced the same problem over ordination as Wilfrid, and so set off to seek ordination amid the chaos caused by the plague. Bede tells us that he travelled first to Canterbury, where he found that Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Deusdedit_of_Canterbury" title="Deusdedit of Canterbury">Deusdedit</a> was dead and his replacement was still awaited. Bede does not tell us why Chad diverted to Canterbury. The journey seems pointless, since the archbishop had died three years previously&#160;– a fact that must have been well known in Northumbria, and was the very reason Wilfrid had to go abroad. The most obvious reason for Chad's tortuous travels would be that he was also on a diplomatic mission from Oswiu, seeking to build an encircling alliance around Mercia, which was rapidly recovering from its position of weakness. From Canterbury he travelled to <a href="/wiki/Wessex" title="Wessex">Wessex</a>, where he was ordained by bishop <a href="/wiki/Wine_(bishop)" title="Wine (bishop)">Wini</a> of the <a href="/wiki/West_Saxons" class="mw-redirect" title="West Saxons">West Saxons</a> and two British, i.e. Welsh, bishops. None of these bishops was recognised by Rome. Bede points out that "at that time there was no other bishop in all Britain canonically ordained except Wini" and the latter had been installed irregularly by the king of the West Saxons.
</p><p>Bede describes Chad at this point as "a diligent performer in deed of what he had learnt in the Scriptures should be done." Bede also tells us that Chad was teaching the values of Aidan and Cedd. His life was one of constant travel. Bede says that Chad visited continually the towns, countryside, cottages, villages and houses to preach the <a href="/wiki/Gospel" title="Gospel">Gospel</a>. Clearly, the model he followed was one of the bishop as <a href="/wiki/Prophet" title="Prophet">prophet</a> or <a href="/wiki/Missionary" title="Missionary">missionary</a>. Basic Christian rites of passage, <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptism</a> and <a href="/wiki/Confirmation" title="Confirmation">confirmation</a>, were almost always performed by a bishop, and for decades to come they were generally carried out in mass ceremonies, probably with little systematic instruction or counselling.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Removal">Removal</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Removal">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In 666, Wilfrid returned from Neustria, "bringing many rules of Catholic observance", as Bede says. He found Chad already occupying the same position. It seems that he did not in fact challenge Chad's pre-eminence in his own area. Rather, he would have worked assiduously to build up his own support in sympathetic monasteries, like Gilling and <a href="/wiki/Ripon" title="Ripon">Ripon</a>. He did, however, assert his episcopal rank by going into <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercia</a> and even <a href="/wiki/Kent" title="Kent">Kent</a> to ordain priests. Bede tells us that the net effect of his efforts on the Church was that the Irish monks who still lived in Northumbria either came fully into line with Catholic practices or left for home. Nevertheless, Bede cannot conceal that Oswiu and Chad had broken significantly with Roman practice in many ways and that the Church in Northumbria had been divided by the ordination of rival bishops.
</p><p>In 669, a new <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, <a href="/wiki/Theodore_of_Tarsus" title="Theodore of Tarsus">Theodore of Tarsus</a>, sent by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Vitalian" title="Pope Vitalian">Pope Vitalian</a> arrived in England. He immediately set off on a tour of the country, tackling abuses of which he had been forewarned. He instructed Chad to step down and Wilfrid to take over.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> According to Bede, Theodore was so impressed by Chad's show of humility that he confirmed his ordination as bishop, while insisting he step down from his position. Chad retired gracefully and returned to his post as abbot of Lastingham, leaving Wilfrid as bishop of the Northumbrians at York.<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-1_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-1-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop_of_the_Mercians">Bishop of the Mercians</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: Bishop of the Mercians">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Recall">Recall</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: Recall">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Later that same year, King <a href="/wiki/Wulfhere_of_Mercia" title="Wulfhere of Mercia">Wulfhere of Mercia</a> requested a bishop. Wulfhere and the other sons of <a href="/wiki/Penda" class="mw-redirect" title="Penda">Penda</a> had converted to Christianity, although Penda himself had remained a <a href="/wiki/Pagan" class="mw-redirect" title="Pagan">pagan</a> until his death (655). Penda had allowed bishops to operate in Mercia, although none had succeeded in establishing the Church securely without active royal support.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Theodore_of_Tarsus" title="Theodore of Tarsus">Archbishop Theodore</a> refused to consecrate a new bishop. Instead he recalled Chad out of his retirement at Lastingham. According to Bede, Theodore was greatly impressed by Chad's humility and holiness. This was displayed particularly in his refusal to use a horse: he insisted on walking everywhere. Despite his regard for Chad, Theodore ordered him to ride on long journeys and went so far as to lift him into the saddle on one occasion.
</p><p>Chad was consecrated bishop of the Mercians (literally, frontier people) and of the Lindsey people (<a href="/wiki/Lindisfaras" title="Lindisfaras">Lindisfaras</a>). Bede tells us that Chad was actually the third bishop sent to Wulfhere, making him the fifth bishop of the Mercians.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Lindsey" title="Kingdom of Lindsey">Kingdom of Lindsey</a>, covering the north-eastern area of modern <a href="/wiki/Lincolnshire" title="Lincolnshire">Lincolnshire</a>, was under Mercian control, although it had in the past sometimes fallen under Northumbrian control. Later Anglo-Saxon episcopal lists sometimes add the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Angles" title="Middle Angles">Middle Angles</a> to his responsibilities.<sup id="cite_ref-pase.ac.uk_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pase.ac.uk-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> They were a distinct part of the Mercian kingdom, centred on the middle <a href="/wiki/River_Trent" title="River Trent">Trent</a> and lower <a href="/wiki/River_Tame_(West_Midlands)" class="mw-redirect" title="River Tame (West Midlands)">Tame</a>&#160;– the area around <a href="/wiki/Tamworth,_Staffordshire" title="Tamworth, Staffordshire">Tamworth</a>, Lichfield and <a href="/wiki/Repton" title="Repton">Repton</a> that formed the core of the wider Mercian polity. It was their sub-king, Peada, who had secured the services of Chad's brother Cedd in 653, and they were frequently considered separately from the Mercians proper, a people who lived further to the west and north.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastic_foundations">Monastic foundations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=16" title="Edit section: Monastic foundations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>When Chad was made Bishop of Mercia in 669, he moved his see from Repton to Lichfield, possibly because this was already a holy site, as the scene of martyrdoms during the Roman period.  Wulfhere donated land at <a href="/wiki/Lichfield" title="Lichfield">Lichfield</a> for Chad to build a monastery. It was because of this that the centre of the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Lichfield" title="Diocese of Lichfield">Diocese of Mercia</a> ultimately became settled at <a href="/wiki/Lichfield" title="Lichfield">Lichfield</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-1_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-1-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The Lichfield monastery was probably similar to that at Lastingham, and Bede makes clear that it was partly staffed by monks from Lastingham, including Chad's faithful retainer, Owin. Lichfield was very close to the old <a href="/wiki/Roman_road" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman road">Roman road</a> of <a href="/wiki/Watling_Street" title="Watling Street">Watling Street</a>, the main route across Mercia, and a short distance from Mercia's main royal centre at <a href="/wiki/Tamworth,_Staffordshire" title="Tamworth, Staffordshire">Tamworth</a>.
It was because of this that the ecclesiastical centre of Mercia became settled as the Diocese of Lichfield.
</p><p>Wulfhere also gave Chad land for a monastery at <a href="/wiki/Barrow_upon_Humber" title="Barrow upon Humber">Barrow upon Humber</a> in <a href="/wiki/North_Lincolnshire" title="North Lincolnshire">North Lincolnshire</a>. He traveled about on foot until the Archbishop of Canterbury gave him a horse and ordered him to ride it, at least on long journeys.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Chad's shrine at Lichfield was destroyed in 1538.
</p><p>Wulfhere also donated land sufficient for fifty families at a place in Lindsey, referred to by Bede as <i>Ad Barwae</i>. This is probably <a href="/wiki/Barrow_upon_Humber" title="Barrow upon Humber">Barrow upon Humber</a>: where an <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon">Anglo-Saxon</a> monastery of a later date has been excavated. This was easily reached by river from the Midlands and close to an easy crossing of the <a href="/wiki/River_Humber" class="mw-redirect" title="River Humber">River Humber</a>, allowing rapid communication along surviving Roman roads with Lastingham. Chad remained abbot of Lastingham throughout his life, as well as heading the communities at both Lichfield and Barrow.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Ministry_among_the_Mercians">Ministry among the Mercians</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=17" title="Edit section: Ministry among the Mercians">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Chad then proceeded to carry out much missionary and pastoral work within the kingdom. Bede tells us that Chad governed the bishopric of the Mercians and of the people of Lindsey 'in the manner of the ancient fathers and in great perfection of life'. However, Bede gives little concrete information about the work of Chad in Mercia, implying that in style and substance it was a continuation of what he had done in Northumbria. The area he covered was very large, stretching across England from coast to coast. It was also, in many places, difficult terrain, with woodland, heath and mountain over much of the centre and large areas of marshland to the east. Bede does tell us that Chad built for himself a small house at Lichfield, a short distance from the church, sufficient to hold his core of seven or eight disciples, who gathered to pray and study with him there when he was not out on business.
</p><p>Chad worked in Mercia and Lindsey for only two years before he too died during a plague.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Yet St. Bede could write in a letter that Mercia came to the faith and Essex was recovered for it by the two brothers Cedd and Chad. In other words, Bede considered that Chad's two years as bishop were decisive in Christianising Mercia.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=18" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Chad died on 2 March 672, and was buried at the Church of Saint Mary which later became part of the <a href="/wiki/Lichfield_Cathedral" title="Lichfield Cathedral">cathedral at Lichfield</a>. Bede relates the death story as that of a man who was already regarded as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>. In fact, Bede has stressed throughout his narrative that Chad's holiness communicated across boundaries of culture and politics, to Theodore, for example, in his own lifetime. The death story is clearly of supreme importance to Bede, confirming Chad's holiness and vindicating his life. The account occupies considerably more space in Bede's account than all the rest of Chad's ministry in Northumbria and Mercia together.
</p><p>Bede tells us that Owin was working outside the oratory at Lichfield. Inside, Chad studied alone because the other monks were at worship in the church. Suddenly Owin heard the sound of joyful singing, coming from heaven, at first to the south-east, but gradually coming closer until it filled the roof of the oratory itself. Then there was silence for half an hour, followed by the same singing going back the way it had come. Owin at first did nothing, but about an hour later Chad called him in and told him to fetch the seven brothers from the church. Chad gave his final address to the brothers, urging them to keep the monastic discipline they had learnt. Only after this did he tell them that he knew his own death was near, speaking of death as "that friendly guest who is used to visiting the brethren". He asked them to pray, then blessed and dismissed them. The brothers left, sad and downcast.
</p><p>Owin returned a little later and saw Chad privately. He asked about the singing. Chad told him that he must keep it to himself for the time being: angels had come to call him to his heavenly reward, and in seven days they would return to fetch him. So it was that Chad weakened and died after seven days&#160;– on 2 March, which remains his feast day. Bede writes that: "he had always looked forward to this day&#160;– or rather his mind had always been on the Day of the Lord". Many years later, his old friend Egbert told a visitor that someone in Ireland had seen the heavenly company coming for Chad's soul and returning with it to heaven. Significantly, with the heavenly host was Cedd. Bede was not sure whether or not the vision was actually Egbert's own.
</p><p>Bede's account of Chad's death strongly confirms the main themes of his life. Primarily he was a monastic leader, deeply involved in the fairly small communities of loyal monks who formed his mission teams, his brothers. His consciousness was strongly eschatological: focussed on the last things and their significance. Finally, he was inextricably linked with Cedd and his other actual brothers.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Cult_and_relics">Cult and relics</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=19" title="Edit section: Cult and relics">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Chad is considered a saint in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-ODS101_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODS101-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Anglican</a> churches, the <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Orthodox_Church" title="Celtic Orthodox Church">Celtic Orthodox Church</a> and is also noted as a saint in a new edition of the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox</a> <a href="/wiki/Synaxarium" title="Synaxarium">Synaxarion</a> (Book of Saints). His feast day is celebrated on 2 March.<sup id="cite_ref-ODS101_21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODS101-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to St. Bede, Chad was venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> immediately after his death, and his <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> were translated to a new shrine. He remained the centre of an important cult, focused on healing, throughout the Middle Ages. The cult had twin foci: his tomb, in the apse, directly behind the high altar of the cathedral; and more particularly his skull, kept in a special Head Chapel, above the south <a href="/wiki/Aisle" title="Aisle">aisle</a>.
</p><p>The transmission of the relics after the Reformation was tortuous. At the dissolution of the Shrine on the instructions of King Henry VIII in about 1538, Prebendary Arthur Dudley of Lichfield Cathedral removed and retained some relics, probably a travelling set. These were eventually passed to his nieces, Bridget and Katherine Dudley, of <a href="/wiki/Russells_Hall" class="mw-redirect" title="Russells Hall">Russells Hall</a>. In 1651, they reappeared when a farmer Henry Hodgetts of <a href="/wiki/Sedgley" title="Sedgley">Sedgley</a> was on his death-bed and kept praying to St Chad. When the priest hearing his last confession, Fr Peter Turner SJ, asked him why he called upon Chad. Henry replied, "because his bones are in the head of my bed". He instructed his wife to give the relics to the priest, whence they found their way to the Seminary at <a href="/wiki/Saint-Omer" title="Saint-Omer">St Omer</a>, in France. After the conclusion of <a href="/wiki/Penal_law_(British)" title="Penal law (British)">penal times</a>, in the early 19th century, they found their way into the hands of Sir Thomas Fitzherbert-Brockholes of Aston Hall, near <a href="/wiki/Stone,_Staffordshire" title="Stone, Staffordshire">Stone, Staffordshire</a>. When his chapel was cleared after his death, his chaplain, Fr Benjamin Hulme, discovered the box containing the relics, which were examined and presented to Bishop Thomas Walsh, the Roman Catholic <a href="/wiki/Vicar_Apostolic_of_the_Midland_District" class="mw-redirect" title="Vicar Apostolic of the Midland District">Vicar Apostolic of the Midland District</a> in 1837 and were enshrined in the new <a href="/wiki/St_Chad%27s_Cathedral,_Birmingham" title="St Chad&#39;s Cathedral, Birmingham">St Chad's Cathedral, Birmingham</a>, opened in 1841, in a new ark designed by <a href="/wiki/Augustus_Pugin" title="Augustus Pugin">Augustus Pugin</a>.
</p><p>The relics, some long bones, are now enshrined on the Altar of St Chad's Cathedral. They were examined by the Oxford Archeological Laboratory by carbon dating techniques in 1985, and all but one of the bones (which was a third femur, and therefore could not have come from Bishop Chad) were dated to the seventh century, and were authenticated as 'true relics' by the Vatican authorities. In 1919, an Annual Mass and Solemn Outdoor Procession of the Relics was held at St Chad's Cathedral in Birmingham. This observance continues to the present, on the Saturday nearest to his Feast Day, 2 March.
</p><p>Chad is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church</a> on 2 March.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Portrayals_of_St_Chad">Portrayals of St Chad</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=20" title="Edit section: Portrayals of St Chad">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There are no portraits, group photos, or descriptions of St Chad from his own time. The only hint that we have comes in the legend of Theodore lifting him bodily into the saddle&#160;– possibly suggesting that he was remembered as small in stature. All attempts to portray him are based entirely on imagination, and nearly all are obviously anachronistic, with a heavy stress on vestments from other periods.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Notable_dedications">Notable dedications</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=21" title="Edit section: Notable dedications">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Churches">Churches</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=22" title="Edit section: Churches">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Chad gives his name to <a href="/wiki/Birmingham" title="Birmingham">Birmingham</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Cathedral_of_Saint_Chad,_Birmingham" class="mw-redirect" title="Cathedral of Saint Chad, Birmingham">cathedral</a>, where there are some <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> of the saint: about eight long bones. It is the only cathedral in England that has the relics of its patron saint enshrined upon its high altar. The <a href="/wiki/Anglican" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican">Anglican</a> <a href="/wiki/Lichfield_Cathedral" title="Lichfield Cathedral">Lichfield Cathedral</a>, at the site of his burial, is dedicated to Chad, and <a href="/wiki/Mary_(mother_of_Jesus)" class="mw-redirect" title="Mary (mother of Jesus)">St Mary</a>, and still has a head chapel, where the skull of the saint was kept until it was lost during the <a href="/wiki/Protestant_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant Reformation">Reformation</a>. The site of the medieval shrine is also marked.
</p><p>Chad also gives his name to a <a href="/wiki/The_Church_of_St_Chad,_Lichfield" class="mw-redirect" title="The Church of St Chad, Lichfield">parish church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Lichfield" title="Lichfield">Lichfield</a> (with Chad's Well, where traditionally Chad <a href="/wiki/Baptised" class="mw-redirect" title="Baptised">baptised</a> converts: now a <a href="/wiki/Listed_building" title="Listed building">listed building</a>).
</p><p>Dedications are densely concentrated in the <a href="/wiki/West_Midlands_(region)" title="West Midlands (region)">West Midlands</a>. The city of <a href="/wiki/Wolverhampton" title="Wolverhampton">Wolverhampton</a>, for example, has two <a href="/wiki/Anglican" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican">Anglican</a> churches and an <a href="/wiki/Our_Lady_and_St_Chad_Catholic_Academy" title="Our Lady and St Chad Catholic Academy">Academy</a> dedicated to Chad, while the nearby village of <a href="/wiki/Pattingham" title="Pattingham">Pattingham</a> has both an Anglican church and <a href="/wiki/Primary_school" title="Primary school">primary school</a>. Shrewsbury had a large medieval church of St Chad which fell down in 1788: it was quickly replaced by a circular church in <a href="/wiki/Classical_architecture" title="Classical architecture">Classical</a> style by George Steuart, on a different site but with the same dedication. Parish Church in <a href="/wiki/Montford,_Shropshire" title="Montford, Shropshire">Montford</a>, built in 1735–38, site of the graves of the parents of Charles Darwin.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Parish Church in <a href="/wiki/Coseley" title="Coseley">Coseley</a> built in 1882. In <a href="/wiki/Rugby,_Warwickshire" title="Rugby, Warwickshire">Rugby, Warwickshire</a>, an <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Orthodox Church</a> is named for him. Further afield, there is a considerable number of dedications in areas associated with Chad's career, like the churches in <a href="/wiki/Church_Wilne" class="mw-redirect" title="Church Wilne">Church Wilne</a> in <a href="/wiki/Derbyshire" title="Derbyshire">Derbyshire</a>, <a href="/wiki/St_Chad%27s_Church,_Far_Headingley" title="St Chad&#39;s Church, Far Headingley">Far Headingley</a> in <a href="/wiki/Leeds" title="Leeds">Leeds</a>, the Parish Church of <a href="/wiki/Rochdale" title="Rochdale">Rochdale</a>, <a href="/wiki/Greater_Manchester" title="Greater Manchester">Greater Manchester</a>, and the <a href="/wiki/St_Chad%27s_Church,_Haggerston" title="St Chad&#39;s Church, Haggerston">Church of St Chad, Haggerston</a> in London, as well as some in the Commonwealth, like <a href="/wiki/Chelsea,_Victoria" title="Chelsea, Victoria">Chelsea</a> in Australia. There is also a <a href="/wiki/St_Chad%27s_College" class="mw-redirect" title="St Chad&#39;s College">St Chad's College</a> within the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Durham" class="mw-redirect" title="University of Durham">University of Durham</a>, founded in 1904 as an Anglican hall.
</p><p>In Canada, St Chad's Chapel and College was built in 1918 in <a href="/wiki/Regina,_Saskatchewan" title="Regina, Saskatchewan">Regina</a>, SK. Originally, it was a Catholic church and boys' school. In 1964, it became an Anglican school for girls, called St. Chad's Girls' School. Today, it is a protected historic building in Regina, and serves as a multi-use space.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Principal Parish of the <a href="/wiki/Personal_Ordinariate_of_Our_Lady_of_the_Southern_Cross" title="Personal Ordinariate of Our Lady of the Southern Cross">Personal Ordinariate of Our Lady of the Southern Cross</a> is named the Church of <a href="/wiki/St_Ninian" class="mw-redirect" title="St Ninian">St Ninian</a> and St Chad.<sup id="cite_ref-gci_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-gci-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Chapel_of_Brasenose_College,_Oxford" class="mw-redirect" title="Chapel of Brasenose College, Oxford">chapel of Brasenose College, Oxford</a> is named the Chapel of <a href="/wiki/Hugh_of_Cluny" title="Hugh of Cluny">St Hugh</a> and St Chad.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Toponyms">Toponyms</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=23" title="Edit section: Toponyms">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>There are many place names containing the element <i>chad</i> or something similar. In many cases, reference to the early forms of the name suggests that the derivation is not from the name Chad, but from some other word. It is possible that even where a name might reasonably be thought to derive from Chad that the individual is some other of the same name. Hence great caution needs to be exercised in explaining ancient <a href="/wiki/Toponyms" class="mw-redirect" title="Toponyms">toponyms</a> by reference to St Chad.
</p><p>That being said, a township located outside Ladysmith, Kwa-Zulu Natal is named St. Chad's after an Anglican Mission in the area.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="Original link is dead, archived version leads to a tourist site that doesn&#39;t verify this statement (July 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>One <a href="/wiki/Toponym" class="mw-redirect" title="Toponym">toponym</a> with a good claim to derivation from the saint's name is <a href="/wiki/Chadkirk_Chapel" title="Chadkirk Chapel">Chadkirk Chapel</a> in <a href="/wiki/Romiley" title="Romiley">Romiley</a>, <a href="/wiki/Greater_Manchester" title="Greater Manchester">Greater Manchester</a>, which dates back to the 14th century&#160;– although the site is much older, possibly dating back to the 7th century when it is believed St Chad visited to bless the well there. <a href="/wiki/Kenneth_Cameron_(academic)" title="Kenneth Cameron (academic)">Kenneth Cameron</a><sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> points out that -<i>kirk</i> toponyms more frequently incorporate the name of the dedicatee, rather than the patron, so there is every reason to believe that Chadkirk really was dedicated to St Chad in the Middle Ages. It is not so certain that Chadsmoor in Staffordshire, Chadwich in Worcestershire, or Chadwick in Warwickshire, were named after the saint.
</p><p><b>St Chad's Well</b><sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> near Battle Bridge on the <a href="/wiki/River_Fleet" title="River Fleet">river Fleet</a> in <a href="/wiki/London" title="London">London</a> was a celebrated medicinal well and had a new pump house built in 1832.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> It was destroyed by the Midland Railway company, and is remembered in the street name of St Chad's Place. There is no independent evidence of Chad's visiting the site, but it clearly is named after him, and he certainly did travel in southern England. His association with wells seems ancient, and no doubt stems from the St Chad's Well at Lichfield, visited by pilgrims and probably the water supply of his monastery. This is the most likely explanation of the name.
</p><p>Numerous place-names like <b>Cheadle</b> and <b>Cheddleton</b>, in the Midlands suggest a link with Chad. However "suggestions" based on late forms of the name count for little: a hypothesis should be framed instead from documentary and topographical evidence. Mostly names of this sort are derived from other Celtic roots, generally <i>ced</i>, cognate with modern Welsh <i>coed</i>, signifying a wood or heath. Cheadle, for example, is generally reckoned a <a href="/wiki/Tautonym" title="Tautonym">tautonym</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> with the Old English <i>leah</i>, also meaning a wood, glossing the original Celtic term.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> This means that the origins of its name are closely related to those of <a href="/wiki/Lichfield" title="Lichfield">Lichfield</a> (originally derived from the Celtic for "grey wood"), to which it bears little superficial resemblance, rather than Chad or even his brother, Cedd.
</p><p>There is a village in <a href="/wiki/Northamptonshire" title="Northamptonshire">Northamptonshire</a> called <b><a href="/wiki/Chadstone,_Northamptonshire" title="Chadstone, Northamptonshire">Chadstone</a></b>, after which the (rather larger) suburb of <a href="/wiki/Chadstone,_Victoria" title="Chadstone, Victoria">Chadstone</a> in <a href="/wiki/Melbourne" title="Melbourne">Melbourne</a> <a href="/wiki/Australia" title="Australia">Australia</a> is named.
</p><p><b><a href="/wiki/Kidderminster" title="Kidderminster">Kidderminster</a></b>, in Worcestershire, is sometimes said to be a corruption of the name of 'St Chad's Minster'.  However, place-names do not "corrupt" randomly, but evolve according to principles inherent in the history of the language. <i>Chad</i> or <i>Ceadda</i> would not normally evolve into <i>Kidder</i>. The existence of a minster dedicated to Chad in this town seems to be a legend traceable to Burton's 1890 History of Kidderminster,<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> in which the author acknowledges that the only evidence for such a place is the name of the town. Later writers seem to assume the existence of the monastery and then explain the name of the town from it&#160;– a circular argument that collapses if a plausible alternative explanation is available for the name. A grant of land by <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelbald_of_Mercia" title="Æthelbald of Mercia">Æthelbald of Mercia</a> in 736<sup id="cite_ref-pase.ac.uk_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pase.ac.uk-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> to one Cyneberht<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-4_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-4-34">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> is generally accepted as the origin of the settlement. Cameron suggests that the minster was named after a lay benefactor (normal with -<i>minster</i> formations) and hypothesises <i>Cydela</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> a suggestion that has found general acceptance.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> Another possibility might be the later Mercian <i>dux</i> Cydda.<sup id="cite_ref-Ref-4_34-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ref-4-34">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Certainly it seems that there was a dynasty of Mercian noblemen, all with similar names beginning <i>Cy</i> and connected to the area. These provide a more plausible explanation for the name of the town than St Chad or his non-existent minster.
</p><p>The settlement of <b>St Chad's</b> (population 57) in <a href="/wiki/Newfoundland_and_Labrador" title="Newfoundland and Labrador">Newfoundland</a> was previously named "St Shad's" (after originally being "Damnable"), but was renamed after postal confusion with nearby "St Shott's" <a rel="nofollow" class="external autonumber" href="http://www.centralnewfoundland.com/stchads.php3">[1]</a>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Schools">Schools</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=24" title="Edit section: Schools">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><a href="/wiki/Denstone_College" title="Denstone College">Denstone College</a> in the Village of <a href="/wiki/Denstone" title="Denstone">Denstone</a>, <a href="/wiki/Uttoxeter" title="Uttoxeter">Uttoxeter</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Staffordshire" title="Staffordshire">Staffordshire</a> was founded by <a href="/wiki/Nathaniel_Woodard" title="Nathaniel Woodard">Nathaniel Woodard</a> as the Flagship Woodard School of the Midlands. The school was founded as St Chad's College, Denstone. The School's <a href="/wiki/Chapel" title="Chapel">Chapel</a> is named as the Chapel of St Chad with depictions of him around the chapel's <a href="/wiki/Narthex" title="Narthex">Narthex</a>. The students of the school wear the famous cross of St Chad which is the school's logo. The motto of the School is ‘Lignum Crucis Arbor Scientae’ which is Latin for ‘The Wood of the Cross is the Tree of Knowledge’. There are also depictions of him in the School's Quadrangle.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Chad_as_a_personal_name">Chad as a personal name</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=25" title="Edit section: Chad as a personal name">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Chad_(name)" title="Chad (name)">Chad (name)</a></div>
<p>Chad remains a fairly popular given name, one of the few personal names current among 7th century Anglo-Saxons to do so. However, it was very little used for many centuries before a modest revival in the mid-20th century. Not all of its bearers are named directly after Chad of Mercia. Perhaps the best-known Chad of modern times who was so-named was <a href="/wiki/Chad_Varah" title="Chad Varah">Chad Varah</a>, an Anglican priest and social activist, whose father was vicar of <a href="/wiki/Barton-upon-Humber" title="Barton-upon-Humber">Barton-upon-Humber</a>&#160;– the probable site of Chad's monastery in the north of Lindsey.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage">Patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Chad_of_Mercia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=26" title="Edit section: Patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Due to the somewhat confused nature of Chad's appointment and the continued references to '<a href="/wiki/Chad_(paper)" title="Chad (paper)">chads</a>'&#160;– small pieces of ballot papers punched out by voters using voting machines&#160;– in the <a href="/wiki/2000_US_Presidential_Election" class="mw-redirect" title="2000 US Presidential Election">2000 US Presidential Election</a>, it has been jocularly suggested that Chad is the patron saint of botched elections. In fact there is no official <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of elections, although the Church has designated a later English official, <a href="/wiki/Thomas_More" title="Thomas More">Thomas More</a>, the patron of politicians.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Spa Research Fellowship</i> states that Chad is the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Mineral_spa" title="Mineral spa">medicinal springs</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> although other listings<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> do not mention this patronage.
</p><p>St. Chad's Day (2 March) is traditionally considered the most propitious day to sow <a href="/wiki/Broad_beans" class="mw-redirect" title="Broad beans">broad beans</a> in England.
</p>
