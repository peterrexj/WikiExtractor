<p><b>Richard Rolle</b> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;1300</span>&#160;– 30 September 1349)<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Jonathan_Hughes_1349_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jonathan_Hughes_1349-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> was an English hermit, mystic, and religious writer.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He is also known as <b>Richard Rolle of Hampole</b> or <b>de Hampole</b>, since at the end of his life he lived near a <a href="/wiki/Cistercian_nunnery" class="mw-redirect" title="Cistercian nunnery">Cistercian nunnery</a> in <a href="/wiki/Hampole" title="Hampole">Hampole</a>, now in <a href="/wiki/South_Yorkshire" title="South Yorkshire">South Yorkshire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> In the words of <a href="/wiki/Nicholas_Watson_(academic)" title="Nicholas Watson (academic)">Nicholas Watson</a>, scholarly research has shown that "[d]uring the fifteenth century he was one of the most widely read of English writers, whose works survive in nearly four hundred English ... and at least seventy Continental manuscripts, almost all written between 1390 and 1500."<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Richard_Rolle&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In his works, Rolle provides little explicit evidence about his early life and education. Most, if not all, of our information about him comes from the Office of Lessons and Antiphons that was composed in the 1380s in preparation for his canonisation, although this never came about.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Born into a small farming family<sup id="cite_ref-Kempe_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kempe-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> and brought up at <a href="/wiki/Thornton-le-Dale" title="Thornton-le-Dale">Thornton-le-Dale</a><sup id="cite_ref-Grove_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Grove-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> near <a href="/wiki/Pickering,_North_Yorkshire" title="Pickering, North Yorkshire">Pickering</a>, he studied at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Oxford" title="University of Oxford">University of Oxford</a> where he was sponsored by Thomas de Neville, the <a href="/wiki/Archdeacon_of_Durham" title="Archdeacon of Durham">Archdeacon of Durham</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Cath_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cath-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> While there, he is said to have been more interested in theology and biblical studies than philosophy and secular studies.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> He left Oxford at age eighteen or nineteen—dropping out before he received his MA—to become a hermit.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Leaving the family home, he first went to Pickering and housed with a <a href="/wiki/Squire" title="Squire">squire</a>, John Dalton, for perhaps three years.
</p><p>It was probably while still living with Dalton, two years and eight months after becoming a hermit, Rolle had his first mystical experience. Around a year later, he felt similarly after listening to a choir, and he began to take less interest in all things temporal.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Dalton himself was arrested and his lands confiscated in 1322; the lack of mention of this fact in accounts of Rolle's life makes it likely that he was no longer living with Dalton by this point.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1062260506">.mw-parser-output .quotebox{background-color:#F9F9F9;border:1px solid #aaa;box-sizing:border-box;padding:10px;font-size:88%;max-width:100%}.mw-parser-output .quotebox.floatleft{margin:.5em 1.4em .8em 0}.mw-parser-output .quotebox.floatright{margin:.5em 0 .8em 1.4em}.mw-parser-output .quotebox.centered{overflow:hidden;position:relative;margin:.5em auto .8em auto}.mw-parser-output .quotebox.floatleft span,.mw-parser-output .quotebox.floatright span{font-style:inherit}.mw-parser-output .quotebox>blockquote{margin:0;padding:0;border-left:0;font-family:inherit;font-size:inherit}.mw-parser-output .quotebox-title{background-color:#F9F9F9;text-align:center;font-size:110%;font-weight:bold}.mw-parser-output .quotebox-quote>:first-child{margin-top:0}.mw-parser-output .quotebox-quote:last-child>:last-child{margin-bottom:0}.mw-parser-output .quotebox-quote.quoted:before{font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;font-weight:bold;font-size:large;color:gray;content:" “ ";vertical-align:-45%;line-height:0}.mw-parser-output .quotebox-quote.quoted:after{font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;font-weight:bold;font-size:large;color:gray;content:" ” ";line-height:0}.mw-parser-output .quotebox .left-aligned{text-align:left}.mw-parser-output .quotebox .right-aligned{text-align:right}.mw-parser-output .quotebox .center-aligned{text-align:center}.mw-parser-output .quotebox .quote-title,.mw-parser-output .quotebox .quotebox-quote{display:block}.mw-parser-output .quotebox cite{display:block;font-style:normal}@media screen and (max-width:640px){.mw-parser-output .quotebox{width:100%!important;margin:0 0 .8em!important;float:none!important}}</style><div class="quotebox pullquote floatright" style="width:23em; ;">
<blockquote class="quotebox-quote left-aligned" style="">
<p>"I felt within me a merry and unknown heat...I was expert it was not from a creature but from my Maker, as it grew hotter and more glad."
</p>
</blockquote>
<p><cite class="left-aligned" style="">—Rolle on his first mystical experience.</cite>
</p>
</div><p>It is unclear where Rolle lived from 1321/2 until his death in 1349. One theory is that Rolle spent the early 1320s at the renowned <a href="/wiki/University_of_Paris" title="University of Paris">Sorbonne</a>, becoming well-trained in theology, and perhaps being ordained there.<sup id="cite_ref-Maynard_Smith,_p._346_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Maynard_Smith,_p._346-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> This theory is based on the entries in three seventeenth-century manuscripts at the Sorbonne, assumed to be copies of medieval originals, which record a Ricardus de Hampole as being admitted to the Sorbonne in 1320, entering the prior's register in 1326, and noting that he died in 1349 among the sisters of Hampole near Doncaster in Yorkshire. Scholars, however, are divided on the authenticity of this material.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Whether or not Rolle studied in Paris, it is probable that most if not all of this time was spent in <a href="/wiki/Richmondshire" title="Richmondshire">Richmondshire</a>, either living with his family at <a href="/wiki/Yafforth" title="Yafforth">Yafforth</a>, or, given the uncertain political conditions in the region at the time, wandering from patron to patron.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Around 1348, Rolle knew the Yorkshire <a href="/wiki/Anchorite" title="Anchorite">anchoress</a> <a href="/wiki/Margaret_Kirkby" title="Margaret Kirkby">Margaret Kirkby</a>, who was his principal disciple and the recipient of much of his writings<sup id="cite_ref-autogenerated2_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated2-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> and would be important in establishing his later reputation.
</p><p>Rolle died in Michaelmas 1349 at the <a href="/wiki/Cistercian_nunnery" class="mw-redirect" title="Cistercian nunnery">Cistercian nunnery</a> at <a href="/wiki/Hampole" title="Hampole">Hampole</a>. Because of his time spent there, where he was director of the inmates, he is sometimes known as Richard Rolle of Hampole, or de Hampole. It is unclear what his function was there: he was not the nuns' official confessor, who was a Franciscan (in any case, it is unlikely he would have had ecclesiastical sanction for this, since unless the theory about his ordination in Paris is correct, he was probably not ordained, since his name is not in the list of those ordained in the dioceses of York or Durham in the relevant years).<sup id="cite_ref-Richard_Rolle_1988_p24_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Richard_Rolle_1988_p24-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> However he wrote <i>The Form of Living</i> and his English Psalter for a nun there, Margaret Kirkby (who later took up a similar life to Rolle, as an <a href="/wiki/Anchorite" title="Anchorite">anchoress</a>), and <i>Ego Dormio</i> for a nun at <a href="/wiki/Yedingham" title="Yedingham">Yedingham</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Maynard_Smith,_p._347_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Maynard_Smith,_p._347-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> It is possible that he died of the <a href="/wiki/Black_Death" title="Black Death">Black Death</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Kempe_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Kempe-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Maynard_Smith,_p._347_21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Maynard_Smith,_p._347-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> but there is no direct evidence for this. He was buried first in the nuns' cemetery at Hampole. Later records of people making offerings of candles at his shrine show that he was moved first to the chancel and then to his own chapel.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Richard_Rolle&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Rolle probably began writing in the early 1330s, and continued until his death – but there is no certain chronology of his various works. He wrote in both Latin and English, with his English works apparently all dating from after c. 1340.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The precise dating of Rolle's works is a matter of much modern dispute. The dates set out by <a href="/wiki/Hope_Emily_Allen" title="Hope Emily Allen">Hope Emily Allen</a> in 1927 have been widely used by later writers, but in 1991 Nicholas Watson set out a rather different vision of the chronology of Rolle's writing.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In one of his best-known works, <i>Incendium Amoris</i> (<i>The Fire of Love</i>), Rolle provides an account of his mystical experiences, which he describes as being of three kinds: a physical warmth in his body, a sense of wonderful sweetness, and a heavenly music that accompanied him as he chanted the <a href="/wiki/Psalms" title="Psalms">Psalms</a>. The book was widely read in the Middle Ages, and described the four <a href="/wiki/Purgative" class="mw-redirect" title="Purgative">purgative</a> stages that one had to go through to become closer to God: described as open door, heat, song, and sweetness.
</p><p>His last work was probably the English <i>The Form of Living</i>, written in autumn 1348 at the earliest. It is addressed to Margaret Kirkby, who entered her enclosure as a recluse on 12 December 1348, and is a <a href="/wiki/Vernacular" title="Vernacular">vernacular</a> guide for her life as an anchorite.<sup id="cite_ref-Richard_Rolle_1988_p41_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Richard_Rolle_1988_p41-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His works are often classified into commentaries, treatises and epistles. As such, the commentaries are:<sup id="cite_ref-Richard_Rolle_1988_p41_24-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Richard_Rolle_1988_p41-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<ul><li><i>Commentary on the Readings in the Office of the Dead taken from Job</i>. This commentary on nine readings from the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Job" title="Book of Job">Book of Job</a> which form part of the readings for the office of the dead was used extensively by York clergy in the fifteenth century. Surviving in forty-two manuscripts, it was the first of Rolle's works to be printed, in Oxford in 1483.<sup id="cite_ref-Jonathan_Hughes_1349_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jonathan_Hughes_1349-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>Commentary on the Canticles</i>, a commentary on the first two and a half verses of the <a href="/wiki/Song_of_Songs" title="Song of Songs">Song of Songs</a>. It survives in thirty manuscripts.<sup id="cite_ref-Jonathan_Hughes_1349_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jonathan_Hughes_1349-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Two <i>Commentaries on the Psalter</i>. One is in Latin, and may belong to the very early period after Rolle left Oxford. The second is an English commentary with translation of the Latin Psalms into English, which was designed to help <a href="/wiki/Margaret_Kirkby" title="Margaret Kirkby">Margaret Kirkby</a> to understand the doctrines behind the Psalms she was to chant in her anchorage. For nearly 200 years this commentary remained the only authorised translation of the Bible into English; it did not need diocesan permission for its use. It exists in about 20 manuscripts.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>Treatise on Psalm 20</i>, in Latin</li>
<li><i>Super Threnos</i>, a commentary on the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Lamentations" title="Book of Lamentations">Lamentations of Jeremiah</a></li>
<li><i>Commentary on the Apocalypse</i>, on the first six chapters of the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Revelation" title="Book of Revelation">Book of Revelation</a></li>
<li>Other commentaries on the <a href="/wiki/Lord%27s_Prayer" title="Lord&#39;s Prayer">Lord's Prayer</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Magnificat" title="Magnificat">Magnificat</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Apostles%27_Creed" title="Apostles&#39; Creed">Apostles' Creed</a></li>
<li>An English explication of the <a href="/wiki/Ten_Commandments" title="Ten Commandments">Ten Commandments</a></li>
<li><i>Super Mulierem Fortem</i>, a comment on Proverbs 31:10</li></ul>
<p>Other works include:
</p>
<ul><li>Two English <i>Meditations on the Passion</i></li>
<li><i>Judica me Deus</i>, probably his first work, written around 1330. This survives in four versions, and is an <i>apologia</i> for his hermit lifestyle, making use of the pastoral manual of a fellow Yorkshireman, <a href="/wiki/William_of_Pagula" title="William of Pagula">William of Pagula</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>Contra Amatores Mundi</i> (<i>Against the Lovers of the World</i>), which survives in 42 manuscripts.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>Incendium Amoris</i> (<i>The Fire of Love</i>), written before 1343 (the date of Rolle's marginal note), which survives in 44 manuscripts (15 from the Continent) and one Middle English translation.<sup id="cite_ref-Bernard_McGinn_2012_p344_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bernard_McGinn_2012_p344-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>Melos Amoris</i> (or <i>Melum Contemplativorum</i>) (<i>The Melody of Love</i>), which survives in 10 manuscripts.<sup id="cite_ref-Jonathan_Hughes_1349_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jonathan_Hughes_1349-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>The Form of Living</i>, his last work, which survives in 30 manuscripts. It contains 12 chapters and was written at Hampole for Margaret Kirkby when she was living in a cell in Richmondshire.<sup id="cite_ref-Jonathan_Hughes_1349_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jonathan_Hughes_1349-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<p>Three letters survive. All are addressed to single recipients, and contain much similar material:<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<ul><li><i>Emendatio Vitae</i> (<i>Emending of Life</i>). This was the most popular work of Rolle, with 110 manuscripts (17 from the Continent), and seven independent Middle English translations.<sup id="cite_ref-Bernard_McGinn_2012_p344_28-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bernard_McGinn_2012_p344-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><i>Ego Dormio</i>, a Middle English <a href="/wiki/Prosimetrum" title="Prosimetrum">prosimetrum</a>, one of two letters written for nuns. (the title comes from the incipit of the work, and is from <a href="/wiki/Song_of_Songs" title="Song of Songs">Song of Songs</a> 5.2)</li>
<li><i>The Commandment</i>, one of two letters written for nuns.</li></ul>
<p>Works once thought to be Rolle's:
</p>
<ul><li>While the most popular poem in Middle English, <i><a href="/wiki/Prick_of_Conscience" title="Prick of Conscience">The Pricke of Conscience</a></i>, was once attributed to him, it is now known to have been written by an anonymous <a href="/wiki/Yorkshire" title="Yorkshire">Yorkshire</a> author in the 14th century.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>It is now thought that <i>De Dei Misericordia</i>, a comment on Psalm 88:2, was written by John Waldeby in the later fourteenth century.</li></ul>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Later_reputation_and_veneration">Later reputation and veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Richard_Rolle&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Later reputation and veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Richard Rolle inspired a flourishing cult, especially in the north of England, which was still active at the time of the <a href="/wiki/English_Reformation" title="English Reformation">English Reformation</a>. Part of this may have been due to the efforts of Margaret Kirkby, who moved to the priory, probably between 1381 and 1383, to be near the body of her master, Rolle. Margaret may have spent the last 10 years of her life here,<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> and between 1381 and 1383 a <a href="/wiki/Liturgy_of_the_Hours" title="Liturgy of the Hours">liturgical office</a> for Rolle, including a great deal of biographical information about him, was written; it likely includes stories about him remembered by older members of the community.
</p><p>Rolle's works were widely read in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, more so even than Chaucer.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> Works of his survive in about 470 manuscripts written between 1390 and 1500, and in 10 sixteenth- and early-seventeenth-century printed editions (including the sixteenth-century edition by <a href="/wiki/Wynkyn_de_Worde" title="Wynkyn de Worde">Wynkyn de Worde</a>). In some manuscripts, Rolle's <i>Commentary on the Psalter</i> is interpolated with <a href="/wiki/Lollard" class="mw-redirect" title="Lollard">Lollard</a> teaching, providing indications of one group who read his work.<sup id="cite_ref-Jonathan_Hughes_1349_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jonathan_Hughes_1349-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Rolle's work was not uncontroversial. He was criticised by <a href="/wiki/Walter_Hilton" title="Walter Hilton">Walter Hilton</a> and the author of <i><a href="/wiki/The_Cloud_of_Unknowing" title="The Cloud of Unknowing">The Cloud of Unknowing</a></i>; a defence of Rolle's work was written by the hermit Thomas Basset in the late fourteenth century against the attack of an unnamed Carthusian.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The shrine and priory at his burial place of Hampole was dissolved on 19 November 1539.<sup id="cite_ref-Richard_Rolle_1988_p24_20-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Richard_Rolle_1988_p24-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> The remains can be seen in an old schoolhouse in Hampole.<sup id="cite_ref-Jonathan_Hughes_1349_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jonathan_Hughes_1349-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<li><i>English Writings of Richard Rolle Hermit of Hampole,</i> ed. <a href="/wiki/Hope_Emily_Allen" title="Hope Emily Allen">Hope Emily Allen</a> (1931)</li>
<li><i>The Contra amatores mundi of Richard Rolle of Hampole</i>, introduced and trans. Paul F. Theiner, (University of California Press, 1968) [translation into English, alongside Latin text, of <i>Liber de amore Dei contra amatores mundi</i>]</li>
<li><i>Le Chant d'Amour (Melos Amoris)</i>, ed. Francois Vandenbroucke, (Cerf, 1971) [This uses the Latin text of the edition of E.J.F. Arnould (1957), alongside a parallel French translation.]</li>
<li><i>The Fire of Love</i>, trans. Clifton Wolters, (Penguin, 1972)</li>
<li><i>Biblical commentaries: Short exposition of Psalm 20, Treatise on the Twentieth Psalm, Comment on the first verses of the Canticle of Canticles, Commentary on the Apocalypse</i>, trans. Robert Boenig, (Institut für Anglistik und Amerikanistik, Universität Salzburg, 1984)</li>
<li><i>Richard Rolle, the English writings</i>, trans., ed., and introduced by Rosamund Allen. Classics of Western Spirituality, (Paulist Press / SPCK, 1988) [includes modern editions of <i>The English Psalter and Commentary, The Ten Commandments, Meditations on the Passion, Ghostly Gladness, The Bee and the Stork, Desire and Delight, Ego Dormio, The Commandment</i>, and <i>The Form of Living</i>]</li>
<li><i>Richard Rolle: Prose and Verse</i>, ed. S.J. Ogilvie-Thomson, Early English Text Society 293, (Oxford: OUP, 1988) [This is the standard modern edition of many of Rolle's Middle English works, with the important exception of the English Psalter.]</li>
<li>Malcolm Robert Moyses, <i>Richard Rolle's Expositio super novem lectiones mortuorum</i>, 2 vols, (Salzburg, 1988)</li>
<li><i>The tractatus super psalmum vicesimum of Richard Rolle of Hampole</i>, ed. and trans. James C. Dolan, (Edwin Mellen Press, 1991)</li>
<li><i>The fire of love</i>, ed. by Halcyon Backhouse, (Hodder and Stoughton, 1992) [translation of <i>Incendium amoris</i>]</li>
<li><i>Richard Rolle: Emendatio Vitae. Orationes ad honorem nominis Ihesu</i>, ed. <a href="/wiki/Nicholas_Watson_(academic)" title="Nicholas Watson (academic)">Nicholas Watson</a>, (PIMS, 1995) [the Latin text of <i>Emendatio Vitae</i>]</li>
<li><i>Richard Rolle: Uncollected Prose and Verse with related Northern text</i>, ed. by Ralph Hanna for the <a href="/wiki/Early_English_Text_Society" title="Early English Text Society">Early English Text Society</a>, (Oxford University Press, 2008)</li>
<li><i>Richard Rolle: Unprinted Latin Writings</i>, ed. Ralph Hanna (Liverpool University Press, 2020)</li>
<li>Spahl, Rüdiger, ed., <i>De emendatione vitae. Eine kritische Ausgabe des lateinischen Textes von Richard Rolle mit einer Übersetzung ins Deutsche und Untersuchungen zu den lateinischen und englischen Handschriften</i> (Vandenhoeck &amp; Ruprecht, 2009). [critical edition of the <i>Emendatio Vitae</i>]</li>
