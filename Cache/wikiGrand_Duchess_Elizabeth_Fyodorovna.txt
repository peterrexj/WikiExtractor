<p><b>Grand Duchess Elizabeth Feodorovna of Russia</b> (born <b>Princess Elisabeth of Hesse and by Rhine</b>; 1 November 1864<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>– 18 July 1918), was a <a href="/wiki/German_Empire" title="German Empire">German</a> <a href="/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Hesse" title="Grand Duchy of Hesse">Hessian</a> and <a href="/wiki/Rhenish_Hesse" title="Rhenish Hesse">Rhenish</a> <a href="/wiki/Princess" title="Princess">princess</a> of the <a href="/wiki/House_of_Hesse-Darmstadt" class="mw-redirect" title="House of Hesse-Darmstadt">House of Hesse-Darmstadt</a>, and the wife of <a href="/wiki/Grand_Duke_Sergei_Alexandrovich_of_Russia" title="Grand Duke Sergei Alexandrovich of Russia">Grand Duke Sergei Alexandrovich of Russia</a>, the fifth son of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Alexander_II_of_Russia" title="Alexander II of Russia">Alexander II of Russia</a> and <a href="/wiki/Maria_Alexandrovna_(Marie_of_Hesse)" title="Maria Alexandrovna (Marie of Hesse)">Princess Marie of Hesse and by Rhine</a>.
</p><p>A granddaughter of <a href="/wiki/Queen_Victoria" title="Queen Victoria">Queen Victoria</a> and an older sister of <a href="/wiki/Alexandra_Feodorovna_(Alix_of_Hesse)" title="Alexandra Feodorovna (Alix of Hesse)">Alexandra</a>, the last Russian Empress, Elisabeth became famous in Russian society for her dignified beauty and charitable works among the poor. After the <a href="/wiki/Socialist_Revolutionary_Party" title="Socialist Revolutionary Party">Socialist Revolutionary Party</a>'s <a href="/wiki/SR_Combat_Organization" title="SR Combat Organization">Combat Organization</a> assassinated her husband with a bomb in 1905, Elisabeth publicly forgave Sergei's murderer, <a href="/wiki/Ivan_Kalyayev" title="Ivan Kalyayev">Ivan Kalyayev</a>, and campaigned without success for him to be pardoned. She then departed the Imperial Court and became a nun, founding the <a href="/wiki/Marfo-Mariinsky_Convent" title="Marfo-Mariinsky Convent">Marfo-Mariinsky Convent</a> dedicated to helping the downtrodden of <a href="/wiki/Moscow" title="Moscow">Moscow</a>. In 1918, she was arrested and ultimately murdered by the <a href="/wiki/Bolsheviks" title="Bolsheviks">Bolsheviks</a>. In 1981, she was canonized by the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church_Abroad" class="mw-redirect" title="Russian Orthodox Church Abroad">Russian Orthodox Church Abroad</a>, and in 1992 by the <a href="/wiki/Moscow_Patriarchate" class="mw-redirect" title="Moscow Patriarchate">Moscow Patriarchate</a>.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Princess_of_Hesse">Princess of Hesse</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1864%E2%80%931918)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Princess of Hesse">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Hessian_family_in_1876.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fc/The_Hessian_family_in_1876.jpg/220px-The_Hessian_family_in_1876.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="276" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fc/The_Hessian_family_in_1876.jpg/330px-The_Hessian_family_in_1876.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fc/The_Hessian_family_in_1876.jpg/440px-The_Hessian_family_in_1876.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1072" data-file-height="1344" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Hessian_family_in_1876.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>The Hessian grand ducal family in May 1875</div></div></div>
<p>Elisabeth was born on 1 November 1864 as the second child of <a href="/wiki/Ludwig_IV,_Grand_Duke_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine" class="mw-redirect" title="Ludwig IV, Grand Duke of Hesse and by Rhine">Ludwig IV, Grand Duke of Hesse and by Rhine</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Princess_Alice_of_the_United_Kingdom" title="Princess Alice of the United Kingdom">Princess Alice</a>, daughter of <a href="/wiki/Queen_Victoria" title="Queen Victoria">Queen Victoria</a>. Though she came from one of the oldest and most noble houses in Germany, Elisabeth and her family lived a rather modest life by royal standards. The children swept the floors and cleaned their own rooms, while their mother sewed dresses herself for the children. During the <a href="/wiki/Austro-Prussian_War" title="Austro-Prussian War">Austro-Prussian War</a>, Princess Alice often took Elisabeth with her while visiting wounded soldiers in a nearby hospital. In this relatively happy and secure environment, Elisabeth grew up surrounded by English domestic habits, and English became her first language. Later in life, she would tell a friend that, within her family, she and her siblings spoke English to their mother and German to their father.
</p><p>In the autumn of 1878, <a href="/wiki/Diphtheria" title="Diphtheria">diphtheria</a> swept through the Hesse household, killing Elisabeth's youngest sister, <a href="/wiki/Princess_Marie_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1874%E2%80%931878)" title="Princess Marie of Hesse and by Rhine (1874–1878)">Marie</a>, on 16 November, as well as her mother Alice on 14 December. Elisabeth had been sent away to her <a href="/wiki/Princess_Elizabeth_of_Prussia" class="mw-redirect" title="Princess Elizabeth of Prussia">paternal grandmother</a>'s home at the beginning of the outbreak and she was the only member of her family to remain unaffected. When she was finally allowed to return home, she described the meeting as "terribly sad" and said that everything was "like a horrible dream".<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (December 2007)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Admirers_and_suitors">Admirers and suitors</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1864%E2%80%931918)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Admirers and suitors">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1883.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1883.jpg/220px-Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1883.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="310" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1883.jpg/330px-Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1883.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1883.jpg/440px-Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1883.jpg 2x" data-file-width="664" data-file-height="936" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1883.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Elisabeth of Hesse, 1883</div></div></div>
<p>Charming and with a very accommodating personality, Elisabeth was considered by many historians and contemporaries to be one of the most beautiful women in Europe at that time. Her cousin <a href="/wiki/Princess_Marie_of_Edinburgh" class="mw-redirect" title="Princess Marie of Edinburgh">Princess Marie of Edinburgh</a> wrote that "one could never take one's eyes off [Ella]"<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> and that Ella's features were "exquisite beyond words, it almost brought tears to your eyes."<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Her older cousin <a href="/wiki/Wilhelm_II,_German_Emperor" title="Wilhelm II, German Emperor">Prince Wilhelm of Prussia</a> called her "exceedingly beautiful, in fact she is the most beautiful girl I ever saw."<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Baroness <a href="/wiki/Sophie_Buxhoeveden" title="Sophie Buxhoeveden">Sophie Buxhoeveden</a>, her <a href="/wiki/Alexandra_Feodorovna_(Alix_of_Hesse)" title="Alexandra Feodorovna (Alix of Hesse)">sister</a>'s lady-in-waiting, reflected that she was "a very pretty girl, tall and fair, with regular features."<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Elisabeth was a young woman her cousin, <a href="/wiki/Wilhelm_II,_German_Emperor" title="Wilhelm II, German Emperor">Prince Wilhelm of Prussia</a>, fell in love with her. In April 1875, the 16-year-old Wilhelm visited Darmstadt to celebrate <a href="/wiki/Princess_Victoria_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine" title="Princess Victoria of Hesse and by Rhine">Princess Victoria of Hesse and by Rhine</a>'s 12th birthday and first expressed interest in the 11-year-old Elisabeth. He wrote in a letter to his mother that "if God grants that I may live till then I shall make her my bride once if you allow it."<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> When he was a student at <a href="/wiki/University_of_Bonn" title="University of Bonn">Bonn University</a>, he often visited his Aunt Alice and his Hessian relatives on the weekends. During these frequent visits, he fell in love with Elisabeth,<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> writing numerous love poems and regularly sending them to her. He proposed to Elisabeth in 1878, but she rejected him.
</p><p>Lord Charles Montagu, the second son of the <a href="/wiki/William_Montagu,_7th_Duke_of_Manchester" title="William Montagu, 7th Duke of Manchester">7th Duke of Manchester</a> courted her unsuccessfully.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Sir_Henry_Wilson,_1st_Baronet" title="Sir Henry Wilson, 1st Baronet">Henry Wilson</a>, later a distinguished soldier, vied unsuccessfully for Elisabeth's hand. 
</p><p>The future <a href="/wiki/Frederick_II,_Grand_Duke_of_Baden" title="Frederick II, Grand Duke of Baden">Frederick II, Grand Duke of Baden</a>, Wilhelm's first cousin, proposed to Elisabeth. Queen Victoria described him as "so good and steady", with "such a safe and happy position,"<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> that when Elisabeth declined to marry him the Queen "deeply regretted it". Frederick's grandmother, the <a href="/wiki/Augusta_of_Saxe-Weimar" class="mw-redirect" title="Augusta of Saxe-Weimar">Empress Augusta</a>, was so furious at Elisabeth's rejection of Frederick that it took some time for her to forgive Elisabeth.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1887_(a).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ce/Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1887_%28a%29.jpg/220px-Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1887_%28a%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="331" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ce/Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1887_%28a%29.jpg/330px-Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1887_%28a%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ce/Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1887_%28a%29.jpg/440px-Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1887_%28a%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="997" data-file-height="1500" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_1887_(a).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Princess Elisabeth of Hesse, 1887</div></div></div>
<p>Other admirers included:
</p>
<ul><li><a href="/wiki/Grand_Duke_Konstantin_Konstantinovich_of_Russia" title="Grand Duke Konstantin Konstantinovich of Russia">Grand Duke Konstantin Konstantinovich of Russia</a> (the poet KR), who wrote a poem about her first arrival in Russia and the general impression she made to all the people present at the time.</li>
<li>As a young girl, <a href="/wiki/Queen_Marie_of_Romania" class="mw-redirect" title="Queen Marie of Romania">Queen Marie of Romania</a> was very fascinated with her cousin Ella. In her memoirs, she wrote that "her beauty and sweetness was a thing of dreams."<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>The French Ambassador to the Russian court, <a href="/wiki/Maurice_Paleologue" class="mw-redirect" title="Maurice Paleologue">Maurice Paleologue</a>, wrote in his memoirs how Elisabeth was capable of arousing what he described as "profane passions".</li></ul>
<p>Ultimately, it was a <a href="/wiki/Grand_duke#Russian_grand_dukes" title="Grand duke">grand duke of Russia</a> who would win Elisabeth's heart; Elisabeth's great-aunt, <a href="/wiki/Maria_Alexandrovna_(Marie_of_Hesse)" title="Maria Alexandrovna (Marie of Hesse)">Empress Maria Alexandrovna of Russia</a>, was a frequent visitor to Hesse. During these visits, she was usually accompanied by her youngest sons, <a href="/wiki/Grand_Duke_Sergei_Alexandrovich_of_Russia" title="Grand Duke Sergei Alexandrovich of Russia">Sergei</a> and <a href="/wiki/Grand_Duke_Paul_Alexandrovich_of_Russia" title="Grand Duke Paul Alexandrovich of Russia">Paul</a>. Elisabeth had known them since they were children, and she initially viewed them as haughty and reserved. Sergei, especially, was a very serious young man, intensely religious, and he found himself attracted to Elisabeth after seeing her as a young woman for the first time in several years.
</p><p>At first, Sergei made little impression on Elisabeth. But after the death of both of his parents within a year of each other, Elisabeth sympathised with Sergei because she had felt this same grief after the death of her mother. Their other similarities (both were artistic and religious) drew them closer together. It was said that Sergei was especially attached to Elisabeth because she had the same character as his beloved mother. So when Sergei proposed to her in the spring of 1883, she accepted—much to the chagrin of her grandmother Queen Victoria, who tried to persuade Elisabeth to end the engagement. But when Sergei proposed again later in the same year, she accepted him once more, and arrangements for their wedding went ahead.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Grand_Duchess_of_Russia">Grand Duchess of Russia</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1864%E2%80%931918)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Grand Duchess of Russia">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Sergei_Alexandrovich_and_his_wife.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Sergei_Alexandrovich_and_his_wife.jpg/220px-Sergei_Alexandrovich_and_his_wife.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="318" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Sergei_Alexandrovich_and_his_wife.jpg/330px-Sergei_Alexandrovich_and_his_wife.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Sergei_Alexandrovich_and_his_wife.jpg/440px-Sergei_Alexandrovich_and_his_wife.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1038" data-file-height="1500" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Sergei_Alexandrovich_and_his_wife.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Elisabeth and her husband Sergei</div></div></div>
<p>Sergei and Elisabeth married on 15 (3) June 1884, at the <a href="/wiki/Grand_Church_of_the_Winter_Palace" title="Grand Church of the Winter Palace">Chapel</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Winter_Palace" title="Winter Palace">Winter Palace</a> in St. Petersburg; upon her conversion to <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodoxy" title="Russian Orthodoxy">Russian Orthodoxy</a>, she took the name <i>Elizaveta Feodorovna</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> It was at the wedding that Sergei's 16-year-old nephew, <a href="/wiki/Nicholas_II_of_Russia" title="Nicholas II of Russia">Tsarevich Nicholas</a>, first met his future wife, Elisabeth's youngest surviving sister <a href="/wiki/Alexandra_Feodorovna_(Alix_of_Hesse)" title="Alexandra Feodorovna (Alix of Hesse)">Alix</a>.
</p><p>Elisabeth was not legally required to convert to <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodoxy</a> from her native <a href="/wiki/Lutheran" class="mw-redirect" title="Lutheran">Lutheran</a> religion, but she voluntarily chose to do so in 1891. <a href="/wiki/Duchess_Marie_of_Mecklenburg-Schwerin" title="Duchess Marie of Mecklenburg-Schwerin">Duchess Marie of Mecklenburg-Schwerin</a>, Elisabeth's Lutheran sister-in-law who had not converted to <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodoxy</a>, insisted that it was "a disgrace for a German Protestant princess to go over to the Orthodox faith."<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Kaiser <a href="/wiki/Wilhelm_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Wilhelm II">Wilhelm II</a>, who had once been in love with her, declared that she converted because of "an inordinate pursuit of popularity, a desire to improve her position at court, a great lack of intelligence, and also a want of true religiousness."<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Grand_Duchess_Elisabeth_Fedorovna_in_1885.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/Grand_Duchess_Elisabeth_Fedorovna_in_1885.jpg/220px-Grand_Duchess_Elisabeth_Fedorovna_in_1885.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="349" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/Grand_Duchess_Elisabeth_Fedorovna_in_1885.jpg/330px-Grand_Duchess_Elisabeth_Fedorovna_in_1885.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/Grand_Duchess_Elisabeth_Fedorovna_in_1885.jpg/440px-Grand_Duchess_Elisabeth_Fedorovna_in_1885.jpg 2x" data-file-width="569" data-file-height="902" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Grand_Duchess_Elisabeth_Fedorovna_in_1885.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Grand Duchess Elisabeth Fedorovna in 1885</div></div></div>
<p>The new Grand Duchess made a good first impression on her husband's family and the Russian people. "Everyone fell in love with her from the moment she came to Russia from her beloved Darmstadt", wrote one of Sergei's cousins, <a href="/wiki/Grand_Duke_Konstantin_Konstantinovich_of_Russia" title="Grand Duke Konstantin Konstantinovich of Russia">Grand Duke Konstantin Konstantinovich of Russia</a> (the poet KR). The French Ambassador to the Russian court, <a href="/wiki/Maurice_Paleologue" class="mw-redirect" title="Maurice Paleologue">Maurice Paleologue</a>, wrote in his memoirs how Elisabeth was capable of arousing what he described as "profane passions". The couple settled in the <a href="/wiki/Beloselsky-Belozersky_Palace" title="Beloselsky-Belozersky Palace">Beloselsky-Belozersky Palace</a> in St. Petersburg; after Sergei was appointed Governor-General of Moscow by his elder brother, <a href="/wiki/Tsar_Alexander_III" class="mw-redirect" title="Tsar Alexander III">Tsar Alexander III</a>, in 1892, they resided in one of the <a href="/wiki/Kremlin" title="Kremlin">Kremlin</a> palaces. During the summer, they stayed at Ilyinskoe, an estate outside Moscow that Sergei had inherited from his mother.
</p><p>The couple never had children of their own, but their Ilyinskoe estate was usually filled with parties that Elisabeth organized especially for children. They eventually became the foster parents of <a href="/wiki/Grand_Duke_Dmitri_Pavlovich_of_Russia" title="Grand Duke Dmitri Pavlovich of Russia">Grand Duke Dmitry Pavlovich</a> and <a href="/wiki/Grand_Duchess_Maria_Pavlovna_of_Russia_(1890-1958)" class="mw-redirect" title="Grand Duchess Maria Pavlovna of Russia (1890-1958)">Grand Duchess Maria Pavlovna</a>, Sergei's niece and nephew. <a href="/wiki/Prince_Felix_Yusupov" class="mw-redirect" title="Prince Felix Yusupov">Prince Felix Yusupov</a> considered Elisabeth a second mother, and stated in his memoirs that she helped him greatly during the most difficult moments of his life.
</p><p>Elisabeth was instrumental in the marriage of her nephew-by-marriage, Tsar Nicholas II, to her youngest sister Alix. Much to the dismay of Queen Victoria, Elisabeth had been encouraging Nicholas, then tsarevich, in his pursuit of Alix. When Nicholas did propose to Alix in 1894, and Alix rejected him on the basis of her refusal to convert to Orthodoxy, it was Elisabeth who spoke with Alix and encouraged her to convert. When Nicholas proposed to her again, a few days later, Alix then accepted.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Ivan_Kalyayev.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/00/Ivan_Kalyayev.jpg/220px-Ivan_Kalyayev.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="290" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/00/Ivan_Kalyayev.jpg/330px-Ivan_Kalyayev.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/00/Ivan_Kalyayev.jpg 2x" data-file-width="387" data-file-height="511" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Ivan_Kalyayev.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Photograph of Ivan Kalyayev taken just after the assassination. <i>I threw the bomb from less than four steps. I was taken by the explosions, I saw the carriage flew to pieces... My overcoat was strewn with splinters of wood all around, it was torn and burnt, there was blood on my face...</i></div></div></div>
<p>On 17 February 1905, Sergei was assassinated in the <a href="/wiki/Kremlin" title="Kremlin">Kremlin</a> by the Socialist-Revolutionary <a href="/wiki/Ivan_Kalyayev" title="Ivan Kalyayev">Ivan Kalyayev</a>. The event came as a terrible shock to Elisabeth, but she never lost her calm. It was as if her prophecy had come true that "God will punish us severely" which she made after the Grand Duke expelled 20,000 Jews from Moscow, by simply surrounding thousands of families' houses with soldiers and expelling the Jews without any notice overnight out of their homes and the city. Her niece Marie later recalled that her aunt's face was "pale and stricken rigid" and she would never forget her expression of infinite sadness. In her rooms, said Marie, Elisabeth "let herself fall weakly into an armchair...her eyes dry and with the same peculiar fixity of gaze, she looked straight into space, and said nothing." As visitors came and went, she looked without ever seeming to see them. Throughout the day of her husband's murder, Elisabeth refused to cry. But Marie recalled how her aunt slowly abandoned her rigid self-control, finally breaking down into sobs. Many of her family and friends feared that she would suffer a nervous breakdown, but she quickly recovered her equanimity.
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/Edvard_Radzinsky" title="Edvard Radzinsky">Edvard Radzinsky</a>, 
</p>
<blockquote><p>Elizabeth spent all the days before the burial in ceaseless prayer. On her husband's tombstone she wrote: 'Father, release them, they know not what they do.' She understood the words of the <a href="/wiki/Gospels" class="mw-redirect" title="Gospels">Gospels</a> heart and soul, and on the eve of the funeral she demanded to be taken to the prison where Kalyayev was being held. Brought into his cell, she asked, 'Why did you kill my husband?' 'I killed Sergei Alexandrovich because he was a weapon of tyranny. I was taking revenge for the people.' 'Do not listen to your pride. Repent... and I will beg the Sovereign to give you your life. I will ask him for you. I myself have already forgiven you.' On the eve of revolution, she had already found a way out; forgiveness! Forgive through the impossible pain and blood -- and thereby stop it then, at the beginning, this bloody wheel. By her example, poor Ella appealed to society, calling upon the people to live in Christian faith. 'No!" replied Kalyayev. 'I do not repent. I must die for my deed and I will... My death will be more useful to my cause than Sergei Alexandrovich's death.' Kalyayev was sentenced to death. 'I am pleased with your sentence,' he told the judges. 'I hope that you will carry it out just as openly and publicly as I carried out the sentence of the <a href="/wiki/Socialist_Revolutionary_Party" title="Socialist Revolutionary Party">Socialist Revolutionary Party</a>. Learn to look the advancing revolution right in the face.'<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote> 
<p>Kalyayev was hanged on 23 May 1905.
</p><p>In 1915, the <a href="/wiki/All-Russian_Zemstvo_Union" title="All-Russian Zemstvo Union">All-Russian Zemstvo Union</a> was organised under Elisabeth's auspices to provide support for sick and injured soldiers during the <a href="/wiki/First_World_War" class="mw-redirect" title="First World War">First World War</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-GME_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-GME-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Religious_life">Religious life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1864%E2%80%931918)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Religious life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Elisabeth_Fyodorovna.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/33/Elisabeth_Fyodorovna.jpg/220px-Elisabeth_Fyodorovna.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="313" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/33/Elisabeth_Fyodorovna.jpg/330px-Elisabeth_Fyodorovna.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/33/Elisabeth_Fyodorovna.jpg 2x" data-file-width="352" data-file-height="500" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Elisabeth_Fyodorovna.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Grand Duchess Elizabeth Feodorovna as a nun after her husband's death</div></div></div>
<p>After Sergei's death, Elisabeth wore mourning clothes and became a vegetarian. In 1909, she sold off her magnificent collection of jewels and other luxurious possessions; even her wedding ring was not spared. With the proceeds, she opened the <a href="/wiki/Marfo-Mariinsky_Convent" title="Marfo-Mariinsky Convent">Convent of Saints Martha and Mary</a> and became its <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a>. She soon opened a hospital, chapel, pharmacy and orphanage on its grounds. 
</p><p>Elisabeth and her fellow nuns worked tirelessly among the poor and the sick of Moscow. She often visited Moscow's worst slums and did all she could to help alleviate the suffering of the poor. For many years, her institution helped the poor and the orphans in Moscow by fostering the prayer and charity of devout women. 
</p><p>In 1916, Elisabeth had what was to be her final meeting with her sister Alexandra, the tsarina, at <a href="/wiki/Tsarskoye_Selo" title="Tsarskoye Selo">Tsarskoye Selo</a>. While the meeting took place in private, the tutor to the tsar's children apparently recalled that the discussion included Elisabeth expressing her concerns over the influence that <a href="/wiki/Grigori_Rasputin" title="Grigori Rasputin">Grigori Rasputin</a> had over Alexandra and the imperial court, and begging her to heed the warnings of both herself and other members of the imperial family.
</p><p>In 2010, a historian claimed that Elisabeth may have been aware that the murder of Rasputin was to take place and secondly, she knew who was going to commit it when she wrote a letter and sent it to the Tsar and two telegrams to Grand Duke <a href="/wiki/Dmitri_Pavlovich_Romanov" class="mw-redirect" title="Dmitri Pavlovich Romanov">Dmitri Pavlovich</a> and her friend <a href="/wiki/Zinaida_Yusupova" title="Zinaida Yusupova">Zinaida Yusupova</a>. The telegrams, which were written the night of the murder, reveal that Elisabeth was aware of who the murderers were before that information had been released to the public, and she stated that she felt that the killing was a "patriotic act."<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1864%E2%80%931918)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1918, <a href="/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin" title="Vladimir Lenin">Vladimir Lenin</a> ordered the <a href="/wiki/Cheka" title="Cheka">Cheka</a> to arrest Elisabeth. They then exiled her first to <a href="/wiki/Perm,_Russia" title="Perm, Russia">Perm</a>, then to <a href="/wiki/Yekaterinburg" title="Yekaterinburg">Yekaterinburg</a>, where she spent a few days and was joined by others: the <a href="/wiki/Grand_Duke_Sergei_Mikhailovich" class="mw-redirect" title="Grand Duke Sergei Mikhailovich">Grand Duke Sergei Mikhailovich</a>; Princes <a href="/wiki/Prince_John_Konstantinovich_of_Russia" title="Prince John Konstantinovich of Russia">John Konstantinovich</a>, <a href="/wiki/Prince_Constantine_Constantinovich_of_Russia" title="Prince Constantine Constantinovich of Russia">Konstantin Konstantinovich</a>, <a href="/wiki/Igor_Konstantinovich_of_Russia" class="mw-redirect" title="Igor Konstantinovich of Russia">Igor Konstantinovich</a> and <a href="/wiki/Vladimir_Paley" title="Vladimir Paley">Vladimir Pavlovich Paley</a>; Grand Duke Sergei's secretary, Fyodor Remez; and <a href="/wiki/Varvara_Yakovleva" class="mw-redirect" title="Varvara Yakovleva">Varvara Yakovleva</a>, a sister from the Grand Duchess's convent. They were all taken to <a href="/wiki/Alapayevsk" title="Alapayevsk">Alapayevsk</a> on 20 May 1918, where they were housed in the Napolnaya School on the outskirts of the town.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:%D0%90%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B0%D0%B5%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D1%88%D0%B0%D1%85%D1%82%D0%B0.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/%D0%90%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B0%D0%B5%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D1%88%D0%B0%D1%85%D1%82%D0%B0.jpg/220px-%D0%90%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B0%D0%B5%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D1%88%D0%B0%D1%85%D1%82%D0%B0.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="290" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/%D0%90%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B0%D0%B5%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D1%88%D0%B0%D1%85%D1%82%D0%B0.jpg/330px-%D0%90%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B0%D0%B5%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D1%88%D0%B0%D1%85%D1%82%D0%B0.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/%D0%90%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B0%D0%B5%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D1%88%D0%B0%D1%85%D1%82%D0%B0.jpg/440px-%D0%90%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B0%D0%B5%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D1%88%D0%B0%D1%85%D1%82%D0%B0.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1102" data-file-height="1452" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:%D0%90%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B0%D0%B5%D0%B2%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D1%88%D0%B0%D1%85%D1%82%D0%B0.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Mine shaft in Siniachikha where Elisabeth and her family were murdered</div></div></div>
<p>At noon on 17 July, Cheka officer Pyotr Startsev and a few Bolshevik workers came to the school. They took from the prisoners whatever money they had left and announced that they would be transferred that night to the Upper Siniachikhensky factory compound. The Red Army guards were told to leave and Cheka men replaced them. That night the prisoners were awakened and driven in carts on a road leading to the village of Siniachikha, some 18 kilometres (11 miles) from Alapayevsk where there was an abandoned iron mine with a pit 20 metres (66 feet) deep. Here they halted. The Cheka beat all the prisoners before throwing their victims into this pit, Elisabeth being the first. Hand grenades were then hurled down the shaft, but only one victim, Fyodor Remez, died as a result of the grenades.
</p><p>According to the personal account of Vasily Ryabov, one of the executioners, Elisabeth and the others survived the initial fall into the mine, prompting Ryabov to toss in a grenade after them. Following the explosion, he claimed to have heard Elisabeth and the others singing an Orthodox hymn from the bottom of the shaft.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Unnerved, Ryabov threw down a second grenade, but the singing continued. Finally a large quantity of brushwood was shoved into the opening and set alight, upon which Ryabov posted a guard over the site and departed.
</p><p>Early on 18 July 1918, the leader of the Alapayevsk Cheka, Abramov, and the head of the Yekaterinburg Regional Soviet, Beloborodov, who had been involved in the murder of the Imperial Family, exchanged a number of telegrams in a pre-arranged plan saying that the school had been attacked by an "unidentified gang". A month later, Alapayevsk fell to the <a href="/wiki/White_Army" title="White Army">White Army</a> of <a href="/wiki/Admiral" title="Admiral">Admiral</a> <a href="/wiki/Alexander_Kolchak" title="Alexander Kolchak">Alexander Kolchak</a>. Lenin welcomed Elisabeth's death, remarking that "virtue with the crown on it is a greater enemy to the <a href="/wiki/World_revolution" title="World revolution">world revolution</a> than a hundred tyrant tsars".<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Fate_of_the_remains">Fate of the remains</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1864%E2%80%931918)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Fate of the remains">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>On 8 October 1918, <a href="/wiki/White_Army" title="White Army">White Army</a> soldiers discovered the remains of Elisabeth and her companions, still within the shaft where they had been murdered. Despite having lain there for almost three months, the bodies were in relatively good condition. Most were thought to have died slowly from injuries or starvation, rather than the subsequent fire. Elisabeth had died of wounds sustained in her fall into the mine, but before her death had still found strength to bandage the head of the dying Prince John with her wimple.  With the <a href="/wiki/Red_Army" title="Red Army">Red Army</a> approaching, their remains were removed further east and buried in the cemetery of the Russian Orthodox Mission in Peking (now <a href="/wiki/Beijing" title="Beijing">Beijing</a>), China.  In 1921, the bodies of Elisabeth and of Sister Barbara (Varvara Yakovleva), one of her nuns, were taken to <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>, where they were laid to rest in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Mary_Magdalene" title="Church of Mary Magdalene">Church of Mary Magdalene</a> at Gethsemane.  The Russian Orthodox Mission in Beijing was demolished in 1957 and its cemetery paved over as a parking lot in 1986.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Canonisation">Canonisation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1864%E2%80%931918)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Canonisation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Elisabeth was <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonised</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church_Outside_Russia" class="mw-redirect" title="Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia">Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia</a> in 1981, and in 1992 by the <a href="/wiki/Moscow_Patriarchate" class="mw-redirect" title="Moscow Patriarchate">Moscow Patriarchate</a> as <b><a href="/wiki/New_Martyr" title="New Martyr">Holy Martyr</a> Elizabeth Feodorovna</b>. Her principal <a href="/wiki/Shrine" title="Shrine">shrines</a> are the <a href="/wiki/Marfo-Mariinsky_Convent" title="Marfo-Mariinsky Convent">Marfo-Mariinsky Convent</a> she founded in Moscow, and the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Mary_Magdalene" title="Church of Mary Magdalene">Saint Mary Magdalene Convent on the Mount of Olives</a>, which she and her husband helped build, and where her <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> (along with those of <a href="/wiki/Barbara_(Yakovleva)" title="Barbara (Yakovleva)">Nun Barbara</a> (Varvara Yakovleva, her former maid) are enshrined.
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Commemoration">Commemoration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1864%E2%80%931918)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Commemoration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>She is one of the ten 20th-century martyrs from across the world who are depicted in statues above the Great West Door of <a href="/wiki/Westminster_Abbey" title="Westminster Abbey">Westminster Abbey</a>, London, England,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> and she is also represented in the restored nave screen installed at <a href="/wiki/St_Albans_Cathedral" title="St Albans Cathedral">St Albans Cathedral</a> in April 2015.<sup id="cite_ref-BBCNewStatues_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-BBCNewStatues-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A statue of Elisabeth was erected in the garden of her convent in Moscow after the <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union" title="Dissolution of the Soviet Union">dissolution of the Soviet Union</a>. Its inscription reads: "To the Grand Duchess Elizabeth Feodorovna: With Repentance."
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Rehabilitation">Rehabilitation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Princess_Elisabeth_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_(1864%E2%80%931918)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Rehabilitation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>On 8 June 2009, the <a href="/wiki/Prosecutor_General_of_Russia" class="mw-redirect" title="Prosecutor General of Russia">Prosecutor General of Russia</a> officially posthumously <a href="/wiki/Rehabilitation_(Soviet)" title="Rehabilitation (Soviet)">rehabilitated</a> Elizabeth Feodorovna, along with other Romanovs: Mikhail Alexandrovich, Sergei Mikhailovich, John Konstantinovich, Konstantin Konstantinovich and Igor Konstantinovich.  "All of these people were subjected to repression in the form of arrest, deportation and being held by the <a href="/wiki/Cheka" title="Cheka">Cheka</a> without charge," said a representative of the office.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
