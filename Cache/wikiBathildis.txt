<p><b>Balthild</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;b&#39; in &#39;buy&#39;">b</span><span title="/ɔː/: &#39;au&#39; in &#39;fraud&#39;">ɔː</span><span title="&#39;l&#39; in &#39;lie&#39;">l</span><span title="&#39;t&#39; in &#39;tie&#39;">t</span><span title="/ɪ/: &#39;i&#39; in &#39;kit&#39;">ɪ</span><span title="&#39;l&#39; in &#39;lie&#39;">l</span><span title="&#39;d&#39; in &#39;dye&#39;">d</span></span>/</a></span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a>&#58; <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">Bealdhild</i></span>, 'bold sword' or 'bold spear; around 626 – 30 January 680),<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> also spelled <b>Bathilda</b>, <b>Bauthieult</b> or <b>Baudour</b>, was <a href="/wiki/Queen_consort" title="Queen consort">queen consort</a> of Neustria and Burgundy by marriage to <a href="/wiki/Clovis_II" title="Clovis II">Clovis II</a>, the King of Neustria and Burgundy (639–658), and regent during the minority of her son. Her <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> was intended to further her successful candidature for sainthood.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Tradition represents her as an <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon">Anglo-Saxon</a> who was originally of noble birth, perhaps a relative of <a href="/wiki/Ricberht_of_East_Anglia" title="Ricberht of East Anglia">Ricberht of East Anglia</a>, the last <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_paganism" title="Anglo-Saxon paganism">pagan</a> king of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">East Anglia</a>, although Pierre Fournet regards this as doubtful.<sup id="cite_ref-Fournet_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fournet-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Ricberht was ousted by <a href="/wiki/Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia" title="Sigeberht of East Anglia">Sigeberht</a>, who had spent time as an exile in the <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Frankish</a> court, during which he had converted to <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a>. Sigeberht was established as the rightful heir to the throne with Frankish help.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Hagiographic_tradition">Hagiographic tradition</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Balthild_of_Chelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Hagiographic tradition">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to <i>Vita S. Bathildis</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg1996264_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg1996264-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Balthild was born circa 626–627. She was beautiful, intelligent, modest and attentive to the needs of others. Balthild was sold into <a href="/wiki/Slavery" title="Slavery">slavery</a> as a young girl and served in the household of <a href="/wiki/Erchinoald" title="Erchinoald">Erchinoald</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Mayor_of_the_palace" title="Mayor of the palace">mayor of the palace</a> of Neustria to Clovis. Erchinoald, whose wife had died, was attracted to Balthild and wanted to marry her, but she did not want to marry him. She hid herself away and waited until Erchinoald had remarried. Later, possibly because of Erchinoald, Clovis noticed her and asked for her hand in marriage.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETheuwsDe_Jongvan_Rhijn2001255_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETheuwsDe_Jongvan_Rhijn2001255-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMadigan199860_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMadigan199860-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Even as queen, Balthild remained humble and modest. She is famous for her charitable service and generous donations. From her donations, the <a href="/wiki/Abbey" title="Abbey">abbeys</a> of <a href="/wiki/Corbie_Abbey" title="Corbie Abbey">Corbie</a> and <a href="/wiki/Chelles_Abbey" title="Chelles Abbey">Chelles</a> were founded; it is likely that others such as <a href="/wiki/Jumi%C3%A8ges_Abbey" title="Jumièges Abbey">Jumièges</a>, <a href="/wiki/Jouarre_Abbey" title="Jouarre Abbey">Jouarre</a> and <a href="/wiki/Luxeuil_Abbey" title="Luxeuil Abbey">Luxeuil</a> were also founded by the queen. She provided support for <a href="/wiki/Claudius_of_Besan%C3%A7on" title="Claudius of Besançon">Claudius of Besançon</a> and his abbey in the <a href="/wiki/Jura_Mountains" title="Jura Mountains">Jura Mountains</a>.
</p><p>Balthild bore Clovis three children, all of whom became kings: <a href="/wiki/Clotaire_III" class="mw-redirect" title="Clotaire III">Clotaire</a>, <a href="/wiki/Childeric_II" title="Childeric II">Childeric</a> and <a href="/wiki/Theuderic_III" title="Theuderic III">Theuderic</a>.
</p><p>When Clovis died (between 655 and 658), his eldest son Clotaire succeeded to the throne. His mother Balthild acted as the queen regent. As queen, she was a capable stateswoman. She abolished the practice of trading Christian slaves and strove to free children who had been sold into slavery. Balthild and Eligius, according to Dado, "worked together on their favourite charity, the buying and freeing of slaves".<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> After a power struggle with mayor <a href="/wiki/Ebroin" title="Ebroin">Ebroin</a>, Balthild withdrew to her favourite Abbey of Chelles near Paris.<sup id="cite_ref-Fournet_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fournet-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Balthild died on 30 January 680 and was buried at the Abbey of Chelles, east of <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a>. Her <i><a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">Vita</a></i> was written soon after her death, probably by one of the community of Chelles. The <i>Vita Baldechildis/Vita Bathildis reginae Francorum</i> in <i>Monumenta Germania Historica, Scriptores Rerum Merovincarum</i>, as with most of the <i>vitae</i> of royal <a href="/wiki/Merovingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingian">Merovingian</a>-era saints, provides some useful details for the historian. Her official cult began when her remains were transferred from the former abbey to a new church, in 833, under the auspices of <a href="/wiki/Louis_the_Pious" title="Louis the Pious">Louis the Pious</a>. Balthild was <a href="/wiki/Canonised" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonised">canonised</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Nicholas_I" title="Pope Nicholas I">Pope Nicholas I</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Fournet_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fournet-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> around 200 years after her death.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_sources">Other sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Balthild_of_Chelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Other sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><i>Sainted Women of the Dark Ages</i> states that Balthild "was not the first Merovingian queen to begin her career in servitude".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg1996264–278_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg1996264–278-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Slavery_in_Merovingian_France" class="mw-redirect" title="Slavery in Merovingian France">Other Merovingian queens who arose from servile status</a> include <a href="/wiki/Fredegund" title="Fredegund">Fredegund</a>, the mother of <a href="/wiki/Clothaire_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Clothaire II">Clothaire II</a>; <a href="/wiki/Bilichild" title="Bilichild">Bilichild</a>, the wife of Theudebert of Austrasia; and possibly <a href="/wiki/Nanthild" title="Nanthild">Nanthild</a>, the mother of <a href="/wiki/Clovis_II" title="Clovis II">Clovis II</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg1996264_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg1996264-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> During the minority of <a href="/wiki/Clotaire_III" class="mw-redirect" title="Clotaire III">Clotaire III</a>, she had to deal with the attempted coup of <a href="/wiki/Grimoald_the_Elder" title="Grimoald the Elder">Grimoald</a>, the major domus of Austrasia, but she enjoyed the continued support of her former master Erchinoald, who became a sort of 'political mentor' to her throughout her marriage to Clovis II.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg1996265_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg1996265-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to some historians, Balthild's creation of and involvement with monasteries was perhaps an act to "balance or even neutralize the efforts of the aristocratic opposition".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg199626_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcNamaraWhateleyHalborg199626-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> By installing her supporters as bishops of different sees, she gained even greater power as a ruler.
</p><p>According to the <i><a href="/wiki/Vita_Sancti_Wilfrithi" title="Vita Sancti Wilfrithi">Vita Sancti Wilfrithi</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/Stephen_of_Ripon" title="Stephen of Ripon">Stephen of Ripon</a>, Bathild was a ruthless ruler, in conflict with the bishops and perhaps responsible for several assassinations.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> However, the bishop she so famously murdered, Dalfinus, is not listed as a bishop of Lyon. The story may have been written to embellish the life of Wilfrid.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A fragment of an apron thought to have belonged to Balthild is taken by scholars as evidence for her piety and frugality. Her devotion to her faith and forsaking of luxury is evident from a cross embroiled on the apron in silk, rather than gold thread.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Balthild_seal_matrix">Balthild seal matrix</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Balthild_of_Chelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Balthild seal matrix">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Baldehilde_seal_from_ring_A.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/00/Baldehilde_seal_from_ring_A.jpg/120px-Baldehilde_seal_from_ring_A.jpg" decoding="async" width="120" height="142" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/00/Baldehilde_seal_from_ring_A.jpg/180px-Baldehilde_seal_from_ring_A.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/00/Baldehilde_seal_from_ring_A.jpg/240px-Baldehilde_seal_from_ring_A.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1486" data-file-height="1760" /></a><figcaption>The Balthild seal matrix</figcaption></figure>
<p>A gold <a href="/wiki/Seal_(emblem)" title="Seal (emblem)">seal matrix</a>, which was originally attached to a seal-ring, was uncovered in 1999 by a <a href="/wiki/Metal_detector" title="Metal detector">metal detector</a> in a field in <a href="/wiki/Postwick_with_Witton" title="Postwick with Witton">Postwick</a>, 4.5 miles (7.2&#160;km) east of <a href="/wiki/Norwich" title="Norwich">Norwich</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Norfolk" title="Norfolk">Norfolk</a>. One side shows a woman's face and her name <i>BALDAHILDIS</i> in Frankish lettering. The other side portrays two naked figures, a man and a woman, embracing one another beneath a cross.<sup id="cite_ref-BBC_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-BBC-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In Merovingian Gaul, one side of the seal was intended to be used with official documents. The other side would have been used only for private papers. It is uncertain why the seal matrix came to East Anglia. It may have been a gift, or a representative of Balthild may have worn it as a form of identification. It has also been suggested that the seal matrix was returned to Balthild's kin after she died.<sup id="cite_ref-BBC_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-BBC-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Paul_Fouracre" title="Paul Fouracre">Paul Fouracre</a> of the University of Manchester speculated that the seal may belong to a different Baldahildis entirely.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> The seal matrix is in the keeping of the <a href="/wiki/Norwich_Castle_Museum" class="mw-redirect" title="Norwich Castle Museum">Norwich Castle Museum</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Chemise_of_Balthild">Chemise of Balthild</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Balthild_of_Chelles&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Chemise of Balthild">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Balthild's ornate <a href="/wiki/Chemise" title="Chemise">chemise</a> both expresses her dedication to the church, as well as her status as a queen to <a href="/wiki/Clovis_II" title="Clovis II">Clovis II</a>. The sleeveless tabard was likely crafted by either Balthid herself, or nuns of <a href="/wiki/Chelles_Abbey" title="Chelles Abbey">Chelles Abbey</a>, and measures 84 centimetres (33&#160;in) wide and 117 centimetres (46&#160;in) tall.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> It intended to loosely hang over the front and back of the body<sup id="cite_ref-:1_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> over a dress, although the back portion is currently lost. The garment consists of simple linen as a gesture of humility towards the church, as linen was a fabric commonly worn by lower classes of the time. The silken details make up a large bejewelled Christian cross 17.5 centimetres (6.9&#160;in) tall,<sup id="cite_ref-:2_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> small human and bird portraits, as well as several patterned rings around the collar mimicking studded golden necklaces.<sup id="cite_ref-:2_20-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> These embroidered necklaces were sewn in the exact likeness of the jewellery Balthild wore during her status as queen, and were also a symbol of Balthild's devotion, trading her actual jewellery for sewn replica as a member of Chelles Abbey.
</p><p>There are conflicting claims on this history of this garment, centred on whether it was ever worn by Balthild herself in life, in death, or at all. The common argument is that it was a burial shirt<sup id="cite_ref-:0_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:1_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> that followed Balthild into her tomb. This is challenged by claims that the shirt was worn frequently by Balthild during her life in service at Chelles but not during her death.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_19-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> These arguments have surfaced due to the dubious history of the dress and Balthild's body, which have been disturbed multiple times muddying attempts at accurate documentation. Balthild's body and dress have been relocated two documented times. The first time they were moved, they were moved behind the Chelles Abbey altar as relics<sup id="cite_ref-:1_19-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> in 833, likely in hopes of attracting Christians on <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimages</a>. The dress was said to be found once again hidden inside a sixteenth-century <a href="/wiki/Reliquary" title="Reliquary">reliquary</a><sup id="cite_ref-:1_19-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> in attempts of saving it from destruction during the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>.
</p>
