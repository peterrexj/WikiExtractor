<p><b>Saint Erhard of Regensburg</b> was <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Regensburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Regensburg">bishop of Regensburg</a> in the 7th century.  He is identified with an <b>Abbot Erhard of Ebersheimmunster</b> mentioned in a <a href="/wiki/Merovingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingian">Merovingian</a> diploma of 684. Ancient documents call him also <b>Erard</b> and <b>Herhard</b>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Erhard_of_Regensburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Bishops_of_Ardagh_list,_Catholic.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b8/Bishops_of_Ardagh_list%2C_Catholic.jpg/220px-Bishops_of_Ardagh_list%2C_Catholic.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b8/Bishops_of_Ardagh_list%2C_Catholic.jpg/330px-Bishops_of_Ardagh_list%2C_Catholic.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b8/Bishops_of_Ardagh_list%2C_Catholic.jpg/440px-Bishops_of_Ardagh_list%2C_Catholic.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3672" data-file-height="4896" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Bishops_of_Ardagh_list,_Catholic.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>List of the <a href="/wiki/Bishops_of_Ardagh" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishops of Ardagh">Bishops of Ardagh</a> in <a href="/wiki/St_Mel%27s_Cathedral" title="St Mel&#39;s Cathedral">St Mel's Cathedral</a>, Ireland, including several spurious medieval bishops. It includes St. Erhard.</div></div></div>
<p>Peter Nugent, writing in the <i><a href="/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia" title="Catholic Encyclopedia">Catholic Encyclopedia</a></i> says, "the legendary account of his life offers little that is historically certain".<sup id="cite_ref-Nugent_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Nugent-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Erhard was born sometime in the 6th century. Modern scholars believe that he was of Frankish origin, perhaps from <a href="/wiki/Narbonne" title="Narbonne">Narbonne</a> in southern Gaul. His 11th century biography says he was 'Scoticus', i.e. from Ireland or Scotland. He is identified with an Erhard of Ebersheimmunster mentioned in a Merovingian diploma of 684.
</p><p>Some believe that Erhard was bishop of Ardagh before crossing to the mainland with <a href="/wiki/Albert_of_Cashel" title="Albert of Cashel">Albert of Cashel</a>, said to be his friend or his brother and who is also celebrated on the same date. Eventually they came to Rome. While Albert then went to Jerusalem, Erhard went to Bavaria. (The difficulty with this part of the story is that there is over 100 years separating Erhard and Albert.)<sup id="cite_ref-Odden_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Odden-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> There he met <a href="/wiki/St._Hildulf" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Hildulf">St. Hildulf</a>, said to have been <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Trier" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Trier">Archbishop of Trier</a>,(although there is no Hidulf on the list of bishops of Trier.) For some time he shared the solitude of Hidulphus who lived as a <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Vosges" title="Vosges">Vosges</a> from 666 to 671.
</p><p>Erhard was a traveling bishop, one of the early <a href="/wiki/Chorbishop" title="Chorbishop">chorepiscopus</a>, that is, a corb bishop or a walking bishop, who without a permanent diocese went from place to place to help the missionaries in their work. Said to have been appointed by <a href="/wiki/Boniface" class="mw-redirect" title="Boniface">Boniface</a>, Erhard was probably a mission bishop at the court of the Agilofinger dukes in Regensburg.  After the martyrdom of Emmeram, Erhard became bishop of Regensburg. Many monastic foundations go back to him, above all the famous monastery <a href="/wiki/Niederm%C3%BCnster,_Regensburg" title="Niedermünster, Regensburg">Niedermünster</a> near Regensburg, which he himself led for a time as abbot. In all, he is said to have founded fourteen monasteries in Bavaria alone, and in the Vosges (Les Vosges) mountain range in the diocese of Strasbourg around seven monasteries. He was possibly the abbot of the monastery of Ebersheim.<sup id="cite_ref-Odden_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Odden-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the biography of <a href="/wiki/Odile_of_Alsace" title="Odile of Alsace">Odile of Alsace</a>, it is said that she was blind from birth, but that she miraculously regained her sight when she was baptized by Bishop Erhard. Another version states that Odilia was baptized by Hidulf, while Erhard was her godfather at the baptism.<sup id="cite_ref-Nugent_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Nugent-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He sent a messenger to her father, <a href="/wiki/Adalrich,_Duke_of_Alsace" title="Adalrich, Duke of Alsace">Adalrich, Duke of Alsace</a>, and reconciled him with his disowned daughter.<sup id="cite_ref-Akker_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Akker-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Ratisbon was the chief centre of his Apostolic labours, and it was there that he died,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> but the date is uncertain.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Erhard_of_Regensburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Erhardi-Schrein_Niederm%C3%BCnster_Regebsburg.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/Erhardi-Schrein_Niederm%C3%BCnster_Regebsburg.JPG/220px-Erhardi-Schrein_Niederm%C3%BCnster_Regebsburg.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/Erhardi-Schrein_Niederm%C3%BCnster_Regebsburg.JPG/330px-Erhardi-Schrein_Niederm%C3%BCnster_Regebsburg.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/Erhardi-Schrein_Niederm%C3%BCnster_Regebsburg.JPG/440px-Erhardi-Schrein_Niederm%C3%BCnster_Regebsburg.JPG 2x" data-file-width="2592" data-file-height="1944" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Erhardi-Schrein_Niederm%C3%BCnster_Regebsburg.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Erhardi-Schrein Niedermünster Regebsburg</div></div></div>
<p>Erhard was interred in the still-extant Erhard-crypt at Niedermünster, and miracles were wrought at his grave, that was guarded in the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a> by <i>Erhardinonnen</i>, a religious community of women who observed there a perpetual round of prayer.<sup id="cite_ref-Nugent_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Nugent-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Otto_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Otto II">Otto II</a>, in 974, made donations of properties in the <a href="/wiki/Danube" title="Danube">Danube</a> valley to the convent "where the holy confessor Erhard rests". On 7 Oct 1052 the remains of the holy bishops Erhard and <a href="/wiki/Wolfgang_of_Regensburg" title="Wolfgang of Regensburg">Saint Wolfgang</a> were raised by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_IX" title="Pope Leo IX">Pope Leo IX</a> in presence of <a href="/wiki/Henry_III,_Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Henry III, Holy Roman Emperor">Emperor Henry III</a> and many bishops, a ceremony which was at that time equivalent to <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a>. Regensburg documents, however, mention only the raising of Wolfgang, not that of Erhard. Nonetheless, he is considered to have been canonized in 1052. His relics can be found in a 19th-century silver shrine on the north side of the church.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> According to an old martyrology from Prague, some of Erhard's relics were transferred there in the time of Emperor Charles IV (1347–78).
</p><p>At the close of the eleventh century, Paul von Bernried, a monk of <a href="/wiki/Fulda" title="Fulda">Fulda</a>, at the suggestion of Abbess Heilika of Niedermünster, wrote a life of Erhard and added a second book containing a number of miracles. The learned canon of Regensburg, Conrad of Megenberg (d. 1374), furnished a new edition. The church in Niedermünster, now a parish church, still preserves his alleged <a href="/wiki/Crosier" title="Crosier">crosier</a> of the saint, made of black buffalo-horn. A bone of his skull was enclosed in a precious receptacle in 1866. In the high altar in the parish church in the village of Attaching near Freising, there is a beautiful reliquary with an arm relic of Erhard. It was given as a gift in 1720 by Archbishop Johann Franz Eckher.<sup id="cite_ref-Eisenstadt_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Eisenstadt-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The most important place of pilgrimage for Erhard has been St. Erhard in Styria in Austria since the 14th century. In the city of Salzburg, the parish church of St. Erhard stands at the foot of Nonnberg.<sup id="cite_ref-Eisenstadt_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Eisenstadt-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Three ancient Latin lives of the saint are found in the <i>Acta Sanctorum</i> (8 Jan).
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage">Patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Erhard_of_Regensburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Saint Erhard is a co-patron saint of the Diocese of Regensburg (with <a href="/wiki/Wolfgang_of_Regensburg" title="Wolfgang of Regensburg">Wolfgang</a> and <a href="/wiki/Emmeram_of_Regensburg" title="Emmeram of Regensburg">Emmeram</a>); for cattle; for shoemakers, blacksmiths and bakers; against eye diseases, plague and cattle diseases. Many hospitals are under his protection.<sup id="cite_ref-Odden_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Odden-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> particularly in Alsace.<sup id="cite_ref-Eisenstadt_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Eisenstadt-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconography">Iconography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Erhard_of_Regensburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Iconography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Erhard is depicted as a bishop (tabberd, mitre, staff) with a book on which lie two eyes (allusion to the healing of Saint Odilia); Odilia baptizing; with ax (either because he chopped down a sacred tree or because he belongs to the so-called mining saints).<sup id="cite_ref-Akker_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Akker-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Trivia">Trivia</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Erhard_of_Regensburg&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Trivia">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<ul><li>A <a href="/wiki/Beer" title="Beer">beer brand</a> from <a href="/wiki/Bamberg" title="Bamberg">Bamberg</a>, <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a> is called <a href="/wiki/St._Erhard_(brewery)" title="St. Erhard (brewery)">St. ERHARD</a>.</li>
<li>Since Erhard of Regensburg was considered a <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> for livestock, images of him were used as <a href="/wiki/Schluckbildchen" class="mw-redirect" title="Schluckbildchen">Schluckbildchen</a> and given to sick animals in the German folk medicine during the eighteenth, nineteenth and the beginning of the twentieth century.</li></ul>
