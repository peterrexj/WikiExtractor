					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Buddhist concept</div>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">For other uses, see <a href="/wiki/Bardo_(disambiguation)" class="mw-disambig" title="Bardo (disambiguation)">Bardo (disambiguation)</a>.</div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Peaceful_%26_Wrathful_Deities_-_of_the_Bardo.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/Peaceful_%26_Wrathful_Deities_-_of_the_Bardo.jpg/220px-Peaceful_%26_Wrathful_Deities_-_of_the_Bardo.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="304" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/Peaceful_%26_Wrathful_Deities_-_of_the_Bardo.jpg/330px-Peaceful_%26_Wrathful_Deities_-_of_the_Bardo.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/Peaceful_%26_Wrathful_Deities_-_of_the_Bardo.jpg/440px-Peaceful_%26_Wrathful_Deities_-_of_the_Bardo.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1662" data-file-height="2300" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Peaceful_%26_Wrathful_Deities_-_of_the_Bardo.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Tibetan_art" title="Tibetan art">Tibetan illustration</a> of the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities of the post-mortem intermediate state (<i>bardo</i>). Some <a href="/wiki/Tibetan_Buddhism" title="Tibetan Buddhism">Tibetan Buddhists</a> hold that when a being goes through the intermediate state, they will have visions of various deities.</div></div></div>
<p>In some schools of <a href="/wiki/Buddhism" title="Buddhism">Buddhism</a>, <i><b>bardo</b></i> (<a href="/wiki/Classical_Tibetan_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Classical Tibetan language">Classical Tibetan</a>: <span lang="xct">བར་དོ་</span> Wylie: <i>bar do</i>) or <i><b>antarābhava</b></i> (<a href="/wiki/Sanskrit" title="Sanskrit">Sanskrit</a>, Chinese and Japanese: 中有, romanized in Chinese as <i>zhōng yǒu</i> and in Japanese as <i>chū'u</i>)<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> is an intermediate, transitional, or liminal state between <a href="/wiki/Death" title="Death">death</a> and <a href="/wiki/Rebirth_(Buddhism)" title="Rebirth (Buddhism)">rebirth</a>. The concept arose soon after <a href="/wiki/Gautama_Buddha" class="mw-redirect" title="Gautama Buddha">Gautama Buddha</a>'s death, with a number of <a href="/wiki/Early_Buddhist_schools" title="Early Buddhist schools">earlier Buddhist schools</a> accepting the existence of such an intermediate state, while other schools rejected it. The concept of <i>antarābhava</i>, an intervening state between death and rebirth, was brought into Buddhism from the <a href="/wiki/Vedas" title="Vedas">Vedic</a>-<a href="/wiki/Upanishads" title="Upanishads">Upanishadic</a> (later <a href="/wiki/Hinduism" title="Hinduism">Hindu</a>) philosophical tradition.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Later Buddhism expanded the bardo concept to six or more states of consciousness covering every stage of life and death.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In <a href="/wiki/Tibetan_Buddhism" title="Tibetan Buddhism">Tibetan Buddhism</a>, <i>bardo</i> is the central theme of the <i><a href="/wiki/Bardo_Thodol" title="Bardo Thodol">Bardo Thodol</a></i> (literally <i>Liberation Through Hearing During the Intermediate State</i>), the <i>Tibetan Book of the Dead</i>, a text intended to both guide the recently deceased person through the death bardo to gain a better rebirth and also to help their loved ones with the grieving process.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Used without qualification, "bardo" is the state of existence intermediate between two lives on earth. According to <a href="/wiki/Tibet" title="Tibet">Tibetan</a> tradition, after death and before one's next birth, when one's consciousness is not connected with a physical body, one experiences a variety of phenomena. These usually follow a particular sequence of degeneration from, just after death, the clearest experiences of reality of which one is spiritually capable, and then proceeding to terrifying hallucinations that arise from the impulses of one's previous unskillful actions. For the prepared and appropriately trained individuals, the bardo offers a state of great opportunity for liberation, since transcendental insight may arise with the direct experience of reality; for others, it can become a place of danger as the <a href="/wiki/Karma" title="Karma">karmically</a> created hallucinations can impel one into a less than desirable <a href="/wiki/Rebirth_(Buddhism)" title="Rebirth (Buddhism)">rebirth</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Metaphorically, <i>bardo</i> can be used to describe times when the usual way of life becomes suspended, as, for example, during a period of illness or during a meditation <a href="/wiki/Retreat_(religious/spiritual)" class="mw-redirect" title="Retreat (religious/spiritual)">retreat</a>. Such times can prove fruitful for spiritual progress because external constraints diminish. However, they can also present challenges because our less skillful impulses may come to the foreground, just as in the <a href="/wiki/Bardo_Thodol" title="Bardo Thodol"><i>sidpa bardo</i></a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Intermediate_state_in_Indian_Buddhism">Intermediate state in Indian Buddhism</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bardo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Intermediate state in Indian Buddhism">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>From the records of early Buddhist schools, it appears that at least six different groups accepted the notion of an intermediate existence (<i>antarabhāva</i>), namely, the <a href="/wiki/Sarv%C4%81stiv%C4%81da" class="mw-redirect" title="Sarvāstivāda">Sarvāstivāda</a>, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Dar%E1%B9%A3%E1%B9%AD%C4%81ntika&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Darṣṭāntika (page does not exist)">Darṣṭāntika</a>, <a href="/w/index.php?title=V%C4%81ts%C4%ABputr%C4%AByas&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Vātsīputrīyas (page does not exist)">Vātsīputrīyas</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83mit%C4%ABya" class="mw-redirect" title="Saṃmitīya">Saṃmitīya</a>, <a href="/w/index.php?title=P%C5%ABrva%C5%9Baila&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Pūrvaśaila (page does not exist)">Pūrvaśaila</a> and late <a href="/wiki/Mah%C4%AB%C5%9B%C4%81saka" title="Mahīśāsaka">Mahīśāsaka</a>.  The first four of these are closely related schools.  Opposing them were the <a href="/wiki/Mah%C4%81s%C4%81%E1%B9%83ghika" title="Mahāsāṃghika">Mahāsāṃghika</a>, early <a href="/wiki/Mah%C4%AB%C5%9B%C4%81saka" title="Mahīśāsaka">Mahīśāsaka</a>, <a href="/wiki/Therav%C4%81da" class="mw-redirect" title="Theravāda">Theravāda</a>, <a href="/wiki/Vibhajyav%C4%81da" title="Vibhajyavāda">Vibhajyavāda</a> and the <i><a href="/wiki/%C5%9A%C4%81riputr%C4%81bhidharma" title="Śāriputrābhidharma">Śāriputrābhidharma</a></i> (possibly <a href="/wiki/Dharmagupta" class="mw-redirect" title="Dharmagupta">Dharmagupta</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Some of the earliest references to an "intermediate existence" are to be found in the Sarvāstivādin text the <a href="/wiki/Mahavibhasa" class="mw-redirect" title="Mahavibhasa">Mahāvibhāṣa</a> (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論).  For instance, the Mahāvibhāṣa indicates a "basic existence" (本有), an "intermediate existence" (中有), a "birth existence" (生有) and a "death existence" (死有) (CBETA, T27, no. 1545, p.&#160;959, etc.). André Bareau's <i>Les sectes bouddhiques du Petit Véhicule</i> provides the arguments of the Sarvāstivāda schools as follows:<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<blockquote><p>The intermediate being who makes the passage in this way from one existence to the next is formed, like every living being, of the five aggregates (skandha). His existence is demonstrated by the fact that it cannot have any discontinuity in time and space between the place and moment of death and those of rebirth, and therefore it must be that the two existences belonging to the same series are linked in time and space by an intermediate stage. The intermediate being is the <a href="/wiki/Gandharva" title="Gandharva">Gandharva</a>, the presence of which is as necessary at conception as the fecundity and union of the parents. Furthermore, the Antarāparinirvāyin is an <a href="/wiki/An%C4%81g%C4%81mi" title="Anāgāmi">Anāgamin</a> who obtains parinirvāṇa during the intermediary existence. As for the heinous criminal guilty of one of the five crimes without interval (ānantarya), he passes in quite the same way by an intermediate existence at the end of which he is reborn necessarily in hell.</p></blockquote>
<p>Deriving from a later period of the same school, though with some differences, <a href="/wiki/Vasubandhu" title="Vasubandhu">Vasubandhu</a>’s <i><a href="/wiki/Abhidharmako%C5%9Ba" class="mw-redirect" title="Abhidharmakośa">Abhidharmakośa</a></i> explains (English trs. p. 383ff):
</p>
<blockquote><p>What is an intermediate being, and an intermediate existence? Intermediate existence, which inserts itself between existence at death and existence at birth, not having arrived at the location where it should go, cannot be said to be born.  Between death—that is, the five skandhas of the moment of death—and arising—that is, the five skandhas of the moment of rebirth—there is found an existence—a "body" of five skandhas—that goes to the place of rebirth. This existence between two realms of rebirth (gatī) is called intermediate existence.</p></blockquote>
<p>He cites a number of texts and examples to defend the notion against other schools which reject it and claim that death in one life is immediately followed by rebirth in the next, without any intermediate state in between the two.  Both the <i><a href="/wiki/Abhidharma_Mah%C4%81vibh%C4%81%E1%B9%A3a_%C5%9A%C4%81stra" title="Abhidharma Mahāvibhāṣa Śāstra">Mahāvibhāṣa</a></i> and the <i>Abhidharmakośa</i> have the notion of the intermediate state lasting "seven times seven days" (i.e. <a href="/wiki/49_Days_(Buddhism)" class="mw-redirect" title="49 Days (Buddhism)">49 days</a>) at most.  This is one view, though, and there were also others.
</p><p>Similar arguments were also used in Harivarman’s <a href="/wiki/Tattvasiddhi" title="Tattvasiddhi">*<i>Satyasiddhi Śāstra</i></a>, and the Upadeśa commentary on the <i><a href="/wiki/Prajnaparamita" title="Prajnaparamita">Prajñāpāramitā</a></i> Sūtras, both of which have strong influence from the Sarvāstivāda school.  Both of these texts had powerful influence in Chinese Buddhism, which also accepts this idea as a rule.
</p><p>The <i>Saddharma-smṛty-upasthāna Sūtra</i> (正法念處經) classifies 17 intermediate states with different experiences.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

</tr><tr><td class="sidebar-navbar"><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1129693374"/><style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1063604349">.mw-parser-output .navbar{display:inline;font-size:88%;font-weight:normal}.mw-parser-output .navbar-collapse{float:left;text-align:left}.mw-parser-output .navbar-boxtext{word-spacing:0}.mw-parser-output .navbar ul{display:inline-block;white-space:nowrap;line-height:inherit}.mw-parser-output .navbar-brackets::before{margin-right:-0.125em;content:"[ "}.mw-parser-output .navbar-brackets::after{margin-left:-0.125em;content:" ]"}.mw-parser-output .navbar li{word-spacing:-0.125em}.mw-parser-output .navbar a>span,.mw-parser-output .navbar a>abbr{text-decoration:inherit}.mw-parser-output .navbar-mini abbr{font-variant:small-caps;border-bottom:none;text-decoration:none;cursor:inherit}.mw-parser-output .navbar-ct-full{font-size:114%;margin:0 7em}.mw-parser-output .navbar-ct-mini{font-size:114%;margin:0 4em}</style><div class="navbar plainlinks hlist navbar-mini"><ul><li class="nv-view"><a href="/wiki/Template:Tibetan_Buddhism_sidebar" title="Template:Tibetan Buddhism sidebar"><abbr title="View this template">v</abbr></a></li><li class="nv-talk"><a href="/wiki/Template_talk:Tibetan_Buddhism_sidebar" title="Template talk:Tibetan Buddhism sidebar"><abbr title="Discuss this template">t</abbr></a></li><li class="nv-edit"><a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Template:Tibetan_Buddhism_sidebar&amp;action=edit"><abbr title="Edit this template">e</abbr></a></li></ul></div></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Fremantle (2001) states that there are six traditional bardo states known as the Six Bardos:  the Bardo of This Life (p.&#160;55); the Bardo of Meditation (p.&#160;58); the Bardo of Dream (p.&#160;62); the Bardo of Dying (p.&#160;64); the Bardo of Dharmata (p.&#160;65); and the Bardo of Existence (p.&#160;66).<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Shugchang, <i>et al.</i> (2000: p.&#160;5) discuss the <a href="/wiki/Zhitro" title="Zhitro">Zhitro</a> (Tibetan: Zhi-khro) cycle of teachings of <a href="/wiki/Karma_Lingpa" title="Karma Lingpa">Karma Lingpa</a> which includes the <i><a href="/wiki/Bardo_Thodol" title="Bardo Thodol">Bardo Thodol</a></i> and list the Six Bardo:  "The first bardo begins when we take birth and endures as long as we live. The second is the bardo of dreams. The third is the bardo of concentration or meditation. The fourth occurs at the moment of death. The fifth is known as the bardo of the luminosity of the true nature. The sixth is called the bardo of transmigration or karmic becoming.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<ol><li><b>Kyenay bardo</b> (<i>skye gnas bar do</i>) is the first bardo of birth and life. This bardo commences from conception until the last breath, when the <a href="/wiki/Mindstream" title="Mindstream">mindstream</a> withdraws from the body.</li>
<li><b>Milam bardo</b> (<i>rmi lam bar do</i>) is the second bardo of the dream state. The Milam Bardo is a subset of the first Bardo. <a href="/wiki/Dream_Yoga" class="mw-redirect" title="Dream Yoga">Dream Yoga</a> develops practices to integrate the dream state into Buddhist <a href="/wiki/Sadhana" class="mw-redirect" title="Sadhana">sadhana</a>.</li>
<li><b>Samten bardo</b> (<i>bsam gtan bar do</i>) is the third bardo of <a href="/wiki/Meditation" title="Meditation">meditation</a>. This bardo is generally only experienced by meditators, though individuals may have spontaneous experience of it. Samten Bardo is a subset of the Shinay Bardo<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="margin-left:0.1em; white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify" title="Wikipedia:Please clarify"><span title="Is Shinay an alternative name for Kyenay, or something else? (October 2021)">clarification needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>.</li>
<li><b>Chikhai bardo</b> ('<i>chi kha'i bar do</i>) is the fourth bardo of the moment of death. According to tradition, this bardo is held to commence when the outer and inner signs presage that the onset of death is nigh, and continues through the dissolution or transmutation of the <a href="/wiki/Mahabhuta" class="mw-redirect" title="Mahabhuta">Mahabhuta</a> until the external and internal breath has completed.</li>
<li><b>Chönyi bardo</b> (<i>chos nyid bar do</i>) is the fifth bardo of the luminosity of the true nature which commences after the final 'inner breath' (Sanskrit: <i><a href="/wiki/Prana" title="Prana">prana</a></i>, <a href="/wiki/Vayu" title="Vayu">vayu</a>; Tibetan: <i><a href="/wiki/Rlung" class="mw-redirect" title="Rlung">rlung</a></i>). It is within this Bardo that visions and auditory phenomena occur. In the Dzogchen teachings, these are known as the spontaneously manifesting <a href="/wiki/T%C3%B6gal" title="Tögal">Tögal</a> (Tibetan: <i>thod-rgyal</i>) visions. Concomitant to these visions, there is a welling of profound peace and pristine awareness. Sentient beings who have not practiced during their lived experience and/or who do not recognize the clear light (Tibetan: <i><a href="/wiki/%C3%96sel_(yoga)" class="mw-redirect" title="Ösel (yoga)">od gsal</a></i>) at the moment of death are usually deluded throughout the fifth bardo of luminosity.</li>
<li><b>Sidpa bardo</b> (<i>srid pa bar do</i>) is the sixth bardo of becoming or transmigration.  This bardo endures until the inner-breath commences in the new transmigrating form determined by the "karmic seeds" within the <a href="/wiki/Storehouse_consciousness" class="mw-redirect" title="Storehouse consciousness">storehouse consciousness</a>.</li></ol>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bardo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Since the <a href="/wiki/Bardo_Thodol" title="Bardo Thodol">Bardo Thodol</a> was translated into English, different conceptions of the bardo have emerged over the years (Lopez, 1998: p. 43 and 83). In the translation of Walter Y. Evans-Wentz in 1927, the description of the bardo was an “esoteric” view of rebirth as an evolutionary system in which regression to the brutish realms was impossible. Almost four decades later, in 1964, Timothy Leary, Ralph Metzner and Richard Alpert saw the intermediate states as being really about life, profitable as an account of an eight-hour acid trip. Chögyam Trungpa portrays the realms of rebirth as psychological states in 1975, and Sogyal Rinpoche uses his discussion of the six realms as an opportunity to lampoon California surfers and New York bankers, in the translation that was published in 1992. Two years later Robert Thurman (1994) interpreted the bardo, which is described originally as a Nyingma text, from a Geluk frame.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><br />
Fremantle (2001: p.&#160;53–54) charts the development of the bardo concept through the <a href="/wiki/Himalayas" title="Himalayas">Himalayan</a> tradition:
</p>
<blockquote><p>Originally bardo referred only to the period between one life and the next, and this is still its normal meaning when it is mentioned without any qualification.  There was considerable dispute over this theory during the early centuries of Buddhism, with one side arguing that rebirth (or conception) follows immediately after death, and the other saying that there must be an interval between the two. With the rise of mahayana, belief in a transitional period prevailed.  Later Buddhism expanded the whole concept to distinguish six or more similar states, covering the whole cycle of life, death, and rebirth.  But it can also be interpreted as any transitional experience, any state that lies between two other states.  Its original meaning, the experience of being between death and rebirth, is the prototype of the bardo experience, while the six traditional bardos show how the essential qualities of that experience are also present in other transitional periods.  By refining even further the understanding of the essence of bardo, it can then be applied to every moment of existence.  The present moment, the now, is a continual bardo, always suspended between the past and the future.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> </p></blockquote><p>Yongey Mingur Rinpoche (2011, p. 66) adds another modern view and emphasises on the bardo of becoming: “When we move past the convenience of language and categories, every second manifests the bardo of becoming. Becoming and becoming. All phenomena always just become [...] When we sensitize ourselves to the subtle transitions of emotions, or of bodily change, or shifts in social circumstances, or environmental transformations such as differences in landscape and light, or developments in language, art, or politics-we see that it’s all always changing, dying, and becoming.”<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><h2><span id="Intermediate_state_in_Therav.C4.81da"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Intermediate_state_in_Theravāda">Intermediate state in Theravāda</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bardo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Intermediate state in Theravāda">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Theravāda Abhidhamma texts like the <a href="/wiki/Kath%C4%81vatthu" title="Kathāvatthu"><i>Kathavatthu</i></a> traditionally reject the view that there is an intermediate or transitional state (<i>antarabhāva</i>) between rebirths, they hold that rebirth happens instantaneously (in one mind moment) through the re-linking consciousness (<i>patisandhi citta</i>).<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>However, as has been noted by various modern scholars like <a href="/wiki/Bhante_Sujato" title="Bhante Sujato">Bhikkhu Sujato</a>, there are passages in the Theravāda <a href="/wiki/P%C4%81li_Canon" class="mw-redirect" title="Pāli Canon">Pali Canon</a> which support the idea of an intermediate state, the most explicit of which is the <i>Kutuhalasāla Sutta</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>
This sutta states: </p><blockquote><p>[The Buddha:] "Vaccha, I declare that there is rebirth for one with fuel [with grasping], not for one without fuel. Vaccha, just as fire burns with fuel, not without fuel, even so, Vaccha, I declare that there is rebirth for one with fuel [with grasping], not for one without fuel."
</p><p>[Vaccha replies:] "But, master Gotama, when a flame is tossed by the wind and goes a long way, what does master Gotama declare to be its fuel?"
</p><p>[Buddha:] "Vaccha, when a flame is tossed by the wind and goes a long way, I declare that it is fueled by the air. For, Vaccha, at that time, the air is the fuel." 
</p><p>[Vaccha:] "Master Gotama, when a being has laid down this body, but has not yet been reborn in another body, what does the master Gotama declare to be the fuel?"
</p><p>
[Buddha:] "Vaccha, when a being has laid down this body, but has not yet been reborn in another body, it is fuelled by craving, I say. For, Vaccha, at that time, craving is the fuel."<sup id="cite_ref-:0_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> </p></blockquote><p>Furthermore, some Theravāda scholars (such as <a href="/wiki/Balangoda_Ananda_Maitreya_Thero" title="Balangoda Ananda Maitreya Thero">Balangoda Ananda Maitreya</a>) have defended the idea of an intermediate state and it is also a very common belief among some monks and laypersons in the Theravāda world (where it is commonly referred to as the <i><a href="/wiki/Gandharva" title="Gandharva">gandhabba</a></i> or <i>antarabhāva</i>). According to Sujato, it is also widely accepted among <a href="/wiki/Thai_Forest_Tradition" title="Thai Forest Tradition">Thai forest tradition</a> teachers.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:0_15-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_East_Asian_Buddhism">In East Asian Buddhism</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bardo&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: In East Asian Buddhism">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/East_Asian_Buddhism" title="East Asian Buddhism">East Asian Buddhism</a> generally accepts the main doctrines of the Yogacara tradition as taught by <a href="/wiki/Vasubandhu" title="Vasubandhu">Vasubandhu</a> and <a href="/wiki/Asanga" title="Asanga">Asanga</a>. This includes the acceptance of the intermediate existence (中有, Chinese romanization: <i>zhōng yǒu</i>, Japanese: <i>chūu</i>). The doctrine of the intermediate existence is mentioned in various Chinese Buddhist scholastic works, such as <a href="/wiki/Xuanzang" title="Xuanzang">Xuanzang's</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Cheng_Weishi_Lun" title="Cheng Weishi Lun">Cheng Weishi Lun</a></i> (<i>Discourse on the Perfection of Consciousness-only).</i><sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The Chinese Buddhist Canon contains a text called the <i>Antarabhava sutra,</i> which is used in funerary rituals.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>
The founder of <a href="/wiki/S%C5%8Dt%C5%8D" title="Sōtō">Soto Zen</a>, <a href="/wiki/D%C5%8Dgen" title="Dōgen">Dogen</a>, wrote the following regarding how to navigate the intermediate state:</p><blockquote><p>“When you leave this life, and before you enter the next life, there is a place called an intermediary realm. You stay there for seven days. You should resolve to keep chanting the names of the three treasures without ceasing while you are there. After seven days you die in the intermediary realm and remain there for no more than seven days. At this time you can see and hear without hindrance, like having a celestial eye. Resolve to encourage yourself to keep chanting the names of the three treasures without ceasing: ‘I take refuge in the Buddha. I take refuge in the Dharma. I take refuge in the Sangha.’ After passing through the intermediary realm, when you approach your parents to be conceived, resolve to maintain authentic wisdom. Keep chanting refuge in the three treasures in your mother’s womb. Do not neglect chanting while you are given birth. Resolve deeply to dedicate yourself to chant and take refuge in the three treasures through the six sense roots. When your life ends, your eye sight will suddenly become dark. Know that this is the end of your life and be determined to chant, ‘I take refuge in the buddha.’ Then, all buddhas in the ten directions will show compassion to you. Even if due to conditions you are bound to an unwholesome realm, you will be able to be born in the deva realm or in the presence of the Buddha. Bow and listen to the Buddha.” 
--- <i><a href="/wiki/Sh%C5%8Db%C5%8Dgenz%C5%8D" title="Shōbōgenzō">Shobogenzo</a>,</i> section 94, "Mind of the Way”, translated by Peter Levitt &amp; Kazuaki Tanahashi (2013):</p></blockquote>
