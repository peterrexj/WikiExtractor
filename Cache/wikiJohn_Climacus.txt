<p><b>John Climacus</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Ἰωάννης τῆς Κλίμακος</span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Ioannes Climacus</i>), also known as <b>John of the Ladder</b>, <b>John Scholasticus</b> and <b>John Sinaites</b>, was a 6th–7th-century <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Monasticism" title="Monasticism">monk</a> at the monastery on <a href="/wiki/Mount_Sinai" title="Mount Sinai">Mount Sinai</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He is revered as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Catholic">Latin Catholic</a>, <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox</a> and <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Catholic_Churches" title="Eastern Catholic Churches">Eastern Catholic</a> churches.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Climacus&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There is almost no information about John's life. There is in existence an ancient <i><a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">vita</a></i> (life) of the saint by a monk named Daniel of <a href="/wiki/El_Tor,_Egypt" title="El Tor, Egypt">Raithu</a> monastery. Daniel, though claiming to be a contemporary, admits to no knowledge of John's origins—any detail on John's birth is the result of much later speculation, and is confined to references in the <a href="/wiki/Menologion" class="mw-redirect" title="Menologion">Menologion</a>. The <i>Vita</i> is generally unhelpful for establishing dates of any kind. Formerly scholarship, on the basis of John's entry in the Menologion, had placed him in the latter 6th century. That view was challenged by J. C. Guy and others, and consensus (such as there is) has shifted to a 7th century provenance.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> If Daniel's <i>vita</i> is trustworthy (there is nothing against which to judge its accuracy), then John came to the <i>Vatos</i> Monastery at <a href="/wiki/Mount_Sinai" title="Mount Sinai">Mount Sinai</a>, now <a href="/wiki/Saint_Catherine%27s_Monastery" title="Saint Catherine&#39;s Monastery">Saint Catherine's Monastery</a>, and became a novice when he was about 16 years old. He was taught about the spiritual life by the elder monk Martyrius. After the death of Martyrius, John, wishing to practice greater <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a>, withdrew to a <a href="/wiki/Hermitage_(religious_retreat)" title="Hermitage (religious retreat)">hermitage</a> at the foot of the mountain. In this isolation he lived for some twenty years, constantly studying the lives of the saints and thus becoming one of the most learned <a href="/wiki/Church_Fathers" title="Church Fathers">Church Fathers</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Clugnet_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Clugnet-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>In the meantime, this tradition has been proven to be historically implausible.<sup id="cite_ref-Johnsén_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Johnsén-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The artful rhetorical figures in his writings, as well as philosophical forms of thought indicate a solid academic education, as was customary for a profession in administration and law during his epoch. Such training could not be acquired in Sinai.<sup id="cite_ref-Duffy_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Duffy-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>In addition, biographical observations indicate that he probably lived by the sea, probably in Gaza, and apparently practiced law there. It was only after his wife's death, in his early forties, that he entered the Sinai Monastery. These findings also explain the horizon and the literary quality of his writings, which have a clear philosophical background. The legend of his renunciation of the world at the age of 16 is based on the motive of portraying him as untouched by secular education, as is found in other biographies of saints. Their roots in theological and philosophical educational traditions are deliberately blurred.
</p><p>When he was about sixty-five years of age, the monks of Sinai persuaded him to become their <a href="/wiki/Hegumen" title="Hegumen">hegumen</a>. He acquitted himself of his functions as abbot with the greatest wisdom, and his reputation spread so far that, according to the <i>Vita</i>, Pope <a href="/wiki/Gregory_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Gregory the Great">Gregory the Great</a> wrote to recommend himself to his prayers, and sent him a sum of money for the hospital of Sinai, in which the pilgrims were wont to lodge.
</p><p>Of John's literary output we know only the <i>Κλῖμαξ</i> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Scala Paradisi</i>) or <i><a href="/wiki/The_Ladder_of_Divine_Ascent" title="The Ladder of Divine Ascent">The Ladder of Divine Ascent</a></i>, composed in the early seventh century at the request of John,<sup id="cite_ref-Duffy2010_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Duffy2010-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Abbot of Raithu, a monastery situated on the shores of the Red Sea, and a shorter work <i>To the Pastor</i> (Latin: <i>Liber ad Pastorem</i>), most likely a sort of appendix to the <i>Ladder</i>. It is in the <i>Ladder</i> that we hear of the ascetic practice of carrying a small notebook to record the thoughts of the monk during contemplation.<sup id="cite_ref-Stroumsa2008_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Stroumsa2008-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Ladder</i> describes how to raise one's soul and body to <a href="/wiki/God" title="God">God</a> through the acquisition of ascetic virtues. Climacus uses the analogy of <a href="/wiki/Jacob%27s_Ladder" title="Jacob&#39;s Ladder">Jacob's Ladder</a> as the framework for his spiritual teaching. Each chapter is referred to as a "step", and deals with a separate spiritual subject. There are thirty Steps of the ladder, which correspond to the age of <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">Jesus</a> at <a href="/wiki/Baptism_of_Jesus" title="Baptism of Jesus">his baptism</a> and the beginning of his earthly ministry. Within the general framework of a 'ladder', Climacus' book falls into three sections. The first seven Steps concern general virtues necessary for the ascetic life, while the next nineteen (Steps 8–26) give instruction on overcoming vices and building their corresponding virtues. The final four Steps concern the higher virtues toward which the ascetic life aims. The final rung of the ladder—beyond prayer (προσευχή), stillness (<a href="/wiki/Hesychasm" title="Hesychasm">ἡσυχία</a>), and even dispassion (<a href="/wiki/Apatheia" title="Apatheia">ἀπάθεια</a>)—is love (<a href="/wiki/Agape" title="Agape">ἀγάπη</a>).
</p><p>Originally written simply for the monks of a neighboring monastery, the <i>Ladder</i> swiftly became one of the most widely read and much-beloved books of <a href="/wiki/Byzantine" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine">Byzantine</a> spirituality. This book is one of the most widely read among <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Orthodox Christians</a>, especially during the season of <a href="/wiki/Great_Lent" title="Great Lent">Great Lent</a> which immediately precedes <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Pascha</a> (Easter). It is often read in the <i><a href="/wiki/Refectory" title="Refectory">trapeza</a></i> (refectory) in Orthodox <a href="/wiki/Monasteries" class="mw-redirect" title="Monasteries">monasteries</a>, and in some places it is read in church as part of the <a href="/wiki/Daily_Office" class="mw-redirect" title="Daily Office">Daily Office</a> on Lenten weekdays, being prescribed in the <i><a href="/wiki/Triodion" title="Triodion">Triodion</a></i>.
</p><p>An <a href="/wiki/Icon" title="Icon">icon</a> known by the same title, <i><a href="/wiki/Ladder_of_Divine_Ascent_(icon)" title="Ladder of Divine Ascent (icon)">Ladder of Divine Ascent</a></i>, depicts a ladder extending from earth to heaven.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Several <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monks</a> are depicted climbing a ladder; at the top is Jesus, prepared to receive them into <a href="/wiki/Heaven" title="Heaven">Heaven</a>. Also shown are angels helping the climbers, and demons attempting to shoot with arrows or drag down the climbers, no matter how high up the ladder they may be. Most versions of the icon show at least one person falling. Often, in the lower right corner John Climacus himself is shown, gesturing towards the ladder, with rows of monastics behind him.
</p><p>Saint John's <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is March 30 in both the East and West. The Eastern Orthodox Church and the Byzantine Catholic churches additionally commemorate him on the Fourth Sunday of <a href="/wiki/Great_Lent" title="Great Lent">Great Lent</a>. Many churches are dedicated to him in <a href="/wiki/Russia" title="Russia">Russia</a>, including <a href="/wiki/Ivan_the_Great_Bell_Tower" title="Ivan the Great Bell Tower">a church and belltower</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Moscow_Kremlin" class="mw-redirect" title="Moscow Kremlin">Moscow Kremlin</a>. John Climacus was also known as "Scholasticus", but he is not to be confused with <a href="/wiki/John_Scholasticus" title="John Scholasticus">John Scholasticus</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_of_Constantinople" title="Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople">Patriarch of Constantinople</a>.
</p><p>Several translations into English have been made, including one by Holy Transfiguration Monastery (Boston, 1978). This volume contains the <i>Life of St. John</i> by Daniel, <i>The Ladder of Divine Ascent</i>, and <i>To the Pastor</i>, and provides footnotes explaining many of the concepts and terminology used from an Orthodox perspective, as well as a General Index.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
