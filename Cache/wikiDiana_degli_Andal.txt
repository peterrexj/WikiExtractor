<blockquote class="quotebox-quote left-aligned" style="">
<p>...She [Diana] and the other two members of the community who were beautified with her personify the three essential graces of monastic life: <a href="/wiki/Amata_(nun)" title="Amata (nun)">Amata</a>, deep humility, <a href="/wiki/Cecilia_Cesarini" title="Cecilia Cesarini">Cecilia</a>, the prioress, wise and creative authority; <b>Diana</b>, the greatest grace of them all, perfect love.
</p>
</blockquote>
<p><cite class="right-aligned" style="">Gerald Vann, O.P.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></cite>

<p><b>Diana degli Andalò</b> (1201 &#8211; 10 June 1236), sometimes <b>d'Andalo</b>, was a <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican nun</a> who founded a convent for her order dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Saint_Agnes" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Agnes">Saint Agnes</a> in Italy.
</p><p>Diana d'Andalò was born into a powerful Roman family in <a href="/wiki/Bologna" title="Bologna">Bologna, Italy</a> in 1201.<sup id="cite_ref-sinsinawa_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sinsinawa-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  Nothing is known of her childhood,<sup id="cite_ref-vann-4_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vann-4-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> but she was described as "rather spoiled".<sup id="cite_ref-blesseds_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-blesseds-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>  She was "of outstanding beauty",<sup id="cite_ref-vann-4_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vann-4-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> charming, eloquent, well-educated, high spirited, courageous, and had good judgment.<sup id="cite_ref-vann-4_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vann-4-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  Dominican scholar Gerald Vann also called her "full of the joy of living, full too of the joy of her own beauty and the power it gave her".<sup id="cite_ref-vann-4_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vann-4-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1218, Diana heard the <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican</a> scholar and preacher <a href="/w/index.php?title=Reginald_of_Orl%C3%A9ans&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Reginald of Orléans (page does not exist)">Reginald of Orléans</a> speak in Bologna.  His sermons inspired her to give up her "worldliness"<sup id="cite_ref-vann-4_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vann-4-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and devote herself to prayer.  Following Reginald's advice, she remained at her family's home and continued to wear the clothes appropriate to her standing, although she wore an iron chain and <a href="/wiki/Cilice" title="Cilice">hairshirt</a> under them.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>  In 1219, <a href="/wiki/Saint_Dominic" title="Saint Dominic">Dominic of Osma</a> came to Bologna; he received her vow of virginity and she proclaimed her intention to join the Dominican order as soon as a <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a> was established in Bologna.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dorcy_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dorcy-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>She persuaded her father to purchase the lands to build a convent, but he refused to allow her to enter it, probably because the family intended that she marry into another prominent family, and the bishop denied permission to build on the site chosen.  In 1222, she ran away to an <a href="/wiki/Augustinians" title="Augustinians">Augustinian</a> convent in <a href="/wiki/Rozzano" title="Rozzano">Ronzano</a>.  Her family forcibly retrieved her and brought her back home; one of her ribs was broken, an almost fatal injury from which she never fully recovered.  Dominic died shortly afterwards, but after she recovered from her injuries, she again escaped to Ronzano in 1223, although her family did not come after her a second time.<sup id="cite_ref-blesseds_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-blesseds-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dorcy_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dorcy-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Shortly after Dominic's death, <a href="/wiki/Jordan_of_Saxony" title="Jordan of Saxony">Jordan of Saxony</a>, Dominic's successor, met Diana when he came to Bologna.  With his assistance, Diana reconciled with her family, her father agreed to allow her to enter religious life, and the bishop agreed to another site for the convent.<sup id="cite_ref-walsh_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-walsh-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>  In 1223, St. Agnes in Bologna was established, and Diana entered the Dominican order.<sup id="cite_ref-dorcy_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dorcy-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>  Four other nuns were also brought from the convent of St. Sixtus at Rome, including <a href="/wiki/Cecilia_Cesarini" title="Cecilia Cesarini">Cecilia Cesarini</a>, who was made prioress, and <a href="/wiki/Amata_(nun)" title="Amata (nun)">Amata</a>; the three are always associated together.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>  Diana remained at St. Agnes until her death in 1236, and was buried there, along with the remains of Cecilia and Amata.  Their relics were moved several times, but always together.  Diana's head was placed in a <a href="/wiki/Reliquary" title="Reliquary">reliquary</a> near Dominic's tomb.<sup id="cite_ref-dorcy_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dorcy-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>  Her feast day is June 9.<sup id="cite_ref-walsh_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-walsh-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>  She, along with Cecilia and Amata, were beatified by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIII" title="Pope Leo XIII">Pope Leo XIII</a> in 1891.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Diana is known for a series of letters written to her and to the other sisters at St. Agnes by Jordan of Saxony; 37 of the 50 letters that have survived were written directly to her.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>  They are "evidence of the deep friendship shared by Diana and Jordan and demonstrate the possibility of warm affection"<sup id="cite_ref-sinsinawa_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sinsinawa-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> between priests and the cloistered nuns that pray for them and their work.  The letters are also a record of the early history of the Dominican order.<sup id="cite_ref-sinsinawa_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sinsinawa-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  Gerald Vann, author of the book <i>To Heaven with Diana!</i>, a compilation of the correspondence between Diana and Jordan, calls the letters "a wonderful treatise on <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> friendship".<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
