He is regarded as the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of boundaries, and by extension, of trade and travel,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> as well as various aspects of <a href="/wiki/Farming" class="mw-redirect" title="Farming">farming</a>. <h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life_and_works">Life and works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Botolph_of_Thorney&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life and works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Botolph%27s_Church,_Iken_-_geograph.org.uk_-_664707.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/St_Botolph%27s_Church%2C_Iken_-_geograph.org.uk_-_664707.jpg/220px-St_Botolph%27s_Church%2C_Iken_-_geograph.org.uk_-_664707.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/St_Botolph%27s_Church%2C_Iken_-_geograph.org.uk_-_664707.jpg/330px-St_Botolph%27s_Church%2C_Iken_-_geograph.org.uk_-_664707.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/St_Botolph%27s_Church%2C_Iken_-_geograph.org.uk_-_664707.jpg/440px-St_Botolph%27s_Church%2C_Iken_-_geograph.org.uk_-_664707.jpg 2x" data-file-width="640" data-file-height="427" /></a><figcaption>St Botolph's Church, <a href="/wiki/Iken" title="Iken">Iken</a>, Suffolk</figcaption></figure>
<p>Little is known about the life of Botolph, other than doubtful details in an account written four hundred years after his death by the 11th-century monk <a href="/wiki/Folcard" title="Folcard">Folcard</a>. Botolph was born sometime  in the early seventh century to noble Saxon parents who were Christians. He and his brother Adulph were educated by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Fursey" title="Saint Fursey">Saint Fursey</a> at <a href="/wiki/Cnobheresburg" title="Cnobheresburg">Cnobheresburg</a> monastery. They were then sent  to study on the Continent, where they became <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">Benedictines</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Thomas_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Thomas-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Adulph remained abroad, where he is said to have become a Bishop.
</p><p>Botolph, returning to England, found favour with a certain "King of the southern Angles", whose sisters he had known in Germany, and was by him permitted to choose a tract of desolate land upon which to build a monastery. The <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> records for the year 654: "The Middle Angles, under earldorman Peada, received the true faith. King <a href="/wiki/Anna_of_East_Anglia" title="Anna of East Anglia">Anna</a> was killed and Botolph began to build the church at Ikanho".<sup id="cite_ref-Thurston_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Thurston-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Botolph founded the monastery of Icanho. Icanho, which means 'ox hill', has been identified as <a href="/wiki/Iken" title="Iken">Iken</a>, located by the <a href="/wiki/Estuary" title="Estuary">estuary</a> of the <a href="/wiki/River_Alde" title="River Alde">River Alde</a> in Suffolk; a church still remains on top of an isolated hill in the parish. At the time, the site was a tidal island all but surrounded by water, but Botolph attracted other monks and hermits and together they turned areas of marsh and scrub into productive grazing and farm land. The monks built several structures, and the monastery grew. Botolph also worked as an itinerant missionary in East Anglia, Kent and Sussex.<sup id="cite_ref-Thomas_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Thomas-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>The Life of St <a href="/wiki/Ceolfrith" title="Ceolfrith">Ceolfrith</a>, written around the time of <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> by an unknown author, mentions an abbot named Botolph in <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">East Anglia</a>, "a man of remarkable life and learning, full of the grace of the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Spirit" title="Holy Spirit">Holy Spirit</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> Ceolfrith visited him about the year 670.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Botolph is supposed to have been originally buried at his foundation of Icanho, but in 970 <a href="/wiki/Edgar_I_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Edgar I of England">Edgar I of England</a> gave permission for the remains of Botolph to be transferred to <a href="/wiki/Burgh,_Suffolk" title="Burgh, Suffolk">Burgh</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Woodbridge,_Suffolk" title="Woodbridge, Suffolk">Woodbridge</a>, to prevent them from being destroyed by invading Danes. They remained for some fifty years before being transferred to their own tomb at <a href="/wiki/Bury_St_Edmunds_Abbey" title="Bury St Edmunds Abbey">Bury St Edmunds Abbey</a> on the instructions of <a href="/wiki/Canute_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Canute the Great">Cnut</a>. The saint's relics were later transferred again, along with those of his brother Adulph, to <a href="/wiki/Thorney_Abbey" title="Thorney Abbey">Thorney Abbey</a>, although his head was transferred to <a href="/wiki/Ely_Cathedral" title="Ely Cathedral">Ely Abbey</a> and various body parts to other houses, including <a href="/wiki/Westminster_Abbey" title="Westminster Abbey">Westminster Abbey</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Thomas_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Thomas-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Church_dedications">Church dedications</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Botolph_of_Thorney&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Church dedications">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Stump%26Ingram.jpeg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c6/Stump%26Ingram.jpeg/220px-Stump%26Ingram.jpeg" decoding="async" width="220" height="208" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c6/Stump%26Ingram.jpeg/330px-Stump%26Ingram.jpeg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c6/Stump%26Ingram.jpeg/440px-Stump%26Ingram.jpeg 2x" data-file-width="2738" data-file-height="2587" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/St_Botolph%27s_Church,_Boston" title="St Botolph&#39;s Church, Boston">St Botolph's Church</a>, the earliest church in <a href="/wiki/Boston,_Lincolnshire" title="Boston, Lincolnshire">Boston, Lincolnshire</a></figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/wiki/St_Botolph%27s_Church_(disambiguation)" class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" title="St Botolph&#39;s Church (disambiguation)">Many English churches</a> are dedicated to Botolph. According to the <i>Oxford Dictionary of Saints</i>, 64 ancient English churches were dedicated to him, but later research has suggested the true number may have been as high as 71, with a high concentration of dedications in East Anglia. <a href="/wiki/The_Stump" class="mw-redirect" title="The Stump">St Botolph's Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Boston,_Lincolnshire" title="Boston, Lincolnshire">Boston, Lincolnshire</a>, known locally as "The Stump", is one of the most famous. Boston, or 'Botolph's town' also gave <a href="/wiki/Boston" title="Boston">Boston</a>, Massachusetts its name.<sup id="cite_ref-Thurston_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Thurston-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St_Botolph%27s_Priory" title="St Botolph&#39;s Priory">St Botolph's Priory</a> in <a href="/wiki/Colchester" title="Colchester">Colchester</a>, <a href="/wiki/Essex" title="Essex">Essex</a>, the first <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Saint_Augustine" title="Order of Saint Augustine">Augustinian</a> <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> in England,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> was built on an earlier Saxon church dedicated to Botolph.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/St_Botolph%27s_Church,_Hardham" title="St Botolph&#39;s Church, Hardham">St Botolph's Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Hardham" title="Hardham">Hardham</a>, <a href="/wiki/West_Sussex" title="West Sussex">West Sussex</a>, houses some of the most ancient surviving wall paintings in Britain, including the earliest known depiction of <a href="/wiki/Saint_George" title="Saint George">St. George</a> in England.
</p><p>In Botolph's role as a patron saint of travellers, four <a href="/wiki/City_of_London" title="City of London">City of London</a> churches were dedicated to him, all of which were close to gates in the City walls: St Botolph <a href="/wiki/Billingsgate" title="Billingsgate">Billingsgate</a>, which was destroyed in the <a href="/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London" title="Great Fire of London">Great Fire</a> and never rebuilt; <a href="/wiki/St_Botolph%27s,_Aldersgate" title="St Botolph&#39;s, Aldersgate">St Botolph's, Aldersgate</a>, <a href="/wiki/St_Botolph-without-Bishopsgate" title="St Botolph-without-Bishopsgate">St Botolph-without-Bishopsgate</a>, where the poet <a href="/wiki/John_Keats" title="John Keats">John Keats</a> was <a href="/wiki/Baptised" class="mw-redirect" title="Baptised">baptised</a>, and <a href="/wiki/St_Botolph%27s_Aldgate" title="St Botolph&#39;s Aldgate">St Botolph's Aldgate</a>. It is believed that these dedications were made because the churches provided places for incoming travellers to give thanks for their safe arrival and for outgoing travellers to pray for a safe journey. An alternative possibility is that the churches were dedicated to the saint because his relics came through the four gates when Edgar moved them from Iken to Westminster Abbey. 
</p><p>Beyond the North Sea, <a href="/wiki/Budolfi_Church" title="Budolfi Church">Budolfi Church</a> (<i>Sankt Budolfi kirke</i>) in <a href="/wiki/Aalborg" title="Aalborg">Aalborg</a>, Denmark, originally a smallish building, grew to be the major church of the town by the late Middle Ages and is now the cathedral church of the diocese of Aalborg.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Secular_connections">Secular connections</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Botolph_of_Thorney&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Secular connections">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Botolph is remembered in the names of the market town of <a href="/wiki/Boston,_Lincolnshire" title="Boston, Lincolnshire">Boston, Lincolnshire</a> in the United Kingdom and <a href="/wiki/Boston" title="Boston">Boston</a>, Massachusetts, in the United States. Boston was originally <i>Botolphston</i> (from "Botolph's stone" or "Botolph's town").
</p><p>In Boston, Massachusetts, Botolph gives his name to the <a href="/wiki/St._Botolph_Club" title="St. Botolph Club">St Botolph Club</a>, a private club,<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> a street in <a href="/wiki/Back_Bay,_Boston" title="Back Bay, Boston">Boston's Back Bay neighborhood</a>, and the President's House at <a href="/wiki/Boston_College" title="Boston College">Boston College</a>.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/University_of_Cambridge" title="University of Cambridge">University of Cambridge</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Poetry" title="Poetry">poetry</a> journal in the 1950s was called <i><a href="/wiki/St_Botolph%27s_Review" class="mw-redirect" title="St Botolph&#39;s Review">St Botolph's Review</a></i>. It was named for <a href="/wiki/St_Botolph%27s_Church,_Cambridge" title="St Botolph&#39;s Church, Cambridge">St Botolph's Church, Cambridge</a> as one of its founders, Lucas Myers, lived at the <a href="/wiki/Rectory" class="mw-redirect" title="Rectory">rectory</a> of St Botolph's Church in <a href="/wiki/Cambridge" title="Cambridge">Cambridge</a>. A second edition of the journal was published in 2006. "St Botolph's College" has been used as a hypothetical college in Cambridge University communications and <a href="/wiki/Tripos" title="Tripos">Tripos</a> examinations.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>The parish of <a href="/wiki/Buttsbury" title="Buttsbury">Buttsbury</a> in <a href="/wiki/Essex" title="Essex">Essex</a> was initially called <i>Botolfvespirie</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> meaning Botolph's Pear Tree. It is sometimes surmised that the name refers to a tree under which St Botolph preached.
</p>
