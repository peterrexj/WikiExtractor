					<div id="contentSub"><div id="mw-content-subtitle"><span class="mw-redirectedfrom">(Redirected from <a href="/w/index.php?title=Sarbel_and_Barbe&amp;redirect=no" class="mw-redirect" title="Sarbel and Barbe">Sarbel and Barbe</a>)</span></div></div>
					
					
					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><p>The <b>Acts of Sharbel</b> or the <b>Hypomnemata of Sharbel</b><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBurkitt1904155_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurkitt1904155-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> is a <a href="/wiki/Syriac_Christianity" title="Syriac Christianity">Syriac Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Christian_martyrs" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian martyrs">martyrdom</a> text pertaining to a pagan high priest who was martyred for converting to <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a>. The setting takes place at <a href="/wiki/Edessa" title="Edessa">Edessa</a> during the fifteenth year of <a href="/wiki/Roman_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Emperor">Roman Emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Trajan" title="Trajan">Trajan</a>'s reign and during the third year of King <a href="/wiki/Abgar_VIII" title="Abgar VIII">Abgar VIII</a>'s reign but is dated by scholars to the 5th century AD.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMillar1993464_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMillar1993464-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Publications">Publications</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Acts_of_Sharbel&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Publications">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: <a href="/wiki/Nicene_and_Post-Nicene_Fathers" title="Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers">Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers</a></div>
<p>The Acts of Sharbel was first translated in <a href="/wiki/English_language" title="English language">English</a> by <a href="/wiki/William_Cureton" title="William Cureton">William Cureton</a> in his <i>Ancient Syriac Documents</i> (<a href="/wiki/London" title="London">London</a>, 1864). He had used a single manuscript from the <a href="/wiki/British_Museum" title="British Museum">British Museum</a> (Brit. Mus. Add. 14, 644). It is written in <a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a> and is dated from the fifth or sixth century AD.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELightfoot188969_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELightfoot188969-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> B. P. Pratten also introduced an English translation to be published in the eighth volume of the <i><a href="/wiki/Ante-Nicene_Fathers_(book)" title="Ante-Nicene Fathers (book)">Ante-Nicene Fathers</a></i> (1871).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERobertsDonaldsonCoxeMenzies1887105_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobertsDonaldsonCoxeMenzies1887105-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In 1874, Moesinger published a <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> translation in his <i>Acta SS. Martyrum Edessenorum</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELightfoot188969_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELightfoot188969-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Narrative_overview">Narrative overview</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Acts_of_Sharbel&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Narrative overview">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The text begins with the current timeline of <a href="/wiki/Trajan" title="Trajan">Trajan</a>'s fifteenth year as the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Emperor" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Emperor">Roman Emperor</a> and the third year of King <a href="/wiki/Abgar_VIII" title="Abgar VIII">Abgar VIII</a>'s rule.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMillar1993464_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMillar1993464-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On the eighth day of the new year (<a href="/wiki/Nisan" title="Nisan">Nisan</a>), the populous commenced a celebration and worship of multiple gods. Particularly, the statues of gods <a href="/wiki/Bel_(mythology)" title="Bel (mythology)">Bel</a> and <a href="/wiki/Nabu" title="Nabu">Nebo</a> were placed at the altar centered in the city. The high priest of the gods, Sharbel, was in charge of preparations of the altar. While organizing the altar, a <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> bishop named <a href="/wiki/Barsamya" class="mw-redirect" title="Barsamya">Barsamya</a> had suddenly walked upon the altar to engage Sharbel publicly. Barsamya preached to him and the public, and doing so, Sharbel was greatly astonished by Barsamya's teachings. He converted to Christianity at that very moment.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDrijvers198035_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDrijvers198035-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After Sharbel's conversion, he would later be prosecuted and put to torture until his death by the orders of judge Lysanias. Sharbel's sister Babai caught his blood while he was beheaded. Seeing this, the executioners decide to kill her. The bodies of Sharbel and his sister would later be stolen from the executioners by a group of followers to be buried. The narrative is concluded with a Marinus and Anatolus claiming to have authored the text.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELightfoot188967_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELightfoot188967-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Composition_and_historicity">Composition and historicity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Acts_of_Sharbel&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Composition and historicity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: <a href="/wiki/Euphemia_and_the_Goth" title="Euphemia and the Goth">Euphemia and the Goth</a></div>
<p>Despite the setting taking place during the reign of Trajan, biblical scholars consider the text spurious and date its composition to the 5th century AD.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEValantasis2000414_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEValantasis2000414-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Because of similar historicity, scholars associate the Acts of Sharbel with the <a href="/wiki/Martyrdom_of_Barsamya" title="Martyrdom of Barsamya">Martyrdom of Barsamya</a> and also compare these texts often to more considerable authentic Syriac Christian writings such as the <a href="/wiki/Acts_of_Shmona_and_of_Gurya" title="Acts of Shmona and of Gurya">Acts of Shmona and of Gurya</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Martyrdom_of_Habib_the_Deacon" title="Martyrdom of Habib the Deacon">Martyrdom of Habib</a> in order to determine their textual historicities.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMillar1993486_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMillar1993486-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In account of the martyrs themselves, Gurya, Shmona, and Habbib's names are present in <a href="/wiki/Martyrology_of_411" title="Martyrology of 411">a Syriac martyrology</a> dated to 411 AD which list names of martyrs from Edessa. Likewise in his <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Carmina_Nisibena&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Carmina Nisibena (page does not exist)">Carmina Nisibena</a></i>, <a href="/wiki/Ephrem_the_Syrian" title="Ephrem the Syrian">Ephrem the Syrian</a> mentions the others but not Sharbel or Barsamya.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAttridgeHata1992223_&amp;_224_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAttridgeHata1992223_&amp;_224-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Sebastian_Brock" title="Sebastian Brock">Sebastian Brock</a> states that the texts originated from the same group of authors.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAttridgeHata1992223_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAttridgeHata1992223-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The names of <a href="/wiki/Thaddeus_of_Edessa" class="mw-redirect" title="Thaddeus of Edessa">Addai</a>'s first Christian converts unique to the <a href="/wiki/Doctrine_of_Addai" title="Doctrine of Addai">Doctrine of Addai</a> are also written in the Acts of Sharbel and the Martyrdom of Barsamya. These names can be found inscribed in former pagan regions of Edessa dating back to the third and fourth century AD and aren't found much in Syriac sources from the fifth century AD or later. Sebastian Brock states that the names were probably genuine ancestral names of those who authored all three texts, however, he doubts their conversion to Christianity. He also states that the names of Addai's first Christian converts mentioned in the Acts of Sharbel and the Martyrdom of Barsamya were written with literary concepts similar to that of the Syriac Christian texts Acts of Shmona and of Gurya and the Martyrdom of Habbib, which he concluded the authors of the Acts of Sharbel and Martyrdom of Barsamya were either inserting the idea that they already had a martyr prior to Shmona, Gurya and Habbib, or were inserting the idea that their pagan ancestors were converted to Christianity at an early period.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAttridgeHata1992228_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAttridgeHata1992228-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
