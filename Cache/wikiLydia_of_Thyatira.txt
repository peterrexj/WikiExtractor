<p><b>Lydia of Thyatira</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Λυδία</span>) is a woman mentioned in the <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a> who is regarded as the first documented convert to <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christianity</a> in <a href="/wiki/Europe" title="Europe">Europe</a>. Several Christian denominations have designated her a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Lydia"><span lang="am" dir="ltr">Lydia</span></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lydia_of_Thyatira&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Lydia">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The name, "Lydia", meaning "the Lydian woman", by which she was known indicates that she was from <a href="/wiki/Lydia" title="Lydia">Lydia</a> in Asia Minor. Though she is commonly known as "St. Lydia" or even more simply "The Woman of Purple," Lydia is given other titles: "of <a href="/wiki/Thyatira" title="Thyatira">Thyatira</a>," "Purpuraria," and "of Philippi ('Philippisia' in Greek)." "[Lydia's] name is an ethnicon, deriving from her place of origin".<sup id="cite_ref-cumming24_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cumming24-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> The first refers to her place of birth, which is a city in the Greek region of Lydia. The second comes from the Latin word for purple and relates to her connection with purple dye. Philippi was the city in which Lydia was living when she met St. Paul and his companions. All these titles expound upon this woman's background.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="New_Testament_narrative">New Testament narrative</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lydia_of_Thyatira&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: New Testament narrative">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p><a href="/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles" title="Acts of the Apostles">Acts 16</a> describes Lydia as follows:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>A certain woman named Lydia, a seller of <a href="/wiki/Tyrian_purple" title="Tyrian purple">purple</a>, of the city of <a href="/wiki/Thyatira" title="Thyatira">Thyatira</a>, one who worshiped God, heard us; whose heart the Lord opened to listen to the things which were spoken by Paul. When she and her household were baptized, she begged us, saying, "If you have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come into my house, and stay." So she persuaded us.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8201;<cite>Acts 16:14–15 World English Bible</cite></div></blockquote>
<p><a href="/wiki/Wayne_Grudem" title="Wayne Grudem">Wayne Grudem</a> sees the story of Lydia as being an example of <a href="/wiki/Effectual_calling" title="Effectual calling">effectual calling</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Grudem692_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Grudem692-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background">Background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lydia_of_Thyatira&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Lydia was most likely from <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greeks" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Greeks">Greek</a> background, since originating from Asia Minor, but probably <a href="/wiki/Romanization_(cultural)" title="Romanization (cultural)">romanized</a> one, while she lived in a Roman settlement.<sup id="cite_ref-Ascough_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ascough-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> She was evidently a well-to-do agent of a <a href="/wiki/Tyrian_purple" title="Tyrian purple">purple-dye</a> firm in Thyatira, a city southeast of Pergamum and approximately 40 miles (64&#160;km) inland, across the Aegean Sea from Athens. Lydia insisted on giving hospitality to <a href="/wiki/Paul_of_Tarsus" class="mw-redirect" title="Paul of Tarsus">Apostle Paul</a> and his companions in <a href="/wiki/Philippi" title="Philippi">Philippi</a>. They stayed with her until their departure, through <a href="/wiki/Amphipolis" title="Amphipolis">Amphipolis</a> and <a href="/wiki/Apollonia_(Mygdonia)" title="Apollonia (Mygdonia)">Apollonia</a>, to <a href="/wiki/Thessalonica" class="mw-redirect" title="Thessalonica">Thessalonica</a> (Acts 16:40–17:1).
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle" title="Paul the Apostle">Paul</a>, <a href="/wiki/Silas" title="Silas">Silas</a> and <a href="/wiki/Saint_Timothy" title="Saint Timothy">Timothy</a> were traveling through the region of Philippi when they encounter "a reputable businesswoman and possibly a widow... [who] was a righteous Gentile or 'God-fearer' attracted to Judaism".<sup id="cite_ref-Hahn_and_Mitch_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hahn_and_Mitch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> "[S]he was one of a large group [considered]... sympathizers with Judaism, believers in the one God, but who had not yet become 'proselytes' or taken the final step to conversion to Judaism".<sup id="cite_ref-Butler_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Butler-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Because these encounters and events take place "in what is now Europe," Lydia is considered "the first 'European' Christian convert".<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Profession">Profession</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lydia_of_Thyatira&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Profession">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>"Thyatira in the province of Lydia (located in what is now western Turkey) was famous for the red [variety of purple] dye".<sup id="cite_ref-cumming24_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cumming24-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Lydia of Thyatira is most known as a "seller" or merchant of purple cloth, which is the likely reason for the Catholic Church naming her "patroness of dyers." It is unclear as to if Lydia simply dealt in the trade of purple dye or whether her business included textiles as well,<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> though all known icons of the saint depict her with some form of purple cloth. Most portray this holy woman wearing a purple shawl or veil, which allows many historians and theologians to believe that she was a merchant of specifically purple cloth.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Social_status">Social status</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lydia_of_Thyatira&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Social status">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>There is some speculation regarding Lydia's social status. Theologians disagree as to whether Lydia was a free woman or servant. "There is no direct evidence that Lydia had once been a slave, but the fact that her name is her place of origin rather than a personal name suggests this as at least a possibility".<sup id="cite_ref-Butler_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Butler-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Ascough cites other examples of noble women named Lydia from the first or second centuries,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> so it is unlikely that she was actually a slave or servant. Furthermore, the book of Acts records, "<i>When she and the members of her household were baptized, she invited us to her home. "If you consider me a believer in the Lord," she said, "come and stay at my house." And she persuaded us.</i>"<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> This implies that Lydia was in charge of the household, as she was able to persuade the household to be baptised, and had authority in the home to invite Paul and his companions to stay in her house – both things that a servant would be far less able to do.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Marital_status">Marital status</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lydia_of_Thyatira&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Marital status">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Because women did not possess the same equality rights as modern women, it appears unusual that Lydia would be capable of inviting a group of foreign men to her house without a man's consent. "The fact that there is no mention of a man has been used to deduce that she was a widow, but this has been challenged as a patriarchal interpretation".<sup id="cite_ref-Butler_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Butler-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Lydia's evident social power exemplified by her control of a household and ownership of a house (which she offered to St. Paul and his companions) indicates that she was most likely a free woman and possibly a widow.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Feast_day">Feast day</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lydia_of_Thyatira&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Feast day">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Many Christian denominations recognize Lydia of Thyatira as a saint, though her feast day varies greatly. In the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>, her feast day is August 3.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">The Episcopal Church</a> honors St. Lydia along in its <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church_in_the_United_States_of_America)" class="mw-redirect" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church in the United States of America)">liturgical calendar</a> on May 21.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Churches</a> remember Lydia on various days, with some jurisdictions remembering her twice during a liturgical year. Many Eastern Orthodox churches, including the Self-Ruled Antiochian Orthodox Christian Archdiocese of North America, remember St. Lydia on <a href="/wiki/May_20_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics)" title="May 20 (Eastern Orthodox liturgics)">May 20</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> However, some divisions of the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodox Church</a> (other than the <a href="/wiki/Orthodox_Church_in_America" title="Orthodox Church in America">Orthodox Church in America</a>) observe both June 25 and March 27 as her feast days.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Lutheran community is also divided. The <a href="/wiki/Evangelical_Lutheran_Church_in_America" title="Evangelical Lutheran Church in America">ELCA</a> commemorates Sts. Lydia, Dorcas and Phoebe on January 27,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> while the <a href="/wiki/Lutheran_Church%E2%80%93Missouri_Synod" title="Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod">LCMS</a> celebrates the three women on October 25.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Devotion">Devotion</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Lydia_of_Thyatira&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Devotion">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Devotion to St. Lydia is greater in the Orthodox Church than in the Latin Rite of the Roman Catholic Church, and this is evident by the myriad of icons depicting this woman. The Orthodox Churches have given her the title of "Equal to the Apostles," which signifies her importance and level of holiness. There is a church located in Philippi, which many consider to be built in St. Lydia's honor. A modern baptistry is located on the traditional site in Krynides where Lydia was baptized by St. Paul near Philippi as well.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
