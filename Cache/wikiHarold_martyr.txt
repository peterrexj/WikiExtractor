<p><b>Harold of Gloucester</b> (died 1168) was a supposed <a href="/wiki/Child_martyr" class="mw-redirect" title="Child martyr">child martyr</a> who was falsely claimed by <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">Benedictine monks</a> to have been <a href="/wiki/Human_sacrifice" title="Human sacrifice">ritually murdered</a> by <a href="/wiki/Jews" title="Jews">Jews</a> in <a href="/wiki/Gloucester" title="Gloucester">Gloucester</a>, <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a>, in 1168.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The claims arose in the aftermath of the circulation of the first <a href="/wiki/Blood_libel" title="Blood libel">blood libel</a> myth following the unsolved murder of <a href="/wiki/William_of_Norwich" title="William of Norwich">William of Norwich</a>. A Christian <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cult</a> and veneration of Harold was briefly promoted in Gloucester, but soon died out.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Context">Context</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Harold_of_Gloucester&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Context">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>He is one of a small group of 12th-century <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">English</a> unofficial <a href="/wiki/Saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Saints">saints</a> of strikingly similar characteristics: they were all young boys, all mysteriously found dead and all hailed as martyrs to alleged anti-Christian practices among <a href="/wiki/Jews" title="Jews">Jews</a>. Contemporary assumptions made about the circumstances of their deaths evolved into the <a href="/wiki/Blood_libel" title="Blood libel">blood libel</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The accusations following Harold's death came after widely circulated claims of Jewish ritual child-murder in the case of William of Norwich, who died in 1144. The stories created about Harold's death were followed by similar claims about <a href="/wiki/Robert_of_Bury" title="Robert of Bury">Robert of Bury</a>. The phenomenon culminated in the trials and executions occasioned by the death of <a href="/wiki/Little_Saint_Hugh_of_Lincoln" title="Little Saint Hugh of Lincoln">Hugh of Lincoln</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Harold_of_Gloucester&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Harold's body was apparently found floating in a river.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> According to Anna Sapir Abulafia, the local <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">Benedictine</a> monks used the discovery to claim that "the child had been spirited away by the Jews on the 21st February for them to torture him to death on the night of 16th March". They proceeded to identify marks on the body which were supposed to suggest that the child had been made to wear a <a href="/wiki/Crown_of_thorns" title="Crown of thorns">crown of thorns</a> and had been subject to some form of crucifixion.<sup id="cite_ref-hil_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hil-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> All of these were features of the mythology created around the death of William of Norwich in Easter 1144.<sup id="cite_ref-hil_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hil-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The story was given credence because Jews had apparently been congregating in Gloucester at the time for a <i><a href="/wiki/Brit_milah" title="Brit milah">brit milah</a></i> circumcision ceremony. The ritual spilling of a child's blood in circumcision was symbolically linked in the minds of Christians to the Crucifixion.<sup id="cite_ref-anna_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-anna-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Events seem to have occurred close to Easter, though inconsistencies the days and dates given in sources suggest a confusion over the year in which the events happened, which may imply a "botched attempt to force dates to fit into the Christian liturgical calendar".<sup id="cite_ref-anna_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-anna-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There is no evidence that any Jews were ever arrested or charged with a crime, which suggests that the claims began as no more than speculation after the missing local child's body was found. The accusation helped to convince Gloucester Jews to lend money to finance <a href="/wiki/Richard_de_Clare,_2nd_Earl_of_Pembroke" title="Richard de Clare, 2nd Earl of Pembroke">Richard de Clare</a>, known as "Strongbow", in his conquest of Ireland.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The story hardened into fact in later retellings.<sup id="cite_ref-anna_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-anna-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The attempt to establish a cult of Harold seems to have been unsuccessful. It was never officially supported and died out long before the <a href="/wiki/Reformation" title="Reformation">Reformation</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-hil_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hil-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Significance">Significance</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Harold_of_Gloucester&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Significance">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to historian Joe Hillaby, the story of Harold was never widely circulated, and was much less well-known than that of <a href="/wiki/William_of_Norwich" title="William of Norwich">William of Norwich</a>, but it was crucially important because it established that the mythology created around William's death could be used as a template for explaining later deaths. For the first time an unexplained child death occurring near the Easter festival was arbitrarily linked to Jews in the vicinity by local Christian churchmen: "they established a pattern quickly taken up elsewhere. Within three years the first ritual murder charge was made in France."<sup id="cite_ref-hil_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hil-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
