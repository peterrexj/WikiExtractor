<p><b>Romanos the Melodist</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Ῥωμανὸς ὁ Μελωδός</span>; late 5th-century —&#160;after 555) was a <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire" title="Byzantine Empire">Byzantine</a> <a href="/wiki/Hymnographer" class="mw-redirect" title="Hymnographer">hymnographer</a> and composer,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEngberg2001_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngberg2001-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> who is a central early figure in the history of <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_music" title="Byzantine music">Byzantine music</a>. Called "the <a href="/wiki/Pindar" title="Pindar">Pindar</a> of rhythmic poetry",<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> he flourished during the sixth century, though the earliest manuscripts of his works are dated centuries after this.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas202024–25_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas202024–25-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He was the foremost <a href="/wiki/Kontakion" title="Kontakion">Kontakion</a> composer of his time.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELevy2001_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELevy2001-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romanos_the_Melodist&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The main source of information about the life of Romanos comes from the <a href="/wiki/Menaion" title="Menaion">Menaion</a> for October. Beyond this, his name is mentioned by only two other ancient sources. once in the eighth-century poet St. Germanos and once in the <a href="/wiki/Suda" title="Suda">Souda</a> (s. v. <i>anaklomenon</i>) where he is called "Romanos the melodist". From this scanty evidence we learn that he was born to a Jewish family in either <a href="/wiki/Emesa" class="mw-redirect" title="Emesa">Emesa</a> (modern-day Homs) or <a href="/wiki/Damascus" title="Damascus">Damascus</a> in <a href="/wiki/Syria" title="Syria">Syria</a>. He was baptized as a young boy (though whether or not his parents also converted is uncertain). Having moved to <a href="/wiki/Beirut" title="Beirut">Berytus</a> (Beirut), he was <a href="/wiki/Ordination" title="Ordination">ordained</a> a <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a> in the Church of the Resurrection there.
</p><p>He later moved to <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a> during the reign of the emperor Anastasius—on the question whether <a href="/wiki/Anastasius_I_(emperor)" class="mw-redirect" title="Anastasius I (emperor)">Anastasius I</a> (491-518) or <a href="/wiki/Anastasius_II_(emperor)" title="Anastasius II (emperor)">Anastasius II</a> (713-716) is meant, the renowned Byzantinologist Prof. <a href="/wiki/Karl_Krumbacher" title="Karl Krumbacher">Karl Krumbacher</a> favours the earlier date.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> There he served as <a href="/wiki/Sacristan" title="Sacristan">sacristan</a> in the "Great Church" (<a href="/wiki/Hagia_Sophia" title="Hagia Sophia">Hagia Sophia</a>), residing to the end of his life at the Monastery of Kyros, where he was buried along with his disciple St. Ananias.
</p><p>If those scholars who believe that he lived during the reign of the earlier Anastasius are correct, then he may have continued writing during the reign of Emperor <a href="/wiki/Justinian" class="mw-redirect" title="Justinian">Justinian</a> (527-65), who was himself a hymn-writer; this would make him a contemporary of two other famous Byzantine hymnographers, Anastasios and Kyriakos.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legend">Legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romanos_the_Melodist&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to legend, Romanus was not at first considered to be either a talented <a href="/wiki/Reader_(liturgy)" title="Reader (liturgy)">reader</a> or singer. He was, however, loved by the <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_of_Constantinople" title="Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople">Patriarch of Constantinople</a> because of his great humility. Once, around the year 518, while serving in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_St._Mary_of_Blachernae_(Istanbul)" class="mw-redirect" title="Church of St. Mary of Blachernae (Istanbul)">Church of the Panagia</a> at <a href="/wiki/Blachernae" title="Blachernae">Blachernae</a>, during the <a href="/wiki/All-Night_Vigil" class="mw-redirect" title="All-Night Vigil">All-Night Vigil</a> for the <a href="/wiki/Christmas_Day" class="mw-redirect" title="Christmas Day">Feast of the Nativity of Christ</a>, he was assigned to read the <a href="/wiki/Kathisma" title="Kathisma">kathisma</a> verses from the <a href="/wiki/Psalter" title="Psalter">Psalter</a>. He read so poorly that another reader had to take his place. Some of the lesser clergy ridiculed Romanus for this, and being humiliated he sat down in one of the <a href="/wiki/Choir_(architecture)" title="Choir (architecture)">choir stalls</a>. Overcome by weariness and sorrow, he soon fell asleep. As he slept, the <a href="/wiki/Theotokos" title="Theotokos">Theotokos</a> (Mother of God) appeared to him with a scroll in her hand. She commanded him to eat the scroll, and as soon as he did so, he awoke. He immediately received a blessing from the Patriarch, mounted the <a href="/wiki/Pulpit" title="Pulpit">ambo</a> (pulpit), and chanted extemporaneously his famous <a href="/wiki/Kontakion" title="Kontakion">Kontakion</a> of the Nativity, "Today the Virgin gives birth to Him Who is above all being…." The emperor, the patriarch, the clergy, and the entire congregation were amazed at both the profound theology of the hymn and Romanos' clear, sonorous voice as he sang. According to tradition, this was the very first kontakion ever sung. The Greek word "kontakion" (<span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">κοντάκιον</span></span>) refers to the shaft on which a scroll is wound, hence the significance of the Theotokos' command for him to swallow a scroll, indicating that his compositions were by divine inspiration.  The scene of Romanos's first performance is often shown in the lower register of <a href="/wiki/Intercession_of_the_Theotokos" title="Intercession of the Theotokos">Pokrov</a> icons (example above).<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works">Works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romanos_the_Melodist&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Romanos_the_Melodist_(Menologion_of_Basil_II).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/Romanos_the_Melodist_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg/220px-Romanos_the_Melodist_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="146" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/Romanos_the_Melodist_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg/330px-Romanos_the_Melodist_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/Romanos_the_Melodist_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg/440px-Romanos_the_Melodist_%28Menologion_of_Basil_II%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="807" data-file-height="536" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Romanos_the_Melodist_(Menologion_of_Basil_II).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Romanos and Virgin Mary, Miniature from the <i><a href="/wiki/Menologion_of_Basil_II" title="Menologion of Basil II">Menologion of Basil II</a></i></div></div></div>
<p>Romanos wrote in an Atticized literary <a href="/wiki/Koine" class="mw-redirect" title="Koine">koine</a>—i.e., he had a popular, but elevated style—and abundant Semiticisms support the view that he was of Jewish origin. Arresting imagery, sharp metaphors and similes, bold comparisons, antitheses, coining of successful maxims, and vivid dramatization characterize his style.
</p><p>He is said to have composed more than 1,000 <a href="/wiki/Hymn" title="Hymn">hymns</a> or <i>kontakia</i> celebrating various festivals of the <a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_year" class="mw-redirect" title="Ecclesiastical year">ecclesiastical year</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Lives_of_the_saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Lives of the saints">lives of the saints</a> and other sacred subjects,<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> some 60 to 80 of which survive (though not all those attributed to him may be genuine).<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> The earliest manuscripts of his works are dated centuries after his lifetime, akin to those of his successors <a href="/wiki/Andrew_of_Crete" title="Andrew of Crete">Andrew of Crete</a> and <a href="/wiki/Kassia" title="Kassia">Kassia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMellas202024–25_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMellas202024–25-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The oldest editions of full texts are dated to the 11th century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEngberg2001_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngberg2001-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Today, usually only the first <a href="/wiki/Strophe" title="Strophe">strophe</a> of each kontakion is chanted during the <a href="/wiki/Canonical_hours" title="Canonical hours">divine services</a>, the full hymn having been replaced by the <a href="/wiki/Canon_(hymnography)" title="Canon (hymnography)">canon</a>. A full kontakion was a poetic <a href="/wiki/Sermon" title="Sermon">sermon</a> composed of from 18 to 30 verses or <i><a href="/wiki/Oikos" title="Oikos">ikoi</a></i>, each with a refrain, and united by an <a href="/wiki/Acrostic" title="Acrostic">acrostic</a>.  When it was sung to an original melody, it was called an <i><a href="/wiki/Idiomelon" title="Idiomelon">idiomelon</a></i>.  Originally, Saint Romanos' works were known simply as "<a href="/wiki/Psalms" title="Psalms">psalms</a>", "<a href="/wiki/Ode" title="Ode">odes</a>", or "<a href="/wiki/Poem" class="mw-redirect" title="Poem">poems</a>".  It was only in the ninth century that the term kontakion came into use.
</p><p>Among his known works are kontakia on:
</p>
<ul><li>The Nativity of Christ</li>
<li>The <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">Martyrdom</a> of <a href="/wiki/St_Stephen" class="mw-redirect" title="St Stephen">St Stephen</a></li>
<li>The Death of a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">Monk</a></li>
<li>The <a href="/wiki/Last_judgment" class="mw-redirect" title="Last judgment">Last Judgment</a></li>
<li>The <a href="/wiki/Prodigal_Son" class="mw-redirect" title="Prodigal Son">Prodigal Son</a></li>
<li>The Raising of Lazarus (for <a href="/wiki/Lazarus_Saturday" title="Lazarus Saturday">Lazarus Saturday</a>, the day before Palm Sunday)</li>
<li>Adam's Lament (for <a href="/wiki/Palm_Sunday" title="Palm Sunday">Palm Sunday</a>)</li>
<li>The Treachery of <a href="/wiki/Judas_Iscariot" title="Judas Iscariot">Judas</a></li></ul>
<p>His Kontakion of the Nativity is still considered to be his masterpiece, and up until the twelfth century it was sung every year at the imperial banquet on that feast by the joint choirs of Hagia Sophia and of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_the_Holy_Apostles" title="Church of the Holy Apostles">Church of the Holy Apostles</a> in Constantinople. Most of the poem takes the form of a dialogue between the Mother of God and the <a href="/wiki/Magi" title="Magi">Magi</a>, whose visit to the newborn <a href="/wiki/Christ_Child" title="Christ Child">Christ Child</a> is celebrated in the Byzantine rite on 25 December, rather than on the 6th of January when Western Christians celebrate the visit (in the Orthodox Church, January 6, the <a href="/wiki/Epiphany_(Christian)#Eastern_Christian_Churches" class="mw-redirect" title="Epiphany (Christian)">Feast of the Theophany</a>, celebrates the Baptism of Christ).
</p><p>Of his other Kontakia, one of the most well-known is the hymn, "My soul, my soul, why sleepest thou..."<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> which is chanted as part of the service of the "Great Canon" of <a href="/wiki/Andrew_of_Crete" title="Andrew of Crete">Andrew of Crete</a> on the fifth Thursday of <a href="/wiki/Great_Lent" title="Great Lent">Great Lent</a>.
</p><p>Romanos is one of many persons who have been credited with composing the famous <a href="/wiki/Akathist" title="Akathist">Akathist</a> Hymn to the Theotokos, which is still sung during <a href="/wiki/Great_Lent" title="Great Lent">Great Lent</a>. Most recent scholarship has asserted that he is not the author of the hymn, although there is significant dissent among scholars.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Karl_Krumbacher" title="Karl Krumbacher">Karl Krumbacher</a> published in Munich several previously unpublished chants of Romanos and other hymnographers, from manuscripts discovered in the library of the Monastery of St John the Theologian in <a href="/wiki/Patmos" title="Patmos">Patmos</a>. There exists in the library of <a href="/wiki/Moscow" title="Moscow">Moscow</a> a Greek <a href="/wiki/Manuscript" title="Manuscript">manuscript</a> which contains kontakia and oikoi for the whole year, but does not include all compositions of Romanos.
</p><p>Krumbacher says of his work:
</p>
<blockquote><p> In poetic talent, fire of inspiration, depth of feeling, and elevation of language, he far surpasses all the other melodes. The literary history of the future will perhaps acclaim Romanos for the greatest ecclesiastical poet of all ages.</p></blockquote>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy_and_depictions">Legacy and depictions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Romanos_the_Melodist&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Legacy and depictions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Although in more recent icons Saint Romanos is depicted standing on the ambo (directly in front of the <a href="/wiki/Iconostasis" title="Iconostasis">iconostasis</a>) and wearing a deacon's <a href="/wiki/Sticharion" title="Sticharion">sticharion</a>, the famous Russian church <a href="/wiki/Musicologist" class="mw-redirect" title="Musicologist">musicologist</a>, <a href="/wiki/Johann_von_Gardner" title="Johann von Gardner">Johann von Gardner</a>, points out that in the oldest icons he is portrayed wearing the shorter red <a href="/wiki/Tunic" title="Tunic">tunic</a> of a <a href="/wiki/Chanter" title="Chanter">singer</a> and standing on a raised platform in the middle of the church.
</p><p>In the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, Saint Romanos is the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of music; he is celebrated yearly on 1 October.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEngberg2001_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngberg2001-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church" title="Armenian Apostolic Church">Armenian Apostolic Church</a> commemorates Saint Romanos on the Saturday before the third Sunday of the <a href="/wiki/Exaltation_of_the_Cross" class="mw-redirect" title="Exaltation of the Cross">Exaltation of the Cross</a>. This is a remarkable fact given that Saint Romanos lived after the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Chalcedon" title="Council of Chalcedon">Council of Chalcedon</a> and the Armenian Apostolic Church is <a href="/wiki/Non-Chalcedonian" class="mw-redirect" title="Non-Chalcedonian">non-Chalcedonian</a>. Nevertheless, his music significantly influenced Armenian hymnography.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
