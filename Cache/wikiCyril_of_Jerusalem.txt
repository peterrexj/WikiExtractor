<p><b>Cyril of Jerusalem</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Κύριλλος Α΄ Ἱεροσολύμων</span>, <i>Kýrillos A Ierosolýmon</i>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Cyrillus Hierosolymitanus</i>; <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;313</span><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> – AD 386) was a theologian of the early Church. About the end of AD 350 he succeeded <a href="/wiki/Maximus_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="Maximus of Jerusalem">Maximus</a> as Bishop of Jerusalem, but was exiled on more than one occasion due to the enmity of <a href="/wiki/Acacius_of_Caesarea" title="Acacius of Caesarea">Acacius of Caesarea</a>, and the policies of various emperors. Cyril left important writings documenting the instruction of <a href="/wiki/Catechumen" class="mw-redirect" title="Catechumen">catechumens</a> and the order of the <a href="/wiki/Liturgy" title="Liturgy">Liturgy</a> in his day.
</p><p>Cyril is venerated as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> within the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Oriental Orthodox Church">Oriental Orthodox Church</a>, and the <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a>. In 1883, Cyril was declared a <a href="/wiki/Doctor_of_the_Church" title="Doctor of the Church">Doctor of the Church</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIII" title="Pope Leo XIII">Pope Leo XIII</a>.
</p><p>Cyril is <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">remembered</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> with a commemoration on 18 March.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life_and_character">Life and character</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyril_of_Jerusalem&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life and character">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Little is known of his life before he became a bishop; the assignment of his birth to the year 315 rests on conjecture.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> According to Butler, Cyril was born at or near the city of Jerusalem, and was apparently well-read in both the writings of the early <a href="/wiki/Church_Fathers" title="Church Fathers">Christian theologians</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Greek_philosophers" class="mw-redirect" title="Greek philosophers">Greek philosophers</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cyril was ordained a <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a> by Bishop <a href="/wiki/Macarius_of_Jerusalem" title="Macarius of Jerusalem">Macarius of Jerusalem</a> in about 335 and a priest some eight years later by Bishop <a href="/wiki/Maximus_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="Maximus of Jerusalem">Maximus</a>. Around the end of 350 he succeeded Maximus in the See of Jerusalem, although the evidence for this relies on the <i>Catecheses</i> written by Cyril where he refers to himself as "bishop". <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> also suggests Cyril was an <a href="/wiki/Arian" class="mw-redirect" title="Arian">Arian</a> at this stage<sup id="cite_ref-laielh_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-laielh-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cyril is described as preacher and liturgist by the pilgrim <a href="/wiki/Egeria_(pilgrim)" title="Egeria (pilgrim)">Egeria</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Episcopacy">Episcopacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyril_of_Jerusalem&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Episcopacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Relations between <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop" title="Metropolitan bishop">Metropolitan</a> <a href="/wiki/Acacius_of_Caesarea" title="Acacius of Caesarea">Acacius of Caesarea</a> and Cyril became strained. Acacius is presented as a leading <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arian</a> by the orthodox historians, and his opposition to Cyril in the 350s is attributed by these writers to this. Sozomen also suggests that the tension may have been increased by Acacius's jealousy of the importance assigned to Cyril's See by the <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Nicaea" title="First Council of Nicaea">Council of Nicaea</a>, as well as by the threat posed to Caesarea by the rising influence of the seat of Jerusalem as it developed into the prime Christian holy place and became a centre of pilgrimage.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Acacius charged Cyril with selling church property.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> The city of Jerusalem had suffered drastic food shortages at which point church historians Sozomen and Theodoret report "Cyril secretly sold sacramental ornaments of the church and a valuable holy robe, fashioned with gold thread that the emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantine_the_Great" title="Constantine the Great">Constantine</a> had once donated for the bishop to wear when he performed the rite of Baptism",<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDrijvers200465_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDrijvers200465-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> possibly to keep people from starving.
</p><p>For two years, Cyril resisted Acacius' summons to account for his actions, but a church council held under Acacius's influence in 357 deposed Cyril in his absence, and Cyril took refuge with Silvanus, Bishop of <a href="/wiki/Tarsus_(city)" class="mw-redirect" title="Tarsus (city)">Tarsus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> The following year, 359, in an atmosphere more hostile to Acacius, the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Seleucia" title="Council of Seleucia">Council of Seleucia</a> reinstated Cyril and deposed Acacius. In 360 this was reversed by Emperor Constantius again,<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> and Cyril suffered another year's exile from Jerusalem until the Emperor <a href="/wiki/Julian_the_Apostate" class="mw-redirect" title="Julian the Apostate">Julian</a>'s accession allowed him to return in 361.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENorris200777_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENorris200777-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cyril was once again banished from Jerusalem by the <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arian</a> Emperor <a href="/wiki/Valens" title="Valens">Valens</a> in 367, but was able to return again after Valens's death in 378, after which he remained undisturbed until his death in 386. In 380, <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Nyssa" title="Gregory of Nyssa">Gregory of Nyssa</a> came to Jerusalem on the recommendation of a council held at Antioch in the preceding year. He seemingly found the faith in good shape, but worried that the city was prey to parties and corrupt in morals.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> Cyril's jurisdiction over Jerusalem was expressly confirmed by the <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Constantinople" title="First Council of Constantinople">First Council of Constantinople</a> (381), at which he was present.<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> At that council he voted for acceptance of the term <i><a href="/wiki/Homoousian" class="mw-redirect" title="Homoousian">homoousios</a></i> (which defined the nature between "God the Father", and "God the Son"), having been finally convinced that there was no better alternative.<sup id="cite_ref-laielh_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-laielh-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> His story is perhaps best representative of those Eastern bishops (perhaps a majority) initially mistrustful of Nicaea, who came to accept the creed of that council, and the doctrine of the <i><a href="/wiki/Homoousion" title="Homoousion">homoousion</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-Andrew_Louth_2010_p284_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Andrew_Louth_2010_p284-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Theological_position">Theological position</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyril_of_Jerusalem&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Theological position">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Though his theology was at first somewhat indefinite in phraseology, he undoubtedly gave a thorough adhesion to the <a href="/wiki/Nicene_Creed" title="Nicene Creed">Nicene Orthodoxy</a>. Even if he did avoid the debatable term <i><a href="/wiki/Homoousian" class="mw-redirect" title="Homoousian">homoousios</a></i>, he expressed its sense in many passages, which exclude equally <a href="/wiki/Patripassianism" title="Patripassianism">Patripassianism</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sabellianism" title="Sabellianism">Sabellianism</a>, and the formula "there was a time when the Son was not" attributed to Arius.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> In other points he takes the ordinary ground of the Eastern Fathers, as in the emphasis he lies on the freedom of the will, the <i>autexousion</i> (αὐτεξούσιον), and in his view of the nature of sin. To him sin is the consequence of freedom, not a natural condition. The body is not the cause, but the instrument of sin. The remedy for it is repentance, on which he insists. Like many of the Eastern Fathers, he focuses on high moral living as essential to true Christianity. His doctrine of the <a href="/wiki/Resurrection_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Resurrection of Christ">Resurrection</a> is not quite so realistic as that of other Fathers; but his conception of the Church is decidedly empirical: the existing Church form is the true one, intended by Christ, the completion of the Church of the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old Testament</a>. His interpretation of the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a> is disputed. Some argue he sometimes seems to approach the symbolic view, though he professes a strong realistic doctrine. The bread and wine are not mere elements, but the body and blood of Christ.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cyril's writings are filled with the loving and forgiving nature of God which was somewhat uncommon during his time period. Cyril fills his writings with great lines of the healing power of forgiveness and the Holy Spirit, like "The Spirit comes gently and makes himself known by his fragrance. He is not felt as a burden for God is light, very light. Rays of light and knowledge stream before him as the Spirit approaches. The Spirit comes with the tenderness of a true friend to save, to heal, to teach, to counsel, to strengthen and to console".  Cyril himself followed God's message of forgiveness many times throughout his life. This is most clearly seen in his two major exiles where Cyril was disgraced and forced to leave his position and his people behind. He never wrote or showed any ill will towards those who wronged him. Cyril stressed the themes of healing and regeneration in his catechesis.<sup id="cite_ref-hell_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-hell-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Catechetical_lectures">Catechetical lectures</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyril_of_Jerusalem&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Catechetical lectures">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Cyrillus_Hierosolymitani_Catecheses.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Cyrillus_Hierosolymitani_Catecheses.jpg/220px-Cyrillus_Hierosolymitani_Catecheses.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="332" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Cyrillus_Hierosolymitani_Catecheses.jpg/330px-Cyrillus_Hierosolymitani_Catecheses.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/25/Cyrillus_Hierosolymitani_Catecheses.jpg 2x" data-file-width="361" data-file-height="545" /></a><figcaption>Parallel Greek and Latin versions of Cyril's <i>Catacheses</i></figcaption></figure>
<p>Cyril's famous twenty-three lectures given to catechumens in Jerusalem being prepared for, and after, <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptism</a> are best considered in two parts: the first eighteen lectures are commonly known as the <i>Catechetical Lectures</i>, <i>Catechetical Orations</i> or <i>Catechetical Homilies</i>, while the final five are often called the <i>Mystagogic Catecheses</i> (μυσταγωγικαί), because they deal with the <i>mysteries</i> (μυστήρια) i.e. <a href="/wiki/Sacraments" class="mw-redirect" title="Sacraments">Sacraments</a> of <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">Baptism</a>, <a href="/wiki/Confirmation" title="Confirmation">Confirmation</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His <a href="/wiki/Catechesis" title="Catechesis">catechetical lectures</a> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a> Κατηχήσεις, <i>Katēchēseis</i>)<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> are generally assumed, on the basis of limited evidence, to have been delivered either in Cyril's early years as a bishop, around 350, or perhaps in 348, while Cyril was still a priest, deputising for his bishop, Maximus.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Catechetical Lectures</i> were given in the <i>Martyrion</i>, the basilica erected by Constantine.<sup id="cite_ref-Andrew_Louth_2010_p284_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Andrew_Louth_2010_p284-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> They contain instructions on the principal topics of Christian faith and practice, in a popular rather than scientific manner, full of a warm pastoral love and care for the catechumens to whom they were delivered. Each lecture is based upon a text of Scripture, and there is an abundance of Scriptural quotation throughout. In the <i>Catechetical Lectures</i>, parallel with the exposition of the <a href="/wiki/Creed" title="Creed">Creed</a> as it was then received in the Church of Jerusalem are vigorous polemics against <a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">pagan</a>, Jewish, and <a href="/wiki/Christian_heresy" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian heresy">heretical</a> errors. They are of great importance for the light which they throw upon the method of instruction usual of that age, as well as upon the liturgical practises of the period, of which they give the fullest account extant.<sup id="cite_ref-Foley_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foley-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<blockquote><p>It is not only among us, who are marked with the name of Christ, that the dignity of faith is great; all the business of the world, even of those outside the Church, is accomplished by faith. By faith, marriage laws join in union persons who were strangers to one another. By faith, agriculture is sustained; for a man does not endure the toil involved unless he believes he will reap a harvest. By faith, seafaring men, entrusting themselves to a tiny wooden craft, exchange the solid element of the land for the unstable motion of the waves."<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>In the 13th lecture, Cyril of Jerusalem discusses the Crucifixion and burial of Jesus Christ. The main themes that Cyril focuses on in these lectures are <a href="/wiki/Original_sin" title="Original sin">Original sin</a> and Jesus' sacrificing himself to save us from our sins. Also, the burial and Resurrection which occurred three days later proving the divinity of Jesus Christ and the loving nature of the Father. Cyril was very adamant about the fact that Jesus went to his death with full knowledge and willingness. Not only did he go willingly but throughout the process he maintained his faith and forgave all those who betrayed him and engaged in his execution. Cyril writes "who did not sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth, who, when he was reviled, did not revile, when he suffered did not threaten".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDrijvers20047_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDrijvers20047-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> This line by Cyril shows his belief in the selflessness of Jesus especially in this last final act of Love. The lecture also gives a sort of insight to what Jesus may have been feeling during the execution from the whippings and beatings, to the crown of thorns, to the nailing on the cross. Cyril intertwines the story with the messages Jesus told throughout his life before his execution relating to his final act. For example, Cyril writes "I gave my back to those who beat me and my cheeks to blows; and my face I did not shield from the shame of spitting".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDrijvers200413-14_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDrijvers200413-14-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> This clearly reflects the teachings of Jesus to turn the other cheek and not raising your hands against violence because violence just begets violence begets violence. The segment of the Catechesis really reflects the voice Cyril maintained in all of his writing. The writings always have the central message of the Bible; Cyril is not trying to add his own beliefs in reference to religious interpretation and remains grounded in true biblical teachings.
</p><p>Danielou sees the baptism rite as carrying eschatological overtones, in that "to inscribe for baptism is to write one's name in the register of the elect in heaven".<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Eschatology">Eschatology</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyril_of_Jerusalem&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Eschatology">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Oded Irshai observed that Cyril lived in a time of intense apocalyptic expectation, when Christians were eager to find apocalyptic meaning in every historical event or natural disaster. Cyril spent a good part of his episcopacy in intermittent exile from Jerusalem. Abraham Malherbe argued that when a leader's control over a community is fragile, directing attention to the imminent arrival of the antichrist effectively diverts attention from that fragility.<sup id="cite_ref-Dayna_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dayna-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Soon after his appointment, Cyril in his <i>Letter to Constantius</i><sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> of 351 recorded the appearance of a cross of light in the sky above <a href="/wiki/Golgotha" class="mw-redirect" title="Golgotha">Golgotha</a>, witnessed by the whole population of Jerusalem. The Greek church commemorates this miracle on 7 May. Though in modern times the authenticity of the <i>Letter</i> has been questioned, on the grounds that the word <i><a href="/wiki/Homoousian" class="mw-redirect" title="Homoousian">homoousios</a></i> occurs in the final blessing, many scholars believe this may be a later interpolation, and accept the letter's authenticity on the grounds of other pieces of internal evidence.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cyril interpreted this as both a sign of support for Constantius, who was soon to face the usurper <a href="/wiki/Magnentius" title="Magnentius">Magnentius</a>, and as announcing the Second Coming, which was soon to take place in Jerusalem. Not surprisingly, in Cyril's eschatological analysis, Jerusalem holds a central position.<sup id="cite_ref-Cain_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Cain-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Matthew 24:6 speaks of "wars and reports of wars", as a sign of the End Times, and it is within this context that Cyril read Julian's war with the Persians. Matthew 24:7 speaks of "earthquakes from place to place", and Jerusalem experienced an <a href="/wiki/Galilee_earthquake_of_363" class="mw-redirect" title="Galilee earthquake of 363">earthquake</a> in 363 at a time when Julian was attempting to rebuild the temple in Jerusalem.<sup id="cite_ref-Dayna_29-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dayna-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Embroiled in a rivalry with Acacius of Caesarea over the relative primacy of their respective sees, Cyril saw even ecclesial discord a sign of the Lord's coming.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Catechesis 15 would appear to cast Julian as the antichrist, although Irshai views this as a later interpolation.<sup id="cite_ref-Dayna_29-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dayna-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>"In His first coming, He endured the Cross, despising shame; in His second, He comes attended by a host of Angels, receiving glory. We rest not then upon His first advent only, but look also for His second."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECyrilGifford1894_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECyrilGifford1894-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> He looked forward to the Second Advent which would bring an end to the world and then the created world to be made anew. At the Second Advent he expected to rise in the resurrection if it came after his time on earth.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFroom1950412–415_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFroom1950412–415-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mystagogic_Catecheses"><i>Mystagogic Catecheses</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cyril_of_Jerusalem&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Mystagogic Catecheses">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There has been considerable controversy over the date and authorship of the <i>Mystagogic Catecheses</i>, addressed to the newly baptized, in preparation for the reception of <a href="/wiki/Holy_Communion" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Communion">Holy Communion</a>, with some scholars having attributed them to Cyril's successor as Bishop of Jerusalem, <a href="/wiki/John_II,_Bishop_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="John II, Bishop of Jerusalem">John</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Many scholars would currently view the <i>Mystagogic Catecheses</i> as being written by Cyril, but in the 370s or 380s, rather than at the same time as the <i>Catechetical Lectures</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to the Spanish pilgrim <a href="/wiki/Egeria_(pilgrim)" title="Egeria (pilgrim)">Egeria</a>, these <i>mystagogical catecheses</i> were given to the newly baptised in the Church of the <i>Anastasis</i> in the course of Easter Week.<sup id="cite_ref-Andrew_Louth_2010_p284_19-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Andrew_Louth_2010_p284-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
