<p><b>Pope Sylvester II</b> (<span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;946</span> – 12 May 1003), originally known as <b>Gerbert of Aurillac</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;n 1&#93;</a></sup> was a French-born scholar and teacher who served as the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Rome">bishop of Rome</a> and ruled the <a href="/wiki/Papal_States" title="Papal States">Papal States</a> from 999 to his death. He endorsed and promoted study of Moorish and <a href="/wiki/Greco-Roman" class="mw-redirect" title="Greco-Roman">Greco-Roman</a> arithmetic, mathematics and astronomy, reintroducing to Europe the <a href="/wiki/Abacus" title="Abacus">abacus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Armillary_sphere" title="Armillary sphere">armillary sphere</a>, which had been lost to <a href="/wiki/Greek_East_and_Latin_West" title="Greek East and Latin West">Latin</a> Europe since the end of the Greco-Roman era. He is said to be the first in Europe to introduce the <a href="/wiki/Decimal" title="Decimal">decimal</a> numeral system using the <a href="/wiki/Arabic_numeral_system" class="mw-redirect" title="Arabic numeral system">Hindu-Arabic numeral system</a>. He is credited with the invention of the first <a href="/wiki/Mechanical_clock" class="mw-redirect" title="Mechanical clock">mechanical clock</a> in 996.
</p><p><span class="anchor" id="Biography"></span><span class="anchor" id="History"></span>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Sylvester_II&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gerbert was born about 946 in the town of Belliac, near the present-day commune of <a href="/wiki/Saint-Simon,_Cantal" title="Saint-Simon, Cantal">Saint-Simon, Cantal</a>, <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Around 963, he entered the <a href="/w/index.php?title=Monastery_of_St._Gerald_of_Aurillac&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Monastery of St. Gerald of Aurillac (page does not exist)">Monastery of St. Gerald of Aurillac</a>. In 967, Count <a href="/wiki/Borrell_II_of_Barcelona" class="mw-redirect" title="Borrell II of Barcelona">Borrell II of Barcelona</a> (947–992) visited the monastery, and the abbot asked the count to take Gerbert with him so that the lad could study mathematics in <a href="/wiki/Catalonia" title="Catalonia">Catalonia</a> and acquire there some knowledge of Arabic learning.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Scholarly_work">Scholarly work</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Sylvester_II&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Scholarly work">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Gerbert studied under the direction of Bishop <a href="/wiki/Atto_of_Vich" class="mw-redirect" title="Atto of Vich">Atto of Vich</a>, some 60&#160;km north of Barcelona, and probably also at the nearby <a href="/wiki/Monastery_of_Santa_Maria_de_Ripoll" class="mw-redirect" title="Monastery of Santa Maria de Ripoll">Monastery of Santa Maria de Ripoll</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Like all Catalan monasteries, it contained manuscripts from <a href="/wiki/Muslim_Spain" class="mw-redirect" title="Muslim Spain">Moslem Spain</a> and especially from <a href="/wiki/C%C3%B3rdoba,_Andalusia" class="mw-redirect" title="Córdoba, Andalusia">Cordoba</a>, one of the intellectual centres of Europe at that time: the library of <a href="/wiki/Al-Hakam_II" title="Al-Hakam II">al-Hakam II</a>, for example, had thousands of books (from science to Greek philosophy). This is where Gerbert was introduced to <a href="/wiki/Mathematics" title="Mathematics">mathematics</a> and <a href="/wiki/Astronomy" title="Astronomy">astronomy</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Borrell II was facing major defeat from the <a href="/wiki/Al-Andalus" title="Al-Andalus">Andalusian</a> powers so he sent a delegation to <a href="/wiki/C%C3%B3rdoba,_Spain" title="Córdoba, Spain">Córdoba</a> to request a truce. Bishop Atto was part of the delegation that met with al-Ḥakam II, who received him with honour. Gerbert was fascinated by the stories of the <a href="/wiki/Mozarab" class="mw-redirect" title="Mozarab">Mozarab</a> Christian bishops and judges who dressed and talked like the Moors, well-versed in mathematics and natural sciences like the great teachers of the Islamic <a href="/wiki/Madrasah" class="mw-redirect" title="Madrasah">madrasahs</a>. This sparked Gerbert's veneration for the Moors and his passion for mathematics and astronomy.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Abacus_and_numerals">Abacus and numerals</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Sylvester_II&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Abacus and numerals">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:%C3%80bac_de_Gerbert.png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/%C3%80bac_de_Gerbert.png/220px-%C3%80bac_de_Gerbert.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="238" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/%C3%80bac_de_Gerbert.png/330px-%C3%80bac_de_Gerbert.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/%C3%80bac_de_Gerbert.png/440px-%C3%80bac_de_Gerbert.png 2x" data-file-width="548" data-file-height="593" /></a><figcaption>Model of the addition 908+95 on part of Gerbert's abacus (with modern numerals, not Gerbert's ones)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Gerbert learned of <a href="/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system" title="Hindu–Arabic numeral system">Hindu–Arabic digits</a> and applied this knowledge to the <a href="/wiki/Abacus" title="Abacus">abacus</a>, but probably without the numeral <a href="/wiki/Zero" class="mw-redirect" title="Zero">zero</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;n 2&#93;</a></sup> According to the 12th-century historian <a href="/wiki/William_of_Malmesbury" title="William of Malmesbury">William of Malmesbury</a>, Gerbert got the idea of the computing device of the abacus from a <a href="/wiki/Moors" title="Moors">Moorish</a> scholar<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> from <a href="/wiki/University_of_al-Qarawiyyin" title="University of al-Qarawiyyin">University of Al-Qarawiyyin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Abacus" title="Abacus">abacus</a> that Gerbert reintroduced into Europe had its length divided into 27 parts with 9 number symbols (this would exclude zero, which was represented by an empty column) and 1,000 characters in all, crafted out of <a href="/wiki/Horn_(anatomy)" title="Horn (anatomy)">animal horn</a> by a shieldmaker of Rheims.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_472_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_472-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETester1987131–132_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETester1987131–132-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBuddhue1941266_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuddhue1941266-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> According to his pupil Richer, Gerbert could perform speedy calculations with his abacus that were extremely difficult for people in his day to think through using only <a href="/wiki/Roman_numerals" title="Roman numerals">Roman numerals</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_472_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_472-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Due to Gerbert's reintroduction, the abacus became widely used in Europe once again during the 11th century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBuddhue1941266_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuddhue1941266-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Armillary_sphere_and_sighting_tube">Armillary sphere and sighting tube</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Sylvester_II&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Armillary sphere and sighting tube">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Although lost to Europe since the terminus of the <a href="/wiki/Greco-Roman" class="mw-redirect" title="Greco-Roman">Greco-Roman</a> era, Gerbert reintroduced the astronomical <a href="/wiki/Armillary_sphere" title="Armillary sphere">armillary sphere</a> to Latin Europe via the Islamic civilization of Al-Andalus, which was at that time at the "cutting edge" of civilization.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETester1987130–131_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETester1987130–131-13">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> The details of Gerbert's armillary sphere are revealed in letters from Gerbert to his former student and monk Remi of <a href="/wiki/Trier" title="Trier">Trèves</a> and to his colleague Constantine, the <a href="/wiki/Abbot" title="Abbot">abbot</a> of <a href="/wiki/Micy_Abbey" title="Micy Abbey">Micy</a>, as well as the accounts of his former student and French nobleman Richer, who served as a monk in <a href="/wiki/Rheims" class="mw-redirect" title="Rheims">Rheims</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Richer stated that Gerbert discovered that stars coursed in an oblique direction across the night sky.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_467_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_467-16">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Richer described Gerbert's use of the armillary sphere as a visual aid for teaching mathematics and astronomy in the classroom.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Sandro_Botticelli_052.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/57/Sandro_Botticelli_052.jpg/170px-Sandro_Botticelli_052.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="207" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/57/Sandro_Botticelli_052.jpg/255px-Sandro_Botticelli_052.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/57/Sandro_Botticelli_052.jpg/340px-Sandro_Botticelli_052.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2024" data-file-height="2469" /></a><figcaption>An <a href="/wiki/Armillary_sphere" title="Armillary sphere">armillary sphere</a> in <a href="/wiki/Saint_Augustine_in_His_Study_(Botticelli,_Ognissanti)" title="Saint Augustine in His Study (Botticelli, Ognissanti)">a painting</a> by <a href="/wiki/Sandro_Botticelli" title="Sandro Botticelli">Sandro Botticelli</a>, c. 1480</figcaption></figure>
<p>Historian Oscar G. Darlington asserts that Gerbert's division by 60 degrees instead of 360 allowed the lateral lines of his sphere to equal to six degrees.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_468_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_468-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> By this account, the <a href="/wiki/Polar_circle" title="Polar circle">polar circle</a> on Gerbert's sphere was located at 54 degrees,  several degrees off from the actual 66° 33'.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_468_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_468-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> His positioning of the <a href="/wiki/Tropic_of_Cancer" title="Tropic of Cancer">Tropic of Cancer</a> at 24 degree was nearly exact, while his positioning of the <a href="/wiki/Equator" title="Equator">equator</a> was correct by definition.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_468_17-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_468-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Richer also revealed how Gerbert made the planets more easily observable in his armillary sphere:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>He succeeded equally in showing the paths of the planets when they come near or withdraw from the earth. He fashioned first an armillary sphere. He joined the two circles called by the Greeks <i>coluri</i> and by the Latins <i>incidentes</i> because they fell upon each other, and at their extremities he placed the poles. He drew with great art and accuracy, across the <i>colures</i>, five other circles called parallels, which, from one pole to the other, divided the half of the sphere into thirty parts. He put six of these thirty parts of the half-sphere between the pole and the first circle; five between the first and the second; from the second to the third, four; from the third to the fourth, four again; five from the fourth to the fifth; and from the fifth to the pole, six. On these five circles he placed obliquely the circles that the Greeks call <i>loxos</i> or <i>zoe</i>, the Latins <i>obliques</i> or <i>vitalis</i> (the zodiac) because it contained the figures of the animals ascribed to the planets. On the inside of this oblique circle he figured with an extraordinary art the orbits traversed by the planets, whose paths and heights he demonstrated perfectly to his pupils, as well as their respective distances.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDarlington1947468–469_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDarlington1947468–469-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Richer wrote about another of Gerbert's last armillary spheres, which had sighting tubes fixed on the axis of the hollow sphere that could observe the constellations, the forms of which he hung on iron and copper wires.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_469_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_469-19">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> This armillary sphere was also described by Gerbert in a letter to his colleague Constantine.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_469_470_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_469_470-20">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Gerbert instructed Constantine that, if doubtful of the position of the <a href="/wiki/Pole_star" title="Pole star">pole star</a>, he should fix the sighting tube of the armillary sphere into position to view the star he suspected was it, and if the star did not move out of sight, it was thus the pole star.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_470_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_470-21">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> Furthermore, Gerbert instructed Constantine that the north pole could be measured with the upper and lower sighting tubes, the Arctic Circle through another tube, the Tropic of Cancer through another tube, the equator through another tube, and the <a href="/wiki/Tropic_of_Capricorn" title="Tropic of Capricorn">Tropic of Capricorn</a> through another tube.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_470_21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_470-21">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Ecclesiastical_career">Ecclesiastical career</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Sylvester_II&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Ecclesiastical career">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 969, Borrell II made a pilgrimage to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, taking Gerbert with him. There Gerbert met <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_XIII" title="Pope John XIII">Pope John XIII</a> and <a href="/wiki/Emperor_Otto_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Emperor Otto I">Emperor Otto I</a>. The pope persuaded Otto I to employ Gerbert as a tutor for his young son, <a href="/wiki/Otto_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Otto II">Otto II</a>. Some years later, Otto I gave Gerbert leave to study at the <a href="/wiki/Cathedral_school_of_Rheims" class="mw-redirect" title="Cathedral school of Rheims">cathedral school of Rheims</a> where he was soon appointed a teacher by Archbishop <a href="/wiki/Adalberon_(archbishop_of_Reims)" class="mw-redirect" title="Adalberon (archbishop of Reims)">Adalberon</a>. When Otto II became sole <a href="/wiki/Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Holy Roman Emperor">emperor</a> in 973, he appointed Gerbert the abbot of the <a href="/wiki/Bobbio_Abbey" title="Bobbio Abbey">monastery of Bobbio</a> and also appointed him as count of the district, but the abbey had been ruined by previous abbots, and Gerbert soon returned to Rheims. After the death of Otto II in 983, Gerbert became involved in the politics of his time. In 985, with the support of his archbishop, he opposed King <a href="/wiki/Lothair_of_France" title="Lothair of France">Lothair of France</a>'s attempt to take <a href="/wiki/Lorraine_(province)" class="mw-redirect" title="Lorraine (province)">Lorraine</a> from Emperor <a href="/wiki/Otto_III" class="mw-redirect" title="Otto III">Otto III</a> by supporting <a href="/wiki/Hugh_Capet" title="Hugh Capet">Hugh Capet</a>. Hugh became <a href="/wiki/King_of_France" class="mw-redirect" title="King of France">king of France</a>, ending the <a href="/wiki/Carolingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Carolingian">Carolingian</a> line of kings in 987.
</p><p>Adalberon died on 23 January 989.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_471_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_471-22">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Gerbert was a natural candidate for his succession,<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_472_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_472-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> but King Hugh appointed <a href="/wiki/Arnulf_(archbishop_of_Reims)" title="Arnulf (archbishop of Reims)">Arnulf</a>, an illegitimate son of King Lothair, instead. Arnulf was deposed in 991 for alleged treason against Hugh, and Gerbert was elected his successor. There was so much opposition to Gerbert's elevation to the See of Rheims, however, that <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_XV" title="Pope John XV">Pope John XV</a> (985–996) sent a legate to France who temporarily suspended Gerbert from his episcopal office. Gerbert sought to show that this decree was unlawful, but a further synod in 995 declared Arnulf's deposition invalid. Gerbert then became the teacher of <a href="/wiki/Otto_III" class="mw-redirect" title="Otto III">Otto III</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_V" title="Pope Gregory V">Pope Gregory V</a> (996–999), Otto III's cousin, appointed him <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Ravenna" class="mw-redirect" title="Archbishop of Ravenna">archbishop of Ravenna</a> in 998.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Silvestr2.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fa/Silvestr2.jpg/220px-Silvestr2.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="104" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fa/Silvestr2.jpg/330px-Silvestr2.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fa/Silvestr2.jpg 2x" data-file-width="382" data-file-height="181" /></a><figcaption>Seal of Sylvester II</figcaption></figure>
<p>With imperial support, Gerbert was elected to succeed Gregory V as <a href="/wiki/Pope" title="Pope">pope</a> in 999. Gerbert took the name of Sylvester II, alluding to <a href="/wiki/Sylvester_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Sylvester I">Sylvester I</a> (314–335),<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> the advisor to Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantine_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Constantine I">Constantine I</a> (324–337). Soon after he became pope, Sylvester II confirmed the position of his former rival Arnulf as archbishop of Rheims. As pope, he took energetic measures against the widespread practices of <a href="/wiki/Simony" title="Simony">simony</a> and concubinage among the clergy, maintaining that only capable men of spotless lives should be allowed to become bishops. In 1001, the Roman populace revolted, forcing Otto III and Sylvester II to flee to <a href="/wiki/Ravenna" title="Ravenna">Ravenna</a>. Otto III led two unsuccessful expeditions to regain control of the city and died on a third expedition in 1002. Sylvester II returned to Rome soon after the emperor's death, although the rebellious nobility remained in power, and died a little later. Sylvester is buried in <a href="/wiki/St._John_Lateran" class="mw-redirect" title="St. John Lateran">St. John Lateran</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Sylvester_II&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:SylvestreII_aurilac.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/SylvestreII_aurilac.jpg/170px-SylvestreII_aurilac.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="256" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/SylvestreII_aurilac.jpg/255px-SylvestreII_aurilac.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/SylvestreII_aurilac.jpg/340px-SylvestreII_aurilac.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2000" data-file-height="3008" /></a><figcaption>Statue of Pope Sylvester II in Aurillac, France</figcaption></figure>
<p>Gerbert of Aurillac was a humanist long before the Renaissance. He read <a href="/wiki/Virgil" title="Virgil">Virgil</a>, <a href="/wiki/Cicero" title="Cicero">Cicero</a> and <a href="/wiki/Boethius" title="Boethius">Boethius</a>; he studied Latin translations of <a href="/wiki/Porphyry_(philosopher)" title="Porphyry (philosopher)">Porphyry</a> and <a href="/wiki/Aristotle" title="Aristotle">Aristotle</a>. He had a very accurate classification of the different disciplines of philosophy. He was the first <a href="/wiki/French_pope" class="mw-redirect" title="French pope">French pope</a>.
</p><p>Gerbert was said to be one of the most noted scientists of his time. Gerbert wrote a series of works dealing with matters of the <a href="/wiki/Quadrivium" title="Quadrivium">quadrivium</a> (<a href="/wiki/Arithmetic" title="Arithmetic">arithmetic</a>, <a href="/wiki/Geometry" title="Geometry">geometry</a>, <a href="/wiki/Astronomy" title="Astronomy">astronomy</a>, <a href="/wiki/Music" title="Music">music</a>), which he taught using the basis of the <a href="/wiki/Trivium_(education)" class="mw-redirect" title="Trivium (education)">trivium</a> (<a href="/wiki/Grammar" title="Grammar">grammar</a>, <a href="/wiki/Logic" title="Logic">logic</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Rhetoric" title="Rhetoric">rhetoric</a>). In Rheims, he constructed a <a href="/wiki/Hydraulic" class="mw-redirect" title="Hydraulic">hydraulic</a>-powered <a href="/wiki/Organ_(music)" title="Organ (music)">organ</a> with brass pipes that excelled all previously known instruments,<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_473_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_473-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> where the air had to be pumped manually. In a letter of 984, Gerbert asks <a href="/wiki/Lupitus_of_Barcelona" title="Lupitus of Barcelona">Lupitus of Barcelona</a> for a book on <a href="/wiki/Astrology" title="Astrology">astrology</a> and <a href="/wiki/Astronomy" title="Astronomy">astronomy</a>, two terms historian S. Jim Tester says Gerbert used synonymously.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETester1987132_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETester1987132-24">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> Gerbert may have been the author of a description of the <a href="/wiki/Astrolabe" title="Astrolabe">astrolabe</a> that was edited by <a href="/wiki/Hermannus_Contractus" class="mw-redirect" title="Hermannus Contractus">Hermannus Contractus</a> some 50 years later. Besides these, as Sylvester II he wrote a dogmatic treatise, <i>De corpore et sanguine Domini</i>—On the Body and Blood of the Lord.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Legends">Legends</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Sylvester_II&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Legends">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Silvester_II._and_the_Devil_Cod._Pal._germ._137_f216v_(cropped).jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d4/Silvester_II._and_the_Devil_Cod._Pal._germ._137_f216v_%28cropped%29.jpg/170px-Silvester_II._and_the_Devil_Cod._Pal._germ._137_f216v_%28cropped%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="167" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d4/Silvester_II._and_the_Devil_Cod._Pal._germ._137_f216v_%28cropped%29.jpg/255px-Silvester_II._and_the_Devil_Cod._Pal._germ._137_f216v_%28cropped%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d4/Silvester_II._and_the_Devil_Cod._Pal._germ._137_f216v_%28cropped%29.jpg/340px-Silvester_II._and_the_Devil_Cod._Pal._germ._137_f216v_%28cropped%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="403" data-file-height="395" /></a><figcaption>Pope Sylvester II and the Devil in an illustration of c. 1460</figcaption></figure>
<p>The legend of Gerbert grows from the work of the English monk William of Malmesbury in <i><a href="/wiki/De_Rebus_Gestis_Regum_Anglorum" class="mw-redirect" title="De Rebus Gestis Regum Anglorum">De Rebus Gestis Regum Anglorum</a></i> and a polemical pamphlet, <i>Gesta Romanae Ecclesiae contra Hildebrandum</i>, by <a href="/wiki/Beno_of_Santi_Martino_e_Silvestro" title="Beno of Santi Martino e Silvestro">Cardinal Beno</a>, a partisan of <a href="/wiki/Emperor_Henry_IV" class="mw-redirect" title="Emperor Henry IV">Emperor Henry IV</a> who opposed <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_VII" title="Pope Gregory VII">Pope Gregory VII</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Investiture_Controversy" title="Investiture Controversy">Investiture Controversy</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="Lacks citations of secondary sources (April 2017)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> According to the legend, Gerbert, while studying mathematics and astrology in the Muslim cities of <a href="/wiki/Cordoba,_Spain" class="mw-redirect" title="Cordoba, Spain">Córdoba</a> and <a href="/wiki/Seville" title="Seville">Seville</a>, was accused of having learned sorcery.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Gerbert was supposed to be in possession of a book of spells stolen from an Arab philosopher in Spain.  Gerbert fled, pursued by the victim, who could trace the thief by the stars, but Gerbert was aware of the pursuit, and hid hanging from a wooden bridge, where, suspended between heaven and earth, he was invisible to the magician.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gerbert was supposed to have built a <a href="/wiki/Brazen_head" title="Brazen head">brazen head</a>. This "robotic" head would answer his questions with "yes" or "no".  He was also reputed to have had a <a href="/wiki/Pact_with_the_devil" class="mw-redirect" title="Pact with the devil">pact</a> with a female demon called Meridiana, who had appeared after he had been rejected by his earthly love, and with whose help he managed to ascend to the papal throne (another legend tells that he won the papacy playing dice with the Devil).<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to the legend, Meridiana (or the bronze head) told Gerbert that if he should ever read a <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">Mass</a> in Jerusalem, the Devil would come for him. Gerbert then cancelled a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, but when he read Mass in the church <a href="/wiki/Santa_Croce_in_Gerusalemme" title="Santa Croce in Gerusalemme">Santa Croce in Gerusalemme</a> ("Holy Cross of Jerusalem") in Rome, he became sick soon afterwards and, dying, he asked his cardinals to cut up his body and scatter it across the city.  In another version, he was even attacked by the Devil while he was reading the Mass, and the Devil mutilated him and gave his gouged-out eyes to demons to play with in the Church. Repenting, Sylvester II then cut off his hand and his tongue.
</p><p>The inscription on Gerbert's tomb reads in part <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Iste locus Silvestris membra sepulti venturo Domino conferet ad sonitum</i></span> ("This place will yield to the sound [of the last trumpet] the limbs of buried Sylvester II, at the advent of the Lord", mis-read as "will make a sound") and has given rise to the curious legend that his bones will rattle in that tomb just before the death of a pope.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The story of the crown and <a href="/wiki/Papal_legate" title="Papal legate">papal legate</a> authority allegedly given to <a href="/wiki/Stephen_I_of_Hungary" title="Stephen I of Hungary">Stephen I of Hungary</a> by Sylvester in the year 1000 (hence the title '<a href="/wiki/Apostolic_king" class="mw-redirect" title="Apostolic king">apostolic king</a>') is noted by the 19th-century historian Lewis L. Kropf as a possible forgery of the 17th century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKropf1898290_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKropf1898290-29">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Likewise, the 20th-century historian Zoltan J. Kosztolnyik states that "it seems more than unlikely that Rome would have acted in fulfilling Stephen's request for a crown without the support and approval of the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Holy Roman Emperor">emperor</a>."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKosztolnyik197735_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKosztolnyik197735-30">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Honours">Honours</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Sylvester_II&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Honours">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Hungary issued a commemorative stamp honouring Pope Sylvester II on 1 January 1938,<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> and France honoured him in 1964 by issuing a postage stamp.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Pope_Sylvester_II_(Gerbert_d%27Aurillac)_-_De_geometria.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/Pope_Sylvester_II_%28Gerbert_d%27Aurillac%29_-_De_geometria.jpg/220px-Pope_Sylvester_II_%28Gerbert_d%27Aurillac%29_-_De_geometria.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="296" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/Pope_Sylvester_II_%28Gerbert_d%27Aurillac%29_-_De_geometria.jpg/330px-Pope_Sylvester_II_%28Gerbert_d%27Aurillac%29_-_De_geometria.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/Pope_Sylvester_II_%28Gerbert_d%27Aurillac%29_-_De_geometria.jpg/440px-Pope_Sylvester_II_%28Gerbert_d%27Aurillac%29_-_De_geometria.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1506" data-file-height="2026" /></a><figcaption>12th century copy of <i>De geometria</i></figcaption></figure>
<p>Gerbert's writings were printed in volume 139 of the <i><a href="/wiki/Patrologia_Latina" title="Patrologia Latina">Patrologia Latina</a></i>. Darlington notes that Gerbert's preservation of his letters might have been an effort of his to compile them into a textbook for his pupils that would illustrate proper letter writing.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_468_17-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_468-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> His books on mathematics and astronomy were not research-oriented; his texts were primarily educational guides for his students.<sup id="cite_ref-darlington_468_17-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-darlington_468-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_popular_culture">In popular culture</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Sylvester_II&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: In popular culture">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<ul><li>Gerbert D'Aurillac appears as an adversary in <a href="/wiki/Deborah_Harkness" title="Deborah Harkness">Deborah Harkness</a>' novel series called the <i><a href="/wiki/All_Souls_Trilogy" class="mw-redirect" title="All Souls Trilogy">All Souls Trilogy</a></i>. He is portrayed by <a href="/wiki/Trevor_Eve" title="Trevor Eve">Trevor Eve</a> in the trilogy's <a href="/wiki/A_Discovery_of_Witches_(TV_series)" title="A Discovery of Witches (TV series)">television adaptation</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Gerbert d'Aurillac is the protagonist of <a href="/wiki/Judith_Tarr" title="Judith Tarr">Judith Tarr</a>'s 1989 novel <i>Ars Magica</i>.</li>
<li>Legends about Gerbert D'Aurillac as Pope Sylvester II inspired the short story “The Demon Pope” by <a href="/wiki/Richard_Garnett_(writer)" title="Richard Garnett (writer)">Richard Garnett</a>, first published in the original, 1888 edition of <a href="/wiki/The_Twilight_of_the_Gods_and_Other_Tales" title="The Twilight of the Gods and Other Tales">The Twilight of the Gods and Other Tales</a>.</li></ul>
