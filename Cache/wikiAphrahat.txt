<p><b>Aphrahat</b> (c. 280–c. 345; <a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a>: <span lang="syr" dir="rtl">ܐܦܪܗܛ</span> <i>Ap̄rahaṭ</i>,<a href="/wiki/Arabic_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Arabic language">Arabic</a>: <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">أفراهاط الحكيم</span>, <a href="/wiki/Persian_language" title="Persian language">Persian</a>: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">فرهاد</span>, <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Greek language">Ancient Greek</a>: <span lang="grc">Ἀφραάτης</span>, and <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> <i>Aphraates</i>) was a <a href="/wiki/Syriac_Christianity" title="Syriac Christianity">Syriac Christian</a> author of the third century from the <a href="/wiki/Persian_Empire" class="mw-redirect" title="Persian Empire">Persian</a> / <a href="/wiki/Sasanian_Empire" title="Sasanian Empire">Sasanian Empire</a> who composed a series of twenty-three expositions or homilies on points of Christian doctrine and practice.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> All his known works, the <i>Demonstrations</i>, come from later on in his life. He was an <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetic</a> and <a href="/wiki/Celibacy" title="Celibacy">celibate</a>, and was almost definitely a <a href="/wiki/Members_of_the_Covenant" title="Members of the Covenant">son of the covenant</a> (an early Syriac form of communal <a href="/wiki/Monasticism" title="Monasticism">monasticism</a>). He may have been a <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a>, and later Syriac tradition places him at the head of <a href="/wiki/Mar_Mattai_Monastery" class="mw-redirect" title="Mar Mattai Monastery">Mar Mattai Monastery</a> near <a href="/wiki/Mosul" title="Mosul">Mosul</a> in what is now northern <a href="/wiki/Iraq" title="Iraq">Iraq</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcLean1911165_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcLean1911165-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was a near contemporary to the slightly younger <a href="/wiki/Ephrem_the_Syrian" title="Ephrem the Syrian">Ephrem the Syrian</a>, but the latter lived within the sphere of the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman Empire</a>. Called the <i>Persian Sage</i> (<a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a>: <span lang="syr" dir="rtl">ܚܟܝܡܐ ܦܪܣܝܐ</span>, <i>Ḥakkimā Pārsāyā</i>), Aphrahat witnessed to the concerns of the early church beyond the eastern boundaries of the Roman Empire.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span id="Life.2C_history_and_identity"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Life,_history_and_identity">Life, history and identity</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Aphrahat&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life, history and identity">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Aphrahat was born near the border of <a href="/wiki/Roman_Syria" title="Roman Syria">Roman Syria</a> in <a href="/wiki/Sasanian_Empire" title="Sasanian Empire">Neo-Persian Iran</a> around 280, during the rule of Sasnian Emperor <a href="/wiki/Shapur_II" title="Shapur II">Shapur II</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Dukhrana_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dukhrana-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
The name Aphrahat is the Syriac version of the Persian name <i>Frahāt</i>, which is the modern Persian <i>Farhād</i> (<span title="Persian-language text"><span lang="fa" dir="rtl">فرهاد</span></span>). His ancestors were originally of <a href="/wiki/Persian_Jewish" class="mw-redirect" title="Persian Jewish">Persian Jewish</a> ancestry.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The author, who was known as "the Persian sage", may have also come from a <a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">pagan</a> family and been himself a convert from paganism, though this appears to be later speculation. However, he tells us that he took the Christian name Jacob at his <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptism</a>, and is so entitled in the <a href="/wiki/Colophon_(publishing)" title="Colophon (publishing)">colophon</a> to a manuscript of 512 which contains twelve of his homilies. Hence he was already confused with <a href="/wiki/Jacob_of_Nisibis" title="Jacob of Nisibis">Jacob of Nisibis</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-ccel_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ccel-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> by the time of <a href="/wiki/Gennadius_of_Massilia" title="Gennadius of Massilia">Gennadius of Massilia</a> (before 496), and the ancient <a href="/wiki/Armenian_language" title="Armenian language">Armenian</a> version of nineteen of The Demonstrations has been published under this latter name. Thorough study of the <i>Demonstrations</i> makes identification with Jacob of Nisibis impossible. Aphrahat, being a Persian subject, cannot have lived at Nisibis, which became Persian only by <a href="/wiki/Jovian_(emperor)" title="Jovian (emperor)">Emperor Jovian</a>'s treaty of 363.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Furthermore, Jacob of Nisibis, who attended the <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Nicaea" title="First Council of Nicaea">First Council of Nicaea</a>, died in 338, and from the internal evidence of Aphrahat's works he must have witnessed the beginning of the <a href="/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians" title="Persecution of Christians">persecution of Christians</a> in the early 340s by Shapur II. The persecutions arose out of political tensions between Rome and Persia, particularly the declaration of <a href="/wiki/Constantine_the_Great" title="Constantine the Great">Constantine the Great</a> that Rome should be a Christian empire. Shapur perhaps grew anxious that the largely Syriac and Armenian Christians within his Empire might secretly support Rome. There are elements in Aphrahat's writing that show great pastoral concern for his harried flock, caught in the midst of all this turmoil.
</p><p>It is understood that his name was Aphrahat from comparatively late writers, such as <a href="/wiki/Bar_Bahlul" class="mw-redirect" title="Bar Bahlul">Bar Bahlul</a> (10th century), Elias of Nisibis (11th), <a href="/wiki/Bar_Hebraeus" title="Bar Hebraeus">Bar Hebraeus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Abdisho_bar_Berika" title="Abdisho bar Berika">Abdisho bar Berika</a>. He appears to have been quite prominent in the Christian Church of the Persian Empire during the first half of the fourth century.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/George,_bishop_of_the_Arabs" class="mw-redirect" title="George, bishop of the Arabs">George, bishop of the Arabs</a>, writing in  714 to a friend who had sent him a series of questions about the "Persian sage", confesses ignorance of his name, home and rank, but gathers from his works that he was a monk, and of high esteem in the clergy. The fact that in 344 he was selected to draw up a circular letter from a council of bishops and other clergy to the churches of <a href="/wiki/Ctesiphon" title="Ctesiphon">Ctesiphon</a> and <a href="/wiki/Seleucia" title="Seleucia">Seleucia</a><sup id="cite_ref-ccel_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ccel-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> and elsewhere (later to become Demonstration 14) is held by <a href="/wiki/William_Wright_(orientalist)" title="William Wright (orientalist)">William Wright</a> and others to prove that he was a bishop. According to a marginal note in a 14th-century manuscript (B.M. Orient. 1017), he was "bishop of Mar Mattai," a famous monastery near Mosul, but it is unlikely that this institution existed so early.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="About_.22The_Demonstrations.22"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="About_&quot;The_Demonstrations&quot;">About "The Demonstrations"</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Aphrahat&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: About &quot;The Demonstrations&quot;">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Aphrahat's works are collectively called the <i>Demonstrations</i>, from the identical first word in each of their titles (<a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a>: <span lang="syr" dir="rtl">ܬܚܘܝܬܐ</span>, taḥwîṯâ). They are sometimes also known as "the homilies". There are twenty-three <i>Demonstrations</i> in all.<sup id="cite_ref-Dukhrana_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Dukhrana-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Each work deals with a different item of faith or practice, and is a pastoral <a href="/wiki/Homily" title="Homily">homily</a> or exposition. According to <a href="/wiki/Francis_Crawford_Burkitt" title="Francis Crawford Burkitt">Francis Crawford Burkitt</a>, they are intended to form "a full and ordered exposition of the Christian faith." The standpoint is that of the Syriac-speaking church, before it was touched by the <a href="/wiki/Arian_controversy" title="Arian controversy">Arian controversy</a>. Beginning with faith as the foundation, the writer proceeds to build up the structure of doctrine and duty.<sup id="cite_ref-EB1911_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB1911-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Demonstrations</i> are works of prose, but frequently, Aphrahat employs a poetic rhythm and imagery to his writing. Each of the first twenty-two <i>Demonstrations</i> begins with each successive letter of the <a href="/wiki/Syriac_alphabet" title="Syriac alphabet">Syriac alphabet</a> (of which there are twenty-two). The <i>Demonstrations</i> were not composed all at one time, but in three distinct periods. The first ten, composed in 337, concern themselves with Christian life and church order, and predate the persecutions. <i>Demonstrations</i> 11–22 were composed at the height of the persecution, in 344. Some of this group deal with matters as before, others focus on <a href="/wiki/Apocalyptic_literature" title="Apocalyptic literature">apocalyptic</a> themes. However, four <i>Demonstrations</i> are concerned with <a href="/wiki/Judaism" title="Judaism">Judaism</a>. It appears that there was a movement within the Persian church by some either to become Jews or return to Judaism, or to incorporate Jewish elements into Christianity. Aphrahat makes his stand by explaining the meaning of the symbols of <a href="/wiki/Circumcision" title="Circumcision">circumcision</a>, <a href="/wiki/Passover" title="Passover">Passover</a> and <a href="/wiki/Shabbat" title="Shabbat">Shabbat</a>. The twenty-third <i>Demonstration</i> falls outside of the alphabetic system of the early works, and appears to be slightly later, perhaps near the end of Aphrahat's life. The twenty-third piece takes the symbolism of the grape, drawn from <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Isaiah" title="Book of Isaiah">Isaiah</a> chapter 65 and elsewhere, as its cue. It deals with the fulfillment of Messianic promise from <a href="/wiki/Adam_(Bible)" class="mw-redirect" title="Adam (Bible)">Adam</a> to Christ.<sup id="cite_ref-ccel_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ccel-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Aphrahat never strays too far from the <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">Bible</a> in the <i>Demonstrations</i>: he is not given to philosophizing. All of his <a href="/wiki/Gospel" title="Gospel">gospel</a> quotations seem to be drawn from the <i><a href="/wiki/Diatessaron" title="Diatessaron">Diatessaron</a></i>, the gospel harmony that served the church at his time. Aphrahat's mode of <a href="/wiki/Exegesis" title="Exegesis">biblical interpretation</a> is strikingly similar to that of the <a href="/wiki/Babylon" title="Babylon">Babylonian</a> <a href="/wiki/Rabbi" title="Rabbi">rabbinic</a> academies of his day. His position within the church is indicated in <i>Demonstration</i> 14, in which Aphrahat appears to be writing a letter on behalf of his <a href="/wiki/Synod" title="Synod">synod</a> to the <a href="/wiki/Clergy" title="Clergy">clergy</a> of Persian capital, <a href="/wiki/Ctesiphon" title="Ctesiphon">Ctesiphon</a>-<a href="/wiki/Seleucia_on_the_Tigris" class="mw-redirect" title="Seleucia on the Tigris">Seleucia on the Tigris</a>.
</p><p>In <i>Demonstrations 5</i>, Aphrahat dealt with eschatology. Concerning the beasts of Daniel 7, he identified the first beast as <a href="/wiki/Babylon" title="Babylon">Babylon</a>; the second, <a href="/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire" title="Achaemenid Empire">Media and Persia</a>; the third, <a href="/wiki/Alexander_the_Great" title="Alexander the Great">Alexander's</a> <a href="/wiki/Macedonia_(ancient_kingdom)" title="Macedonia (ancient kingdom)">Macedonian empire</a>. The four heads of the leopard were the four successors of Alexander. The fourth beast appeared to include both the Macedonian successors of Alexander and the Roman emperors. Its horns he applied to the Seleucid kings down to <a href="/wiki/Antiochus_IV_Epiphanes" title="Antiochus IV Epiphanes">Antiochus</a>, whom he identified as the Little Horn.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Translations">Translations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Aphrahat&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Translations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <i>Demonstrations</i> were originally composed in the <a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac language</a>, but were quickly translated into other languages. The <a href="/wiki/Armenian_language" title="Armenian language">Armenian</a> version, published by <a href="/wiki/Nicol%C3%B2_Maria_Antonelli" title="Nicolò Maria Antonelli">Antonelli</a> in 1756 and containing only 19 homilies, circulated mistakenly under the name <i>Jacob of Nisibis</i>. Important versions in <a href="/wiki/Georgian_language" title="Georgian language">Georgian</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ge%27ez_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ge&#39;ez language">Ge'ez</a> exist. A few of the <i>Demonstrations</i> were translated into <a href="/wiki/Arabic_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Arabic language">Arabic</a>, but wrongly attributed to <a href="/wiki/Ephrem_the_Syrian" title="Ephrem the Syrian">Ephrem the Syrian</a>.
</p>
