					<div id="contentSub"><div id="mw-content-subtitle"><span class="mw-redirectedfrom">(Redirected from <a href="/w/index.php?title=Latin_Patriarch_of_Constantinople&amp;redirect=no" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Patriarch of Constantinople">Latin Patriarch of Constantinople</a>)</span></div></div>
					
					
					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Roman Catholic office (1204–1964)</div>
<figure class="mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Antoio_Atanasio_Rossi.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/Antoio_Atanasio_Rossi.jpg/220px-Antoio_Atanasio_Rossi.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="318" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/Antoio_Atanasio_Rossi.jpg/330px-Antoio_Atanasio_Rossi.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/Antoio_Atanasio_Rossi.jpg/440px-Antoio_Atanasio_Rossi.jpg 2x" data-file-width="611" data-file-height="882" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Antonio_Anastasio_Rossi" title="Antonio Anastasio Rossi">Antonio Anastasio Rossi</a>, last Latin Patriarch of Constantinople (d. 1948)</figcaption></figure>
<p>The <b>Latin Patriarchate of Constantinople</b> was an office established as a result of the <a href="/wiki/Fourth_Crusade" title="Fourth Crusade">Fourth Crusade</a> and its <a href="/wiki/Sack_of_Constantinople_(1204)" class="mw-redirect" title="Sack of Constantinople (1204)">conquest of Constantinople</a> in 1204. It was a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> replacement for the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Orthodox">Eastern Orthodox</a> <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarchate_of_Constantinople" title="Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople">Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople</a>, and remained in the city until the <a href="/wiki/Reconquest_of_Constantinople" title="Reconquest of Constantinople">reconquest of Constantinople</a> by the Byzantines in 1261, whereupon it became a <a href="/wiki/Titular_see" title="Titular see">titular see</a>. The office was abolished in 1964.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Latin_Patriarchate_of_Constantinople&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Before the <a href="/wiki/East%E2%80%93West_Schism" title="East–West Schism">East–West Schism</a> in 1054, the Christian Church within the borders of the ancient <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman Empire</a> was effectively ruled by five <a href="/wiki/Patriarch" title="Patriarch">patriarchs</a> (the "<a href="/wiki/Pentarchy_(Christianity)" class="mw-redirect" title="Pentarchy (Christianity)">Pentarchy</a>"): In descending order of precedence: <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Rome" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Rome">Bishop of Rome</a> (who rarely used the title "Patriarch") and those of <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>, <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_of_Alexandria" title="Patriarch of Alexandria">Alexandria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Patriarch_of_Antioch" title="Patriarch of Antioch">Antioch</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Latin_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Patriarch of Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>.
</p><p>In the West the Bishop of Rome was recognized as having superiority over the other Patriarchs, while in the East, the Patriarch of Constantinople gradually came to occupy a leading position. The sees of Rome and Constantinople were often at odds with one another, just as the Greek and Latin Churches as a whole were often at odds both politically and in things ecclesiastical. There were complex cultural currents underlying these difficulties. The tensions led in 1054 to a serious rupture between the <a href="/wiki/Greek_East_and_Latin_West" title="Greek East and Latin West">Greek East and Latin West</a> called the <a href="/wiki/East%E2%80%93West_Schism" title="East–West Schism">East–West Schism</a>, which while not in many places absolute, still dominates the ecclesiastical landscape.
</p><p>In 1204, the <a href="/wiki/Fourth_Crusade" title="Fourth Crusade">Fourth Crusade</a> invaded, <a href="/wiki/Sack_of_Constantinople" title="Sack of Constantinople">seized and sacked Constantinople</a>, and established the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Empire" title="Latin Empire">Latin Empire</a>. <a href="/wiki/Pope_Innocent_III" title="Pope Innocent III">Pope Innocent III</a>, who was not involved, initially spoke out against the Crusade, writing in a letter to his legate, "How, indeed, is the Greek church to be brought back into ecclesiastical union and to a devotion for the Apostolic See when she has been beset with so many afflictions and persecutions that she sees in the Latins only an example of perdition and the works of darkness, so that she now, and with reason, detests the Latins more than dogs?"<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> However the popes accepted the Latin patriarchate established by Catholic clergy that accompanied the Crusade, similar to Latin patriarchates previously established in the <a href="/wiki/Crusader_states" title="Crusader states">Crusader states</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Land" title="Holy Land">Holy Land</a>. The pope recognised these "<a href="/wiki/Latins_(Middle_Ages)" title="Latins (Middle Ages)">Latin</a>" sees at the <a href="/wiki/Fourth_Council_of_the_Lateran" title="Fourth Council of the Lateran">Fourth Council of the Lateran</a>. Furthermore, those Orthodox bishops left in their place were made to swear an oath of allegiance to the pope.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>However, the Latin Empire in Constantinople was eventually <a href="/wiki/Reconquest_of_Constantinople" title="Reconquest of Constantinople">defeated and dispossessed</a> by a resurgent <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Empire_under_the_Palaiologos_dynasty" title="Byzantine Empire under the Palaiologos dynasty">Byzantium</a> in 1261. Since that time Latin Patriarch <a href="/wiki/Pantaleon%D0%B5_Giustinian" class="mw-redirect" title="Pantaleonе Giustinian">Pantaleonе Giustinian</a> (d. 1286) resided in the West, though continuing to oversee the remaining Latin Catholic dioceses in various parts of <a href="/wiki/Latin_Greece" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Greece">Latin Greece</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The continuing threat of a Catholic Crusade to restore the Latin Empire, championed by the ambitious <a href="/wiki/Charles_I_of_Anjou" title="Charles I of Anjou">Charles I of Anjou</a>, led to the first attempts, on the Byzantine side, to effect a Union of the Churches. After the <a href="/wiki/Union_of_Lyon_(1274)" class="mw-redirect" title="Union of Lyon (1274)">Union of Lyon (1274)</a>, <a href="/wiki/John_Bekkos" class="mw-redirect" title="John Bekkos">John Bekkos</a> was installed as a Greek Catholic Patriarch of Constantinople in 1275, but that did not affect the position of Pantaleonе Giustinian. His Greek Catholic counterpart was deposed in 1282 by <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox" class="mw-redirect" title="Eastern Orthodox">Eastern Orthodox</a> hierarchy, thus ending a short-lived union. in 1286, Latin Patriarch Pantaleonе Giustinian was succeeded by <a href="/wiki/Pietro_Correr_(patriarch)" title="Pietro Correr (patriarch)">Pietro Correr</a> who was the first holder of that office in a new form of a <a href="/wiki/Titular_see" title="Titular see">titular see</a>.
</p><p>On 8 February 1314, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_V" title="Pope Clement V">Pope Clement V</a> united the Patriarchate with the episcopal see of Negroponte (<a href="/wiki/Chalcis" title="Chalcis">Chalcis</a>), hitherto a <a href="/wiki/Suffragan" class="mw-redirect" title="Suffragan">suffragan</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Archbishopric_of_Athens" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Archbishopric of Athens">Latin Archbishopric of Athens</a>, so that the patriarchs could once more have a territorial diocese on Greek soil and exercise a direct role as the head of the Latin clergy in what remained of <a href="/wiki/Latin_Greece" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin Greece">Latin Greece</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELoenertz1966266–267_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELoenertz1966266–267-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>For a time, like many ecclesiastical offices in the West, it had rival contenders who were supporters or protégés of the rival popes.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (October 2010)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> As to the title Latin Patriarch of Constantinople, this was the case at least from 1378 to 1423. Thereafter the office continued as an honorific title, during the later centuries attributed to a leading clergyman in Rome, until it ceased to be assigned after 1948 and in January 1964, along with the titles of the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Patriarchate_of_Alexandria" title="Latin Patriarchate of Alexandria">Latin Patriarchate of Alexandria</a> and <a href="/wiki/Latin_Patriarchate_of_Antioch" title="Latin Patriarchate of Antioch">Antioch</a>, it was no longer mentioned in the Vatican yearbook (rather than being announced as being abolished).<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> This was after <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI" title="Pope Paul VI">Pope Paul VI</a> met with <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_Athenagoras_I_of_Constantinople" class="mw-redirect" title="Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras I of Constantinople">Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras I of Constantinople</a> (see <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI_and_ecumenism" title="Pope Paul VI and ecumenism">Pope Paul VI and ecumenism</a>), showing the Latin Church by this point was more interested in reconciliation with the Eastern Church, abolishing the titular title. <sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A <a href="/wiki/Vicariate_Apostolic_of_Istanbul" class="mw-redirect" title="Vicariate Apostolic of Istanbul">Vicariate Apostolic of Istanbul</a> (until 1990, Constantinople) has existed from 1742 into the present day.
</p>
