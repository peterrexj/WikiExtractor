					<div id="contentSub"><div id="mw-content-subtitle"><span class="mw-redirectedfrom">(Redirected from <a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Tanaka&amp;redirect=no" class="mw-redirect" title="John Tanaka">John Tanaka</a>)</span></div></div>
					
					
					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><p>The <b>Empty Chair Memorial</b> (<a href="/wiki/Japanese_language" title="Japanese language">Japanese</a>: <span lang="ja">空席の椅子の記念碑</span>,<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> <i>Kūseki no Isu no Kinenhi</i>) is a memorial located at Capital School Park in downtown <a href="/wiki/Juneau,_Alaska" title="Juneau, Alaska">Juneau, Alaska</a>, <a href="/wiki/United_States" title="United States">United States</a>. It is dedicated to the 53 Juneau residents of Japanese origin who were <a href="/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans" title="Internment of Japanese Americans">forcibly relocated and imprisoned in inland internment camps</a> during <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">World War II</a>, as well as to recognize Juneau citizens for their helpful response when the families returned after the war. It is the first memorial in Alaska regarding the internment of Japanese Americans during the war.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The title of the memorial refers to a student named John Tanaka, a <a href="/wiki/Juneau-Douglas_High_School" title="Juneau-Douglas High School">Juneau High School</a> <a href="/wiki/Valedictorian" title="Valedictorian">valedictorian</a> who was relocated a month before his graduation in May 1942. His classmates left an empty chair to recognize him, and, by extension, other local Japanese families, at their graduation ceremonies.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Background">Background</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Empty_Chair_Memorial&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Background">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Much like the rest of the <a href="/wiki/West_Coast_of_the_United_States" title="West Coast of the United States">West Coast</a>, Japanese immigrants began arriving at <a href="/wiki/Alaska" title="Alaska">Alaska</a> in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, where they were drawn by fisheries, mining, logging, agriculture, and other labor-intensive industries. At the time of the Japanese immigration to Alaska, it was under a <a href="/wiki/District_of_Alaska" title="District of Alaska">district status</a> since 1884 and under a <a href="/wiki/Territory_of_Alaska" title="Territory of Alaska">territorial status</a> since 1912. Some migrated to <a href="/wiki/Juneau,_Alaska" title="Juneau, Alaska">Juneau</a>, where they later operated businesses within the city. Unlike most of the West Coast, by 1941, the Japanese population in Alaska was generally small of roughly 200. Also, Japanese residents in Juneau were fairly well treated by their white neighbors. Their children were popular in school and most ethnic Japanese were well integrated into the city's community.<sup id="cite_ref-GBFE_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-GBFE-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Internment_during_World_War_II_and_aftermath">Internment during World War II and aftermath</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Empty_Chair_Memorial&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Internment during World War II and aftermath">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Tanaka_JohnTanakaGraduation.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Tanaka_JohnTanakaGraduation.jpg/220px-Tanaka_JohnTanakaGraduation.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="249" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Tanaka_JohnTanakaGraduation.jpg/330px-Tanaka_JohnTanakaGraduation.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Tanaka_JohnTanakaGraduation.jpg/440px-Tanaka_JohnTanakaGraduation.jpg 2x" data-file-width="992" data-file-height="1123" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Tanaka_JohnTanakaGraduation.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Valedictorian" title="Valedictorian">Valedictorian</a> John Tanaka received his diploma at the <a href="/wiki/Juneau-Douglas_High_School" title="Juneau-Douglas High School">Juneau High School</a> gym during a special graduation ceremony on April 15, 1942</div></div></div>
<p>Following the <a href="/wiki/Attack_on_Pearl_Harbor" title="Attack on Pearl Harbor">attack on Pearl Harbor</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Empire_of_Japan" title="Empire of Japan">Empire of Japan</a> and the U.S. entry into <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">World War II</a>, President <a href="/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt" title="Franklin D. Roosevelt">Franklin D. Roosevelt</a> signed <a href="/wiki/Executive_Order_9066" title="Executive Order 9066">Executive Order 9066</a> on February 19, 1942, which gave military commanders the authority to exclude specified persons from certain areas, paving way for the <a href="/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans" title="Internment of Japanese Americans">internment of Japanese Americans</a>. Unlike <a href="/wiki/California" title="California">California</a>, <a href="/wiki/Oregon" title="Oregon">Oregon</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Washington_(state)" title="Washington (state)">Washington</a> which were designated as military exclusion zones that evicted all Japanese residents there to inland internment camps, Alaska was not included on the list due to its small Japanese populace.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Nevertheless, the territory's Japanese residents were forced to leave by the <a href="/wiki/Western_Defense_Command" title="Western Defense Command">Western Defense Command</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-TREER_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TREER-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>One of the notable ethnic Japanese from Juneau was John Tanaka, a <a href="/wiki/Juneau-Douglas_High_School" title="Juneau-Douglas High School">Juneau High School</a> <a href="/wiki/Valedictorian" title="Valedictorian">valedictorian</a>, who later served as an inspiration for the Empty Chair Memorial. His father, Shonosuke Tanaka, owned the popular City Cafe in the city for decades and was among the 15 Japanese nationals arrested by the <a href="/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation" title="Federal Bureau of Investigation">FBI</a> immediately after the Pearl Harbor attack, where they were incarcerated along with two German citizens for several months at <a href="/wiki/Fort_Richardson_(Alaska)" title="Fort Richardson (Alaska)">Fort Richardson</a> in <a href="/wiki/Anchorage,_Alaska" title="Anchorage, Alaska">Anchorage, Alaska</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-GBFE_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-GBFE-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> In April 1942, when all ethnic Japanese in Alaska were ordered to leave for internment camps inland, a special ceremony was held on the 15th at the school gymnasium where John received his diploma. During the official graduation in May 1942, Tanaka's classmates placed an empty wooden folding chair to symbolize his absence.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>By the end of April, a total of 53 persons of Japanese ancestry left their homes and businesses in Juneau to internment camps inland. John, his brother (William), his mother (Nobu), and his two sisters (Alice and Mary) were taken on a <a href="/wiki/United_States_Army" title="United States Army">United States Army</a> transport ship to <a href="/wiki/Seattle" title="Seattle">Seattle</a> for processing at <a href="/wiki/Camp_Harmony" title="Camp Harmony">Puyallup Assembly Center</a> and then onto <a href="/wiki/Minidoka_National_Historic_Site" title="Minidoka National Historic Site">Minidoka War Relocation Center</a> in <a href="/wiki/Idaho" title="Idaho">Idaho</a>, where they were incarcerated during the remainder of the war. Shonosuke would later be sent to the <a href="/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice" title="United States Department of Justice">United States Department of Justice</a> prison in <a href="/wiki/Lordsburg,_New_Mexico" title="Lordsburg, New Mexico">Lordsburg, New Mexico</a> for a few years before rejoining his family in 1944.<sup id="cite_ref-TREER_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TREER-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> While at Minidoka, John volunteered for the U.S. Army and served with the <a href="/wiki/442nd_Infantry_Regiment_(United_States)" title="442nd Infantry Regiment (United States)">442nd Infantry Regiment</a> in Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-%@RQG_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-%@RQG-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After the war, most Juneau Japanese families returned to the city and re-established themselves, including the Tanaka family. Welcomed back, they found that residents had watched out for their interests in their absence. Unlike Japanese elsewhere in the country, they were able to resume their prior lives without serious difficulties, a testament to the decency of the people living in Juneau in the mid 20th Century.<sup id="cite_ref-GBFE_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-GBFE-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Following his military discharge in 1946, John enrolled and graduated at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Washington" title="University of Washington">University of Washington</a> where he later went to the <a href="/wiki/Saint_Louis_University_School_of_Medicine" title="Saint Louis University School of Medicine">St. Louis University School of Medicine</a> and became an anesthesiologist. He died in 1978, survived by his wife, Jeanne Tanaka, and his five children.<sup id="cite_ref-%@RQG_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-%@RQG-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Memorial">Memorial</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Empty_Chair_Memorial&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Memorial">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In July 2012, Seattle artist Peter Reiquam submitted a preliminary proposal to the Empty Chair Project Steering Committee which has met with their approval. His design was inspired by a wooden folding chair that would have been used for John Tanaka, who left Juneau a month before the actual graduation ceremony in 1942 in which his classmates left for him to honor his absence. The project then took over a span of two years through fundraising and grants from various organizations including the <a href="/wiki/National_Park_Service" title="National Park Service">National Park Service</a>'s Japanese American Confinement Sites program.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On July 12, 2014, the Empty Chair Memorial was dedicated at Capital School Park in Juneau, with 200 people in attendance. More than 1,000 <a href="/wiki/One_thousand_origami_cranes" title="One thousand origami cranes">origami cranes</a> were made for the ceremony. The memorial is a bronze reproduction of a simple folding chair and has a spartan aura, sequestered in the park with a jagged chunk of planked flooring. Reiquam said that:<sup id="cite_ref-FESE_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FESE-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>[T]he irregular edge of the floor was intended to represent a section of the gymnasium floor that had just been ripped out of the gymnasium as a metaphor for the way that the people were ripped from their homes and their community.</p></blockquote>
<p>The names of the 53 people forced to leave their homes for internment camps during World War II are etched into the floor planks. The communal, military-style camps were ringed by barbed wire and guard towers. Mary Tanaka Abo, one of John Tanaka's sisters, attended the dedication ceremony along with her extended family, as well as Alice Tanaka Hikido, and Jeanne Tanaka, John's widow.<sup id="cite_ref-FESE_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FESE-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
