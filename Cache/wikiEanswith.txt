<p>Saint <b>Eanswith</b> (<a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a>&#58; <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">Ēanswīþ</i></span>; born c. 630, <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent" title="Kingdom of Kent">Kent</a>, <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a>. Died c. 650, <a href="/wiki/Folkestone" title="Folkestone">Folkestone</a>, England), also spelled <b>Eanswythe</b> or <b>Eanswide</b>, was an <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxon</a> princess, who is said to have founded <a href="/wiki/Folkestone_Priory" title="Folkestone Priory">Folkestone Priory</a>, one of the first Christian monastic communities for women in Britain. Her possible remains were the subject of research, published in 2020.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eanswith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Eanswith was a princess of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent" title="Kingdom of Kent">Kingdom of Kent</a>. Her father was <a href="/wiki/Eadbald_of_Kent" title="Eadbald of Kent">Eadbald</a>, who ruled as king of Kent from 616 to 640 CE. Her mother, Eadbald's second wife, was <a href="/wiki/Emma_of_Austrasia" title="Emma of Austrasia">Emma</a>, who may have been a Frankish princess; she also bore him two sons, <a href="/wiki/Eormenred_of_Kent" title="Eormenred of Kent">Eormenred</a> and <a href="/wiki/Eorcenberht_of_Kent" title="Eorcenberht of Kent">Eorcenberht</a>. Eanswith's grandfather, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelberht_of_Kent" title="Æthelberht of Kent">Æthelberht of Kent</a> had been the first king of Anglo-Saxon England to accept Christian baptism.
</p><p>Tradition has it that Eanswith founded the <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> <a href="/wiki/Folkestone_Priory" title="Folkestone Priory">Folkestone Priory</a>, the first <a href="/wiki/Nunnery" class="mw-redirect" title="Nunnery">nunnery</a> in England. She was supported in this by her father, Eadbald.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>While the monastery was under construction, a pagan prince came to Kent seeking to marry Eanswith. King Eadbald, whose sister <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelburh_of_Kent" title="Æthelburh of Kent">Æthelburh (Ethelburga)</a> had married the pagan <a href="/wiki/Edwin_of_Northumbria" title="Edwin of Northumbria">King Edwin</a> two or three years before, recalled that this wedding resulted in Edwin's conversion. Eanswith, however, refused.<sup id="cite_ref-Orthodox_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Orthodox-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>This was the first women's monastery to be founded in England. Eanswith lived there with her companions in the monastic life, and they may have been guided by some of the monks who had come to England with <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury" title="Augustine of Canterbury">Augustine</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_mission" title="Gregorian mission">Gregorian mission</a> of 597.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (February 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eanswith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Church_of_St_Mary_and_St_Eanswythe,_Folkestone_02.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/65/Church_of_St_Mary_and_St_Eanswythe%2C_Folkestone_02.JPG/220px-Church_of_St_Mary_and_St_Eanswythe%2C_Folkestone_02.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/65/Church_of_St_Mary_and_St_Eanswythe%2C_Folkestone_02.JPG/330px-Church_of_St_Mary_and_St_Eanswythe%2C_Folkestone_02.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/65/Church_of_St_Mary_and_St_Eanswythe%2C_Folkestone_02.JPG/440px-Church_of_St_Mary_and_St_Eanswythe%2C_Folkestone_02.JPG 2x" data-file-width="3216" data-file-height="4288" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/St_Mary_and_St_Eanswythe%27s_Church,_Folkestone" title="St Mary and St Eanswythe&#39;s Church, Folkestone">St Mary and St Eanswythe's Church, Folkestone</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>She remained at the abbey until her death.<sup id="cite_ref-Orthodox_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Orthodox-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The first monastic site became abandoned by the 10th century, and began to be eroded by the sea, a problem which also afflicted a new foundation of 1095. A site further inland was provided for a new foundation of <a href="/wiki/Folkestone_Priory" title="Folkestone Priory">Folkestone Priory</a> by William de Abrincis in 1137, with a church dedicated to St Mary and to Eanswith. Saint Eanswith's day falls on 12 September;<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>  traditionally, this is the date on which her <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">remains were translated</a> to the new church in 1138. The priory was closed at the Reformation, and the priory church became the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England_parish_church" title="Church of England parish church">parish church</a> of Folkestone, <a href="/wiki/St_Mary_and_St_Eanswythe%27s_Church,_Folkestone" title="St Mary and St Eanswythe&#39;s Church, Folkestone">St Mary and St Eanswythe's Church</a>. During restoration work at the church in 1885, human remains were discovered in a lead <a href="/wiki/Reliquary" title="Reliquary">reliquary</a>, embedded within the church's north wall, which were identified as a 12th-century vessel, and the bones of a young woman.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> This led to the supposition that they could be the translated relics of Saint Eanswith, hidden at the Reformation.<sup id="cite_ref-PallMall_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-PallMall-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> 
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconography">Iconography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eanswith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Iconography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Eanswith is sometimes portrayed with a fish, along with her abbess's staff, crown and a book. This appears to be a recent attribute, from Folkestone's fishing port connection.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Documentary_sources">Documentary sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eanswith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Documentary sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<ul><li>Eanswith is named in the genealogies of some versions of the <a href="/wiki/Kentish_Royal_Legend" title="Kentish Royal Legend">Kentish Royal Legend</a></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Goscelin" title="Goscelin">Goscelin</a> of Saint-Bertin mentions Eanswith in his 11th-century <i>Vita Sancta Werburge</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/John_of_Tynemouth_(chronicler)" title="John of Tynemouth (chronicler)">John of Tynemouth</a> (14th-century chronicler) has a substantive account in his <i>Sanctilogium</i>. As with all the Kentish Abbesses, <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> does not name Eanswith, so John must have had another source.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="New_research">New research</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eanswith&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: New research">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 2017 a collaboration began between Kent historians and archaeologists from <a href="/wiki/Canterbury_Christ_Church_University" title="Canterbury Christ Church University">Canterbury Christ Church University</a> and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Folkestone_Museum&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Folkestone Museum (page does not exist)">Folkestone Museum</a>. Church legislation was required for the removal and examination of the human remains that had been uncovered in 1885. In 2020, osteoarchaeologists were given the opportunity to examine the remains. <a href="/wiki/Osteology" title="Osteology">Osteologists</a> tested teeth and bones and determined that they had come from one person, probably a woman, aged between 17 and 21, with no signs of malnutrition, all consistent with the history of Eanswith. Samples were sent to scientists at <a href="/wiki/Queen%27s_University_Belfast" title="Queen&#39;s University Belfast">Queen's University Belfast</a> and <a href="/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating" title="Radiocarbon dating">radiocarbon dating</a> confirmed that the remains were from the mid-7th century. The 'Finding Eanswythe' project received a grant from the <a href="/wiki/Heritage_Lottery_Fund" class="mw-redirect" title="Heritage Lottery Fund">Heritage Lottery Fund</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Keys_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Keys-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It can never be proven that the remains are St Eanswith but, if they were, they would be the earliest remains yet discovered of an English saint, and of a relative of the British monarch.<sup id="cite_ref-Keys_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Keys-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Williams_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Williams-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
