<p><b>Epiphanius of Salamis</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc">Ἐπιφάνιος</span></span>; c. 310–320 – 403) was the bishop of <a href="/wiki/Salamis,_Cyprus" title="Salamis, Cyprus">Salamis, Cyprus</a>, at the end of the <a href="/wiki/Christianity_in_the_4th_century" title="Christianity in the 4th century">4th century</a>. He is considered a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and a <a href="/wiki/Church_Father" class="mw-redirect" title="Church Father">Church Father</a> by both the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox</a> and <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Churches</a>. He gained a reputation as a strong defender of <a href="/wiki/Orthodoxy" title="Orthodoxy">orthodoxy</a>. He is best known for composing the <i><a href="/wiki/Panarion" title="Panarion">Panarion</a></i>, a very large <a href="/wiki/Compendium" title="Compendium">compendium</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heresies</a> up to his own time, full of quotations that are often the only surviving fragments of suppressed texts. According to <a href="/wiki/Ernst_Kitzinger" title="Ernst Kitzinger">Ernst Kitzinger</a>, he "seems to have been the first cleric to have taken up the matter of Christian religious images as a major issue", and there has been much controversy over how many of the quotations attributed to him by the <a href="/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasts" class="mw-redirect" title="Byzantine Iconoclasts">Byzantine Iconoclasts</a> were actually by him. Regardless of this he was clearly strongly <a href="/wiki/Aniconism_in_Christianity" title="Aniconism in Christianity">against some contemporary uses of images</a> in the church.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Epiphanius_of_Salamis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Epiphanius was either born into a <a href="/wiki/Romaniote_Jews" title="Romaniote Jews">Romaniote</a> <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christian</a> family or became a Christian in his youth. Either way, he was a <a href="/wiki/Romaniote_Jew" class="mw-redirect" title="Romaniote Jew">Romaniote Jew</a> who was born in the small settlement of Besanduk, near <a href="/wiki/Bayt_Jibrin" title="Bayt Jibrin">Eleutheropolis</a> (modern-day <a href="/wiki/Beit_Guvrin,_Israel" title="Beit Guvrin, Israel">Beit Guvrin</a> in Israel), and lived as a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> in Egypt, where he was educated and came into contact with <a href="/wiki/Valentinius" class="mw-redirect" title="Valentinius">Valentinian groups</a>. He returned to Roman Palestine around 333, when he was still a young man, and he founded a monastery at Ad nearby,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> which is often mentioned in the <a href="/wiki/Polemics" class="mw-redirect" title="Polemics">polemics</a> of <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> with <a href="/wiki/Tyrannius_Rufinus" title="Tyrannius Rufinus">Rufinus</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bishop_John_II_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop John II of Jerusalem">John, Bishop of Jerusalem</a>. He was <a href="/wiki/Ordained" class="mw-redirect" title="Ordained">ordained</a> a priest, and lived and studied as superior of the monastery in Ad that he founded for thirty years and gained much skill and knowledge in that position. In that position he gained the ability to speak in several tongues, including <a href="/wiki/Hebrew_language" title="Hebrew language">Hebrew</a>, <a href="/wiki/Syriac_language" title="Syriac language">Syriac</a>, <a href="/wiki/Egyptian_language" title="Egyptian language">Egyptian</a>, <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Greek language">Greek</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a>, and was called by Jerome on that account <i>Pentaglossos</i> ("Five tongued").<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His reputation for learning prompted his nomination and <a href="/wiki/Consecration" title="Consecration">consecration</a> as Bishop of <a href="/wiki/Salamis,_Cyprus" title="Salamis, Cyprus">Salamis, Cyprus</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> in 365 or 367, a post which he held until his death. He was also the <a href="/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop" title="Metropolitan bishop">Metropolitan</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Cyprus" title="Church of Cyprus">Church of Cyprus</a>. He served as bishop for nearly forty years, as well as travelled widely to combat differing beliefs. He was present at a synod in <a href="/wiki/Antioch" title="Antioch">Antioch</a> (376) where the <a href="/wiki/Trinitarianism" class="mw-redirect" title="Trinitarianism">Trinitarian</a> questions were debated against the heresy of <a href="/wiki/Apollinarism" title="Apollinarism">Apollinarianism</a>. He upheld the position of Bishop <a href="/wiki/Paulinus,_Bishop_of_Antioch" class="mw-redirect" title="Paulinus, Bishop of Antioch">Paulinus</a>, who had the support of Rome, over that of <a href="/wiki/Meletius_of_Antioch" title="Meletius of Antioch">Meletius of Antioch</a>, who was supported by the Eastern Churches. In 382 he was present at the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Rome" title="Council of Rome">Council of Rome</a>, again upholding the cause of Paulinus.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Origenist_controversy_and_death">Origenist controversy and death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Epiphanius_of_Salamis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Origenist controversy and death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Origenist_Crises" class="mw-redirect" title="Origenist Crises">Origenist Crises</a></div>
<p>During a visit to Palestine in 394 or 395, while preaching in Jerusalem, he attacked <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>'s followers and urged the Bishop of <a href="/wiki/Jerusalem" title="Jerusalem">Jerusalem</a>, <a href="/wiki/John_II,_Bishop_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="John II, Bishop of Jerusalem">John II</a>, to condemn his writings. He urged John to be careful of the "offence" of images in the churches. He noted that when travelling in Palestine he went into a church to pray and saw a curtain with an image of Christ or a saint which he tore down. He told Bishop John that such images were "opposed . . . to our religion" (see below).<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> This event sowed the seeds of conflict which erupted in the dispute between <a href="/wiki/Tyrannius_Rufinus" title="Tyrannius Rufinus">Rufinus</a> and <a href="/wiki/John_II,_Bishop_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="John II, Bishop of Jerusalem">John</a> against <a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> and Epiphanius. Epiphanius fuelled this conflict by ordaining a priest for Jerome's monastery at Bethlehem, thus trespassing on John's jurisdiction. This dispute continued during the 390s, in particular in the literary works by Rufinus and Jerome attacking one another.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:184px;"><a href="/wiki/File:H._Epiphanius_van_Salamis,_kerkvader,_RP-P-1896-A-19368-1298.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/H._Epiphanius_van_Salamis%2C_kerkvader%2C_RP-P-1896-A-19368-1298.jpg/182px-H._Epiphanius_van_Salamis%2C_kerkvader%2C_RP-P-1896-A-19368-1298.jpg" decoding="async" width="182" height="299" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/H._Epiphanius_van_Salamis%2C_kerkvader%2C_RP-P-1896-A-19368-1298.jpg/274px-H._Epiphanius_van_Salamis%2C_kerkvader%2C_RP-P-1896-A-19368-1298.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/H._Epiphanius_van_Salamis%2C_kerkvader%2C_RP-P-1896-A-19368-1298.jpg/364px-H._Epiphanius_van_Salamis%2C_kerkvader%2C_RP-P-1896-A-19368-1298.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3888" data-file-height="6378" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:H._Epiphanius_van_Salamis,_kerkvader,_RP-P-1896-A-19368-1298.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Engraving by <a href="/wiki/Jan_Luyken" title="Jan Luyken">Jan Luyken</a> depicting Epiphanius and the return of his body to Constantinople</div></div></div>
<p>In 399, the dispute took on another dimension, when the Bishop of Alexandria, Theophilus, who had initially supported <a href="/wiki/John_II,_Bishop_of_Jerusalem" class="mw-redirect" title="John II, Bishop of Jerusalem">John</a>, changed his views and started persecuting Origenist monks in Egypt. As a result of this persecution, four of these monks, the so-called Tall Brothers, fled to Palestine, and then travelled to Constantinople, seeking support and spreading the controversy. <a href="/wiki/Saint_John_Chrysostom" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint John Chrysostom">John Chrysostom, Bishop of Constantinople</a>, gave the monks shelter. Bishop Theophilus of Alexandria saw his chance to use this event to bring down his enemy <a href="/wiki/Saint_John_Chrysostom" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint John Chrysostom">Chrysostom</a>: in 402 he summoned a council in Constantinople, and invited those supportive of his anti-Origenist views. Epiphanius, by this time nearly 80, was one of those summoned, and began the journey to Constantinople. However, when he realised he was being used as a tool by Theophilus against Chrysostom, who had given refuge to the monks persecuted by Theophilus and who were appealing to the emperor, Epiphanius started back to Salamis, only to die on the way home in 403.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="The_curtain_incident">The curtain incident</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Epiphanius_of_Salamis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: The curtain incident">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Letter LI in Jerome's letters gives Jerome's Latin translation, made at Epiphanius' request, of his letter, originally in Greek from c. 394, "From Epiphanius, Bishop of Salamis, in Cyprus, to John, Bishop of Jerusalem" (see previous section for wider context).  The final section covers the often quoted incident of the curtain, which unlike other passages attributed to Epiphanius and quoted by the Iconoclasts, is accepted as authentic by modern scholars:<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<blockquote><p>
9. Moreover, I have heard that certain persons have this grievance against me: When I accompanied you to the holy place called Bethel, there to join you in celebrating the Collect, after the use of the Church, I came to a villa called Anablatha and, as I was passing, saw a lamp burning there. Asking what place it was, and learning it to be a church, I went in to pray, and found there a curtain hanging on the doors of the said church, dyed and embroidered. It bore an image either of Christ or of one of the saints; I do not rightly remember whose the image was. Seeing this, and being loth that an image of a man should be hung up in Christ’s church contrary to the teaching of the Scriptures, I tore it asunder and advised the custodians of the place to use it as a winding sheet for some poor person. They, however, murmured, and said that if I made up my mind to tear it, it was only fair that I should give them another curtain in its place. As soon as I heard this, I promised that I would give one, and said that I would send it at once. Since then there has been some little delay, due to the fact that I have been seeking a curtain of the best quality to give to them instead of the former one, and thought it right to send to Cyprus for one. I have now sent the best that I could find, and I beg that you will order the presbyter of the place to take the curtain which I have sent from the hands of the Reader, and that you will afterwards give directions that curtains of the other sort—opposed as they are to our religion—shall not be hung up in any church of Christ. A man of your uprightness should be careful to remove an occasion of offence unworthy alike of the Church of Christ and of those Christians who are committed to your charge. Beware of Palladius of Galatia—a man once dear to me, but who now sorely needs God's pity—for he preaches and teaches the heresy of Origen; and see to it that he does not seduce any of those who are intrusted to your keeping into the perverse ways of his erroneous doctrine. I pray that you may fare well in the Lord.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Epiphanius_of_Salamis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Panarion"><i>Panarion</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Epiphanius_of_Salamis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Panarion">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Panarion" title="Panarion">Panarion</a></div>
<p>His best-known book is the <i>Panarion</i> (from Latin <i>panarium</i>, "bread basket" &lt; <i>panis</i>, "bread"), also known as <i>Adversus Haereses</i>, "Against Heresies", presented as a book of antidotes for those bitten by the serpent of heresy. Written between 374 and 377, it forms a handbook for dealing with the arguments of heretics.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:207px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Epifaniy.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e1/Epifaniy.jpg/205px-Epifaniy.jpg" decoding="async" width="205" height="222" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e1/Epifaniy.jpg/308px-Epifaniy.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e1/Epifaniy.jpg 2x" data-file-width="370" data-file-height="400" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Epifaniy.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>11th century depiction of St. Epiphanius in <a href="/wiki/Saint_Sophia_Cathedral,_Kyiv" title="Saint Sophia Cathedral, Kyiv">Saint Sophia Cathedral, Kiev</a></div></div></div>
<p>It lists, and refutes, 80 <a href="/wiki/Heresy#Early_Christian_heresies" title="Heresy">heresies</a>, some of which are not described in any other surviving documents from the time. Epiphanius begins with the 'four mothers' of pre-Christian heresy – 'barbarism', 'Scythism', 'Hellenism' and 'Judaism' – and then addresses the 16 pre-Christian heresies that have flowed from them: four philosophical schools (Stoics, Platonists, Pythagoreans and Epicureans), and 12 Jewish sects. There then follows an interlude, telling of the Incarnation of the Word. After this, Epiphanius embarks on his account of the 60 Christian heresies, from assorted gnostics to the various trinitarian heresies of the fourth century, closing with the <a href="/wiki/Collyridians" class="mw-redirect" title="Collyridians">Collyridians</a> and <a href="/wiki/Messalians" class="mw-redirect" title="Messalians">Messalians</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While Epiphanius often let his zeal come before facts – he admits on one occasion that he writes against the Origenists-based only on hearsay (<i>Panarion</i>, Epiphanius 71) – the <i>Panarion</i> is a valuable source of information on the Christian Church of the fourth century. It is also an important source regarding the early Jewish gospels such as the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_the_Hebrews" title="Gospel of the Hebrews">Gospel according to the Hebrews</a> circulating among the <a href="/wiki/Ebionites" title="Ebionites">Ebionites</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Nazarene_(sect)" title="Nazarene (sect)">Nazarenes</a>, as well as the followers of <a href="/wiki/Cerinthus" title="Cerinthus">Cerinthus</a> and Merinthus.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>One unique feature of the <i>Panarion</i> is in the way that Epiphanius compares the various heretics to different poisonous beasts, going so far as to describe in detail the animal's characteristics, how it produces its poison, and how to protect oneself from the animal's bite or poison. For example, he describes his enemy <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a> as "a toad noisy from too much moisture which keeps croaking louder and louder." He compares the <a href="/wiki/Gnostic" class="mw-redirect" title="Gnostic">Gnostics</a> to a particularly dreaded snake "with no fangs." The <a href="/wiki/Ebionites" title="Ebionites">Ebionites</a>, a Christian sect that followed Jewish law, were described by Epiphanius as "a monstrosity with many shapes, who practically formed the snake-like shape of the mythical many-headed Hydra in himself." In all, Epiphanius describes fifty animals, usually one per sect.<sup id="cite_ref-Verheyden_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Verheyden-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Another feature of the <i>Panarion</i> is the access its earlier sections provide to lost works, notably Justin Martyr's work on heresies, the Greek of Irenaeus' <i>Against Heresies</i>, and Hippolytus' <i>Syntagma</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Panarion</i> was first translated into English in 1987 and 1990.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_works">Other works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Epiphanius_of_Salamis&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Other works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>His earliest known work is the <i><a href="/wiki/Ancoratus" title="Ancoratus">Ancoratus</a></i> (the well anchored man), which includes arguments against <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a> and the teachings of <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>. Aside from the <a href="/wiki/Polemics" class="mw-redirect" title="Polemics">polemics</a> by which he is known, Epiphanius wrote a work of biblical <a href="/wiki/Antiquarian" title="Antiquarian">antiquarianism</a>, called, for one of its sections, <i><a href="/wiki/On_Weights_and_Measures_(Epiphanius)" class="mw-redirect" title="On Weights and Measures (Epiphanius)">On Weights and Measures</a></i> (περὶ μέτρων καὶ στάθμων). It was composed in Constantinople for a Persian priest, in 392,<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> and survives in Syriac, Armenian, and Georgian translations (this last is found in <i>Shatberd ms 1141</i> along with <i>Physiologus</i> and <i>De Gemmis</i>).<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> The first section discusses the canon of the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old Testament</a> and its versions, the second of measures and weights, and the third, the geography of <a href="/wiki/Palestine_(region)" title="Palestine (region)">Palestine</a>. The texts appear not to have been given a polish but consist of rough notes and sketches, as Allen A. Shaw, a modern commentator, concluded; nevertheless Epiphanius' work on <a href="/wiki/Metrology" title="Metrology">metrology</a> was important in the <a href="/wiki/History_of_measurement" title="History of measurement">history of measurement</a>.
</p><p>Another work, <i>On the Twelve Gems</i> (<i>De Gemmis</i>), survives in a number of fragments, the most complete of which is the Georgian.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup>  The letter written by Epiphanius to John, Bishop of Jerusalem, in 394 and preserved in Jerome's translation, is discussed above.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>  The collection of <a href="/wiki/Homily" title="Homily">homilies</a> traditionally ascribed to a "Saint Epiphanius, bishop" are dated in the late fifth or sixth century and are not connected with Epiphanius of Salamis by modern scholars.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Such was Epiphanius's reputation for learning that the <i><a href="/wiki/Physiologus" title="Physiologus">Physiologus</a></i>, the principal source of medieval bestiaries, came to be widely falsely attributed to him.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
