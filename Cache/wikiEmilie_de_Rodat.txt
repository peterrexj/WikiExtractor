<p><b>Émilie de Rodat</b> (6 September 1787 – 19 September 1852), born <b>Marie Guillemette (Wilhelmina) Emilie de Rodat</b>, also known as <b>Emily de Rodat</b>, was a <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nun</a>, <a href="/wiki/Virginity" title="Virginity">virgin</a>, <a href="/wiki/Mysticism" title="Mysticism">mystic</a>, and the founder of the <a href="/wiki/Sisters_of_the_Holy_Family_of_Villefranche" title="Sisters of the Holy Family of Villefranche">Sisters of the Holy Family of Villefranche</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> She was born to a noble family near <a href="/wiki/Rodez" title="Rodez">Rodez</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Southern_France" title="Southern France">southern France</a>. When she was 18 months old, she was sent to live with her maternal grandmother in <a href="/wiki/Villefranche-de-Rouergue" title="Villefranche-de-Rouergue">Villefranche</a>, to protect her from the oppression of Christians during the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>. When she was 16, she had a spiritual experience, and at the age of 18, she became a teacher. In 1815, she started a school for poor girls in Villefranche, which became the Sisters of the Holy Family of Villefranche. Despite Rodat's spiritual and physical difficulties, the community expanded, eventually founding 38 houses, 25 cloistered communities, and 32 schools with over 5,000 students; they also visited prisoners and cared for abandoned infants in China. By 1999, there were 520 Sisters of the Holy Family of Villefranche worldwide.
</p><p>Rodat's feast day is 19 September. She was beatified in 1940 and canonized in 1950.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%89milie_de_Rodat&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Émilie de Rodat was born on 6 September 1787, to a noble family, in "a handsome manor-house called Druelle"<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> facing the plateau on which <a href="/wiki/Rodez" title="Rodez">Rodez</a> stands, in <a href="/wiki/Southern_France" title="Southern France">southern France</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-270_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> When she was 18 months old, at the beginning of the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>, she was taken to live with her maternal grandmother in the Château of Ginals outside <a href="/wiki/Villefranche-de-Rouergue" title="Villefranche-de-Rouergue">Villefranche</a>, where her family lived in peace during the Revolution and Napoleon's reign because it was in a remote area and was relatively unscathed from the persecution of Catholics and other religious groups. Her aunt, a secularized nun due to religious oppression, also lived with them.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Rodat was described as "a normally lively child with a strong but not excessive religious sensitivity"<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and "a pious young woman".<sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> She went to school in <a href="/wiki/Maison_Saint-Cyr" class="mw-redirect" title="Maison Saint-Cyr">Maison Saint-Cyr</a> in Villefranche, receiving a Christian education in secret due to the Revolution.<sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> When she was eleven, she received her <a href="/wiki/First_Communion" title="First Communion">First Communion</a>. When she was 16, "her enthusiasm for religion cooled somewhat"<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and she changed confessors because she thought hers was too strict; after quarrelling with her grandmother, she returned to live with her parents at Ginals, where in 1804, she had a spiritual experience that drew her into religious life.<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Rodat was 18, she returned to Villefranche, where she became a lay teacher at Maison Saint-Cyr, where she had gone to school, for eleven years. She was in charge of the girls' recreation, taught geography, and prepared them for communion.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> According to <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographer</a> Agnes Dunbar, "She took the deepest interest in their spiritual progress, and never recommended them any penance without first performing it herself".<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-270_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> She met the Abbé Marty, the school's spiritual director, who became her confessor from 1805 to 1839; with his support and encouragement, Rodat investigated joining three communities, but always returned to Villefranche because none seemed to suit her; she blamed herself for her "restlessness and instability".<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> In the spring of 1815, she was inspired to start a school for girls after overhearing a group of poor women discussing how their daughters were growing up without religious instruction because they were unable to pay for it themselves and the <a href="/wiki/Ursulines" title="Ursulines">Ursuline nuns</a> that taught them for no cost were driven away during the Revolution.<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-270_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Rodat taught 40 students, with the help of three assistants, in her small room at Villefranche. This was the beginning of what became the <a href="/wiki/Sisters_of_the_Holy_Family_of_Villefranche" title="Sisters of the Holy Family of Villefranche">Sisters of the Holy Family of Villefranche</a>, also called the Sisters of Villefranche.<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Career_and_ministry">Career and ministry</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%89milie_de_Rodat&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Career and ministry">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In May 1816, with the support of the Abbé Marty, Rodat rented her own building and started a free school for girls. Marty wrote a rule based upon the <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Augustine" title="Rule of Saint Augustine">Rule of St. Augustine</a> and "helped her to expand the focus of this new community".<sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> She was able to purchase the house at the Maison Saint-Cyr, which was closing, with 100 students and eight other sisters. Two years later, she was able to buy better buildings for her school, but the congregation's existence was threatened by a series of unexplained illnesses and deaths of the students and teachers that was attributed to "diabolic influence".<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Rodat was inclined to take this as a sign not to continue, and seriously considered merging the community with the Daughters of Mary, which had just been founded by <a href="/wiki/Ad%C3%A8le_de_Batz_de_Trenquell%C3%A9on" title="Adèle de Batz de Trenquelléon">Adèle de Batz de Trenquellèon</a>. The sisters at Villefranche refused to accept any other abbess but Rodat, so the community was established, anyway. Rodat and her nuns took perpetual vows in the autumn of 1820, and according to church historian and hagiographer <a href="/wiki/Alban_Butler" title="Alban Butler">Alban Butler</a>, they adopted a habit "of which the distinguishing feature was the transparent edge of the veil covering the upper part of the face".<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> &#160;The first months of the Congregation did not go smoothly; according to Dunbar, "They were laughed at, jeered at, stoned".<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-270_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> They were also threatened by legal action, some members of the Maison Saint-Cyr community were hostile to them, and they had to endure ridicule and criticism from lay people and clergy.<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1832, the Sisters of the Holy Family of Villefranche was formally approved by the bishop of <a href="/wiki/Rodez" title="Rodez">Rodez</a>. It was reported that Rodat had "great faith in God’s providential care and money and materials seemed to miraculously appear when needed".<sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Founding schools were their main focus, but they gradually expanded to other ministries. They began to visit prisoners in 1863, "with encouraging results",<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> opened orphanages, rescue homes for prostitutes, and a retirement home for "aged religious".<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> Rodat also founded groups of contemplative nuns to pray for the community's charitable works,<sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> "seeing in the two branches a personification of <a href="/wiki/Jesus_at_the_home_of_Martha_and_Mary" title="Jesus at the home of Martha and Mary">Martha and Mary</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> By 1852, there were 32 Villefranche convents that ran schools, and five contemplative communities.<sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Rodat "had to endure a lot both spiritually, health-wise and in her work"<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> throughout her life. She developed cancer in her left eye, a cancerous tumor in her nose, and an unknown disease, probably <a href="/wiki/M%C3%A9ni%C3%A8re%27s_disease" title="Ménière&#39;s disease">Ménière's disease</a>, which gave her permanent <a href="/wiki/Tinnitus" title="Tinnitus">tinnitus</a> in her ears. Her health difficulties, along with the loss of direct support from the Abbé Marty when he was appointed vicar general of the diocese of Rodez, caused her to experience a "period of spiritual despair".<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> &#160;A life of intense prayer brought her inner peace of mind, but outwardly she seemed sullen, strict, and unattractive; she was sometimes careless with her personal appearance, especially how she dressed, to counteract her pride, what she perceived as her main weakness. One of her supervisors said that "she was a saint, but a stubborn saint".<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> She was prone to arguments with even her supporters like the Abbé Marty due to "the uncompromising side of her personality",<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> but responded politely to her critics. Few people knew that she was a "mystic with tremendous healing powers."<sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In April 1852, after 30 years as head of the community she founded, Rodat retired due to the development of cancer in her left eye and a tumor.<sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> At the time, the Villefranche Sisters had begun working with abandoned infants in China, a work she was greatly interested in.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-297_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-297-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> She died on 19 September 1852, at the age of 65, and was buried in the crypt in the chapel of the first home she founded in Villefrance, which became a pilgrimage destination and where many people received prayers through her.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-297_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-297-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> At the time of her death, there were 38 houses, 25 cloistered communities, and 32 schools with over 5,000 students sponsored by the Sisters of the Holy Family of Villefranche in several countries. Streets in her home district, including Villefranche and Rodez, were named after her. As of 1999, there were 520 Sisters of the Holy Family of Villefranche worldwide.<sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-270_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Rodat's feast day is 19 September; it is celebrated in the house of Holy Family of Villefranche the third Sunday in September.<sup id="cite_ref-butler-296_1-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-296-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-dunbar-270_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dunbar-270-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-norwaysite_3-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-norwaysite-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> She was beatified on 9 June 1940 and canonized by Pope Pius XII on 23 April 1950.<sup id="cite_ref-stemilychurch_4-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stemilychurch-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
