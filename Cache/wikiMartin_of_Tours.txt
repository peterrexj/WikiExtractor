<p><b>Martin of Tours</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Sanctus Martinus Turonensis</i>; 316/336 – 8 November 397), also known as <b>Martin the Merciful</b>, was the third <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Tours">bishop of Tours</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-succession_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-succession-2">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> He has become one of the most familiar and recognizable Christian saints in France, heralded as the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Third_French_Republic" class="mw-redirect" title="Third French Republic">Third Republic</a>, and is patron saint of many communities and organizations across Europe. A native of <a href="/wiki/Pannonia" title="Pannonia">Pannonia</a> (in central Europe), he converted to Christianity at a young age. He served in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_cavalry" title="Roman cavalry">Roman cavalry</a> in <a href="/wiki/Roman_Gaul" title="Roman Gaul">Gaul</a>, but left military service at some point prior to 361, when he became a disciple of <a href="/wiki/Hilary_of_Poitiers" title="Hilary of Poitiers">Hilary of Poitiers</a>, establishing the <a href="/wiki/Ligug%C3%A9_Abbey" title="Ligugé Abbey">monastery at Ligugé</a>. He was consecrated as Bishop of Caesarodunum (Tours) in 371. As bishop, he was active in the suppression of the remnants of <a href="/wiki/Gallo-Roman_religion" title="Gallo-Roman religion">Gallo-Roman religion</a>, but he opposed the violent persecution of the <a href="/wiki/Priscillian" title="Priscillian">Priscillianist</a> sect of ascetics.
</p><p>His life was recorded by a contemporary hagiographer, <a href="/wiki/Sulpicius_Severus" title="Sulpicius Severus">Sulpicius Severus</a>. Some of the accounts of his travels may have been interpolated into his <i>vita</i> to validate early sites of his <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religion)" class="mw-redirect" title="Cult (religion)">cult</a>.  He is best known for the account of his using his sword to cut his cloak in two, to give half to a beggar clad only in rags in the depth of winter. His shrine in <a href="/wiki/Tours" title="Tours">Tours</a> became a famous stopping-point for <a href="/wiki/Camino_de_Santiago" title="Camino de Santiago">pilgrims on the road</a> to <a href="/wiki/Santiago_de_Compostela" title="Santiago de Compostela">Santiago de Compostela</a> in <a href="/wiki/Spain" title="Spain">Spain</a>. His cult was revived in <a href="/wiki/French_nationalism" title="French nationalism">French nationalism</a> during the <a href="/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War" title="Franco-Prussian War">Franco-Prussian War</a> of 1870/1, and as a consequence he was seen as a patron saint of France during the <a href="/wiki/French_Third_Republic" title="French Third Republic">French Third Republic</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Hagiography">Hagiography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Hagiography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The early life of Martin was written by <a href="/wiki/Sulpicius_Severus" title="Sulpicius Severus">Sulpicius Severus</a>, a contemporary <a href="/wiki/Christian" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian">Christian</a> writer, who knew him personally.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESulpicius_Severus1894_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESulpicius_Severus1894-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> This biography expresses, among other things, the immediacy the 4th-century Christian felt with the Devil in all his disguises, and has some accounts of miracles. Some follow familiar conventions— casting out devils, raising the <a href="/wiki/Paralytic" class="mw-redirect" title="Paralytic">paralytic</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Dead" class="mw-redirect" title="Dead">dead</a>. 
Sulpicius Severus recounts in which manner St Martin raised a dead man as follows:
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St.Martins.Skull.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cb/St.Martins.Skull.jpg/220px-St.Martins.Skull.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cb/St.Martins.Skull.jpg/330px-St.Martins.Skull.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cb/St.Martins.Skull.jpg/440px-St.Martins.Skull.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2448" data-file-height="3264" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St.Martins.Skull.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>A part of St Martin's skull in the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint_Martin,_Tours" title="Basilica of Saint Martin, Tours">Basilica of Saint Martin, Tours</a></div></div></div>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>But, after the lapse only of a few days, the catechumen, seized with a languor, began to suffer from a violent fever. It so happened that Martin had then left home, and having remained away three days, he found on his return that life had departed from the catechumen; and so suddenly had death occurred, that he had left this world without receiving baptism. The body being laid out in public was being honored by the last sad offices on the part of the mourning brethren, when Martin hurries up to them with tears and lamentations. But then laying hold; as it were, of the Holy Spirit, with the whole powers of his mind, he orders the others to quit the cell in which the body was lying; and bolting the door, he stretches himself at full length on the dead limbs of the departed brother. Having given himself for some time to earnest prayer, and perceiving by means of the Spirit of God that power was present, he then rose up for a little, and gazing on the countenance of the deceased, he waited without misgiving for the result of his prayer and of the mercy of the Lord. And scarcely had the space of two hours elapsed, when he saw the dead man begin to move a little in all his members, and to tremble with his eyes opened for the practice of sight. Then indeed, turning to the Lord with a loud voice and giving thanks, he filled the cell with his ejaculations. Hearing the noise, those who had been standing at the door immediately rush inside. And truly a marvelous spectacle met them, for they beheld the man alive whom they had formerly left dead. Thus being restored to life, and having immediately obtained baptism, he lived for many years afterwards; and he was the first who offered himself to us both as a subject that had experienced the virtues of Martin, and as a witness to their existence.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8201;<cite><a href="#CITEREFSulpicius_Severus1894">Sulpicius Severus 1894</a></cite></div></blockquote>
<p>Others are: turning back the flames from a house while Martin was burning down the Roman temple it adjoined; deflecting the path of a felled <a href="/w/index.php?title=Sacred_pine&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Sacred pine (page does not exist)">sacred pine</a>; the healing power of a letter written from Martin.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Soldier">Soldier</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Soldier">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Martin was born in AD 316 or 336<sup id="cite_ref-birthday_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-birthday-5">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> in <a href="/wiki/Szombathely" title="Szombathely">Savaria</a> in the  <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Pannonia" title="Diocese of Pannonia">Diocese of Pannonia</a> (now Szombathely, Hungary). His father was a senior officer (<a href="/wiki/Tribunus_Militum" class="mw-redirect" title="Tribunus Militum">tribune</a>) in the Roman army. A few years after Martin's birth, his father was given veteran status and was allocated land on which to retire at <a href="/wiki/Ticinum" title="Ticinum">Ticinum</a> (now <a href="/wiki/Pavia" title="Pavia">Pavia</a>), in northern Italy, where Martin grew up.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEClugnet1910_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEClugnet1910-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPernoud200620_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPernoud200620-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:227px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Simone_Martini_028.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Simone_Martini_028.jpg/225px-Simone_Martini_028.jpg" decoding="async" width="225" height="270" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Simone_Martini_028.jpg/338px-Simone_Martini_028.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Simone_Martini_028.jpg/450px-Simone_Martini_028.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2024" data-file-height="2433" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Simone_Martini_028.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>St Martin leaves the life of chivalry and renounces the army. Fresco by Simone Martini.</div></div></div>
<p>At the age of 10 he attended the Christian church against the wishes of his parents and became a <a href="/wiki/Catechumen" class="mw-redirect" title="Catechumen">catechumen</a>. Christianity had been made a legal religion (in 313) in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman Empire</a>. It had many more adherents in the Eastern Empire, whence it had sprung, and was concentrated in cities, brought along the trade routes by converted Jews and Greeks (the term 'pagan' literally means 'country-dweller'). Christianity was far from accepted among the higher echelons of society; among members of the army the worship of <a href="/wiki/Mithras" class="mw-redirect" title="Mithras">Mithras</a> would have been stronger. Although the conversion of the <a href="/wiki/Emperor_Constantine" class="mw-redirect" title="Emperor Constantine">Emperor Constantine</a> and the subsequent programme of church-building gave a greater impetus to the spread of the religion, it was still a minority faith.
</p><p>As the son of a veteran officer, Martin at 15 was required to join a cavalry <i><a href="/wiki/Auxilia#Alae" title="Auxilia">ala</a>.</i> At the age of 18 (around 334 or 354), he was stationed at <i>Ambianensium civitas</i> or <a href="/wiki/Samarobriva" class="mw-redirect" title="Samarobriva">Samarobriva</a> in <a href="/wiki/Roman_Gaul" title="Roman Gaul">Gaul</a> (now <a href="/wiki/Amiens" title="Amiens">Amiens</a>, France).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEClugnet1910_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEClugnet1910-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> It is likely that he joined the <i><a href="/wiki/Equites_cataphractarii" title="Equites cataphractarii">Equites catafractarii</a> Ambianenses</i>, a <a href="/wiki/Heavy_cavalry" title="Heavy cavalry">heavy cavalry</a> unit listed in the <i><a href="/wiki/Notitia_Dignitatum" title="Notitia Dignitatum">Notitia Dignitatum</a></i>. As the unit was stationed at Milan and is also recorded at <a href="/wiki/Trier" title="Trier">Trier</a>, it is likely to have been part of the elite cavalry bodyguard of the Emperor, which accompanied him on his travels around the Empire. 
</p><p>Martin's biographer, <a href="/wiki/Sulpicius_Severus" title="Sulpicius Severus">Sulpicius Severus</a>, provided no dates in his chronology, so although he indicated that Martin served in the military "for nearly two years after his baptism," it is difficult for the historian to pin down the exact date of Martin's exit from military service.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPernoud200629_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPernoud200629-8">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Still, historian Andre Mertens has provided this guidance: "He [Martin] served under the Roman emperor Constantine II (ruled 337-61) and afterwards under Julian (ruled 355-60)."<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Regardless of the difficulties in chronology, Sulpicius reports that just before a battle in the <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gallic provinces</a> at <a href="/wiki/Worms,_Germany" title="Worms, Germany">Borbetomagus (now Worms, Germany)</a>, Martin determined that his switch of allegiance to a new commanding officer (away from antichristian Julian and to Christ), along with reluctance to receive Julian's pay just as Martin was retiring, prohibited his taking the money and continuing to submit to the authority of the former now, telling him, "I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESulpicius_Severus1894_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESulpicius_Severus1894-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> He was charged with cowardice and jailed, but in response to the charge, he volunteered to go unarmed to the front of the troops. His superiors planned to take him up on the offer, but before they could, the invaders sued for peace, the battle never occurred, and Martin was released from military service.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKurlansky200626–27_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKurlansky200626–27-10">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Monk_and_hermit">Monk and hermit</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Monk and hermit">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Martin declared his vocation, and made his way to the city of <a href="/wiki/Caesarodunum" class="mw-redirect" title="Caesarodunum">Caesarodunum</a> (now <a href="/wiki/Tours" title="Tours">Tours</a>), where he became a disciple of <a href="/wiki/Hilary_of_Poitiers" title="Hilary of Poitiers">Hilary of Poitiers</a>' Christian orthodoxy.<sup id="cite_ref-crawley_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-crawley-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> He opposed the <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a> of the Imperial Court. When Hilary was forced into exile from Pictavium (now <a href="/wiki/Poitiers" title="Poitiers">Poitiers</a>), Martin returned to Italy. According to Sulpicius, he converted an <a href="/wiki/Alps" title="Alps">Alpine</a> brigand on the way, and confronted the <a href="/wiki/Devil" title="Devil">Devil</a> himself. Having heard in a dream a summons to revisit his home, Martin crossed the Alps, and from <a href="/wiki/Milan" title="Milan">Milan</a> went over to Pannonia. There he converted his mother and some other persons; his father he could not win over. While in Illyricum he took sides against the Arians with so much zeal that he was publicly whipped and forced to leave.<sup id="cite_ref-crawley_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-crawley-11">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Returning from <a href="/wiki/Illyria" title="Illyria">Illyria</a>, he was confronted by <a href="/wiki/Auxentius_of_Milan" title="Auxentius of Milan">Auxentius</a>, the Arian <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Milan" title="Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Milan">Archbishop of Milan</a>, who expelled him from the city. According to the early sources, Martin decided to seek shelter on the island then called Gallinaria, now <a href="/wiki/Isola_d%27Albenga" class="mw-redirect" title="Isola d&#39;Albenga">Isola d'Albenga</a>, in the <a href="/wiki/Ligurian_Sea" title="Ligurian Sea">Ligurian Sea</a>, where he lived the solitary life of a hermit. Not entirely alone, since the chronicles indicate that he would have been in the company of a <i>priest, a man of great virtues</i>, and for a period with <a href="/wiki/Hilary_of_Poitiers" title="Hilary of Poitiers">Hilary of Poitiers</a>, on this island, where the wild hens lived. Martin lived on a diet of <a href="/wiki/Herb" title="Herb">herbs</a> and wild <a href="/wiki/Root" title="Root">roots</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHones18351469–1470_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHones18351469–1470-12">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> It is alleged he ate <a href="/wiki/Hellebore" title="Hellebore">hellebore</a>, a plant that he did not know was poisonous. A legend tells that being on the verge of death for having eaten this herb, he prayed and was miraculously cured.
</p><p>With the return of Hilary to his <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_see" title="Episcopal see">see</a> in 361, Martin joined him and established a hermitage nearby, which soon attracted converts and followers. The crypt under the parish church (not the current Abbey Chapel) reveals traces of a Roman villa, probably part of the bath complex, which had been abandoned before Martin established himself there. This site was developed into the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_St._Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of St. Benedict">Benedictine</a> <a href="/wiki/Ligug%C3%A9_Abbey" title="Ligugé Abbey">Ligugé Abbey</a>, the oldest monastery known in Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-zenith_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-zenith-13">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> It became a centre for the <a href="/wiki/Evangelisation" class="mw-redirect" title="Evangelisation">evangelisation</a> of the country districts. Martin travelled and preached through western <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a>: "The memory of these apostolic journeyings survives to our day in the numerous local legends of which Martin is the hero and which indicate roughly the routes that he followed."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEClugnet1910_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEClugnet1910-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Bishop">Bishop</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Bishop">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In AD 371 Martin was acclaimed <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of Tours">bishop of Tours</a>, where he impressed the city with his demeanour. He had been drawn to Tours by a ruse&#160;— he was urged to come to minister to someone sick&#160;— and was brought to the church, where he reluctantly allowed himself to be consecrated bishop.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-14">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> According to one version, he was so unwilling to be made bishop that he hid in a barn full of geese, but their cackling at his intrusion gave him away to the crowd; that may account for complaints by a few that his appearance was too disheveled to be commensurate with a bishopric, but the critics were hugely outnumbered.
</p><p>As bishop, Martin set to enthusiastically ordering the destruction of pagan temples, altars and sculptures:
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"/><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>[W]hen in a certain village he had demolished a very ancient temple, and had set about cutting down a pine-tree, which stood close to the temple, the chief priest of that place, and a crowd of other heathens began to oppose him; and these people, though, under the influence of the Lord, they had been quiet while the temple was being overthrown, could not patiently allow the tree to be cut down.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8201;<cite><a href="#CITEREFSulpicius_Severus1894">Sulpicius Severus 1894</a>, ch. xiii</cite></div></blockquote>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Anthony_van_Dyck_-_Saint_Martin_sharing_his_cloak_with_a_beggar.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Anthony_van_Dyck_-_Saint_Martin_sharing_his_cloak_with_a_beggar.jpg/220px-Anthony_van_Dyck_-_Saint_Martin_sharing_his_cloak_with_a_beggar.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="234" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Anthony_van_Dyck_-_Saint_Martin_sharing_his_cloak_with_a_beggar.jpg/330px-Anthony_van_Dyck_-_Saint_Martin_sharing_his_cloak_with_a_beggar.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Anthony_van_Dyck_-_Saint_Martin_sharing_his_cloak_with_a_beggar.jpg/440px-Anthony_van_Dyck_-_Saint_Martin_sharing_his_cloak_with_a_beggar.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2442" data-file-height="2598" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Anthony_van_Dyck_-_Saint_Martin_sharing_his_cloak_with_a_beggar.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i><a href="/wiki/Saint_Martin_Dividing_his_Cloak_(van_Dyck)" title="Saint Martin Dividing his Cloak (van Dyck)">Saint Martin Dividing his Cloak</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/Anthony_van_Dyck" title="Anthony van Dyck">van Dyck</a>, c. 1618</div></div></div>
<p>Sulpicius affirms that Martin withdrew from the city to live in <a href="/wiki/Marmoutier_Abbey_(Tours)" class="mw-redirect" title="Marmoutier Abbey (Tours)">Marmoutier</a> (<i>Majus Monasterium</i>), the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> he founded, which faces Tours from the opposite shore of the river <a href="/wiki/Loire" title="Loire">Loire</a>. Recent excavations under the abbey church have revealed the traces of a Roman posting station, beside the main Roman road along the north bank of the Loire, which seems to have been the original dwelling for the community; the "caves" on the site are post-Roman and are probably the result of quarrying the coteau for the Romanesque abbey buildings.
"Here Martin and some of the monks who followed him built cells of wood; others lived in caves dug out of the rock." (Sulpicius Severus).
</p><p>Martin introduced a rudimentary <a href="/wiki/Parish" title="Parish">parish</a> system. Once a year, the bishop visited each of his parishes, traveling on foot, or by donkey or boat. He continued to set up monastic communities, and extended the bounds of his episcopate from Touraine to such distant points as Chartres, Paris, Autun, and Vienne.
</p><p>In one instance, the  pagans agreed to fell their sacred pine tree, if Martin would stand directly in its path. He did so, and it miraculously missed him. Sulpicius, a classically educated aristocrat, related this anecdote with dramatic details, as a set piece. Sulpicius could not have failed to know the incident the Roman poet <a href="/wiki/Horace" title="Horace">Horace</a> recalls in several <i>Odes</i> of his own narrow escape from a falling tree.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Martin was so dedicated to the freeing of prisoners that when authorities, even emperors, heard he was coming, they refused to see him because they knew he would request mercy for someone and they would be unable to refuse.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="On_behalf_of_the_Priscillianists">On behalf of the Priscillianists</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: On behalf of the Priscillianists">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The churches of other parts of Gaul and in Spain were being disturbed by the Priscillianists, an ascetic sect, named after its leader, <a href="/wiki/Priscillian" title="Priscillian">Priscillian</a>. The <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Saragossa" class="mw-redirect" title="First Council of Saragossa">First Council of Saragossa</a> had forbidden several of Priscillian's practices (albeit without mentioning Priscillian by name), but Priscillian was elected bishop of Avila shortly thereafter. Ithacius of Ossonoba appealed to the emperor <a href="/wiki/Gratian" title="Gratian">Gratian</a>, who issued a <a href="/wiki/Rescript" title="Rescript">rescript</a> against Priscillian and his followers. After failing to obtain the support of <a href="/wiki/Ambrose" title="Ambrose">Ambrose of Milan</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pope_Damasus_I" title="Pope Damasus I">Pope Damasus I</a>, Priscillian appealed to <a href="/wiki/Magnus_Maximus" title="Magnus Maximus">Magnus Maximus</a>, who had usurped the throne from Gratian.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChadwick197642–44_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChadwick197642–44-16">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:182px;"><a href="/wiki/File:El_Greco_-_San_Mart%C3%ADn_y_el_mendigo.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/El_Greco_-_San_Mart%C3%ADn_y_el_mendigo.jpg/180px-El_Greco_-_San_Mart%C3%ADn_y_el_mendigo.jpg" decoding="async" width="180" height="333" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/El_Greco_-_San_Mart%C3%ADn_y_el_mendigo.jpg/270px-El_Greco_-_San_Mart%C3%ADn_y_el_mendigo.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/El_Greco_-_San_Mart%C3%ADn_y_el_mendigo.jpg/360px-El_Greco_-_San_Mart%C3%ADn_y_el_mendigo.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2159" data-file-height="4000" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:El_Greco_-_San_Mart%C3%ADn_y_el_mendigo.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i><a href="/wiki/Saint_Martin_and_the_Beggar_(El_Greco)" title="Saint Martin and the Beggar (El Greco)">Saint Martin and the Beggar</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/El_Greco" title="El Greco">El Greco</a>, c. 1577–1579</div></div></div>
<p>Although greatly opposed to the Priscillianists, Martin traveled to the Imperial court of <a href="/wiki/Trier" title="Trier">Trier</a> to remove them from the secular jurisdiction of the emperor. With Ambrose, Martin rejected Bishop Ithacius's principle of putting heretics to death—as well as the intrusion of the emperor into such matters. He prevailed upon the emperor to spare the life of the heretic Priscillian. At first, Maximus acceded to his entreaty, but, when Martin had departed, yielded to Ithacius and ordered Priscillian and his followers to be beheaded (in 385). Martin then pleaded for a cessation of the persecution of Priscillian's followers in Spain.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-14">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Deeply grieved, Martin refused to communicate with Ithacius, until pressured by the Emperor.
</p><p>Martin died in <a href="/wiki/Candes-Saint-Martin" title="Candes-Saint-Martin">Candes-Saint-Martin</a>, <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a> (central France) in 397.  After he died, local citizens of the <a href="/wiki/Poitou" title="Poitou">Poitou</a> region and residents of Tours quarreled over where Martin would be buried.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKenny1914_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKenny1914-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> One evening after dark, several residents of Tours carried Martin's body to a waiting boat on the river <a href="/wiki/Loire" title="Loire">Loire</a>, where teams of rowers ferried his body on the river to Tours, where a huge throng of people waited on the river banks to meet and pay their last respects to Martin's body.  One chronicle states that “2,000 monks, and nearly as many white-robed virgins, walked in the procession” accompanying the body from the river to a small grove outside of Tours, where Martin was buried.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKenny1914_17-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKenny1914-17">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Abbey_of_Marmoutier">Abbey of Marmoutier</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Abbey of Marmoutier">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The Abbey of Marmoutier was a monastery just outside today's city of Tours in Indre-et-Loire, France established by Martin around 372. Martin founded the monastery to escape attention and live life as a monastic. The Abbey at Tours was one of the most prominent and influential establishments in medieval France. <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a> awarded the position of Abbot to his friend and adviser <a href="/wiki/Alcuin" title="Alcuin">Alcuin</a>. 
At this time the abbot could travel between Tours and the court at <a href="/wiki/Trier" title="Trier">Trier</a> in Germany and always stay overnight at one of his own properties. It was at Tours that Alcuin's <a href="/wiki/Scriptorium" title="Scriptorium">scriptorium</a> (a room in <a href="/wiki/Monasteries" class="mw-redirect" title="Monasteries">monasteries</a> devoted to the copying of manuscripts by monastic <a href="/wiki/Scribe" title="Scribe">scribes</a>) developed <a href="/wiki/Caroline_minuscule" class="mw-redirect" title="Caroline minuscule">Caroline minuscule</a>, the clear round hand that made manuscripts far more legible.
</p><p>In later times the abbey was destroyed by fire on several occasions and ransacked by <a href="/wiki/Normans" title="Normans">Norman</a> <a href="/wiki/Viking" class="mw-redirect" title="Viking">Vikings</a> in 853 and in 903. It burned again in 994, and was rebuilt by Hervé de Buzançais, treasurer of Saint Martin, an effort that took 20 years to complete. Expanded to accommodate the crowds of pilgrims and to attract them, the shrine of St. Martin of Tours became a major stopping-point on <a href="/wiki/Pilgrimage" title="Pilgrimage">pilgrimages</a>. In 1453 the remains of Saint Martin were transferred to a magnificent new reliquary donated by <a href="/wiki/Charles_VII_of_France" title="Charles VII of France">Charles VII of France</a> and <a href="/wiki/Agnes_Sorel" class="mw-redirect" title="Agnes Sorel">Agnes Sorel</a>.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:La_charit%C3%A9_de_saint_Martin.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/La_charit%C3%A9_de_saint_Martin.jpg/220px-La_charit%C3%A9_de_saint_Martin.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="297" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/La_charit%C3%A9_de_saint_Martin.jpg/330px-La_charit%C3%A9_de_saint_Martin.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/La_charit%C3%A9_de_saint_Martin.jpg/440px-La_charit%C3%A9_de_saint_Martin.jpg 2x" data-file-width="445" data-file-height="600" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:La_charit%C3%A9_de_saint_Martin.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Saint Martin Dividing his Cloak</i> by <a href="/wiki/Jean_Fouquet" title="Jean Fouquet">Jean Fouquet</a></div></div></div>
<p>During the <a href="/wiki/French_Wars_of_Religion" title="French Wars of Religion">French Wars of Religion</a>, the basilica was sacked by the Protestant <a href="/wiki/Huguenot" class="mw-redirect" title="Huguenot">Huguenots</a> in 1562. It was disestablished during the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFarmer199178–96_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarmer199178–96-18">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> It was deconsecrated, used as a stable, then utterly demolished. Its dressed stones were sold in 1802 after two streets were built across the site, to ensure the abbey would not be reconstructed.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legend_of_Saint_Martin_Dividing_his_Cloak">Legend of Saint Martin Dividing his Cloak</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Legend of Saint Martin Dividing his Cloak">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>While Martin was a soldier in the Roman army and stationed in Gaul (modern-day France), he experienced a vision, which became the most-repeated story about his life. One day as he was approaching the gates of the city of Amiens, he met a scantily clad beggar. He impulsively cut his military cloak in half to share with the man. That night, Martin dreamed of Jesus wearing the half-cloak he had given away. He heard Jesus say to the angels: "Martin, who is still but a <a href="/wiki/Catechumen" class="mw-redirect" title="Catechumen">catechumen</a>, clothed me with this robe." (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://web.archive.org/web/20060909225230/http://www.users.csbsju.edu/~eknuth/npnf2-11/sulpitiu/lifeofst.html#tp">Sulpicius, ch 2</a>). In another version, when Martin woke, he found his cloak restored to wholeness. The dream confirmed Martin in his piety, and he was baptised at the age of 18.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_14-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-14">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The part kept by himself became the famous relic preserved in the oratory of the <a href="/wiki/Merovingians" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingians">Merovingian</a> kings of the <a href="/wiki/Franks" title="Franks">Franks</a> at the <a href="/wiki/Marmoutier_Abbey_(Tours)" class="mw-redirect" title="Marmoutier Abbey (Tours)">Marmoutier Abbey</a> near <a href="/wiki/Tours" title="Tours">Tours</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEClugnet1910_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEClugnet1910-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> During the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>, the supposed relic of St. Martin's miraculous cloak (<i>cappa Sancti Martini</i>) was carried by the king even into battle, and used as a holy relic upon which oaths were sworn. The cloak is first attested to in the royal treasury in 679, when it was conserved at the <i><a href="/wiki/Palatium" class="mw-redirect" title="Palatium">palatium</a></i> of <a href="/wiki/Luzarches" title="Luzarches">Luzarches</a>, a royal villa that was later ceded to the monks of <a href="/wiki/Basilique_Saint-Denis" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilique Saint-Denis">Saint-Denis</a> by <a href="/wiki/Charlemagne" title="Charlemagne">Charlemagne</a>, in 798/99.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrunterch198890–93_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrunterch198890–93-19">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTETouati1998216note_100_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTETouati1998216note_100-20">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The priest who cared for the cloak in its reliquary was called a <i>cappellanu</i>, and ultimately all priests who served the military were called <i>cappellani</i>. The French translation is <i>chapelains</i>, from which the English word <i>chaplain</i> is derived.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-21">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A similar linguistic development took place for the term referring to the small temporary churches built for the relic. People called them a "capella", the word for a little cloak. Eventually, such small churches lost their association with the cloak, and all small churches began to be referred to as "chapels".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMacCulloch2009_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacCulloch2009-22">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:172px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Head_reliquary_Martin_Louvre_OA6459.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Head_reliquary_Martin_Louvre_OA6459.jpg/170px-Head_reliquary_Martin_Louvre_OA6459.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="240" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Head_reliquary_Martin_Louvre_OA6459.jpg/255px-Head_reliquary_Martin_Louvre_OA6459.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Head_reliquary_Martin_Louvre_OA6459.jpg/340px-Head_reliquary_Martin_Louvre_OA6459.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1840" data-file-height="2600" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Head_reliquary_Martin_Louvre_OA6459.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Reliquary for the head of St. Martin, silver and copper, part gilt, from the church at <a href="/wiki/Soudeilles" title="Soudeilles">Soudeilles</a>, late 14th century, Louvre</div></div></div>
<p>The veneration of Martin was widely popular in the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>, above all in the region between the <a href="/wiki/Loire" title="Loire">Loire</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Marne_(river)" title="Marne (river)">Marne</a>, where Le Roy Ladurie and Zysberg noted the densest accretion of <a href="/wiki/Toponym" class="mw-redirect" title="Toponym">place names</a> commemorating Martin.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELadurieZysberg19831331map_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELadurieZysberg19831331map-23">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Venantius_Fortunatus" title="Venantius Fortunatus">Venantius Fortunatus</a> had earlier declared, "Wherever Christ is known, Martin is honored."<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When Bishop <a href="/wiki/Saint_Perpetuus" title="Saint Perpetuus">Perpetuus</a> took office at Tours in 461, the little chapel over Martin's grave, built in the previous century by Martin's immediate successor, <a href="/wiki/Bricius_of_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Bricius of Tours">Bricius</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-aedificavit_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-aedificavit-25">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup> was no longer sufficient for the crowd of pilgrims it was already drawing. Perpetuus built a larger <a href="/wiki/Basilica" title="Basilica">basilica</a>, 38&#160;m (125&#160;ft) long and 18&#160;m (59&#160;ft) wide, with 120 columns.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGregory_of_Toursn.d.Book_2,_Ch_14_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGregory_of_Toursn.d.Book_2,_Ch_14-26">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Martin's body was taken from the simple chapel at his hermitage at <a href="/wiki/Candes-Saint-Martin" title="Candes-Saint-Martin">Candes-St-Martin</a> to Tours and his <a href="/wiki/Sarcophagus" title="Sarcophagus">sarcophagus</a> was reburied behind the high altar of the new basilica.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> A large block of marble above the tomb, the gift of bishop Euphronius of Autun (472–475), rendered it visible to the faithful gathered behind the high altar. Werner Jacobsen suggests it may also have been visible to pilgrims encamped in the <a href="/wiki/Atrium_(architecture)" title="Atrium (architecture)">atrium</a> of the basilica.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEJacobsen19971108-_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJacobsen19971108--28">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> Contrary to the usual arrangement, the atrium was situated behind the church, close to the tomb in the <a href="/wiki/Apse" title="Apse">apse</a>, which may have been visible through a <i>fenestrella</i> in the apse wall.
</p><p>St. Martin's popularity can be partially attributed to his adoption by successive royal houses of France. <a href="/wiki/Clovis_I" title="Clovis I">Clovis</a>, King of the <a href="/wiki/Salian_Franks" title="Salian Franks">Salian Franks</a>, one of many warring tribes in sixth-century France, promised his Christian wife <a href="/wiki/Clotilde" title="Clotilde">Clotilda</a> that he would be baptised if he was victorious over the <a href="/wiki/Alemanni" title="Alemanni">Alemanni</a>. He credited the intervention of St Martin with his success, and with several following triumphs, including the defeat of <a href="/wiki/Alaric_II" title="Alaric II">Alaric II</a>. The popular devotion to St Martin continued to be closely identified with the <a href="/wiki/Merovingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Merovingian">Merovingian</a> monarchy: in the early seventh century <a href="/wiki/Dagobert_I" title="Dagobert I">Dagobert I</a> commissioned the goldsmith <a href="/wiki/Saint_Eligius" title="Saint Eligius">Saint Eligius</a> to make a work in gold and gems for the tomb-shrine.<sup id="cite_ref-VitaEligii_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-VitaEligii-29">&#91;d&#93;</a></sup> The bishop <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Tours" title="Gregory of Tours">Gregory of Tours</a> wrote and distributed an influential <i>Life</i> filled with miraculous events of St. Martin's career. Martin's <i>cultus</i> survived the passage of power to the Merovingians' successors, the <a href="/wiki/Carolingian" class="mw-redirect" title="Carolingian">Carolingian</a> dynasty.
</p><p>Martin is honored in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">Church of England</a> and in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church</a> on <a href="/wiki/November_11" title="November 11">11 November</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Revival_of_the_popular_devotion_to_St._Martin_in_the_Third_Republic">Revival of the popular devotion to St. Martin in the Third Republic</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Revival of the popular devotion to St. Martin in the Third Republic">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>

<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Excavations_and_rediscovery_of_the_tomb">Excavations and rediscovery of the tomb</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Excavations and rediscovery of the tomb">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_St._Martin,_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica of St. Martin, Tours">Basilica of St. Martin, Tours</a></div>
<p>In 1860 excavations by <a href="/wiki/Leo_Dupont" title="Leo Dupont">Leo Dupont</a> (1797–1876) established the dimensions of the former abbey and recovered some fragments of architecture. The tomb of St. Martin was rediscovered on 14 December 1860, which aided in the nineteenth-century revival of the popular devotion to St. Martin.
</p><p>After the radical <a href="/wiki/Paris_Commune" title="Paris Commune">Paris Commune</a> of 1871, there was a resurgence of conservative Catholic piety, and the church decided to build a basilica to St. Martin. They selected <a href="/wiki/Victor_Laloux" title="Victor Laloux">Victor Laloux</a> as architect. He eschewed <a href="/wiki/Gothic_Revival" class="mw-redirect" title="Gothic Revival">Gothic</a> for a mix of Romanesque and Byzantine, sometimes defined as neo-Byzantine.<sup id="cite_ref-pilgrimage_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pilgrimage-32">&#91;e&#93;</a></sup> The new <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_St._Martin,_Tours" class="mw-redirect" title="Basilica of St. Martin, Tours">Basilique Saint-Martin</a> was erected on a portion of its former site, which was purchased from the owners. Started in 1886, the church was consecrated 4 July 1925.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Franco-Prussian_War">Franco-Prussian War</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Franco-Prussian War">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Martin's renewed popularity in France was related to his promotion as a <a href="/wiki/Military_saint" title="Military saint">military saint</a> during the <a href="/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War" title="Franco-Prussian War">Franco-Prussian War</a> of 1870–1871.  
During the military and political crisis of the Franco-Prussian war, <a href="/wiki/Napoleon_III" title="Napoleon III">Napoleon III</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Second_French_Empire" title="Second French Empire">Second Empire</a> collapsed. After the surrender of Napoleon to the Prussians after the <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Sedan" title="Battle of Sedan">Battle of Sedan</a> in September 1870, a provisional government of national defense was established, and France's <a href="/wiki/French_Third_Republic" title="French Third Republic">Third Republic</a> was proclaimed. Paris was evacuated due to the advancing enemy and for a brief time (September–December 1870), Tours became the effective capital of France.
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:292px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Tombeau_de_Saint-Martin_de_Tours.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Tombeau_de_Saint-Martin_de_Tours.jpg/290px-Tombeau_de_Saint-Martin_de_Tours.jpg" decoding="async" width="290" height="193" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Tombeau_de_Saint-Martin_de_Tours.jpg/435px-Tombeau_de_Saint-Martin_de_Tours.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Tombeau_de_Saint-Martin_de_Tours.jpg/580px-Tombeau_de_Saint-Martin_de_Tours.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4288" data-file-height="2848" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Tombeau_de_Saint-Martin_de_Tours.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Tomb of Saint Martin</div></div></div>
<p>St Martin was promoted by the clerical right as the protector of the nation against the German threat. Conservatives associated the dramatic collapse of Napoleon III's regime as a sign of divine retribution on the irreligious emperor.  Priests interpreted it as punishment for a nation led astray due to years of <a href="/wiki/Anti-clericalism" title="Anti-clericalism">anti-clericalism</a>.  They preached repentance and a return to religion for political stability.  The ruined towers of the old royal basilica of St. Martin at Tours came to symbolize the decline of traditional Catholic France.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997489–491_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997489–491-34">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>With the government's relocation to Tours during the <a href="/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War" title="Franco-Prussian War">Franco-Prussian War</a>, 1870, numerous pilgrims were attracted to St. Martin's tomb. It was covered by a temporary chapel built by  <a href="/wiki/Joseph-Hippolyte_Guibert" title="Joseph-Hippolyte Guibert">archbishop Guibert</a>.  The popular devotion to St. Martin was also associated with the nationalistic devotion to the <a href="/wiki/Sacred_Heart" title="Sacred Heart">Sacred Heart</a>.  The flag of Sacre-Coeur, borne by <a href="/wiki/Ultramontane" class="mw-redirect" title="Ultramontane">Ultramontane</a> Catholic <a href="/wiki/Pontifical_Zouaves" class="mw-redirect" title="Pontifical Zouaves">Pontifical Zouaves</a> who fought at Patay, had been placed overnight in St. Martin's tomb before being taken into battle on 9 October 1870.  The banner read "Heart of Jesus Save France" and on the reverse side <a href="/wiki/Carmelite" class="mw-redirect" title="Carmelite">Carmelite</a> nuns of Tours embroidered "Saint Martin Protect France".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997499_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997499-35">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>As the French army was victorious in Patay, many among the faithful took the victory to be the result of divine favor. Popular hymns of the 1870s developed the theme of national protection under the cover of Martin's cloak, the "first flag of France".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997489–491_34-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997489–491-34">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During the nineteenth-century Frenchmen, influenced by secularism, agnosticism, and <a href="/wiki/Anti-clericalism" title="Anti-clericalism">anti-clericalism</a>, deserted the church in great numbers. As Martin was a man's saint, the devotion to him was an exception to this trend.  For men serving in the military, Martin of Tours was presented by the Catholic Right as the masculine model of principled behavior. He was a brave fighter, knew his obligation to the poor, shared his goods, performed his required military service, followed legitimate orders, and respected secular authority.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997491–492_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997491–492-36">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Opposition_from_Anticlericals">Opposition from Anticlericals</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Opposition from Anticlericals">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>During the 1870s, the procession to St. Martin's tomb at Tours became a display of ecclesiastical and military cooperation. Army officers in full uniform acted as military escorts, symbolically protecting the clergy and clearing the path for them. Anti-clerics viewed the staging of public religious processions as a violation of civic space. In 1878, M. Rivière, the provisional mayor of Tours, with anticlerical support banned the November procession in honor of St. Martin. President <a href="/wiki/Patrice_de_Mac-Mahon" class="mw-redirect" title="Patrice de Mac-Mahon">Patrice de Mac-Mahon</a> was succeeded by the Republican <a href="/wiki/Jules_Gr%C3%A9vy" title="Jules Grévy">Jules Grévy</a>, who created a new national anticlerical offensive. Bishop <a href="/wiki/Louis-%C3%89douard-Fran%C3%A7ois-Desir%C3%A9_Pie" title="Louis-Édouard-François-Desiré Pie">Louis-Édouard-François-Desiré Pie</a> of Poitiers united conservatives and devised a massive demonstration for the November 1879 procession. Pie's ultimate hope was that St Martin would stop the “chariot” of modern society, and lead to the creation of a France where the religious and secular sectors merged.
</p><p>The struggle between the two men was reflective of that between conservatives and anti-clerics over the church's power in the army. From 1874, military chaplains were allowed in the army in times of peace, but anti-clerics viewed the chaplains as sinister monarchists and counter-revolutionaries. Conservatives responded by creating the short-lived Legion de <a href="/wiki/Saint_Maurice" title="Saint Maurice">Saint Maurice</a> in 1878 and the society, Notre Dame de Soldats, to provide unpaid voluntary chaplains with financial support. The legislature passed the anticlerical Duvaux Bill of 1880, which reduced the number of chaplains in the French army. Anticlerical legislators wanted commanders, not chaplains, to provide troops with moral support and to supervise their formation in the established faith of "patriotic Republicanism."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997495–496_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997495–496-37">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="St._Martin_as_a_French_Republican_patron">St. Martin as a French Republican patron</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: St. Martin as a French Republican patron">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>St. Martin has long been associated with France's royal heritage. Monsignor René François Renou (Archbishop of Tours, 1896–1913) worked to associate St. Martin as a specifically "republican" patron. Renou had served as a chaplain to the 88<sup>e</sup> Régiment des mobils d'Indre-et-Loire  during the Franco-Prussian war and was known as the "army bishop." Renou was a strong supporter of St. Martin and believed that the national destiny of France and all its victories were attributed to him. He linked the military to the cloak of St. Martin, which was the "first flag of France" to the French tricolor, "the symbol of the union of the old and new."  This flag symbolism connected the devotion to St. Martin with the Third Republic. But, the tensions of the <a href="/wiki/Dreyfus_Affair" class="mw-redirect" title="Dreyfus Affair">Dreyfus Affair</a> renewed anti-clericalism in France and drove a wedge between the Church and the Republic. By 1905, the influence of <a href="/wiki/Rene_Waldeck-Rousseau" class="mw-redirect" title="Rene Waldeck-Rousseau">Rene Waldeck-Rousseau</a> and <a href="/wiki/Emile_Combes" class="mw-redirect" title="Emile Combes">Emile Combes</a>, combined with deteriorating relations with the <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Vatican</a>, led to the separation of church and state.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997497–499_38-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997497–499-38">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>St. Martin's popularity was renewed during the First World War. Anticlericalism declined, and priests served in the French forces as chaplains. More than 5,000 of them died in the war. In 1916, Assumptionists organized a national pilgrimage to Tours that attracted people from all of France. The devotion to St. Martin was amplified in the dioceses of France, where special prayers were offered to the patron saint. When the armistice was signed on Saint Martin's Day, 11 November 1918, the French people saw it was a sign of his intercession in the affairs of France.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997499–501_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrennan1997499–501-39">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage">Patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: Patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<p>He is the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of beggars (because of his sharing his cloak), wool-weavers and tailors (also because of his cloak), he is also the patron saint of the US Army Quartermaster Corps even though he detested violence (also because of sharing his cloak), geese (some say because they gave his hiding place away when he tried to avoid being chosen as bishop, others because their migration coincides with his feast), vintners and innkeepers (because his feast falls just after the late grape harvest), and France. He was proclaimed patron of Italian volunteering by the Italian bishops in the spring of 2021.
</p><p>Beyond his patronage of the <a href="/wiki/French_Third_Republic" title="French Third Republic">French Third Republic</a>, Saint Martin more recently has also been  described in terms of "a spiritual bridge across Europe"  due to his "international" background, being a native of <a href="/wiki/Pannonia" title="Pannonia">Pannonia</a> who spent his adult life in <a href="/wiki/Roman_Gaul" title="Roman Gaul">Gaul</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELanzi2004104_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELanzi2004104-40">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconography">Iconography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=16" title="Edit section: Iconography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Martin is most generally portrayed on horseback dividing his cloak with the beggar. His emblem in English art is often that of a goose, whose annual migration is about late autumn.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Hammer_of_Martin_of_Tours">Hammer of Martin of Tours</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=17" title="Edit section: Hammer of Martin of Tours">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:MCC-41976_Hamer_van_St._Maarten_(1).tif" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/MCC-41976_Hamer_van_St._Maarten_%281%29.tif/lossy-page1-220px-MCC-41976_Hamer_van_St._Maarten_%281%29.tif.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="171" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/MCC-41976_Hamer_van_St._Maarten_%281%29.tif/lossy-page1-330px-MCC-41976_Hamer_van_St._Maarten_%281%29.tif.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/MCC-41976_Hamer_van_St._Maarten_%281%29.tif/lossy-page1-440px-MCC-41976_Hamer_van_St._Maarten_%281%29.tif.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3543" data-file-height="2746" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:MCC-41976_Hamer_van_St._Maarten_(1).tif" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Hammer of Martin of Tours, Catharijne Convent, Utrecht, the Netherlands</div></div></div>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Museum_Catharijneconvent" title="Museum Catharijneconvent">Museum Catharijneconvent</a> in Utrecht<sup id="cite_ref-cc_42-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cc-42">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> has a relic in its collection which is called "the hammer of St. Martin of Tours" (Latin: <i>maleus beati Martini</i>). It was made in the 13th or 14th century from a late <a href="/wiki/Bronze_Age" title="Bronze Age">Bronze Age</a> stone <a href="/wiki/Axe" title="Axe">axe</a> from c. 1,000 – 700 BC,<sup id="cite_ref-cc_42-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cc-42">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> though the dating is uncertain.<sup id="cite_ref-cc_42-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cc-42">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> The grip contains a Latin text saying "<i>Ydola vanurunt Martini cesa securi nemo deos credat qui sic fuerant ruicuri</i>" ("the pagan statues fall down, hit by St. Martin's axe. Let nobody believe that those are gods, who so easily fall down"). Legend says that the axe belonged to St. Martin, and was used to hit the devil and to destroy the heathen temples and statues.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Influence">Influence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=18" title="Edit section: Influence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:172px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Coat_of_Arms_of_Senica.svg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Coat_of_Arms_of_Senica.svg/170px-Coat_of_Arms_of_Senica.svg.png" decoding="async" width="170" height="197" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Coat_of_Arms_of_Senica.svg/255px-Coat_of_Arms_of_Senica.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Coat_of_Arms_of_Senica.svg/340px-Coat_of_Arms_of_Senica.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="500" data-file-height="580" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Coat_of_Arms_of_Senica.svg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Saint Martin as a heraldic symbol (the coat of arms of <a href="/wiki/Senica" title="Senica">Senica</a>, Slovakia)</div></div></div>
<p>By the early 9th century, respect for Saint Martin was well-established in Ireland. His monastery at Marmoûtiers became the training ground for many Celtic missions and missionaries. Some believe that <a href="/wiki/St._Patrick" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Patrick">St. Patrick</a> was his nephew and that Patrick was one of many Celtic notables who lived for a time at Marmoûtiers. <a href="/wiki/St._Ninian" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Ninian">St. Ninian</a> definitely studied at Marmoûtiers and was profoundly influenced by Martin, carrying a deep love and respect for his teacher and his methods back to Scotland. Ninian was in the process of building a church when news reached him of Martin's death. Ninian dedicated that church to Martin.
<a href="/wiki/The_Book_of_Armagh" class="mw-redirect" title="The Book of Armagh">The Book of Armagh</a> contains three distinct groups of material: (1) a complete text of the New Testament, (2) a dossier of materials on Saint Patrick, and (3) almost the complete body of writings on Saint Martin by Sulpicius Severus.<sup id="cite_ref-conleth_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-conleth-43">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Jonas_of_Bobbio" title="Jonas of Bobbio">Jonas of Bobbio</a>'s <i>Vita Columbani</i>, Jonas relates that <a href="/wiki/Saint_Columbanus" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Columbanus">Saint Columbanus</a>, while travelling, requested to be allowed to pray at the tomb of St Martin. The Irish palimpsest sacramentary from the mid-7th century contains the text of a mass for St Martin. In the <i>Life of Columba</i>, <a href="/wiki/Adamnan" class="mw-redirect" title="Adamnan">Adamnan</a> mentions in passing that St Martin was commemorated during Mass at <a href="/wiki/Iona" title="Iona">Iona</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-conleth_43-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-conleth-43">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In his <i>Ireland and Her Neighbours in the Seventh Century</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERichter1999225–230_44-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERichter1999225–230-44">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> Michael Richter attributes this to the mission of <a href="/wiki/Palladius_(bishop_of_Ireland)" title="Palladius (bishop of Ireland)">Palladius</a> seen within the wider context of the mission of <a href="/wiki/Germanus_of_Auxerre" title="Germanus of Auxerre">Germanus of Auxerre</a> to Britain around 429. Thus, this could be the context in which the Life of St Martin was brought from Gaul to Ireland at an early date, and could explain how Columbanus was familiar with it before he ever left Ireland.<sup id="cite_ref-conleth_43-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-conleth-43">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=19" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span id="Ligug.C3.A9_Abbey"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Ligugé_Abbey">Ligugé Abbey</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=20" title="Edit section: Ligugé Abbey">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Founded by Martin of Tours in 360, <a href="/wiki/Ligug%C3%A9_Abbey" title="Ligugé Abbey">Ligugé Abbey</a> is one of the earliest monastic foundations in France. The reputation of the founder attracted a large number of disciples to the new monastery; the disciples initially living in locaciacum or small huts, this name later evolved to Ligugé. Its reputation was soon eclipsed by Martin's later foundation at Marmoutier. As of 2013, the Benedictine community at Ligugé numbered twenty-five.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="European_folk_traditions">European folk traditions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=21" title="Edit section: European folk traditions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/St._Martin%27s_Day" title="St. Martin&#39;s Day">St. Martin's Day</a></div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:182px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Martini_Toren.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Martini_Toren.JPG/180px-Martini_Toren.JPG" decoding="async" width="180" height="334" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Martini_Toren.JPG/270px-Martini_Toren.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Martini_Toren.JPG/360px-Martini_Toren.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1214" data-file-height="2256" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Martini_Toren.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i><a href="/wiki/Martinitoren" title="Martinitoren">Martinitoren</a></i>, the 97-metre-high (318&#160;ft) Martini Tower in Groningen, The Netherlands</div></div></div>
<p>From the late 4th century to the late <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a>, much of <a href="/wiki/Western_Europe" title="Western Europe">Western Europe</a>, including <a href="/wiki/Great_Britain" title="Great Britain">Great Britain</a>, engaged in a period of <a href="/wiki/Fasting" title="Fasting">fasting</a> beginning on the day after <a href="/wiki/St._Martin%27s_Day" title="St. Martin&#39;s Day">St. Martin's Day</a>, November 11. This fast period lasted 40 days (not including Saturdays and Sundays), and was, therefore, called <i>Quadragesima Sancti Martini</i>, which means in <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> "the forty days of St. Martin". At St. Martin's eve and on the feast day, people ate and drank very heartily for a last time before they started to fast. This fasting time was later called "<a href="/wiki/Advent" title="Advent">Advent</a>" by the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Church</a> and was considered a time for spiritual preparation for Christmas.
</p><p>On St. Martin's Day, children in <a href="/wiki/Flanders" title="Flanders">Flanders</a>, the southern and northern parts of the <a href="/wiki/Netherlands" title="Netherlands">Netherlands</a>, and the Catholic areas of <a href="/wiki/Germany" title="Germany">Germany</a> and <a href="/wiki/Austria" title="Austria">Austria</a> still participate in <a href="/wiki/Paper_lantern" title="Paper lantern">paper lantern</a> processions. Often, a man dressed as St. Martin rides on a horse in front of the procession. The children sing songs about St. Martin and about their lanterns. The food traditionally eaten on the day is <a href="/wiki/Goose" title="Goose">goose</a>, a rich bird. According to legend, Martin was reluctant to become bishop, which is why he hid in a stable filled with geese. The noise made by the geese betrayed his location to the people who were looking for him.
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Saintmartinoftours.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/4/4d/Saintmartinoftours.jpg/220px-Saintmartinoftours.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/4/4d/Saintmartinoftours.jpg/330px-Saintmartinoftours.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/4/4d/Saintmartinoftours.jpg/440px-Saintmartinoftours.jpg 2x" data-file-width="640" data-file-height="480" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Saintmartinoftours.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Statue of Saint Martin in the grounds of Saint Martin de Tours Roman Catholic Church in <a href="/wiki/Saint_Martinville" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Martinville">Saint Martinville</a>, Louisiana</div></div></div>
<p>In the eastern part of the <a href="/wiki/Belgium" title="Belgium">Belgian</a> province of <a href="/wiki/East_Flanders" title="East Flanders">East Flanders</a> (Aalst) and the western part of <a href="/wiki/West_Flanders" title="West Flanders">West Flanders</a> (<a href="/wiki/Ypres" title="Ypres">Ypres</a>), traditionally children receive presents from St. Martin on November 11, instead of from <a href="/wiki/Saint_Nicholas" title="Saint Nicholas">Saint Nicholas</a> on December 6 or <a href="/wiki/Santa_Claus" title="Santa Claus">Santa Claus</a> on December 25. They also have lantern processions, for which children make lanterns out of <a href="/wiki/Mangelwurzel" title="Mangelwurzel">beets</a>. In recent years, the lantern processions have become widespread as a popular ritual, even in <a href="/wiki/Protestant" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant">Protestant</a> areas of Germany and the Netherlands. Most Protestant churches no longer officially recognize <a href="/wiki/Saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Saints">Saints</a>.
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Portugal" title="Portugal">Portugal</a>, where the saint's day is celebrated across the country, it is common for families and friends to gather around the fire in reunions called <i><a href="/wiki/Casta%C3%B1ada" title="Castañada">magustos</a></i>, where they typically eat roasted <a href="/wiki/Chestnuts" class="mw-redirect" title="Chestnuts">chestnuts</a> and drink <a href="/wiki/Wine" title="Wine">wine</a>, <i><a href="/wiki/Jeropiga" title="Jeropiga">jeropiga</a></i> (a drink made of <a href="/wiki/Grape_must" class="mw-redirect" title="Grape must">grape must</a> and <a href="/wiki/Aguardente" class="mw-redirect" title="Aguardente">aguardente</a>) and <i>aguapé</i> (a sort of weak and watered-down wine). According to the most widespread variation of the cloak story, Saint Martin cut off half of his cloak in order to offer it to a <a href="/wiki/Beggar" class="mw-redirect" title="Beggar">beggar</a> and along the way, he gave the remaining part to a second beggar. As he faced a long ride in a freezing weather, the dark clouds cleared away and the sun shone so intensely that the frost melted away. Such weather was rare for early November, so was credited to God's intervention. The phenomenon of a sunny break to the chilly weather on Saint Martin's Day (11 November) is called <i>Verão de São Martinho</i> (Saint Martin's Summer, <i>veranillo de san Martín</i> in Spanish) in honor of the cloak legend.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Rogale_%C5%9Bwi%C4%99tomarci%C5%84skie_RB1.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Rogale_%C5%9Bwi%C4%99tomarci%C5%84skie_RB1.JPG/220px-Rogale_%C5%9Bwi%C4%99tomarci%C5%84skie_RB1.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="147" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Rogale_%C5%9Bwi%C4%99tomarci%C5%84skie_RB1.JPG/330px-Rogale_%C5%9Bwi%C4%99tomarci%C5%84skie_RB1.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Rogale_%C5%9Bwi%C4%99tomarci%C5%84skie_RB1.JPG/440px-Rogale_%C5%9Bwi%C4%99tomarci%C5%84skie_RB1.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1889" data-file-height="1260" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Rogale_%C5%9Bwi%C4%99tomarci%C5%84skie_RB1.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Rogal świętomarciński</i>, baked for St. Martin's Day in <a href="/wiki/Pozna%C5%84" title="Poznań">Poznań</a></div></div></div>
<p>In <a href="/wiki/Malta" title="Malta">Malta</a> on the night of the eve of Saint Martin's day children leave an empty bag next to the bed. This bag is found full of fruit on the next day.
</p><p>Many churches are named after Saint Martin of Tours. <a href="/wiki/St_Martin-in-the-fields" class="mw-redirect" title="St Martin-in-the-fields">St Martin-in-the-fields</a>, at <a href="/wiki/Trafalgar_Square" title="Trafalgar Square">Trafalgar Square</a> in the centre of London, has a history appropriately associated with Martin's renunciation of war; <a href="/wiki/Dick_Sheppard_(priest)" title="Dick Sheppard (priest)">Dick Sheppard</a>, founder of the <a href="/wiki/Peace_Pledge_Union" title="Peace Pledge Union">Peace Pledge Union</a>, was Vicar 1914–26, and there is a memorial chapel for him, with a plaque for <a href="/wiki/Vera_Brittain" title="Vera Brittain">Vera Brittain</a>, also a noted <a href="/wiki/Anglican" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican">Anglican</a> <a href="/wiki/Pacifist" class="mw-redirect" title="Pacifist">pacifist</a>; the steps of the church are often used for peace vigils. <a href="/wiki/Saint_Martin%27s_Cathedral" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Martin&#39;s Cathedral">Saint Martin's Cathedral</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Ypres" title="Ypres">Ypres</a>, <a href="/wiki/Belgium" title="Belgium">Belgium</a>, is dedicated to him. St. Martin is the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Szombathely" title="Szombathely">Szombathely</a>, Hungary, with a church dedicated to him, and also the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Buenos_Aires" title="Buenos Aires">Buenos Aires</a>. In the <a href="/wiki/Netherlands" title="Netherlands">Netherlands</a>, he is the patron of the cathedral and city of <a href="/wiki/Utrecht_(city)" class="mw-redirect" title="Utrecht (city)">Utrecht</a>. He is the patron of the city of <a href="/wiki/Groningen_(city)" class="mw-redirect" title="Groningen (city)">Groningen</a>; its <a href="/wiki/Martini_tower" class="mw-redirect" title="Martini tower">Martini tower</a> and <a href="/wiki/Martinikerk_(Groningen)" title="Martinikerk (Groningen)">Martinikerk (Groningen)</a> (Martin's Church) were named for him.
</p><p>He is also the patron of the church and town of <a href="/wiki/Bocaue,_Bulacan" class="mw-redirect" title="Bocaue, Bulacan">Bocaue</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>St. Martin's Church in <a href="/wiki/Kaiserslautern,_Germany" class="mw-redirect" title="Kaiserslautern, Germany">Kaiserslautern, Germany</a> is a major city landmark. It is located in the heart of the city's downtown in St. Martin's Square, and is surrounded by a number of restaurants and shops. The church was originally built as a <a href="/wiki/Franciscan" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan">Franciscan</a> monastery in the 14th century and has a number of unique architectural features.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:282px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St-martin-of-tours-stained-glass.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/0/03/St-martin-of-tours-stained-glass.jpg/280px-St-martin-of-tours-stained-glass.jpg" decoding="async" width="280" height="187" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/0/03/St-martin-of-tours-stained-glass.jpg/420px-St-martin-of-tours-stained-glass.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/0/03/St-martin-of-tours-stained-glass.jpg/560px-St-martin-of-tours-stained-glass.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1536" data-file-height="1024" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St-martin-of-tours-stained-glass.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Stained_glass" title="Stained glass">Stained glass</a> in St. Martin of Tours Church in <a href="/wiki/Vegreville" title="Vegreville">Vegreville</a>, <a href="/wiki/Alberta" title="Alberta">Alberta</a>, Canada</div></div></div>
<p>St. Martin is the patron saint of the <a href="/wiki/Poland" title="Poland">Polish</a> towns of <a href="/wiki/Bydgoszcz" title="Bydgoszcz">Bydgoszcz</a> and <a href="/wiki/Opat%C3%B3w" title="Opatów">Opatów</a>. His day is celebrated with a procession and festivities in the city of <a href="/wiki/Pozna%C5%84" title="Poznań">Poznań</a>, where the  main street (<i><a href="/wiki/%C5%9Awi%C4%99ty_Marcin" title="Święty Marcin">Święty Marcin</a></i>) is named for him, after a 13th-century church in his honor. A special type of crescent cake (<i>rogal świętomarciński</i>) is baked for the occasion. As November 11 is also <a href="/wiki/Polish_Independence_Day" class="mw-redirect" title="Polish Independence Day">Polish Independence Day</a>, it is a public holiday.
</p><p>The Monastery of Saint Martin of Castañeda has been a national historic monument since 1931. It is located in Galende, Sanabria, province of Zamora, Spain. It now functions as an interpretation center.<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/Latin_America" title="Latin America">Latin America</a>, St. Martin has a strong popular following and is frequently referred to as <i><b>San Martín Caballero</b></i>, in reference to his common depiction on horseback. Mexican folklore believes him to be a particularly helpful saint toward business owners.
</p><p>The largest Anglican church in North America is St Martin's Episcopal in Houston, Texas. It was the home church for many years of President and Mrs. George H. W. Bush and still is for former Secretary of State and Treasury James Baker and his wife Susan.
</p><p><br />
<a href="/wiki/San_Mart%C3%ADn_de_Loba" title="San Martín de Loba">San Martín de Loba</a> is the name of a municipality in the <a href="/wiki/Bol%C3%ADvar_Department" title="Bolívar Department">Bolívar Department</a> of <a href="/wiki/Colombia" title="Colombia">Colombia</a>. Saint Martin, as San Martín de Loba, is the patron saint of Vasquez, a small village in Colombia.
</p>

<p>In <a href="/wiki/Finland" title="Finland">Finland</a>, the town and municipality <a href="/wiki/Marttila" title="Marttila">Marttila</a> (<i>S:t Mårtens</i> in Swedish) is named after St. Martin and depicts him on its coat of arms.
</p><p>Though no mention of St. Martin's connection with <a href="/wiki/Viticulture" title="Viticulture">viticulture</a> is made by <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Tours" title="Gregory of Tours">Gregory of Tours</a> or other early hagiographers, he is now credited with a prominent role in spreading wine-making throughout the <a href="/wiki/Touraine" title="Touraine">Touraine</a> region and the planting of many vines. The Greek myth that <a href="/wiki/Aristaeus" title="Aristaeus">Aristaeus</a> first discovered the concept of <a href="/wiki/Pruning" title="Pruning">pruning</a> the vines, after watching a goat eat some of the foliage, has been adopted for Martin.<sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> He is also credited with introducing the <a href="/wiki/Chenin_blanc" title="Chenin blanc">Chenin blanc</a> grape varietal, from which most of the white wine of western Touraine and <a href="/wiki/Anjou_(wine)" class="mw-redirect" title="Anjou (wine)">Anjou</a> is made.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Martin_Luther" title="Martin Luther">Martin Luther</a> was named after St. Martin, as he was baptised on November 11 (St. Martin's Day), 1483. Many older <a href="/wiki/Lutheran" class="mw-redirect" title="Lutheran">Lutheran</a> congregations are named after St. Martin, which is unusual (for Lutherans) because he is a saint who does not appear in the Bible. (Lutherans regularly name congregations after the evangelists and other saints who appear in the Bible but are hesitant to name congregations after post-Biblical saints.)
</p><p>Martin of Tours is the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the <a href="/wiki/United_States_Army_Quartermaster_Corps" title="United States Army Quartermaster Corps">U.S. Army Quartermaster Corps</a>, which has a <a href="/wiki/Military_decoration" class="mw-redirect" title="Military decoration">medal</a> in his name.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Church_Lads%27_and_Church_Girls%27_Brigade" title="Church Lads&#39; and Church Girls&#39; Brigade">Church Lads' and Church Girls' Brigade</a>, a 5–7 age group, was renamed 'Martins' in his honour in 1998.
</p><p>Many schools have St Martin as their Patron, one being <a href="/wiki/St._Martin%27s_School_(Rosettenville)" title="St. Martin&#39;s School (Rosettenville)">St. Martin's School (Rosettenville)</a> in <a href="/wiki/Johannesburg" title="Johannesburg">Johannesburg</a>.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="In_art_and_modern_film">In art and modern film</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Martin_of_Tours&amp;action=edit&amp;section=22" title="Edit section: In art and modern film">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The Dutch film <i><a href="/wiki/Flesh_and_Blood_(1985_film)" title="Flesh and Blood (1985 film)">Flesh and Blood</a></i> (1985) prominently features a statue of Saint Martin. A mercenary in Renaissance Italy, named Martin, finds a statue of Saint Martin cutting his cloak and takes it as a sign to desert and rogue around under the saint's protection.
