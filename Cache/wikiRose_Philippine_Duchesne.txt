					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">18th and 19th-century French Catholic religious sister and missionary in the United States</div>
<p class="mw-empty-elt">
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1066479718">.mw-parser-output .infobox-subbox{padding:0;border:none;margin:-3px;width:auto;min-width:100%;font-size:100%;clear:none;float:none;background-color:transparent}.mw-parser-output .infobox-3cols-child{margin:auto}.mw-parser-output .infobox .navbar{font-size:100%}body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-header,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-subheader,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-above,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-title,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-image,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-full-data,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-below{text-align:center}</style><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div class="honorific-prefix" style="display:inline;font-size: 77%; <p><b>Rose Philippine Duchesne</b>, RCSJ (August 29, 1769 – November 18, 1852),<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> was a French <a href="/wiki/Religious_Sister" class="mw-redirect" title="Religious Sister">religious sister</a> and educator whom <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> canonized in 1988.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> She is the only fully canonized female Roman Catholic saint to share a feast day with the <a href="/wiki/Dedication_of_Saints_Peter_and_Paul" title="Dedication of Saints Peter and Paul">Dedication of Saints Peter and Paul</a> on November 18th.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> A native of <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>, she immigrated as a missionary to America, and is recognized for her care and education of Indigenous American survivors of the <a href="/wiki/United_States" title="United States">United States</a> <a href="/wiki/Indian_removal" title="Indian removal">Indian removal</a> programs.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Along with the founder, <a href="/wiki/Madeleine-Sophie_Barat" class="mw-redirect" title="Madeleine-Sophie Barat">Madeleine-Sophie Barat</a>, she was an early member of the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_the_Sacred_Heart" title="Society of the Sacred Heart">Society of the Sacred Heart</a>, and established the <a href="/wiki/Religious_congregation" title="Religious congregation">congregation</a>'s first communities in the United States. She spent the last half of her life teaching and serving the people of the <a href="/wiki/Midwestern_United_States" title="Midwestern United States">Midwestern United States</a>, which was at that time considered the western frontier of the nation. 
</p><p>Duchesne was beatified on May 12, 1940, and canonized on July 3, 1988, by the Roman Catholic Church.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Rose Philippine Duchesne was born in <a href="/wiki/Grenoble" title="Grenoble">Grenoble</a>, then the capital of the ancient <a href="/wiki/Provinces_of_France" title="Provinces of France">Province</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Dauphin%C3%A9" title="Dauphiné">Dauphiné</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_France" title="Kingdom of France">Kingdom of France</a>, the second of seven daughters, along with one son. Her father, Pierre-François Duchesne (1748–1797), was a prominent lawyer during the <a href="/wiki/Day_of_the_Tiles" title="Day of the Tiles">Day of the Tiles</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-RSCJ_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RSCJ-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Her mother, Rose-Euphrosine Périer (1743–1814), was the sister of <a href="/wiki/Claude_Perier" title="Claude Perier">Claude Périer</a>, an industrialist who later helped finance the rise to power of <a href="/wiki/Napoleon" title="Napoleon">Napoleon</a>. Pierre-Francois and  Rose-Euphrosine shared her parents' home in Grenoble with her brother, Claude Perier, and his wife – the two young couples living on separate floors. Claude Périer's son, <a href="/wiki/Casimir_Pierre_P%C3%A9rier" title="Casimir Pierre Périer">Casimir</a>, later a <a href="/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_France" title="Prime Minister of France">Prime Minister of France</a>, was the grandfather of <a href="/wiki/Jean_Casimir-Perier" title="Jean Casimir-Perier">Jean Casimir-Perier</a>, a <a href="/wiki/President_of_France" title="President of France">President of France</a>. She was raised in an enormous family home across from the Parliament building or Palace of Justice in Grenoble.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastery_of_the_Visitation">Monastery of the Visitation</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Monastery of the Visitation">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>After surviving a bout of smallpox which left her slightly scarred, in 1781, Rose Duchesne and her cousin Josephine were sent to be educated in the <a href="/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_dauphinois" title="Musée dauphinois">Monastery of Sainte-Marie-d'en-Haut</a> (known for the social status of its members), located on a mountainside near Grenoble, by the community of <a href="/wiki/Order_of_the_Visitation_of_Holy_Mary" title="Order of the Visitation of Holy Mary">Visitandine nuns</a>. When she began to show a strong attraction to the monastic life, her father withdrew her from the monastery school the following year and had her tutored with her cousins in the family home. In 1788 she decided to enter the Visitation of Holy Mary religious order, despite her family's opposition. She convinced an aunt to accompany her on a visit to the monastery, where she immediately requested admission, leaving her aunt to return home without her and to tell her father what happened.<sup id="cite_ref-FM_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FM-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1792, however, revolutionaries shut down the monastery during the <a href="/wiki/French_Revolution" title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Reign_of_Terror" title="Reign of Terror">Reign of Terror</a> and dispersed the <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nuns</a>. Duchesne returned to her family where she lived at their country home, along with two aunts, who had been <a href="/wiki/Visitandines" class="mw-redirect" title="Visitandines">Visitandines</a> at <a href="/wiki/Romans-sur-Is%C3%A8re" title="Romans-sur-Isère">Romans-sur-Isère</a>. She attempted to continue living the Rule of Life of her Order while serving her family and those suffering from the Reign of Terror, including those imprisoned at the former monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-RSCJ_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RSCJ-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>With the Catholic Church again able to operate openly in France under <a href="/wiki/Napoleon" title="Napoleon">Napoleon</a>, in 1801, Duchesne attempted to re-establish the Visitation Monastery, acquiring the buildings from its new owner. The buildings were in shambles, having been used as military barracks and a prison. Though a few of the nuns and the mother superior did return temporarily, the nuns found that the austere living conditions were too much for them in their advanced years. Eventually, Duchesne, now the mother superior of the house, was left with only three companions.<sup id="cite_ref-FM_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FM-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Society_of_the_Sacred_Heart">Society of the Sacred Heart</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Society of the Sacred Heart">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>While the restored Visitandine community was floundering, in northern France, <a href="/wiki/Madeleine-Sophie_Barat" class="mw-redirect" title="Madeleine-Sophie Barat">Madeleine-Sophie Barat</a> founded the new <a href="/wiki/Society_of_the_Sacred_Heart" title="Society of the Sacred Heart">Society of the Sacred Heart</a>—whose members were long known as the "Madames of the Sacred Heart" from their use of that title, due to the hostility to religious communities which lingered in post-Revolutionary France. She wanted to establish a new foundation in Grenoble. Encouraged by her mentor, the Jesuit priest, Joseph Varin, to meet Duchesne in 1804, she traveled there. Duchesne accepted Barat's offer to merge the Visitation community into the Society of the Sacred Heart. The new congregation had a similar religious mission as that of the Visitandines, educating young women, but without being an <a href="/wiki/Enclosed_religious_order" class="mw-redirect" title="Enclosed religious order">enclosed religious order</a>. The two women became immediate and lifelong friends.<sup id="cite_ref-RSCJ_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RSCJ-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1815, after the end of the <a href="/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars" title="Napoleonic Wars">Napoleonic Wars</a>, Duchesne followed Barat's instructions and established a Convent of the Sacred Heart in <a href="/wiki/Paris" title="Paris">Paris</a>, where she both opened a school and became the <a href="/wiki/Master_of_novices" class="mw-redirect" title="Master of novices">Mistress of novices</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Missionary_in_America">Missionary in America</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Missionary in America">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>During her childhood, Duchesne had heard many stories in her <a href="/wiki/Parish_church" title="Parish church">parish church</a> from missionary priests of life in <a href="/wiki/Louisiana" title="Louisiana">Louisiana</a>, founded as a colony of <a href="/wiki/Louisiana_(New_France)" title="Louisiana (New France)">New France</a>, and had long felt a desire to serve the <a href="/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States" title="Native Americans in the United States">Native Americans</a> who lived there. In 1817, <a href="/wiki/Louis_William_Valentine_Dubourg" class="mw-redirect" title="Louis William Valentine Dubourg">William Dubourg</a>, <a href="/wiki/Catholic_bishop" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic bishop">Bishop</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Louisiana_and_the_Two_Floridas" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Louisiana and the Two Floridas">Diocese of Louisiana and the Two Floridas</a>, visited the convent in Paris. Bishop Dubourg was looking for a congregation of educators to help him evangelize the Indian and French children of his diocese.<sup id="cite_ref-va_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-va-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> After meeting him, Duchesne, who had never lost her desire to serve as a missionary, begged permission from Barat to serve in the bishop's diocese.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2019)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Missouri">Missouri</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Missouri">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>In 1818, with Barat's blessing, Duchesne headed out to the United States with four other Sisters of the Society. After ten weeks at sea, they arrived in <a href="/wiki/New_Orleans" title="New Orleans">New Orleans</a>. To their shock, however, the bishop had made no provisions for housing them. After they had rested briefly with the <a href="/wiki/Ursuline_nuns" class="mw-redirect" title="Ursuline nuns">Ursuline nuns</a>, they took advantage of the newly established <a href="/wiki/Steamboat" title="Steamboat">steamboat</a> service up the <a href="/wiki/Mississippi_River" title="Mississippi River">Mississippi River</a> to travel to <a href="/wiki/St._Louis,_Missouri" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Louis, Missouri">St. Louis</a>, and finally settled in <a href="/wiki/St._Charles,_Missouri" title="St. Charles, Missouri">St. Charles</a>, in what was then the <a href="/wiki/Missouri_Territory" title="Missouri Territory">Missouri Territory</a>, a journey of seven weeks. She was later to describe the location as <i>"the remotest village in the U.S."</i>;<sup id="cite_ref-AC_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AC-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> nonetheless the community established a new Sacred Heart convent in a log cabin there, known as the Duquette Mansion, the first house of the Society ever built outside France<sup id="cite_ref-va_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-va-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> the first in <a href="/wiki/St._Charles_County,_Missouri" title="St. Charles County, Missouri">St. Charles County, Missouri</a>, and the first free school west of the Mississippi. "Poverty and Christian heroism are here", she wrote of the site, "and trials are the riches of priests in this land".<sup id="cite_ref-CE_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CE-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> The following year Dubourg moved the community across the river to the town of <a href="/wiki/Florissant,_Missouri" title="Florissant, Missouri">Florissant, Missouri</a>, where they opened a school and a <a href="/wiki/Novitiate" title="Novitiate">novitiate</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The United States had <a href="/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase" title="Louisiana Purchase">purchased the area from France</a> only fifteen years earlier, and settlers, many poor but others with money and slaves, were streaming in from the East. Their new foundation faced many struggles, including lack of funds, inadequate housing, hunger, and freezing weather, and the Sisters struggled to learn English. By 1828, the Society's first five members in America had grown to six communities, operating several schools.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Other foundations in <a href="/wiki/Louisiana" title="Louisiana">Louisiana</a> followed: at <a href="/wiki/Grand_Coteau,_Louisiana" title="Grand Coteau, Louisiana">Grand Coteau, near Opelousas</a>, at <a href="/wiki/Natchitoches,_Louisiana" title="Natchitoches, Louisiana">Natchitoches</a>, at <a href="/wiki/Baton_Rouge,_Louisiana" title="Baton Rouge, Louisiana">Baton Rouge</a>, at <a href="/wiki/New_Orleans,_Louisiana" class="mw-redirect" title="New Orleans, Louisiana">New Orleans</a>, and at <a href="/wiki/Convent,_Louisiana" title="Convent, Louisiana">Convent, Louisiana</a>. In 1826 <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_XII" title="Pope Leo XII">Pope Leo XII</a>, through a <i><a href="/wiki/Decretum_laudis" title="Decretum laudis">decretum laudis</a></i>, formally approved the Society of the Sacred Heart, recognizing their work. The Jesuits acquired the Sisters' former school property in St. Charles in 1828, where they built a parish church, and asked the Sisters to return&#160;&#8211;&#32;to that same log cabin where they had lived because it was still the biggest house in town&#160;&#8211;&#32;and conduct the parish school.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Kansas">Kansas</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Kansas">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>In 1841 the Jesuits asked the Sisters to join them in a new mission with the <a href="/wiki/Potawatomi" title="Potawatomi">Potawatomi</a> tribe in eastern Kansas, along <a href="/wiki/Sugar_Creek_(Missouri_River)" class="mw-redirect" title="Sugar Creek (Missouri River)">Sugar Creek</a>, where <a href="/wiki/Christian_Hoecken" title="Christian Hoecken">Christian Hoecken</a> was taking charge. At age seventy-one, she was not among those initially selected for the trip. <a href="/wiki/Peter_Verhaegen" title="Peter Verhaegen">Peter Verhaegen</a> insisted, "She may not be able to do much work, but she will assure success to the mission by praying for us." Since Duchesne was unable to master their language, she could not teach, so she would spend long periods in prayer. The children named her <i>Quahkahkanumad</i>, which translates as <i>Woman Who Prays Always</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-Callan_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Callan-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> At the request of the Jesuit priest <a href="/wiki/Pierre-Jean_De_Smet" title="Pierre-Jean De Smet">Pierre-Jean De Smet</a>, she continued her work, and helped Native Americans as far as the Rocky Mountains.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Slavery">Slavery</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Slavery">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Like many of this period in American history (see <a href="/wiki/List_of_slave_owners" title="List of slave owners">List of slave owners</a>) some of the Sisters in America bought and sold enslaved people, which surprised Duchesne when she arrived, as by that time <a href="/wiki/Slavery_in_France" title="Slavery in France">slavery in France</a> had been outlawed.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> According to Rachel L. Swarns, writing in the <a href="/wiki/New_York_Times" class="mw-redirect" title="New York Times">New York Times</a> “In spite of my repugnance for having Negro slaves, we may be obliged to purchase some,” Rose Philippine Duchesne, who established the Society of the Sacred Heart in the United States, wrote in 1822.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> A year later, the Sacred Heart sisters in Grand Coteau purchased their first person, an enslaved man named Frank Hawkins, for $550."<sup id="cite_ref-:0_21-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> According to the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_the_Sacred_Heart" title="Society of the Sacred Heart">Society of the Sacred Heart</a>, Xavier Murphy also purchased the rest of Mr. Hawkins' family members from their former owners for purposes of family reunification.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Upon discovering Duchesne's links to slavery, Digby Stuart College, one of the constituent Colleges of the University of Roehampton which was founded by the Sisters of the Sacred Heart, renamed the Duchesne Building after Nelson Mandela.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In 1842, after a year among the Potawatomi, it was clear that Duchesne's health could not sustain the regime of village life, and she returned to St Charles.<sup id="cite_ref-va_12-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-va-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> She spent the last decade of her life living there in a tiny room under a stairway near the chapel in solitude and prayer. Toward the end of her life, she was alone, going blind, feeble, and yearned for letters from Barat. She wrote, "I live now in solitude and am able to use my time reflecting on the past and preparing for death. I cannot put away the thought of the Indians and in my ambition I fly to the Rockies".<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>  She died on November 18, 1852, aged 83.<sup id="cite_ref-Callan_18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Callan-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Rose_Philippine_Duchesne&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Initially buried in the convent cemetery, Duchesne's remains were exhumed three years later and intact. She was then reburied in a crypt within a small shrine on the convent grounds. The cause for Duchesne's <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a> was introduced in 1895. She was declared <a href="/wiki/Venerable" class="mw-redirect" title="Venerable">Venerable</a> in 1909 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_X" title="Pope Pius X">Pope Pius X</a> and was <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII" title="Pope Pius XII">Pope Pius XII</a> in 1940. The <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Holy See</a> ordered in 1951 that she be buried more suitably. Construction was begun on a larger shrine, and her remains were moved there on June 13 of the following year.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> canonized her on July 3, 1988.<sup id="cite_ref-Willard_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Willard-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
