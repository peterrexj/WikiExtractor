<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">For other uses, see <a href="/wiki/Caedmon_(disambiguation)" class="mw-disambig" title="Caedmon (disambiguation)">Caedmon (disambiguation)</a>.</div>
<p class="mw-empty-elt">

</p>

<p><b>Cædmon</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;k&#39; in &#39;kind&#39;">k</span><span title="/æ/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;bad&#39;">æ</span><span title="&#39;d&#39; in &#39;dye&#39;">d</span><span title="&#39;m&#39; in &#39;my&#39;">m</span><span title="/ən/: &#39;on&#39; in &#39;button&#39;">ən</span></span>,<span class="wrap"> </span><span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="&#39;k&#39; in &#39;kind&#39;">k</span><span title="/æ/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;bad&#39;">æ</span><span title="&#39;d&#39; in &#39;dye&#39;">d</span><span title="&#39;m&#39; in &#39;my&#39;">m</span><span title="/ɒ/: &#39;o&#39; in &#39;body&#39;">ɒ</span><span title="&#39;n&#39; in &#39;nigh&#39;">n</span></span>/</a></span></span>; fl. c. 657–684) is the earliest English poet whose name is known.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> A <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbrian</a> cowherd who cared for the animals at the <a href="/wiki/Double_monastery" title="Double monastery">double monastery</a> of Streonæshalch (now known as <a href="/wiki/Whitby_Abbey" title="Whitby Abbey">Whitby Abbey</a>) during the abbacy of <a href="/wiki/Hilda_of_Whitby" title="Hilda of Whitby">St. Hilda</a>, he was originally ignorant of "the art of song" but learned to compose one night in the course of a dream, according to the 8th-century historian <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>. He later became a zealous monk and an accomplished and inspirational Christian poet.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cædmon is one of twelve <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_literature" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon literature">Anglo-Saxon poets</a> identified in <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">mediaeval</a> sources, and one of only three of these for whom both roughly contemporary biographical information and examples of literary output have survived.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His story is related in the <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_ecclesiastica_gentis_Anglorum" class="mw-redirect" title="Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum">Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum</a></i> ("Ecclesiastical History of the English People") by Bede who wrote, "[t]here was in the Monastery of this Abbess a certain brother particularly remarkable for the Grace of God, who was wont to make religious verses, so that whatever was interpreted to him out of <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">scripture</a>, he soon after put the same into poetical expressions of much sweetness and humility in <a href="/wiki/Old_English" title="Old English">Old English</a>, which was his native language. By his verse the minds of many were often excited to despise the world, and to aspire to heaven."
</p><p>Cædmon's only known surviving work is <i><a href="/wiki/C%C3%A6dmon%27s_Hymn" title="Cædmon&#39;s Hymn">Cædmon's Hymn</a></i>, a nine-line <a href="/wiki/Alliterative_verse" title="Alliterative verse">alliterative</a> <a href="/wiki/Vernacular" title="Vernacular">vernacular</a> praise poem in honour of God. The poem is one of the earliest attested examples of <a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a> and is, with the <a href="/wiki/Runic" class="mw-redirect" title="Runic">runic</a> <a href="/wiki/Ruthwell_Cross" title="Ruthwell Cross">Ruthwell Cross</a> and <a href="/wiki/Franks_Casket" title="Franks Casket">Franks Casket</a> inscriptions, one of three candidates for the earliest attested example of <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_literature" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon literature">Old English poetry</a>. It is also one of the earliest recorded examples of sustained poetry in a Germanic language. In 1898, Cædmon's Cross was erected in his honour in the graveyard of <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Mary,_Whitby" class="mw-redirect" title="Church of Saint Mary, Whitby">St Mary's Church</a> in <a href="/wiki/Whitby" title="Whitby">Whitby</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Andrew,_Bethune_Road_-_Stained_glass_window_-_geograph.org.uk_-_4398049.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/St_Andrew%2C_Bethune_Road_-_Stained_glass_window_-_geograph.org.uk_-_4398049.jpg/220px-St_Andrew%2C_Bethune_Road_-_Stained_glass_window_-_geograph.org.uk_-_4398049.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="439" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/St_Andrew%2C_Bethune_Road_-_Stained_glass_window_-_geograph.org.uk_-_4398049.jpg/330px-St_Andrew%2C_Bethune_Road_-_Stained_glass_window_-_geograph.org.uk_-_4398049.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/St_Andrew%2C_Bethune_Road_-_Stained_glass_window_-_geograph.org.uk_-_4398049.jpg/440px-St_Andrew%2C_Bethune_Road_-_Stained_glass_window_-_geograph.org.uk_-_4398049.jpg 2x" data-file-width="501" data-file-height="1000" /></a><figcaption>Caedmon and Bede depicted in <a href="/wiki/Stained_glass" title="Stained glass">stained glass</a> at <a href="/wiki/St_Andrew,_Stoke_Newington" title="St Andrew, Stoke Newington">St Andrew, Stoke Newington</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<h3><span id="Bede.27s_account"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Bede's_account">Bede's account</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Bede&#039;s account">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The sole source of original information about Cædmon's life and work is <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>'s <i>Historia ecclesiastica</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> According to Bede, Cædmon was a <a href="/wiki/Lay_brother" title="Lay brother">lay brother</a> who cared for the animals at the monastery Streonæshalch (now known as <a href="/wiki/Whitby_Abbey" title="Whitby Abbey">Whitby Abbey</a>). One evening, while the monks were feasting, singing, and playing a harp, Cædmon left early to sleep with the animals because he knew no songs. The impression clearly given by St. Bede is that he lacked the knowledge of how to compose the lyrics to songs. While asleep, he had a dream in which "someone" (<i>quidam</i>) approached him and asked him to sing <i>principium creaturarum</i>, "the beginning of created things." After first refusing to sing, Cædmon subsequently produced a short <a href="/wiki/Eulogy" title="Eulogy">eulogistic</a> poem praising God, the Creator of heaven and earth.
</p><p>Upon awakening the next morning, Caedmon remembered everything he had sung and added additional lines to his poem. He told his foreman about his dream and gift and was taken immediately to see the <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a>, believed to be <a href="/wiki/Hilda_of_Whitby" title="Hilda of Whitby">St Hilda of Whitby</a>. The abbess and her counsellors asked Cædmon about his vision and, satisfied that it was a gift from God, gave him a new commission, this time for a poem based on "a passage of sacred history or doctrine", by way of a test. 
</p><p>When Cædmon returned the next morning with the requested poem, he was invited to take <a href="/wiki/Monastic_vows" class="mw-redirect" title="Monastic vows">monastic vows</a>. The abbess ordered her scholars to teach Cædmon sacred history and doctrine, which after a night of thought, Bede records, Cædmon would turn into the most beautiful verse. According to Bede, Cædmon was responsible for a large number of splendid vernacular poetic texts on a variety of Christian topics.
</p><p>After a long and zealously pious life, Cædmon died like a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>: receiving a <a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/premonition" class="extiw" title="wikt:premonition">premonition</a> of death, he asked to be moved to the abbey's hospice for the terminally ill where, having gathered his friends around him, he died after receiving the Holy Eucharist, just before <a href="/wiki/Nocturns" title="Nocturns">nocturns</a>. Although he is often listed as a saint, this is not confirmed by Bede and it has been argued that such assertions are incorrect.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The details of Bede's story, and in particular of the miraculous nature of Cædmon's poetic inspiration, are not generally accepted by scholars as being entirely accurate, but there seems no good reason to doubt the existence of a poet named Cædmon. Bede's narrative has to be read in the context of the Christian belief in miracles, and it shows at the very least that Bede, an educated and intelligent man, believed Cædmon to be an important figure in the history of English intellectual and religious life.<sup id="cite_ref-odonnell2005_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-odonnell2005-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Dates">Dates</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Dates">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Bede gives no specific dates in his story. Cædmon is said to have taken <a href="/wiki/Holy_Orders" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Orders">holy orders</a> at an advanced age and it is implied that he lived at Streonæshalch at least in part during Hilda's abbacy (657&#8211;680). Book IV Chapter 25 of the <i>Historia ecclesiastica</i> appears to suggest that Cædmon's death occurred at about the same time as the fire at <a href="/wiki/Coldingham_Priory" title="Coldingham Priory">Coldingham Abbey</a>, an event dated in the E text of the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> to 679, but after 681 by Bede.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>  
</p><p>The reference to <i>his temporibus</i> "at this time" in the opening lines of Chapter 25 may refer more generally to Cædmon's career as a poet. However, the next datable event in the <i>Historia ecclesiastica</i> is King <a href="/wiki/Ecgfrith_of_Northumbria" title="Ecgfrith of Northumbria">Ecgfrith</a>'s raid on <a href="/wiki/Ireland" title="Ireland">Ireland</a> in 684 (Book IV, Chapter 26). Taken together, this evidence suggests an active period beginning between 657 and 680 and ending between 679 and 684.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Modern_discoveries">Modern discoveries</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Modern discoveries">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The only biographical or historical information that modern scholarship has been able to add to Bede's account concerns the Brittonic origins of the poet's name. Although Bede specifically notes that English was Cædmon's "own" language, the poet's name is of <a href="/wiki/Celt" class="mw-redirect" title="Celt">Celtic</a> origin: from <a href="/wiki/Old_Welsh" title="Old Welsh">Proto-Welsh</a> &#42;<i>Cadṽan</i> (from <a href="/wiki/British_language_(Celtic)" class="mw-redirect" title="British language (Celtic)">Brythonic</a> &#42;<i>Catumandos</i>).<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>  Several scholars have suggested that Cædmon himself may have been bilingual on the basis of this etymology, Hilda's close contact with Celtic political and religious hierarchies, and some (not very close) analogues to the <i>Hymn</i> in <a href="/wiki/Old_Irish" title="Old Irish">Old Irish</a> poetry.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Other scholars have noticed a possible <a href="/wiki/Onomastics" title="Onomastics">onomastic</a> allusion to '<a href="/wiki/Adam_Kadmon" title="Adam Kadmon">Adam Kadmon</a>' in the poet's name, perhaps suggesting that the entire story is allegorical.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_medieval_sources">Other medieval sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Other medieval sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Whitby_Abbey_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_16785.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4b/Whitby_Abbey_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_16785.jpg/220px-Whitby_Abbey_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_16785.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="160" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4b/Whitby_Abbey_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_16785.jpg/330px-Whitby_Abbey_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_16785.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4b/Whitby_Abbey_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_16785.jpg/440px-Whitby_Abbey_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_16785.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1520" data-file-height="1104" /></a><figcaption>Ruins of <a href="/wiki/Whitby_Abbey" title="Whitby Abbey">Whitby Abbey</a> in <a href="/wiki/North_Yorkshire" title="North Yorkshire">North Yorkshire</a>, England&#8212; founded in 657 by <a href="/wiki/St._Hilda" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Hilda">St. Hilda</a>, the original abbey fell to a <a href="/wiki/Viking" class="mw-redirect" title="Viking">Viking</a> attack in 867 and was abandoned. It was re-established in 1078 and flourished until 1540 when it was destroyed by <a href="/wiki/Henry_VIII_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Henry VIII of England">Henry VIII</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<p>No other independent accounts of Cædmon's life and work are known to exist. The only other reference to Cædmon in English sources before the 12th century is found in the 10th-century Old English translation of Bede's Latin <i>Historia</i>. Otherwise, no mention of Cædmon is found in the corpus of surviving Old English. The Old English translation of the <i>Historia ecclesiastica</i> does contain several minor details not found in Bede's Latin original account.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Of these, the most significant is that Cædmon felt "shame" for his inability to sing vernacular songs before his vision, and the suggestion that Hilda's scribes copied down his verse <i><span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">æt muðe</i></span></i> "from his mouth".<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>  These differences are in keeping with the Old English translator's practice in reworking Bede's Latin original,<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> however, and need not, as Wrenn argues, suggest the existence of an independent English tradition of the Cædmon story.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Heliand"><i>Heliand</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Heliand">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>A second, possibly pre-12th-century allusion to the Cædmon story is found in two Latin texts associated with the <a href="/wiki/Old_Saxon" title="Old Saxon">Old Saxon</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Heliand" title="Heliand">Heliand</a></i> poem. These texts, the <i>Praefatio</i> (Preface) and <i>Versus de Poeta</i> (Lines about the poet), explain the origins of an Old Saxon biblical translation (for which the <i>Heliand</i> is the only known candidate)<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> in language strongly reminiscent of, and indeed at times identical to, Bede's account of Cædmon's career.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>  According to the prose <i>Praefatio</i>, the Old Saxon poem was composed by a renowned vernacular poet at the command of the emperor <a href="/wiki/Louis_the_Pious" title="Louis the Pious">Louis the Pious</a>. The text then adds that this poet had known nothing of vernacular composition until he was ordered to translate the precepts of sacred law into vernacular song in a dream.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Versus de Poeta</i> contain an expanded account of the dream itself, adding that the poet had been a herdsman before his inspiration and that the inspiration itself had come through the medium of a heavenly voice when he fell asleep after pasturing his cattle. While our knowledge of these texts is based entirely on a 16th-century edition by <a href="/wiki/Matthias_Flacius" title="Matthias Flacius">Flacius Illyricus</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> both are usually assumed on semantic and grammatical grounds to be of medieval composition.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>  This apparent debt to the Cædmon story agrees with semantic evidence attested to by Green demonstrating the influence of Old English biblical poetry and terminology on early continental Germanic literatures.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Sources_and_analogues">Sources and analogues</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Sources and analogues">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In contrast to his usual practice elsewhere in the <i>Historia ecclesiastica</i>, Bede provides no information about his sources for the Cædmon story. Since a similar paucity of sources is also characteristic of other stories from Whitby Abbey in his work, this may indicate that his knowledge of Cædmon's life was based on tradition current at his home monastery in (relatively) nearby <a href="/wiki/Wearmouth-Jarrow" class="mw-redirect" title="Wearmouth-Jarrow">Wearmouth-Jarrow</a>.
</p><p>Perhaps as a result of this lack of documentation, scholars have devoted considerable attention since the 1830s to tracking down possible sources or analogues to Bede's account. These parallels have been drawn from all around the world, including <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">biblical</a> and <a href="/wiki/Classical_literature" class="mw-redirect" title="Classical literature">classical literature</a>, stories told by the aboriginal peoples of <a href="/wiki/Indigenous_Australians" title="Indigenous Australians">Australia</a>, <a href="/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas" title="Indigenous peoples of the Americas">North America</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Fijians" title="Fijians">Fiji Islands</a>, mission-age accounts of the conversion of the <a href="/wiki/Xhosa_people" title="Xhosa people">Xhosa</a> in Southern <a href="/wiki/Africa" title="Africa">Africa</a>, the lives of English <a href="/wiki/Romantic_poetry" title="Romantic poetry">romantic poets</a>, and various elements of <a href="/wiki/Hindu_scripture" class="mw-redirect" title="Hindu scripture">Hindu</a> and <a href="/wiki/Qur%27an" class="mw-redirect" title="Qur&#39;an">Muslim scripture</a> and tradition.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Although the search was begun by scholars such as <a href="/wiki/Sir_Francis_Palgrave" class="mw-redirect" title="Sir Francis Palgrave">Sir Francis Palgrave</a>, who hoped either to find Bede's source for the Cædmon story or to demonstrate that its details were so commonplace as to hardly merit consideration as legitimate historiography,<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> subsequent research has instead ended up demonstrating the uniqueness of Bede's version: as Lester shows, no "analogue" to the Cædmon story found before 1974 mirrors Bede's chapter in more than about half its main properties;<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> the same observation can be extended to cover all analogues since identified.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Seerah_of_Muhammad"><i><a href="/wiki/Seerah" class="mw-redirect" title="Seerah">Seerah</a> of <a href="/wiki/Muhammad" title="Muhammad">Muhammad</a></i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Seerah of Muhammad">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The strong affinities between Cædmon's vision and that of the <a href="/wiki/Prophet_Muhammad" class="mw-redirect" title="Prophet Muhammad">Prophet Muhammad</a> have been widely remarked upon. While meditating in a cave, Muhammad was visited by the angel Gabriel, who commanded him to read, just as Cædmon had a vision of an otherworldly visitor as he slept in a cowshed. Muhammad was also illiterate, like Cædmon. When the visitor asks them both to "sing" in Cædmon's case and "read" in Muhammad's case, both refuse to, saying they cannot. Then miraculously both recite divinely-inspired poetry, in Muhammad's case the first verses of the Qur'an. In 1983, <a href="/wiki/Klaus_von_See" title="Klaus von See">Klaus von See</a>, the scholar of Scandinavian and German literature, first put forward the theory that Bede's story of Cædmon had a direct relationship with <a href="/wiki/Ibn_Ishaq" title="Ibn Ishaq">ibn Ishaq</a>'s account of the revelation of the Qur'an to Muhammad, though he was not the first to note the remarkable parallels. <a href="/wiki/Gregor_Schoeler" title="Gregor Schoeler">Gregor Schoeler</a> also provided a definitive account of the evolution of the story of Muhammad's call to prophecy into Bede's narrative.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Work">Work</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Work">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="General_corpus">General corpus</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: General corpus">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Bede's account indicates that Cædmon was responsible for the composition of a large <a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/oeuvre" class="extiw" title="wikt:oeuvre">oeuvre</a> of vernacular religious poetry. In contrast to Saints <a href="/wiki/Aldhelm" title="Aldhelm">Aldhelm</a> and <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">Dunstan</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> Cædmon's poetry is said to have been exclusively religious. Bede reports that Cædmon "could never compose any foolish or trivial poem, but only those which were concerned with devotion", and his list of Cædmon's output includes work on religious subjects only: accounts of creation, translations from the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old</a> and <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testaments</a>, and songs about the "terrors of future judgment, horrors of hell, ... joys of the heavenly kingdom, ... and divine mercies and judgments." 
</p><p>Of this corpus, only his first poem survives. While vernacular poems matching Bede's description of several of Cædmon's later works are found in <a href="/wiki/Caedmon_manuscript" class="mw-redirect" title="Caedmon manuscript">London, British Library, Junius 11</a>, traditionally referred to as the "Junius" or "Cædmon" manuscript, the older traditional attribution of these texts to Cædmon or Cædmon's influence cannot stand. The poems show significant stylistic differences both internally and with Cædmon's original <i>Hymn</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> and there is nothing about their order or content to suggest that they could not have been composed and anthologised without any influence from Bede's discussion of Cædmon's oeuvre. 
</p><p>The first three Junius poems are in their biblical order and, while <i><a href="/wiki/Christ_and_Satan" title="Christ and Satan">Christ and Satan</a></i> could be understood as partially fitting Bede's description of Cædmon's work on future judgment, pains of hell and joys of the heavenly kingdom,<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> the match is not exact enough to preclude independent composition. As Fritz and Day have shown, Bede's list itself may owe less to direct knowledge of Cædmon's actual output than to traditional ideas about the subjects fit for Christian poetry<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> or the order of the <a href="/wiki/Catechism" title="Catechism">catechism</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>  Similar influences may, of course, also have affected the makeup of the Junius volume.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="C.C3.A6dmon.27s_Hymn"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Cædmon's_Hymn"><i>Cædmon's Hymn</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Cædmon&#039;s Hymn">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/C%C3%A6dmon%27s_Hymn" title="Cædmon&#39;s Hymn">Cædmon's Hymn</a></div>
<figure typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Caedmon%27s_Hymn_Moore_mine01.gif" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Caedmon%27s_Hymn_Moore_mine01.gif/500px-Caedmon%27s_Hymn_Moore_mine01.gif" decoding="async" width="500" height="67" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Caedmon%27s_Hymn_Moore_mine01.gif/750px-Caedmon%27s_Hymn_Moore_mine01.gif 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8f/Caedmon%27s_Hymn_Moore_mine01.gif 2x" data-file-width="878" data-file-height="117" /></a><figcaption>One of two candidates for the earliest surviving copy of <i>Cædmon's Hymn</i> is found in "The Moore Bede" (ca. 737) which is held by the <a href="/wiki/Cambridge_University_Library" title="Cambridge University Library">Cambridge University Library</a> (Kk. 5. 16, often referred to as <b>M</b>). The other candidate is St. Petersburg, National Library of Russia, lat. Q. v. I. 18 (P)</figcaption></figure>
<p>The only known survivor from Cædmon's oeuvre is his <i>Hymn</i> (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DAZyc8M5Q4I">audio version</a><sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>). The poem is known from 21 <a href="/wiki/Manuscript" title="Manuscript">manuscript</a> copies,<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> making it the best-attested Old English poem after <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede's <i>Death Song</i></a> (with 35 <a href="/wiki/Textual_criticism" title="Textual criticism">witnesses</a>) and the best attested in the poetic corpus in manuscripts copied or owned in the British Isles during the Anglo-Saxon period.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Hymn</i> also has by far the most complicated known textual history of any surviving Old English poem.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It is found in two dialects and five distinct <a href="/wiki/Recension" title="Recension">recensions</a> (Northumbrian <i><span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">aelda</i></span></i>, Northumbrian <i><span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">eordu</i></span></i>, West-Saxon <i><span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">eorðan</i></span></i>, West-Saxon <i><span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">ylda</i></span></i>, and West-Saxon <i><span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">eorðe</i></span></i>), all but one of which are known from three or more witnesses.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> It is one of the earliest attested examples of written Old English and one of the earliest recorded examples of sustained poetry in a <a href="/wiki/Germanic_languages" title="Germanic languages">Germanic language</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> Together with the runic <a href="/wiki/Ruthwell_Cross" title="Ruthwell Cross">Ruthwell Cross</a> and <a href="/wiki/Franks_Casket" title="Franks Casket">Franks Casket</a> inscriptions, <i>Cædmon's Hymn</i> is one of three candidates for the earliest attested example of <a href="/wiki/Old_English_literature" title="Old English literature">Old English poetry</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There is continuing critical debate about the status of the poem as it is now available to us. While some scholars accept the texts of the Hymn as more or less accurate transmissions of Cædmon's original, others argue that they originated as a back-translation from Bede's Latin, and that there is no surviving witness to the original text.<sup id="cite_ref-odonnell2005_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-odonnell2005-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#What_information_to_include" title="Wikipedia:Citing sources"><span title="A complete citation is needed. (July 2020)">full citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Manuscript_evidence">Manuscript evidence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Manuscript evidence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>All copies of <i>Hymn</i> are found in manuscripts of the <i>Historia ecclesiastica</i> or its translation, where they serve as either a <a href="/wiki/Gloss_(annotation)" title="Gloss (annotation)">gloss</a> to Bede's Latin translation of the Old English poem, or, in the case of the Old English version, a replacement for Bede's translation in the main text of the History. Despite this close connection with Bede's work, the <i>Hymn</i> does not appear to have been transmitted with the <i>Historia ecclesiastica</i> regularly until relatively late in its textual history. Scribes other than those responsible for the main text often copy the vernacular text of the <i>Hymn</i> in manuscripts of the Latin Historia. In three cases, <i>Oxford, Bodleian Library, Laud Misc. 243</i>, <i>Oxford, Bodleian Library, Hatton 43</i>, and <i>Winchester, Cathedral I</i>, the poem is copied by scribes working a quarter-century or more after the main text was first set down.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>Even when the poem is in the same hand as the manuscript's main text, there is little evidence to suggest that it was copied from the same exemplar as the Latin <i>Historia</i>: nearly identical versions of the Old English poem are found in manuscripts belonging to different recensions of the Latin text; closely related copies of the Latin <i>Historia</i> sometimes contain very different versions of the Old English poem. With the exception of the Old English translation, no single recension of the <i>Historia ecclesiastica</i> is characterised by the presence of a particular recension of the vernacular poem.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Earliest_text">Earliest text</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=C%C3%A6dmon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Earliest text">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The oldest known version of the poem is the <a href="/wiki/Northumbria" title="Northumbria">Northumbrian</a> <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">aelda</i></span> <a href="/wiki/Recension" title="Recension">recension</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> The surviving witnesses to this text, <a href="/wiki/Cambridge,_University_Library,_Kk._5._16" class="mw-redirect" title="Cambridge, University Library, Kk. 5. 16">Cambridge, University Library, Kk. 5. 16</a> (M) and <a href="/wiki/Leningrad_Bede" class="mw-redirect" title="Leningrad Bede">St. Petersburg, National Library of Russia, lat. Q. v. I. 18</a> (P), date to at least the mid-8th century. <b>M</b> in particular is traditionally ascribed to Bede's own monastery and lifetime, though there is little evidence to suggest it was copied much before the mid-8th century.<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup>

</p>

<li id="cite_note-3"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-3">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">The twelve named Anglo-Saxon poets are <a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86duwen&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Æduwen (page does not exist)">Æduwen</a>, <a href="/wiki/Aldhelm" title="Aldhelm">Aldhelm</a>, <a href="/wiki/Alfred_the_Great" title="Alfred the Great">Alfred the Great</a>, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Anlaf_(poet)&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Anlaf (poet) (page does not exist)">Anlaf</a>, <a href="/wiki/Baldulf" class="mw-redirect" title="Baldulf">Baldulf</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>, Cædmon, <a href="/wiki/Cnut" title="Cnut">Cnut</a>, <a href="/wiki/Cynewulf" title="Cynewulf">Cynewulf</a>, <a href="/wiki/Dunstan" title="Dunstan">Dunstan</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hereward" class="mw-redirect" title="Hereward">Hereward</a> and <a href="/wiki/Wulfstan_the_Cantor" title="Wulfstan the Cantor">Wulfstan</a> (or perhaps Wulfsige). Most of these are considered by modern scholars to be spurious—see <a href="#odonnell2005">O'Donnell 2005, Introduction 1.22</a>. The three for whom biographical information and documented texts survive are Alfred, Bede, and Cædmon. Cædmon is the only Anglo-Saxon poet known primarily for his ability to compose vernacular verse, and no vernacular verse survives that is known to have been written by either Bede or Alfred. There are a number of verse texts known to have been composed by <a href="/wiki/Cynewulf" title="Cynewulf">Cynewulf</a>, but we know nothing of his biography. (No study appears to exist of the "named" Anglo-Saxon poets—the list here has been compiled from <a href="#frank1993">Frank 1993</a>, <a href="#opland1980">Opland 1980</a>, <a href="#sisam1953">Sisam 1953</a> and <a href="#robinson1990">Robinson 1990</a>.)</span>
