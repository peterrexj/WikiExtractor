<p><b>Kateri Tekakwitha</b> (<small>pronounced&#160;</small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="moh-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA" title="Help:IPA">[ˈɡaderi deɡaˈɡwita]</a></span> in <a href="/wiki/Mohawk_language" title="Mohawk language">Mohawk</a>), given the name <b>Tekakwitha</b>, baptized as <b>Catherine</b>, and informally known as <b>Lily of the Mohawks</b> (1656 – April 17, 1680), is a <a href="/wiki/Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic">Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and <a href="/wiki/Virgin_(title)" title="Virgin (title)">virgin</a> who was an <a href="/wiki/Algonquin_people" title="Algonquin people">Algonquin</a>–<a href="/wiki/Mohawk_people" title="Mohawk people">Mohawk</a>. Born in the Mohawk village of Ossernenon, in present-day New York State, she contracted <a href="/wiki/Smallpox" title="Smallpox">smallpox</a> in an epidemic; her family died and her face was scarred. She converted to Catholicism at age nineteen. She took a vow of perpetual virginity, left her village, and moved for the remaining five years of her life to the <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a> mission village of <a href="/wiki/Kahnawake" title="Kahnawake">Kahnawake</a>, just south of <a href="/wiki/Montreal" title="Montreal">Montreal</a>. She was <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> in 1980 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> and canonized by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a> at <a href="/wiki/Saint_Peter%27s_Basilica" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Peter&#39;s Basilica">Saint Peter's Basilica</a> on 21 October 2012.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life_and_education">Early life and education</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life and education">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:KateriTekakwitha.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/61/KateriTekakwitha.jpg/220px-KateriTekakwitha.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="331" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/61/KateriTekakwitha.jpg/330px-KateriTekakwitha.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/61/KateriTekakwitha.jpg/440px-KateriTekakwitha.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2000" data-file-height="3008" /></a><figcaption>Sculpture of Saint Kateri Tekakwitha</figcaption></figure>
<p><i>Tekakwitha</i> is the name the girl was given by her Mohawk people. It translates to "She who bumps into things."<sup id="cite_ref-National_Saint_Kateri_Tekakwitha_Shrine_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-National_Saint_Kateri_Tekakwitha_Shrine-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> She was born around 1656 in the Mohawk village of Ossernenon in Northeastern <a href="/wiki/New_York_(state)" title="New York (state)">New York</a> state. A nineteenth-century claim that Auriesville developed at the site of Ossernenon has been disproved by archaeological findings, according to Dean R. Snow and other specialists in Native American history in New York.<sup id="cite_ref-intro_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-intro-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>She was the daughter of Kenneronkwa, a Mohawk chief, and Kahenta, an <a href="/wiki/Algonquin_people" title="Algonquin people">Algonquin</a> woman, who had been captured in a raid and then adopted and assimilated into the tribe. Kahenta had been <a href="/wiki/Baptized" class="mw-redirect" title="Baptized">baptized</a> Catholic and educated by French missionaries in <a href="/wiki/Trois-Rivi%C3%A8res" title="Trois-Rivières">Trois-Rivières</a>, east of Montreal. Mohawk warriors captured her and took her to their homeland.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Kahenta eventually married Kenneronkwa.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Tekakwitha was the first of their two children. A brother followed.
</p><p>Tekakwitha's original village was highly diverse. The Mohawk were absorbing many captured natives of other tribes, particularly their competitors, the <a href="/wiki/Huron_people" class="mw-redirect" title="Huron people">Huron</a>, to replace people who died from warfare or diseases.
</p><p>When Tekakwitha was around four years old, her baby brother and both her parents died of smallpox. She survived but was left with facial scars and impaired eyesight.<sup id="cite_ref-Bonaparte_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bonaparte-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> She was adopted by her father's sister and her husband, a chief of the Turtle Clan.
</p><p>The Jesuits' account of Tekakwitha said that she was a modest girl who avoided social gatherings and covered her head because of the scars. She became skilled at traditional women's arts like making clothing, weaving mats, and preparing food. As was the custom, she was pressured to consider marriage around age thirteen, but she refused.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Upheaval_and_invasions">Upheaval and invasions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Upheaval and invasions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Tekakwitha grew up in a period of upheaval, as the Mohawk interacted with French and Dutch colonists, who were competing in the lucrative fur trade. Trying to make inroads in <a href="/wiki/Iroquois" title="Iroquois">Iroquois</a> territory, the French attacked the Mohawk in present-day central New York in 1666. After driving the people from their homes, the French burned the three Mohawk villages. Tekakwitha, around ten years old, fled with her new family.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After the defeat by the French forces, the Mohawk accepted a peace treaty that required them to tolerate Jesuit missionaries in their villages. The Jesuits established a mission near Auriesville, New York. They spoke of Christianity in terms with which the Mohawk could identify.
</p><p>The Mohawk crossed their river to rebuild Caughnawaga on the north bank, west of the present-day town of <a href="/wiki/Fonda,_New_York" title="Fonda, New York">Fonda, New York</a>. In 1667, when Tekakwitha was 11 years old, she met the Jesuit missionaries Jacques Frémin, Jacques Bruyas, and <a href="/wiki/Jean_Pierron" title="Jean Pierron">Jean Pierron</a>, who had come to the village.<sup id="cite_ref-TheMaking_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TheMaking-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Her uncle opposed any contact with them because he did not want her to convert to Christianity. One of his older daughters had already become Catholic.
</p><p>In the summer of 1669, several hundred <a href="/wiki/Mohican_people" class="mw-redirect" title="Mohican people">Mohican</a> warriors, advancing from the east, launched an attack on Caughnawaga. Tekakwitha, at that point around 13 years old, joined other girls to help priest Jean Pierron tend to the wounded, bury the dead, and carry food and water.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Feast_of_the_Dead">Feast of the Dead</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Feast of the Dead">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Later in 1669, the Iroquois Feast of the Dead was convened at Caughnawaga. The remains of Tekakwitha's parents, along with others, were to be part of the ceremony.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Father Pierron criticized the Feast of the Dead, but the assembled Iroquois ordered him to be silent. Afterwards, however, they relented. Exchanging gifts with priest, they promised to give up the Feast.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Garakontié later converted to Christianity.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Family_pressures">Family pressures</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Family pressures">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>By the time Tekakwitha turned 17, around 1673, her adoptive mother and aunt tried to arrange her marriage to a young Mohawk man.<sup id="cite_ref-Rev._Edward_Sherman_2007_106_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Rev._Edward_Sherman_2007_106-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Tekakwitha fled the cabin and hid in a nearby field and continued to resist marriage.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Eventually, her aunts gave up their efforts to get her to marry.
</p><p>In the spring of 1674, at age eighteen, Tekakwitha met the Jesuit priest Jacques de Lamberville, who was visiting the village. In the presence of others, Tekakwitha told him her story and her desire to become a Christian. She started studying the catechism with him.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Conversion_and_Kahnawake">Conversion and Kahnawake</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Conversion and Kahnawake">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Lamberville wrote in his journal in the years after her death about Tekakwitha. This text described her before she was baptized as a mild-mannered girl. Lamberville also stated that Kateri did everything she could to stay holy in a secular society, which often caused minor conflicts with her longhouse residents. The journal, however, does not mention violence toward Kateri, while other sources do.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Lamberville baptized Tekakwitha at the age of 19, on <a href="/wiki/Easter_Sunday" class="mw-redirect" title="Easter Sunday">Easter Sunday</a>, April 18, 1676.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Tekakwitha was renamed "Catherine" after St. Catherine of Siena (Kateri was the Mohawk form of the name).<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>She remained in Caughnawauga for another six months. Some Mohawks opposed her conversion and accused her of sorcery.<sup id="cite_ref-TheMaking_9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TheMaking-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Lamberville suggested that she go to the Jesuit mission of Kahnawake, located south of Montreal on the St. Lawrence River, where other native converts had gathered. Catherine joined them in 1677.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Tekakwitha was said to have put thorns on her sleeping mat and lain on them while praying for her relatives' conversion and forgiveness. Piercing the body to draw blood was a traditional practice of the Mohawk and other Iroquois nations. She lived at Kahnawake the remaining two years of her life.
</p><p>Father Cholonec wrote that Tekakwitha said:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>I have deliberated enough. For a long time, my decision on what I will do has been made. I have consecrated myself entirely to Jesus, son of Mary, I have chosen Him for husband, and He alone will take me for wife.<sup id="cite_ref-TheMaking_9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TheMaking-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>The Church considers that in 1679, with her decision on the <a href="/wiki/Feast_of_the_Annunciation" title="Feast of the Annunciation">Feast of the Annunciation</a>, Tekakwitha's conversion was truly completed, and with regard to biographies of the early Jesuits, she is regarded as the "first Iroquois <a href="/wiki/Virgin_(title)" title="Virgin (title)">virgin</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-TheMaking_9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TheMaking-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Although Tekakwitha is rather often regarded as a <a href="/wiki/Consecrated_virgin" title="Consecrated virgin">consecrated virgin</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> she could, owing to circumstances, never receive the Consecration of Virgins by a bishop.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> Nevertheless, the United States Association of Consecrated Virgins took Kateri Tekakwitha as its patroness.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Mission_du_Sault_St._Louis:_Kahnawake">Mission du Sault St. Louis: Kahnawake</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Mission du Sault St. Louis: Kahnawake">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Jesuits had founded Kahnawake for the religious conversion of the natives. When it began, the natives built their traditional longhouses for residences. They also built a longhouse to be used as a chapel by the Jesuits. As a missionary settlement, Kahnawake was at risk of being attacked by the Iroquois Confederacy members who had not converted to Catholicism.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> (While it attracted other Iroquois, it was predominantly Mohawk, the prominent tribe in eastern New York.)
</p><p>After Catherine's arrival, she shared the longhouse of her older sister and her husband. She would have known other people in the longhouse who had migrated from their former village of Gandaouagué (<a href="/wiki/Caughnawaga_Indian_Village_Site" title="Caughnawaga Indian Village Site">Caughnawaga</a>). Her mother's close friend, Anastasia Tegonhatsiongo, was clan matron of the longhouse. Anastasia and other Mohawk women introduced Tekakwitha to the regular practices of Christianity.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> This was normal for the women in the village, with many of the missionaries being preoccupied with other religious tasks. Pierre Cholenec reported that "all the Iroquois who come here and then become Christians owe their conversion mainly to the zeal of their relatives".<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Kahnawake was a village set-up like normal Iroquois villages, moving from location to location after running out of resources. The village was originally not wholly French but with northward migration towards Canada started by the <a href="/wiki/Iroquois" title="Iroquois">Five Nations</a>, the village started to gain more native members. The Five Nations all happened to start migrating north<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> around the same time, without any communication between them. In Kahnawake, there was representation from multiple tribes<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> and when the French came, there were people from different ethnicities. The village was recognized by <a href="/wiki/New_France" title="New France">New France</a> as well, it was given autonomy to deal with the problems that would arise. They were also able to form a friendship with New York through this autonomy.
</p><p>There was fur trade in Kahnawake. The division between the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_France" title="Catholic Church in France">French Church</a> and the natives was clear-cut in the village; there were few interactions between the two.
</p><p>Kahnawake was drawn into a war among the different tribes that lasted around two and a half years.
</p>
<h3><span id="Chaucheti.C3.A8re_and_Cholenec"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Chauchetière_and_Cholenec">Chauchetière and Cholenec</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Chauchetière and Cholenec">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p><a href="/wiki/Claude_Chaucheti%C3%A8re" title="Claude Chauchetière">Claude Chauchetière</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pierre_Cholenec" title="Pierre Cholenec">Pierre Cholenec</a> were Jesuit priests who played important roles in Tekakwitha's life. Both were based in New France and Kahnawake. Chauchetière was the first to write a biography of Tekakwitha (1695), and Cholenec followed (1696).<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Cholenec, who had arrived first, introduced traditional items of Catholic mortification to the converts at Kahnawake. He wanted them to adopt these rather than use Mohawk ritual practices.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Both Chauchetière and Tekakwitha arrived in Kahnawake the same year, in 1677.
</p><p>Chauchetière came to believe that Tekakwitha was a saint. In his biography of Kateri, he stressed her "charity, industry, purity, and fortitude."<sup id="cite_ref-Choquette_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Choquette-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> In contrast, Cholenec stressed her virginity, perhaps to counter colonial stereotypes characterizing Indian women as promiscuous.<sup id="cite_ref-Choquette_26-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Choquette-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_appearances">Death and appearances</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Death and appearances">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Around <a href="/wiki/Holy_Week" title="Holy Week">Holy Week</a> of 1680, friends noted that Tekakwitha's health was failing. When people knew she had but a few hours left, villagers gathered together, accompanied by the priests Chauchetière and Cholenec, the latter providing the <a href="/wiki/Last_rites" title="Last rites">last rites</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Catherine Tekakwitha died at around 15:00 (3 p.m.) on <a href="/wiki/Holy_Wednesday" title="Holy Wednesday">Holy Wednesday</a>, April 17, 1680, at the age of 23 or 24, in the arms of her friend Marie-Therèse. Chauchetière reports her final words were, "Jesus, Mary, I love you."<sup id="cite_ref-cccb_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cccb-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After her death, the people noticed a physical change. Cholenec later wrote, "This face, so marked and swarthy, suddenly changed about a quarter of an hour after her death and became in a moment so beautiful and so white that I observed it immediately."<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> Her smallpox scars were said to disappear.
</p><p>Tekakwitha purportedly appeared to three individuals in the weeks after her death; her mentor Anastasia Tegonhatsiongo, her friend Marie-Therèse Tegaiaguenta, and Chauchetière. Anastasia said that, while crying over the death of her spiritual daughter, she looked up to see Catherine "kneeling at the foot" of her mattress, "holding a wooden cross that shone like the sun." Marie-Thérèse reported that she was awakened at night by a knocking on her wall, and a voice asked if she were awake, adding, "I've come to say good-bye; I'm on my way to heaven." Marie-Thérèse went outside but saw no one; she heard a voice murmur, "Adieu, Adieu, go tell the father that I'm going to heaven." Chauchetière meanwhile said he saw Catherine at her grave; he said she appeared in "baroque splendor; for two hours he gazed upon her" and "her face lifted toward heaven as if in ecstasy."<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Chauchetière had a chapel built near Kateri's gravesite. By 1684, pilgrimages had begun to honor her there. The Jesuits turned her bones to dust and set the ashes within the "newly rebuilt mission chapel." This symbolized her presence on earth, and her remains were sometimes used as relics for healing.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Kateri_Tekakwitha_au_Qu%C3%A9bec.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/Kateri_Tekakwitha_au_Qu%C3%A9bec.JPG/220px-Kateri_Tekakwitha_au_Qu%C3%A9bec.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/Kateri_Tekakwitha_au_Qu%C3%A9bec.JPG/330px-Kateri_Tekakwitha_au_Qu%C3%A9bec.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/Kateri_Tekakwitha_au_Qu%C3%A9bec.JPG/440px-Kateri_Tekakwitha_au_Qu%C3%A9bec.JPG 2x" data-file-width="1944" data-file-height="2592" /></a><figcaption>Statue of Saint Kateri Tekakwitha by <a href="/wiki/Joseph-%C3%89mile_Brunet" title="Joseph-Émile Brunet">Joseph-Émile Brunet</a> at the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupr%C3%A9" title="Basilica of Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré">Basilica of Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Quebec_City" title="Quebec City">Quebec City</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>The first account of Kateri Tekakwitha was not published until 1715. Because of Tekakwitha's unique path to <a href="/wiki/Chastity" title="Chastity">chastity</a>, she is often referred to as a <a href="/wiki/Lily_flower" class="mw-redirect" title="Lily flower">lily</a>, a traditional symbol of purity. Religious images of Tekakwitha are often decorated with a lily and cross, with feathers or turtle as cultural accessories alluding to her Native American birth. Colloquial epithets for Tekakwitha are <i>The Lily of the Mohawks</i> (most notable), the <i>Mohawk Maiden</i>, the <i>Pure and Tender Lily</i>, the <i>Flower among True Men</i>, the <i>Lily of Purity</i> and <i>The New Star of the New World</i>. Her tribal neighbors – and her gravestone – referred to her as "the fairest flower that ever bloomed among the redmen."<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Her virtues are considered an <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical" class="mw-redirect" title="Ecumenical">ecumenical</a> bridge between Mohawk and European cultures.
</p><p>Fifty years after her death, a convent for Native American nuns opened in Mexico.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> Indian<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="margin-left:0.1em; white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify" title="Wikipedia:Please clarify"><span title="The text near this tag may need clarification or removal of jargon. (November 2022)">clarification needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> Catholic missions and bishops in the 1880s initiated a petition for officially allowing veneration of Kateri. They asked for the veneration of Tekakwitha in tandem with the Jesuits <a href="/wiki/Isaac_Jogues" title="Isaac Jogues">Isaac Jogues</a> and René Goupil, two Catholic missionaries who had been slain by the Mohawk in Osernnenon a few decades before Kateri's birth. They concluded their petition by stating that these venerations would help encourage Catholicism among other Native Americans.<sup id="cite_ref-Marquette_U_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Marquette_U-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The process for Kateri Tekakwitha's canonization was initiated by United States Catholics at the <a href="/wiki/Third_Plenary_Council_of_Baltimore" class="mw-redirect" title="Third Plenary Council of Baltimore">Third Plenary Council of Baltimore</a> in 1885, followed by Canadian Catholics. Some 906 Native Americans signed 27 letters in the US and Canada urging her canonization.<sup id="cite_ref-marquette_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-marquette-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On January 3, 1943, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII" title="Pope Pius XII">Pope Pius XII</a> declared her <a href="/wiki/Venerable" class="mw-redirect" title="Venerable">venerable</a>. She was beatified as on June 22, 1980, by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On December 19, 2011, the Congregation for the Causes of Saints certified a second miracle through her intercession, signed by Pope Benedict XVI.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> On February 18, 2012, Pope Benedict XVI decreed that Tekakwitha be canonized. Speaking in Latin, he used the form "Catharina Tekakwitha"; the official booklet of the ceremony referred to her in English and Italian as "Kateri Tekakwitha."<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> She was canonized on October 21, 2012, by Pope Benedict XVI.<sup id="cite_ref-cccb_27-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cccb-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> In the official canonization rite booklet, "Catherine" is used in the English and French biographies and "Kateri" in the translation of the rite itself.<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup> She is the first Native American woman of North America to be canonized by the Catholic Church.<sup id="cite_ref-blessed_21october_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-blessed_21october-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 2022, the <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal Church of the United States</a> gave final approval to a feast dedicated to Kateri on 17 April on the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">liturgical calendar</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Kateri Tekakwitha is featured in four national shrines in the United States: the <a href="/w/index.php?title=National_Shrine_of_Blessed_Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="National Shrine of Blessed Kateri Tekakwitha (page does not exist)">National Shrine of Blessed Kateri Tekakwitha</a> in Fonda, New York; the <a href="/wiki/National_Shrine_of_the_North_American_Martyrs" title="National Shrine of the North American Martyrs">National Shrine of the North American Martyrs</a> in Auriesville, New York; the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_the_National_Shrine_of_the_Immaculate_Conception" title="Basilica of the National Shrine of the Immaculate Conception">Basilica of the National Shrine of the Immaculate Conception</a> in Washington, D.C.; and The National Shrine of <a href="/wiki/The_Cross_in_the_Woods" class="mw-redirect" title="The Cross in the Woods">the Cross in the Woods</a>, an open-air sanctuary in <a href="/wiki/Indian_River,_Michigan" title="Indian River, Michigan">Indian River, Michigan</a>. The latter shrine's design was inspired by Kateri's habit of placing small wooden crosses throughout the woods.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> The National Shrine of Blessed Kateri Tekakwitha is also home to Caughnawaga, an excavated Haudenosaunee village.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Miracles">Miracles</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Miracles">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Saint_John_Neumann_Catholic_Church_(Sunbury,_Ohio)_-_interior,_statue_of_Saint_Kateri_Tekakwitha.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3e/Saint_John_Neumann_Catholic_Church_%28Sunbury%2C_Ohio%29_-_interior%2C_statue_of_Saint_Kateri_Tekakwitha.jpg/220px-Saint_John_Neumann_Catholic_Church_%28Sunbury%2C_Ohio%29_-_interior%2C_statue_of_Saint_Kateri_Tekakwitha.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="352" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3e/Saint_John_Neumann_Catholic_Church_%28Sunbury%2C_Ohio%29_-_interior%2C_statue_of_Saint_Kateri_Tekakwitha.jpg/330px-Saint_John_Neumann_Catholic_Church_%28Sunbury%2C_Ohio%29_-_interior%2C_statue_of_Saint_Kateri_Tekakwitha.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3e/Saint_John_Neumann_Catholic_Church_%28Sunbury%2C_Ohio%29_-_interior%2C_statue_of_Saint_Kateri_Tekakwitha.jpg/440px-Saint_John_Neumann_Catholic_Church_%28Sunbury%2C_Ohio%29_-_interior%2C_statue_of_Saint_Kateri_Tekakwitha.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1707" data-file-height="2729" /></a><figcaption>A statue of Saint Kateri Tekakwitha in Saint John Neumann Catholic Church, Sunbury, Ohio</figcaption></figure>
<p>Joseph Kellogg was a Protestant child captured by Natives in the eighteenth century and eventually returned to his home. Twelve months later, he caught smallpox. The Jesuits helped treat him, but he was not recovering. They had relics from Tekakwitha's grave but did not want to use them on a non-Catholic. One Jesuit told Kellogg that if he would become a Catholic, help would come to him. Joseph did so. The Jesuit gave him a piece of decayed wood from Kateri's coffin, which is said to have made him heal. The historian Allan Greer takes this account to mean that Tekakwitha was known in 18th-century New France, and she was already perceived to have healing abilities.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Other miracles were attributed to Kateri: Father Rémy recovered his hearing, and a nun in Montreal was cured by using items formerly belonging to Kateri. Such incidents were evidence that Kateri was possibly a saint. Following the death of a person, sainthood is symbolized by events that show the rejection of death. It is also represented by a duality of pain and neutralization of the other's pain (all shown by her reputed miracles in New France).<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Chauchetière told settlers in La Prairie to pray to Kateri for intercession with illnesses. Due to the Jesuits' superior system of publicizing material, his words and Kateri's fame were said to reach Jesuits in China and their converts.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As people believed in her healing powers, some collected earth from her gravesite and wore it in bags as a relic. One woman said she was saved from pneumonia ("grande maladie du rhume"); she gave the pendant to her husband, who was healed from his disease.<sup id="cite_ref-MohawkSaint_6-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-MohawkSaint-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On December 19, 2011, Pope Benedict XVI approved the second miracle needed for Kateri's canonization.<sup id="cite_ref-52" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-52">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup> The authorized miracle dates from 2006, when a young boy in Washington state survived a severe flesh-eating bacterium. Doctors had been unable to stop the disease's progress by surgery and advised his parents he was likely to die. The boy received the sacrament of <a href="/wiki/Anointing_of_the_Sick_(Catholic_Church)" class="mw-redirect" title="Anointing of the Sick (Catholic Church)">Anointing of the Sick</a> from a Catholic priest. As the boy is half <a href="/wiki/Lummi_Indian" class="mw-redirect" title="Lummi Indian">Lummi Indian</a>, the parents said they prayed to Tekakwitha for divine <a href="/wiki/Intercession" title="Intercession">intercession</a>, as did their family and friends, and an extended network contacted through their son's classmates.<sup id="cite_ref-53" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-53">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> Sister Kateri Mitchell visited the boy's bedside and placed a relic of Tekakwitha, a bone fragment, against his body and prayed together with his parents.<sup id="cite_ref-54" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-54">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup> The next day, the infection stopped its progression.<sup id="cite_ref-55" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-55">&#91;55&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Controversy">Controversy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Controversy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Mohawk scholar Orenda Boucher noted that despite extensive support for the canonization of Tekakwitha, some traditional Mohawk see her as a connection to the worst aspects of colonialism. They do not believe that she embodied or reflected traditional Mohawk womanhood.<sup id="cite_ref-Esch2012_56-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Esch2012-56">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup> Yet, the same article quotes Russell Roundpoint, director of the Mohawk history and cultural center at Akwesasne, who said her sainthood is "not a contentious issue by any stretch of the imagination," and that the "Mohawk people are very proud of the fact that she has attained such a high level."<sup id="cite_ref-Esch2012_56-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Esch2012-56">&#91;56&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Cultural_references">Cultural references</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Cultural references">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>More than 300 books have been published in more than 20 languages on the life of Kateri Tekakwitha.<sup id="cite_ref-Bonaparte_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bonaparte-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> The historian K. I. Koppedrayer has suggested that the Catholic Church fathers' <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiography</a> of Tekakwitha reflected "trials and rewards of the European presence in the New World."<sup id="cite_ref-TheMaking_9-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-TheMaking-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> 
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Kateri_Tekakwitha&amp;action=edit&amp;section=18" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Kateri_Tekakwitha_devotional_medal.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Kateri_Tekakwitha_devotional_medal.jpg/220px-Kateri_Tekakwitha_devotional_medal.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="295" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Kateri_Tekakwitha_devotional_medal.jpg/330px-Kateri_Tekakwitha_devotional_medal.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Kateri_Tekakwitha_devotional_medal.jpg/440px-Kateri_Tekakwitha_devotional_medal.jpg 2x" data-file-width="585" data-file-height="784" /></a><figcaption>Blessed Kateri <a href="/wiki/Devotional_medal" title="Devotional medal">devotional medal</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>After Tekakwitha's beatification in 1980, Paula E. Holmes, in the late 1990s, interviewed several elderly Native American women about their childhoods and hearing stories from their ancestors about Tekakwitha. She found that Kateri is "as part of their Indian familiar and familial heritage."<sup id="cite_ref-61" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-61">&#91;61&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Clarence_A._Walworth" title="Clarence A. Walworth">Clarence A. Walworth</a> was one of the strongest proponents of Tekakwitha's veneration. He personally financed a granite monument in Kahnawake out of a gesture for international co-operation for her veneration.<sup id="cite_ref-62" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-62">&#91;62&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In traditional fashion, numerous churches, schools and other Catholic institutions have been named for her, particularly since her canonization, including several Catholic elementary schools. Among these are St. Kateri Tekakwitha Catholic Elementary School in <a href="/wiki/Kitchener,_Ontario" title="Kitchener, Ontario">Kitchener</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-63" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-63">&#91;63&#93;</a></sup> Kateri Tekakwitha Catholic Elementary School in <a href="/wiki/Markham,_Ontario" title="Markham, Ontario">Markham</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-64" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-64">&#91;64&#93;</a></sup> St. Kateri Tekakwitha Catholic Elementary School in <a href="/wiki/Hamilton,_Ontario" title="Hamilton, Ontario">Hamilton</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-65" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-65">&#91;65&#93;</a></sup> Saint Kateri Tekakwitha Catholic School in Orléans (<a href="/wiki/Ottawa" title="Ottawa">Ottawa</a>),<sup id="cite_ref-66" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-66">&#91;66&#93;</a></sup> and St. Kateri Tekakwitha School in <a href="/wiki/Calgary" title="Calgary">Calgary</a>, Alberta.<sup id="cite_ref-67" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-67">&#91;67&#93;</a></sup> In the United States, St. Kateri Tekakwitha Catholic Churches are Dearborn, MI<sup id="cite_ref-68" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-68">&#91;68&#93;</a></sup> and Buffalo, TX.<sup id="cite_ref-69" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-69">&#91;69&#93;</a></sup> in Saint Kateri is the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/John_Cabot_Catholic_Secondary_School" title="John Cabot Catholic Secondary School">John Cabot Catholic Secondary School</a> in <a href="/wiki/Mississauga" title="Mississauga">Mississauga</a>.
</p><p>The St. Kateri Tekakwitha School in <a href="/wiki/Niskayuna,_New_York" title="Niskayuna, New York">Niskayuna, New York</a> was named after her. St. Kateri Tekakwitha Parish, located in adjacent <a href="/wiki/Schenectady" class="mw-redirect" title="Schenectady">Schenectady</a>, was founded in 2012. A parish in <a href="/wiki/Irondequoit,_New_York" title="Irondequoit, New York">Irondequoit, New York</a>, was named after her in 2010. Kateri Residence, a nursing home in Manhattan, is named for her. The chapel of <a href="/wiki/Welsh_Family_Hall_(University_of_Notre_Dame)" class="mw-redirect" title="Welsh Family Hall (University of Notre Dame)">Welsh Family Hall</a> at the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Notre_Dame" title="University of Notre Dame">University of Notre Dame</a>, built in 1997, is dedicated to her.
</p><p>On April 4, 2021, on <a href="/wiki/Easter_Sunday" class="mw-redirect" title="Easter Sunday">Easter Sunday</a>, the only Catholic Church at St. Theresa Point in Northern Manitoba, Canada, burned down. The only things that were left after the rubble was an image of St. Kateri Tekakwitha, almost completely intact as well as a Mary statue.<sup id="cite_ref-Clarke_70-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Clarke-70">&#91;70&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In May 2021, a church that was built in St. Kateri's honour burned for the second time on the <a href="/wiki/Bay_Mills_Indian_Community" title="Bay Mills Indian Community">Bay Mills Indian Community</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Upper_Peninsula" class="mw-redirect" title="Upper Peninsula">Upper Peninsula</a> of <a href="/wiki/Michigan" title="Michigan">Michigan</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-71" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-71">&#91;71&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Since 1939, the <a href="/wiki/Tekakwitha_Conference" title="Tekakwitha Conference">Tekakwitha Conference</a> meets annually to support Catholic missions among Native Americans. People gather in <i>Kateri Circles</i> to pray together, seeking to become better Catholics. In 1991, the conference reported 130 registered Kateri Circles.<sup id="cite_ref-Marquette_U_30-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Marquette_U-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Tekakwitha Island (French: île Tekakwitha) in the St-Lawrence River, part of the Kahnawake reserve, is named after her.
</p>
