<p class="mw-empty-elt">

</p>

<p><b>Seaxburh</b>, also <b>Saint Sexburga of Ely</b> (died about 699) was a Queen as well as an <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a>, and is a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Christian_Church" title="Christian Church">Christian Church</a>. She was married to King <a href="/wiki/Eorcenberht_of_Kent" title="Eorcenberht of Kent">Eorcenberht of Kent</a>.
</p><p>After her husband's death in 664, Seaxburh remained in <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent" title="Kingdom of Kent">Kent</a> to bring up her children. She acted as regent until her young son <a href="/wiki/Ecgberht_of_Kent" title="Ecgberht of Kent">Ecgberht</a> came of age.
</p><p>Seaxburh founded the abbeys at <a href="/wiki/Milton-next-Sittingbourne" class="mw-redirect" title="Milton-next-Sittingbourne">Milton Regis</a> and <a href="/wiki/Minster-in-Sheppey" class="mw-redirect" title="Minster-in-Sheppey">Minster-in-Sheppey</a> where her daughter <a href="/wiki/Ermenilda_of_Ely" title="Ermenilda of Ely">Ermenilda</a> was also a nun. She moved to the double monastery at <a href="/wiki/Ely,_Cambridgeshire" title="Ely, Cambridgeshire">Ely</a> where her sister Æthelthryth was abbess and succeeded her when Æthelthryth died in 679.
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>, in 695, Seaxburh organised the movement (or <a href="/wiki/Translation_(relic)" title="Translation (relic)">translation</a>) of Æthelthryth's remains to a marble <a href="/wiki/Sarcophagus" title="Sarcophagus">sarcophagus</a>, after they had lain for sixteen years in a common grave. On opening the grave, it was discovered that her body was miraculously preserved. The legend is described in Bede's <i><a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People" title="Ecclesiastical History of the English People">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i>, which celebrates the saintly virtues of Æthelthryth, but speaks less highly of Seaxburh, referring only to her marriage, succession as abbess and translation of her sister's relics. The date of Seaxburh's death at Ely is not known. The surviving versions of the <i>Vita Sexburge</i>, compiled after 1106, describe her early life, marriage to Eorcenberht, retirement from secular life and her final years as a nun and abbess at Ely.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Family">Family</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seaxburh_of_Ely&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Family">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Kentish_Royal_Legend" title="Kentish Royal Legend">Kentish Royal Legend</a></div>
<p>Seaxburh was a daughter of <a href="/wiki/Anna_of_East_Anglia" title="Anna of East Anglia">Anna</a>, <a href="/wiki/King_of_East_Anglia" class="mw-redirect" title="King of East Anglia">King of East Anglia</a>, the son of <a href="/wiki/Eni_of_East_Anglia" title="Eni of East Anglia">Eni</a>, who ruled the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">East Angles</a> from the early 640s and was slain together with his son <a href="/wiki/Jurmin" title="Jurmin">Jurmin</a> at the Battle of Bulcamp in 653 or 654.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Seaxburh married <a href="/wiki/Eorcenberht_of_Kent" title="Eorcenberht of Kent">Eorcenberht of Kent</a>, and was the mother of kings <a href="/wiki/Ecgberht_of_Kent" title="Ecgberht of Kent">Ecgberht</a> (d. 673), <a href="/wiki/Hlothhere_of_Kent" title="Hlothhere of Kent">Hlothhere</a> (d. 685), and of Saints <a href="/wiki/Eormenhild" class="mw-redirect" title="Eormenhild">Eormenhild</a> and <a href="/wiki/Ercengota" class="mw-redirect" title="Ercengota">Ercengota</a>. Her marriage to Eorcenberht produced two sons, both of whom ruled, and two daughters.
</p><p>Seaxburh's sisters were <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelburg_of_Faremoutiers" class="mw-redirect" title="Æthelburg of Faremoutiers">Æthelburg of Faremoutiers</a>, <a href="/wiki/Saethryth" class="mw-redirect" title="Saethryth">Saethryth</a>, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelthryth" title="Æthelthryth">Æthelthryth</a> and possibly <a href="/wiki/Withburga" class="mw-redirect" title="Withburga">Withburga</a>.
She was the sister of Æthelburg and Saethryth, who were both abbesses of <a href="/wiki/Faremoutiers_Abbey" title="Faremoutiers Abbey">Faremoutiers Abbey</a> in <a href="/wiki/Brie" title="Brie">Brie</a>, and also the sister of Æthelthryth, who married firstly Tonberht an ealderman of the South <a href="/wiki/Gyrwas" title="Gyrwas">Gyrwe</a> in the <a href="/wiki/The_Fens" title="The Fens">Fens</a>, and secondly <a href="/wiki/Ecgfrith_of_Northumbria" title="Ecgfrith of Northumbria">Ecgfrith of Northumbria</a>.
Withburga, who died in 743, may also have been her sister.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The historian <a href="/wiki/Barbara_Yorke" title="Barbara Yorke">Barbara Yorke</a> mentions the possibility that Seaxburh and her namesake <a href="/wiki/Seaxburh_of_Wessex" title="Seaxburh of Wessex">Seaxburh of Wessex</a> were the same person, but also notes that the accounts of Seaxburh's religious life at Ely contradict this suggestion.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Seaxburh was buried at Ely with her sisters Æthelthryth Wihtburh, and with her daughter Eormenhild.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Marriage_and_widowhood">Marriage and widowhood</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seaxburh_of_Ely&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Marriage and widowhood">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:252px;"><a href="/wiki/File:England_green_top.svg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0b/England_green_top.svg/250px-England_green_top.svg.png" decoding="async" width="250" height="314" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0b/England_green_top.svg/375px-England_green_top.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0b/England_green_top.svg/500px-England_green_top.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="963" data-file-height="1210" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:England_green_top.svg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>A map of the <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxon kingdoms</a> in the 7th century</div></div></div>
<p>Seaxburh was connected with the royal family of the <a href="/wiki/Magons%C3%A6tan" class="mw-redirect" title="Magonsætan">Magonsætan</a> by her marriage to Eorcenberht, who was king of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent" title="Kingdom of Kent">Kent</a> from 640 to 664. Eorcenberht was the great-uncle of <a href="/wiki/Mildburh" title="Mildburh">Mildburh</a> and her sisters, the daughters of King <a href="/wiki/Merewalh" title="Merewalh">Merewalh</a> of the Magonsætan.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Their sons Ecgberht and Hlothhere both became kings of Kent. Their daughter <a href="/wiki/Ercongota" class="mw-redirect" title="Ercongota">Ercongota</a> was a nun at Faremoutiers, who was eventually canonised.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Eorcenberht is mentioned in the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle" title="Anglo-Saxon Chronicle">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</a></i> within the annal for 640: "Then his son Eorcenberht succeeded to the kingdom; he overthrew all devil-worship in his kingdom, and was the first of the English kings to establish the Easter festival". In the same passage is the <i>Chronicle'</i>s single reference to Seaxburh and Eorcengota, <i>"...þaes dohter wæs ge haten Erchongata halifemne. and wundorlic man. thære modor wæs Sexburh Annan dohter East Engla ciningas"</i>&#160;– '...his daughter was called Eorcengota, a holy and a remarkable person, whose mother was Seaxburh, daughter of Anna, king of the East Anglians'.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Seaxburh and Eorcenberht had a second daughter, Ermenilda, who married <a href="/wiki/Wulfhere_of_Mercia" title="Wulfhere of Mercia">Wulfhere of Mercia</a> and after his death became a nun and was later canonised. According to Barbara Yorke, Seaxburh's marriage was itself of seminal importance in the establishment of monastic life for women during the Anglo-Saxon period, as she became an example of an ex-queen who made retreating to an nunnery a desirable royal vocation.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Eorcenberht died on 14 July 664,<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> in an outbreak of <a href="/wiki/Plague_(disease)" title="Plague (disease)">plague</a> that occurred that year. After her husband's death, Seaxburh remained in Kent to bring up her children. She played an important political and religious influence in the kingdom: she acted as regent for her son Ecgberht, ruling Kent until her young son came of age, and was the founder of Kent's first abbey for women at <a href="/wiki/Milton-next-Sittingbourne" class="mw-redirect" title="Milton-next-Sittingbourne">Milton</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Thereafter, Seaxburh became a nun and founded the abbey of <a href="/wiki/Minster-in-Sheppey" class="mw-redirect" title="Minster-in-Sheppey">Minster-in-Sheppey</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;note 1&#93;</a></sup> According to the <i><a href="/wiki/Liber_Eliensis" title="Liber Eliensis">Liber Eliensis</a></i>, a 12th-century chronicle and history written at <a href="/wiki/Ely,_Cambridgeshire" title="Ely, Cambridgeshire">Ely</a>, an English source related that Seaxburh received "the veil of holiness" from <a href="/wiki/Theodore_of_Tarsus" title="Theodore of Tarsus">Theodore</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, in her church on the <a href="/wiki/Isle_of_Sheppey" title="Isle of Sheppey">Isle of Sheppey</a> and that her daughter Eormenhild also became a nun there.<sup id="cite_ref-Fairweather-69_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fairweather-69-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Seaxburh is said by her <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">hagiographer</a> to have sought refuge as a nun after living a secular role that she had found hard to tolerate: having reluctantly submitted to marriage, she hastened from queenhood to "a timely widowhood and a hasty withdrawal to the religious life", according to Susan Ridyard.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Religious_life_at_Ely">Religious life at Ely</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seaxburh_of_Ely&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Religious life at Ely">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Refektorium_Ostfenster_4_St.Sexburg.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Refektorium_Ostfenster_4_St.Sexburg.jpg/220px-Refektorium_Ostfenster_4_St.Sexburg.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="329" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Refektorium_Ostfenster_4_St.Sexburg.jpg/330px-Refektorium_Ostfenster_4_St.Sexburg.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Refektorium_Ostfenster_4_St.Sexburg.jpg/440px-Refektorium_Ostfenster_4_St.Sexburg.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1712" data-file-height="2560" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Refektorium_Ostfenster_4_St.Sexburg.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>A stained glass window depicting St. Seaxburh, from the Refectory of <a href="/wiki/Chester_Cathedral" title="Chester Cathedral">Chester Cathedral</a>.</div></div></div>
<p>Shortly afterwards Seaxburh moved to the <a href="/wiki/Double_monastery" title="Double monastery">double monastery</a> at Ely, which was the precursor to <a href="/wiki/Ely_Cathedral" title="Ely Cathedral">Ely Cathedral</a>, and where her sister Æthelthryth was abbess.
</p><p>According to Yorke, Seaxburh's retirement to Ely is an example an Anglo-Saxon custom represented in a <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_law" title="Anglo-Saxon law">law</a>: whereby a married woman remained the responsibility of the paternal side of her family, perhaps to spend the rest of her days as a nun or an abbess.<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke,_Nunneries,_p._31_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke,_Nunneries,_p._31-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Described by the <i>Liber Eliensis</i> as a "<i>pretiosa virago</i>" (precious lady-warrior)<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> she succeeded as abbess when Æthelthryth died, probably of plague, in 679.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Seaxburh's previous political experience in East Anglia and Kent would have been useful in preparing her for the role of abbess at the double monastery at Ely.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 695 Seaxburh decided to translate the remains of her sister Æthelthryth (who had been dead for sixteen years) from a common grave to the new church at Ely in a vivid demonstration of the dynastic value of the cult of royal saints in Anglo-Saxon England,<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>  Professor Patrick Sims-Williams has identified Seaxburh as "the chief mover behind the translation of her body and the promulagation of her cult".<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Liber Eliensis</i> describes these events in detail.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> When her grave was opened, Æthelthryth's body was discovered to be uncorrupted and her coffin and clothes proved to possess miraculous powers. A <a href="/wiki/Sarcophagus" title="Sarcophagus">sarcophagus</a> made of white marble was taken from the Roman ruins at <a href="/wiki/Grantchester" title="Grantchester">Grantchester</a>, which was found to be the right fit for Æthelthryth.
</p><p>The architectural historian John Crook questions how such miraculous coincidences feature in hagiographies (the studies of the lives of saints), when he observes that "the miraculous discovery of a suitable coffin is, however, a hagiographic commonplace".<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>  Seaxburh's supervised the preparation of her sister's body, which was washed and wrapped in new robes before being reburied.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> She apparently oversaw the translation of her sister's remains without the supervision of her bishop, using her knowledge of procedures gained from her family's links with the abbey at Faremoutiers as a basis for the ceremony.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The fourth book of the <i><a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People" title="Ecclesiastical History of the English People">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i>, completed by the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of Northumbria">Northumbrian</a> monk <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> in 731, celebrates the monastery at Ely and focuses on Æthelthryth's piety and the translation of her relics. Bede does not mention the matrilinear succession established at Ely by Æthelthryth, where power passed in turn to Seaxburh before subsequently transferring to Seaxburh's daughter Eormenhild and to her granddaughter, <a href="/wiki/Werburh" class="mw-redirect" title="Werburh">Werburh</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> He praises the virtues of Æthelthryth, a princess who was married twice but still preserved her virginity. Seaxburh receives little praise from Bede, as she had borne children before becoming a nun.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> He only mentions Seaxburh's marriage to Eorcenberht, succession as abbess and translation of her sister's relics.<sup id="cite_ref-Ridyard,_Royal_Saints,_p._56_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ridyard,_Royal_Saints,_p._56-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death_and_veneration">Death and veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Seaxburh_of_Ely&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Death and veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The date of Seaxburh's death is not known, but when she died at Ely,<sup id="cite_ref-Yorke,_Nunneries,_p._31_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Yorke,_Nunneries,_p._31-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> she was at "a good, late age", according to the <i>Liber Eliensis</i>, which also related that Richard, the last Abbot of Ely, translated the remains of Seaxburh and of "all the saintly women reposing in that place".<sup id="cite_ref-Fairweather-69_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Fairweather-69-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> Her <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is 6 July.<sup id="cite_ref-Butler-132_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Butler-132-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Seaxburh is mentioned in a written account of Kent's earliest Christian kings and their canonised relatives, known as the <a href="/wiki/Kentish_Royal_Legend" title="Kentish Royal Legend">Kentish Royal Legend</a> (Old English: <i>Þá hálgan</i>). These kings, queens and princesses were unified by their holiness and royal connections. <a href="/wiki/Pauline_Stafford" title="Pauline Stafford">Pauline Stafford</a> notes that the Legend "may have been a Christian alternative to pagan genealogy" to the rulers of 10th- and 11th-century mediaeval England, as it described an earlier period of sustained Christian piety within the royal dynasty of Kent. Being both a queen and a saint, Seaxburh was held in high regard within the Legend:<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> within it her role as queen and the founder of the minster at Sheppey was highlighted.<sup id="cite_ref-Ridyard,_Royal_Saints,_p._56_27-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ridyard,_Royal_Saints,_p._56-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The 1913 edition of the <i>Catholic Encyclopedia</i> lists several separate accounts of the saint's deeds and miracles, or <i>Lives</i>. The <i>Life</i> (or <i>Vita</i>) printed in <a href="/wiki/John_Capgrave" title="John Capgrave">John Capgrave</a>'s <i>Nova, Legenda</i> and used by the <a href="/wiki/Bollandists" class="mw-redirect" title="Bollandists">Bollandists</a>, was perhaps copied from a <a href="/wiki/Cotton_library" title="Cotton library">Cotton manuscript</a> in the <a href="/wiki/British_Museum" title="British Museum">British Museum</a>. There is another <i>Vita</i> in <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> in the same collection, but it was so damaged by fire that it is useless. The surviving versions of the <i>Vita Sexburge</i> were compiled after 1106 (the year the relics of Seaxburh were translated) and are copies from an earlier manuscript, now lost. The <i>Vita</i> describes Seaxburh's early life, marriage to Eorcenberht, withdrawal to Milton and then Minster-in-Sheppey, and her final years as a nun and the abbess at Ely. The section relating to her life at Sheppey is similar to another fragment, dating between the 9th and 11th centuries, and currently kept at <a href="/wiki/Lambeth_Palace" title="Lambeth Palace">Lambeth Palace</a>. It has been suggested that part of the <i>Vita Sexburge</i> was derived from this manuscript, or that both parts originated from an earlier version of Seaxburh's <i>Life</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
