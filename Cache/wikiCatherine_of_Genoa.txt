<p><b>Catherine of Genoa</b> (<b>Caterina Fieschi Adorno</b>, 1447&#160;– 15 September 1510) was an Italian <a href="/wiki/Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholic">Catholic</a> saint and mystic, admired for her work among the sick and the poor<sup id="cite_ref-EB_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and remembered because of various writings describing both these actions and her mystical experiences. She was a member of the <a href="/wiki/Nobility" title="Nobility">noble</a> <a href="/wiki/Fieschi" class="mw-redirect" title="Fieschi">Fieschi</a> family,<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> and spent most of her life and her means serving the sick, especially during the <a href="/wiki/Bubonic_plague" title="Bubonic plague">plague</a> which ravaged <a href="/wiki/Genoa" title="Genoa">Genoa</a> in 1497 and 1501.  She died in that city in 1510.
</p><p>Her fame outside her native city is connected with the publication in 1551 of the book known in English as the <i>Life and Doctrine of Saint Catherine of Genoa</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>She and her teaching were the subject of Baron <a href="/wiki/Friedrich_von_H%C3%BCgel" title="Friedrich von Hügel">Friedrich von Hügel</a>'s classic work <i>The Mystical Element of Religion</i> (1908).<sup id="cite_ref-EB_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Catherine_of_Genoa&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Catherine was born in Genoa in 1447, the last of five children.<sup id="cite_ref-zenit_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-zenit-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
Catherine's parents were Jacopo Fieschi and Francesca di Negro, both of illustrious Italian birth. The family was connected to two previous popes, and Jacopo became <a href="/wiki/Viceroy_of_Naples" class="mw-redirect" title="Viceroy of Naples">Viceroy of Naples</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-CE_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CE-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Catherine wished to enter a <a href="/wiki/Convent" title="Convent">convent</a> when about 13,<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> perhaps inspired by her sister <a href="/w/index.php?title=Limbania&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Limbania (page does not exist)">Limbania</a><span class="noprint" style="font-size:85%; font-style: normal;">&#160;&#91;<a href="https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Limbania" class="extiw" title="it:Santa Limbania">it</a>&#93;</span> who was an <a href="/wiki/Augustinian_nuns" title="Augustinian nuns">Augustinian nun</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Jones_1999_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jones_1999-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> However, the nuns to whom her confessor applied on her behalf refused her on account of her youth. After this Catherine  appears to have put the idea aside without any further attempt.<sup id="cite_ref-CE_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CE-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>After her father's death in 1463, aged 16, she was married by her parents' wish to a young Genoese nobleman, Giuliano Adorno, a man who, after several experiences in the area of trade and in the military world in the Middle East, had returned to Genoa to get married.<sup id="cite_ref-zenit_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-zenit-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Their marriage was probably a ploy to end the feud between their two families.<sup id="cite_ref-Flinders_1993_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Flinders_1993-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The marriage turned out wretchedly:<sup id="cite_ref-Jones_1999_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jones_1999-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> it was  childless and Giuliano proved to be faithless, violent-tempered and a spendthrift, and he made his wife's life a misery. Details are scant, but it seems at least clear that Catherine spent the first five years of her marriage in silent, melancholy submission to her husband; and that she then, for another five years, turned a little to the world for consolation in her troubles.<sup id="cite_ref-CE_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CE-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Then, after ten years of marriage, desperate for an escape, she prayed for three months that God would keep her sick in bed, but her prayer went unanswered.<sup id="cite_ref-Flinders_1993_8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Flinders_1993-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Conversion">Conversion</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Catherine_of_Genoa&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Conversion">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>After ten years of marriage,<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> she was converted by a mystical experience during <a href="/wiki/Sacrament_of_Penance_(Catholic_Church)" class="mw-redirect" title="Sacrament of Penance (Catholic Church)">confession</a> on 22 March 1473; her conversion is described as an overpowering sense of God's love for her. After this revelation occurred, she abruptly left the church, without finishing her confession. This marked the beginning of her life of close union with God in prayer,<sup id="cite_ref-EB_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> without using forms of prayer such as the <a href="/wiki/Rosary" title="Rosary">rosary</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Jones_1999_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jones_1999-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> She began to receive <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Communion</a> almost daily, a practice extremely rare for lay people in the Middle Ages, and she underwent remarkable mental and at times almost pathological experiences, the subject of <a href="/wiki/Friedrich_von_H%C3%BCgel" title="Friedrich von Hügel">Friedrich von Hügel</a>'s study <i>The Mystical Element of Religion</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>She combined this with unselfish service to the sick in a hospital at Genoa, in which her husband joined her after he, too, had been converted.<sup id="cite_ref-EB_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He later became a <a href="/wiki/Franciscan" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan">Franciscan</a> tertiary, but she joined no <a href="/wiki/Religious_order" title="Religious order">religious order</a>. Her husband's spending had ruined them financially. He and Catherine decided to live in the Pammatone, a large hospital in Genoa, and to dedicate themselves to works of charity there.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>  She eventually became manager and treasurer of the hospital.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>She died on 15 September 1510,<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> worn out with labours of body and soul. Her death had been slow with many days of pain and suffering as she experienced visions and wavered between life and death.<sup id="cite_ref-Flinders_1993_8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Flinders_1993-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Spiritual_teaching">Spiritual teaching</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Catherine_of_Genoa&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Spiritual teaching">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>For about 25 years, Catherine, though frequently going to confession, was unable to open her mind for direction to anyone; but towards the end of her life a Father Marabotti was appointed to be her spiritual guide.<sup id="cite_ref-CE_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CE-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He had been a director of the hospital where her husband died in 1497.<sup id="cite_ref-Jones_1999_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Jones_1999-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> To him she explained her states, past and present, and he compiled the <i>Memoirs</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-CE_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CE-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> During this period, her life was devoted to her relationship with God, through "interior inspiration" alone.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1551, 41 years after her death, a book about her life and teaching was published, entitled <i>Libro de la vita mirabile et dottrina santa de la Beata Caterinetta de Genoa</i> ("Book of the marvellous life and holy teaching of the Blessed Catherine of Genoa").<sup id="cite_ref-EB_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> This is the source of her "Dialogues on the Soul and the Body" and her "Treatise on <a href="/wiki/Purgatory" title="Purgatory">Purgatory</a>", which are often printed separately.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Her authorship of these has been denied, and it used to be thought that another mystic, the <a href="/wiki/Augustinians" title="Augustinians">Augustinian</a> <a href="/wiki/Canon_regular" title="Canon regular">canoness regular</a> <a href="/wiki/Battistina_Vernazza" title="Battistina Vernazza">Battistina Vernazza</a>, a nun who lived in a <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> in Genoa from 1510 till her death in 1587, had edited the two works. This suggestion is now discredited by recent scholarship, which attributes a large part of both works to Catherine, even though they received their final literary form only after her death.<sup id="cite_ref-EB_2-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-EB-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Catherine's thought on purgatory, for which she is particularly known, and her way of describing it, is original in some features for the period.<sup id="cite_ref-zenit_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-zenit-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Beatification_and_canonization">Beatification and canonization</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Catherine_of_Genoa&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Beatification and canonization">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Catherine's writings were examined by the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Office" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Office">Holy Office</a> and declared to contain doctrine that would alone be enough to prove her sanctity, and she was accordingly <a href="/wiki/Beatified" class="mw-redirect" title="Beatified">beatified</a> in 1675 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_X" title="Pope Clement X">Pope Clement X</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> in 1737 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_XII" title="Pope Clement XII">Pope Clement XII</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-CE_5-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CE-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Her writings also became sources of inspiration for other religious leaders such as <a href="/wiki/Robert_Bellarmine" title="Robert Bellarmine">Robert Bellarmine</a> and <a href="/wiki/Francis_de_Sales" title="Francis de Sales">Francis de Sales</a> and Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Henry_Edward_Manning" title="Henry Edward Manning">Henry Edward Manning</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
Catherine of Genoa's liturgical feast is celebrated in local calendars on 15 September. <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII" title="Pope Pius XII">Pope Pius XII</a> declared her patroness of the hospitals in Italy.<sup id="cite_ref-ODCC_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ODCC-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 2022, Catherine was officially added to the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church liturgical calendar</a> with a feast day on 15 September.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
