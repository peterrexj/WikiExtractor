<p><b>King Charles the Martyr</b>, or <b>Charles, King and Martyr</b>, is a title of <a href="/wiki/Charles_I_of_England" title="Charles I of England">Charles I</a>, who was <a href="/wiki/King_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="King of England">King of England</a>, <a href="/wiki/King_of_Scotland" class="mw-redirect" title="King of Scotland">Scotland</a> and <a href="/wiki/King_of_Ireland" class="mw-redirect" title="King of Ireland">Ireland</a> from 1625 until <a href="/wiki/Execution_of_Charles_I" title="Execution of Charles I">his execution</a> on 30 January 1649. The title is used by <a href="/wiki/High_church" title="High church">high church</a> <a href="/wiki/Anglicans" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglicans">Anglicans</a> who regard Charles's execution as a <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyrdom</a>. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Anglican_calendar_of_saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican calendar of saints">Anglican calendar of saints</a> is 30 January,<sup id="cite_ref-churchofengland_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-churchofengland-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> the anniversary of his execution in 1649. The <a href="/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)" title="Cult (religious practice)">cult</a> of Charles the Martyr was historically popular with <a href="/wiki/Tories_(British_political_party)" title="Tories (British political party)">Tories</a>. The observance was one of several "state services" <a href="/wiki/Anniversary_Days_Observance_Act_1859" title="Anniversary Days Observance Act 1859">removed in 1859</a> from the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Common_Prayer" title="Book of Common Prayer">Book of Common Prayer</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Ireland" title="Church of Ireland">Church of Ireland</a>. There remain some churches and parishes <a href="/wiki/Dedication_(ritual)" title="Dedication (ritual)">dedicated</a> to Charles the Martyr, and his cult is maintained by some <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Catholic">Anglo-Catholic</a> societies, including the <a href="/wiki/Society_of_King_Charles_the_Martyr" title="Society of King Charles the Martyr">Society of King Charles the Martyr</a> founded in 1894 and the <a href="/wiki/Royal_Martyr_Church_Union" title="Royal Martyr Church Union">Royal Martyr Church Union</a> founded in 1906.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Reign">Reign</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=King_Charles_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Reign">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Charles_I_of_England" title="Charles I of England">Charles I of England</a></div>
<p>Charles I, head of the <a href="/wiki/House_of_Stuart" title="House of Stuart">House of Stuart</a>, was King of <a href="/wiki/England" title="England">England</a>, <a href="/wiki/Scotland" title="Scotland">Scotland</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Ireland" title="Ireland">Ireland</a> from 27 March 1625 until his death on 30 January 1649. He believed in a sacramental version of the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a>, called <a href="/wiki/Anglicanism" title="Anglicanism">High Anglicanism</a>, with a theology based upon <a href="/wiki/Arminianism" title="Arminianism">Arminianism</a>, a belief shared by his main political advisor, Archbishop <a href="/wiki/William_Laud" title="William Laud">William Laud</a>. Laud was appointed by Charles as the <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a> in 1633 and started a series of reforms in the Church to make it more ceremonial. This was actively hostile to the <a href="/wiki/Reformed_churches" class="mw-redirect" title="Reformed churches">Reformist</a> tendencies of many of his English and Scottish subjects. He rejected the <a href="/wiki/Calvinism" title="Calvinism">Calvinism</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Presbyterian" class="mw-redirect" title="Presbyterian">Presbyterians</a>, insisted on an <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_polity" title="Episcopal polity">episcopal</a> (hierarchical) form of church government as opposed to <a href="/wiki/Presbyterian_polity" title="Presbyterian polity">presbyterian</a> or <a href="/wiki/Congregationalist_polity" title="Congregationalist polity">congregational</a> forms, and required that the Church of England's <a href="/wiki/Liturgy" title="Liturgy">liturgy</a> be celebrated with all of the ceremony and <a href="/wiki/Vestment" title="Vestment">vestments</a> called for by the 1604 <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Common_Prayer" title="Book of Common Prayer">Book of Common Prayer</a>. Many of his subjects thought these policies brought the Church of England too close to <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholicism</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Parliament_of_England" title="Parliament of England">Parliament of England</a> objected both to Charles's religious policies and to his <a href="/wiki/Personal_Rule" title="Personal Rule">Personal Rule</a> from 1629 to 1640, during which he never summoned Parliament. These disputes contributed to the <a href="/wiki/English_Civil_War" title="English Civil War">English Civil War</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Trial_and_execution">Trial and execution</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=King_Charles_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Trial and execution">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: <a href="/wiki/High_Court_of_Justice_for_the_trial_of_Charles_I" class="mw-redirect" title="High Court of Justice for the trial of Charles I">High Court of Justice for the trial of Charles I</a> and <a href="/wiki/Execution_of_Charles_I" title="Execution of Charles I">Execution of Charles I</a></div>
<p>After the royalists were defeated by the Parliamentarians, Charles was <a href="/wiki/High_Court_of_Justice_for_the_trial_of_Charles_I" class="mw-redirect" title="High Court of Justice for the trial of Charles I">put on trial</a>. He was charged with attempting to govern as an <a href="/wiki/Absolute_monarch" class="mw-redirect" title="Absolute monarch">absolute monarch</a> rather than in combination with Parliament; with fighting against his people; with continuing the war after the defeat of his forces (the <a href="/wiki/Second_English_Civil_War" title="Second English Civil War">Second English Civil War</a>); with conspiring after defeat to promote yet another continuation; and with encouraging his troops to kill prisoners of war.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He was sentenced to death.
</p><p>There is some historical debate over the identity of the man who beheaded the King, who was masked at the scene. It is known the regicides approached <a href="/wiki/Richard_Brandon" title="Richard Brandon">Richard Brandon</a>, the common Hangman of London, but that he refused, and contemporary sources do not generally identify him as the King's headsman. Ellis's Historical Inquiries, however, name him as the executioner, stating that he stated so before dying. It is possible he relented and agreed to do the deed, but there are others who have been identified. <a href="/wiki/William_Hewlett_(regicide)" title="William Hewlett (regicide)">William Hewlett</a> was tried for the murder after the Restoration and convicted. In 1661, two people identified as "Dayborne and Bickerstaffe" were arrested but then discharged. Henry Walker, a revolutionary journalist, or his brother William, were suspected but never charged. Various local legends around England name local figures.
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/Philip_Henry_(clergyman)" class="mw-redirect" title="Philip Henry (clergyman)">Philip Henry</a>, the decapitation was greeted by a moan from the assembled crowd, some of whom then dipped their handkerchiefs in Charles'  blood, thus starting the cult of the Martyr King. Henry was a royalist propagandist; neither <a href="/wiki/Samuel_Pepys" title="Samuel Pepys">Samuel Pepys</a> nor any other eyewitness source corroborates him.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It was common practice for the head of a traitor to be held up and exhibited to the crowd with the words "Behold the head of a traitor!" Although Charles's head was exhibited, the words were not used. In an unprecedented gesture, one of the prominent leaders of the revolutionaries, <a href="/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell" title="Oliver Cromwell">Oliver Cromwell</a>, allowed the King's head to be sewn back on his body so the family could pay its respects. Charles was buried privately and at night on 7 February 1649, in the <a href="/wiki/Henry_VIII_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Henry VIII of England">Henry VIII</a> vault inside <a href="/wiki/St._George%27s_Chapel,_Windsor" class="mw-redirect" title="St. George&#39;s Chapel, Windsor">St George's Chapel</a> in <a href="/wiki/Windsor_Castle" title="Windsor Castle">Windsor Castle</a>. The King's son, King <a href="/wiki/Charles_II_of_England" title="Charles II of England">Charles II</a>, later planned an elaborate royal mausoleum which was never built.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Martyrdom">Martyrdom</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=King_Charles_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Martyrdom">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Eikon.png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Eikon.png/220px-Eikon.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="198" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Eikon.png/330px-Eikon.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Eikon.png/440px-Eikon.png 2x" data-file-width="600" data-file-height="541" /></a><figcaption>The <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><i lang="grc-Latn"><a href="/wiki/Eikon_Basilike" title="Eikon Basilike">Eikon Basilike</a></i></span>, a purported spiritual autobiography attributed to Charles I, published days after his execution</figcaption></figure>
<p>Charles is regarded by many members of the Church of England as a <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a> because, it is said,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> he was offered his life if he would abandon the historic <a href="/wiki/Episcopacy" class="mw-redirect" title="Episcopacy">episcopacy</a> in the Church of England. It is said he refused, however, believing that the Church of England was truly "<a href="/wiki/Catholic_(term)" title="Catholic (term)">Catholic</a>" and should maintain the Catholic episcopate. His designation in the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">Church of England's calendar</a> is "Charles, King and Martyr, 1649".<sup id="cite_ref-churchofengland_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-churchofengland-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>  <a href="/wiki/Mandell_Creighton" title="Mandell Creighton">Mandell Creighton</a>, <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_London" title="Bishop of London">Bishop of London</a>, wrote "Had Charles been willing to abandon the Church and give up episcopacy, he might have saved his throne and his life. But on this point Charles stood firm: for this he died, and by dying saved it for the future." In fact, Charles had already made an <a href="/wiki/Engagers" title="Engagers">Engagement with the Scots</a> to introduce <a href="/wiki/English_Presbyterianism" title="English Presbyterianism">Presbyterianism in England</a> for three years in return for the aid of Scots forces in the <a href="/wiki/Second_English_Civil_War" title="Second English Civil War">Second English Civil War</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Both high church Anglicans and royalists fashioned an image of martyrdom,<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> and after the 1660 <a href="/wiki/Restoration_(1660)" class="mw-redirect" title="Restoration (1660)">Restoration of the monarchy</a> the Church of England's <a href="/wiki/Convocations_of_Canterbury_and_York" title="Convocations of Canterbury and York">Convocations of Canterbury and York</a> added the <a href="/wiki/January_30" title="January 30">date of Charles's martyrdom</a> to <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">its liturgical calendar</a><sup id="cite_ref-Mitchell2012_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mitchell2012-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> (<a href="/wiki/Lesser_Festival_(Anglicanism)" title="Lesser Festival (Anglicanism)">Lesser Festival</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Observance">Observance</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=King_Charles_the_Martyr&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Observance">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Calendar of the Book of Common Prayer included among the <a href="/wiki/Red_letter_day" title="Red letter day">red letter days</a> "state services" commemorations of the <a href="/wiki/Gunpowder_Plot" title="Gunpowder Plot">Gunpowder Plot</a>, the birth and <a href="/wiki/Restoration_(1660)" class="mw-redirect" title="Restoration (1660)">restoration</a> of <a href="/wiki/Charles_II_of_England" title="Charles II of England">Charles II</a>, and the execution of Charles I. In addition, a <a href="/wiki/Proclamation" title="Proclamation">proclamation</a> made at the beginning of each reign from Charles II to <a href="/wiki/Victoria_of_the_United_Kingdom" class="mw-redirect" title="Victoria of the United Kingdom">Victoria</a> annexed special services for these days to the Prayer Book by royal mandate (approved unanimously by <a href="/wiki/Convocations_of_Canterbury_and_York" title="Convocations of Canterbury and York">Convocation</a>). Special sermons were preached, and hundreds of sermons on King Charles the Martyr were printed from the 1660s until the late eighteenth century. The title of the service for 30 January was:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><div class="poem">
<p>A FORM OF PRAYER WITH FASTING, <br />
To be used yearly on the Thirtieth of January, <br />
Being the Day of the Martyrdom of the Blessed King CHARLES the First; <br />
to implore the mercy of God, that neither the Guilt of that sacred and innocent Blood, nor those other sins, by which God was provoked to deliver up both us and our King into the hands of cruel and unreasonable men, may at any time hereafter be visited upon us or our posterity.
</p>
</div></blockquote>
<p>In 1859 <a href="/wiki/Anniversary_Days_Observance_Act_1859" title="Anniversary Days Observance Act 1859">the State Services were omitted from the Prayer Book</a> by royal and parliamentary authority but without the consent of Convocation. <a href="/wiki/Vernon_Staley" title="Vernon Staley">Vernon Staley</a> in 1907 described the deletion as <i><a href="/wiki/Ultra_vires" title="Ultra vires">ultra vires</a></i> and  "a distinct violation of the compact between Church and Realm, as set forth in the Act of Uniformity which imposed the Book of Common Prayer in 1662".<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Of the three commemorations, only that of King Charles I has been restored in the calendar in the <a href="/wiki/Alternative_Service_Book" title="Alternative Service Book">Alternative Service Book</a> of 1980 – although not as a Red Letter Day – and a new collect composed for <a href="/wiki/Common_Worship" title="Common Worship">Common Worship</a> in 2000.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Society_of_King_Charles_the_Martyr" title="Society of King Charles the Martyr">Society of King Charles the Martyr</a> campaigns for restoration in England of the observance to the Book of Common Prayer. It is included in some of the calendars of other Churches of the Anglican Communion.
</p>

<li><a href="/w/index.php?title=Church_of_King_Charles_the_Martyr,_Potters_Bar&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Church of King Charles the Martyr, Potters Bar (page does not exist)">Church of King Charles the Martyr, Potters Bar</a>, Hertfordshire</li>

Destroyed in <a href="/wiki/The_Blitz" title="The Blitz">the Blitz</a>; only ruins remain.</li>
<li>Chapel of the <a href="/wiki/Royal_Hospital,_Kilmainham" class="mw-redirect" title="Royal Hospital, Kilmainham">Royal Hospital, Kilmainham</a>, Dublin, Ireland</li></ul>
