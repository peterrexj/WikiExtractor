<p><b>Shenoute of Atripe</b>, also known as <b>Shenoute the Great</b> or <b>Saint Shenoute the <a href="/wiki/Archimandrite" title="Archimandrite">Archimandrite</a></b> (<a href="/wiki/Coptic_language" title="Coptic language">Coptic</a>: <style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1107333977">.mw-parser-output .script-coptic{font-family:"Sophia Nubian","Noto Sans Coptic","FreeSerif","Quivira","Segoe UI Historic","Segoe UI Symbol","New Athena Unicode","MPH 2B Damase","Arial Coptic","Analecta","Antinoou"}</style><span class="script-coptic">Ⲁⲃⲃⲁ Ϣⲉⲛⲟⲩϯ ⲡⲓⲁⲣⲭⲓⲙⲁⲛ'ⲇⲣⲓⲧⲏⲥ</span>; (347-465 or 348-466<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2013)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>) was the abbot of the <a href="/wiki/White_Monastery" title="White Monastery">White Monastery</a> in <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a>. He is considered a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox_Churches" title="Oriental Orthodox Churches">Oriental Orthodox Churches</a>, and is one of the most renowned <a href="/wiki/Saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Saints">saints</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church" title="Coptic Orthodox Church">Coptic Orthodox Church</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Shenoute&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:The_Life_of_Shenoute,_Sahidic_Coptic_script,_papyrus,_6th-7th_century_CE._From_Egypt._British_Museum.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/The_Life_of_Shenoute%2C_Sahidic_Coptic_script%2C_papyrus%2C_6th-7th_century_CE._From_Egypt._British_Museum.jpg/220px-The_Life_of_Shenoute%2C_Sahidic_Coptic_script%2C_papyrus%2C_6th-7th_century_CE._From_Egypt._British_Museum.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/The_Life_of_Shenoute%2C_Sahidic_Coptic_script%2C_papyrus%2C_6th-7th_century_CE._From_Egypt._British_Museum.jpg/330px-The_Life_of_Shenoute%2C_Sahidic_Coptic_script%2C_papyrus%2C_6th-7th_century_CE._From_Egypt._British_Museum.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/The_Life_of_Shenoute%2C_Sahidic_Coptic_script%2C_papyrus%2C_6th-7th_century_CE._From_Egypt._British_Museum.jpg/440px-The_Life_of_Shenoute%2C_Sahidic_Coptic_script%2C_papyrus%2C_6th-7th_century_CE._From_Egypt._British_Museum.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3332" data-file-height="4444" /></a><figcaption>The Life of Shenoute, Sahidic Coptic script, papyrus, 6th-7th century AD. From Egypt. British Museum</figcaption></figure>
<p>Shenoute was born in the middle of the fourth century AD<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> (the date 348 AD, often mentioned but not universally accepted, is based on an inscription in his monastery, dating from the twelfth or thirteen century<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>).
</p><p>Around 385 AD, Shenoute became the father of the <a href="/wiki/White_Monastery" title="White Monastery">White Monastery</a> in <a href="/wiki/Upper_Egypt" title="Upper Egypt">Upper Egypt</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> It has often been assumed that Shenoute was the immediate successor of the White Monastery's founder, Pcol. However, the reconstruction of Shenoute's literary corpus made it possible to realize that Pcol died in the 370s and was then succeeded not by Shenoute but by another father, Ebonh, and that a spiritual crisis during Ebonh's tenure as head of the White Monastery, a crisis which seems to have involved carnal sin, enabled Shenoute to come to prominence and to become Ebonh's immediate successor.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="At_the_Council_of_Ephesus">At the Council of Ephesus</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Shenoute&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: At the Council of Ephesus">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Because of his popularity in <a href="/wiki/Upper_Egypt" title="Upper Egypt">Upper Egypt</a> and his zeal for <a href="/wiki/Orthodoxy" title="Orthodoxy">Orthodoxy</a>, Shenoute was chosen by <a href="/wiki/Cyril_of_Alexandria" title="Cyril of Alexandria">Saint Cyril the Great</a> to accompany him in representing the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_Alexandria" title="Church of Alexandria">Church of Alexandria</a> at the <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Ephesus" class="mw-redirect" title="First Council of Ephesus">Ecumenical Council of Ephesus</a> in 431 AD.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> There he provided the moral support that Saint Cyril needed to defeat the heresy of <a href="/wiki/Nestorius" title="Nestorius">Nestorius</a>, Bishop of <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>. The eventual exile of the latter to <a href="/wiki/Akhmim" title="Akhmim">Akhmim</a>, Shenoute's backyard, was a testimony to the impression that Shenoute had made upon the attendees of this council.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Death">Death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Shenoute&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On 7 <a href="/wiki/Epip" title="Epip">Epip</a> (14 July) 466 AD, following a short illness possibly brought upon by advanced age, Shenoute died in the presence of his monks.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Influence_on_the_monastic_movement">Influence on the monastic movement</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Shenoute&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Influence on the monastic movement">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>From his uncle, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Pigol&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Saint Pigol (page does not exist)">Saint Pigol</a>, Shenoute inherited a <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> based on the <a href="/wiki/Pachomius" class="mw-redirect" title="Pachomius">Pachomian</a> system, though more austere and stringent. This made its followers few in number and probably promoted decline rather than growth. Shenoute implemented a more comprehensive system that was less stringent and more suitable to the surroundings and the background of the people. This new system had an unusual component, which was a covenant (diatheke) to be recited and adhered to literally by the new novices. It read as follows:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>I vow before God in His Holy Place, the word which I have spoken with my mouth being my witness; I will not defile my body in any way, I will not steal, I will not bear false witness, I will not lie, I will not do anything deceitful secretly. If I transgressed what I have vowed, I will see the Kingdom of Heaven, but will not enter it. God before whom I made the covenant will destroy my soul and my body in the fiery Hell because I transgressed the covenant I made.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite>Bell, the Life of Shenute by Besa, pp.9-10</cite></div></blockquote>
<p>Transgressors of that covenant were expelled from the monastery altogether. This was considered a near death sentence for those peasant monks.
</p><p>Another interesting feature of Shenoute's monastic system was the requirement for the new novices to live outside the monastery for a period of time before they were deemed worthy to be consecrated as monks. This seemed to be at odds with the <a href="/wiki/Nitrian_Desert" title="Nitrian Desert">Nitrian</a> monastic system, which allowed the monks to live away from the monastic settlements only after they became proficient in the monastic life. Shenoute also utilized the time of the monks, outside prayer and worship, in more varied tasks within the monastery than the <a href="/wiki/Nitrian_Desert" title="Nitrian Desert">Nitrian</a> monks were exposed to. Aside from the traditional trades of rope and basket weaving, the monks engaged in weaving and tailoring linen, cultivation of flax, leather work and shoe-making, writing and book-binding, carpentry, and metal and pottering-making. All in all, Shenouda tried as much as possible to employ the monks in their old professions. Such activities made the monastery a vast self-supporting complex, which occupied some 20 square miles (52&#160;km<sup>2</sup>) of land.
</p><p>As a monastic leader, Shenoute recognized the need for literacy among monks. So he required all his monks and nuns to learn to read and encouraged more of them to pursue the art of writing manuscripts. This made the monastery more and more appealing to belong to and consequently made the threat of expulsion more painful.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy_as_a_national_leader">Legacy as a national leader</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Shenoute&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Legacy as a national leader">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: <a href="/wiki/Apocalypse_of_Shenute" title="Apocalypse of Shenute">Apocalypse of Shenute</a></div>
<p>In his laudatory <i>Life of Saint Shenoute</i>, his disciple and biographer <a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Wissa&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Saint Wissa (page does not exist)">Saint Wissa</a> (Besa) recounts several incidents of Shenoute coming to the aid of poor Coptic peasants. One time he went to <a href="/wiki/Akhmim" title="Akhmim">Akhmim</a> to chastise a pagan because of the oppression he was inflicting on the poor (Vita #81-2). Another time he acted to eliminate the cause of grief of the peasants, that the pagan landlords of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Paneleou&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Paneleou (page does not exist)">Paneleou</a> forced them to buy the latter's spoiled wine (Vita #85-6). On a third occasion he risked his life to successfully ask for the freedom of the captives at <a href="/w/index.php?title=Psoi&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Psoi (page does not exist)">Psoi</a> from the hands of the <a href="/wiki/Blemmyes" title="Blemmyes">Blemmyes</a> warriors (Vita #89). He also at times appealed on behalf of the peasants to those in power, including the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Empire" title="Roman Empire">Roman</a> <a href="/wiki/Emperor" title="Emperor">emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Theodosius_I" title="Theodosius I">Theodosius I</a>. In summary, Shenoute fully recognized the misery of his people and emerged as their sincere advocate and popular leader.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life_as_a_writer">Life as a writer</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Shenoute&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Life as a writer">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>To talk about Shenoute's writing is to discuss <a href="/wiki/Coptic_literature" title="Coptic literature">Coptic literature</a> at its best. He wrote in a style that was essentially his own, with writings based on a careful study of the scholastic rhetoric of his time, which displayed the wide and deep range of knowledge he possessed. They were adorned with endless quotations from the Holy Scriptures, a typical feature of patristic writings. The scriptures were quoted whenever a presented argument needed support. In doing so Shenoute also displayed an astonishing memory as he rendered these passages with amazing accuracy.
</p><p>Shenoute's knowledge was not confined to the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Bible" class="mw-redirect" title="Holy Bible">Holy Bible</a>, as was the case for the majority of the monks in <a href="/wiki/Egypt" title="Egypt">Egypt</a>. He was fluent in both <a href="/wiki/Coptic_language" title="Coptic language">Coptic</a> and <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>, and was fairly well acquainted with <a href="/wiki/Greeks" title="Greeks">Greek</a> thought and theology. The sprinkling of Greek loan-words in his writings was both extensive and sophisticated, and it was definitely not a product of his living environment. He also expressed knowledge of the works of <a href="/wiki/Aristotle" title="Aristotle">Aristotle</a>, <a href="/wiki/Aristophanes" title="Aristophanes">Aristophanes</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Plato" title="Plato">Platonic</a> school, and even some of the <a href="/wiki/Greek_mythology" title="Greek mythology">Greek</a> legends. He certainly read some of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Athanasius" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Athanasius">Saint Athanasius</a>' works like the Life of <a href="/wiki/Anthony_the_Great" title="Anthony the Great">Saint Anthony</a> and some of his homiletic works. Shenoute also knew the letters of Saint Anthony, some of the letters of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Pachomius" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Pachomius">Saint Pachomius</a>, and most likely some of the works of <a href="/wiki/Evagrius_Ponticus" title="Evagrius Ponticus">Evagrius</a>. His knowledge further extended to such popular non-canonical texts as the <a href="/w/index.php?title=Acts_of_Archelaus&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Acts of Archelaus (page does not exist)">Acts of Archelaus</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Gospel_of_Thomas" title="Gospel of Thomas">Gospel of Thomas</a>.
</p><p>The writing of Saint Shenoute can be grouped into four categories:
</p>
<ul><li>Moral sermons: This category includes the richest collection that have survived from Shenoute's writings. Among his works here is one about the disobedience to clerics <i>De Disoboedientia ad Clericos</i>, in which he stressed the benefit of obedience and the punishment of the disobedient. He also wrote about the <a href="/wiki/Nativity_of_Jesus" title="Nativity of Jesus">Nativity</a> and the glorification of the Lord, where he discussed <a href="/wiki/Free_will" title="Free will">free will</a> and the place of chastity in the monastic life <i>De Castitate et Nativitate</i>.</li>
<li>Sermons against the pagans: This category represents an important side of Shenoute's thinking. In one place, he portrayed the <a href="/wiki/Paganism" title="Paganism">pagans</a> as worse than demons whose idols shall rightly be destroyed by the <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christians</a>. In another sermon he aimed his attack against a pagan, probably a magistrate, who troubles the monks <i>Adversus Saturnum</i>. In a third sermon he attacks the concept of fate, in the opinion of the idolaters, as the controlling factor in the life of a person. He encounters with the teaching that nothing actually happens without the will of God <i>Contra Idolatras, de Spatio Vitae</i>.</li>
<li>Sermons against the heretics: This category is similar conceptually to the preceding one. Here Shenoute directs his attack against the heretics who corrupted the faith. One encounters in this category one of Shenoute's longest works, which was probably written as a treatise rather than just a sermon. This is the work against the <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origenists</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Gnostic" class="mw-redirect" title="Gnostic">Gnostics</a> <i>Contra Origenistas et Gnosticos</i>. The aim of this work was to oppose heretics in general and <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origenists</a> in particular, with regards to their apocryphal books that they used and circulated. He also touched upon the subjects of the plurality of the worlds, the position and the work of the Savior, and the meaning of <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Easter</a>. Other subjects mentioned in the treatise included the relationship between the Father and the Son, the origin of souls, <a href="/wiki/Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Christ">Christ</a>'s Conception, the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a>, resurrection of the body, and the four elements. Among the other works within this category were against the <a href="/wiki/Melitians" title="Melitians">Melitians</a>, in regard to the multiple celebration of the <a href="/wiki/Eucharist" title="Eucharist">Eucharist</a> in one day; against the <a href="/wiki/Manichaeans" class="mw-redirect" title="Manichaeans">Manichaeans</a>, concerning the value of the <a href="/wiki/Old_Testament" title="Old Testament">Old Testament</a> alongside the <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a>; and against <a href="/wiki/Nestorius" title="Nestorius">Nestorius</a> in relations to the preexistence of Christ before His birth from the <a href="/wiki/Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Virgin Mary">Virgin</a>.</li>
<li>Sermons based on interviews with magistrates that visited him: This final category represents sermons that were based on miscellaneous interviews that he held with magistrates who visited him as a consequence of his fame and great authority. In those sermons Shenoute touches upon such arguments as the appropriateness of him correcting even generals in spiritual matters, the dimensions of the sky and the earth, the devil and free will, and the punishment of sinners. He also discussed the duties of judges and other such important personages as bishops, wealthy people, and generals.</li></ul>
<p>As more and more identifications of Saint Shenoute's literary works are made, his contribution to Coptic literature appears to be even greater than previously assumed. On the one hand, it is becoming clear that he treated a wide range of subjects, not only monastic ones. This suggests a more favorable assessment of the theological character of his writing, his spirituality, and his moral and nationalistic behavior. On the other hand, he accepted the inclusion of literary activity in the religious field. This sets him apart from the <a href="/wiki/Pachomius" class="mw-redirect" title="Pachomius">Pachomian</a> system that tended to treat religious literature as mere written instructions with no regard to style being given. He further developed a style that is clearly a product of careful study of the scholastic <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a> rhetoric of his time.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Monasteries_named_after_Saint_Shenoute">Monasteries named after Saint Shenoute</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Shenoute&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Monasteries named after Saint Shenoute">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:SaintShenouda.JPG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/85/SaintShenouda.JPG/220px-SaintShenouda.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/85/SaintShenouda.JPG/330px-SaintShenouda.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/85/SaintShenouda.JPG/440px-SaintShenouda.JPG 2x" data-file-width="2816" data-file-height="2112" /></a><figcaption>St. Shenouda Monastery, New South Wales, Australia</figcaption></figure>
<p>Four Coptic Orthodox monasteries worldwide are named after Saint Shenouda the Archimandrite, namely:
</p>
<ul><li><a href="/wiki/White_Monastery" title="White Monastery">Monastery of Saint Shenouda</a> near <a href="/wiki/Sohag" title="Sohag">Sohag</a>, Egypt, also known as the White Monastery</li>
<li>Monastery of Saint Shenouda in <a href="/wiki/Milano" class="mw-redirect" title="Milano">Milano</a>, Italy</li>
<li>Coptic Monastery of St. Shenouda in <a href="/wiki/Rochester,_New_York" title="Rochester, New York">Rochester, New York</a>, U.S.</li>
<li>St. Shenouda Coptic Orthodox Monastery in <a href="/wiki/Putty,_New_South_Wales" title="Putty, New South Wales">Putty, New South Wales</a>, Australia</li></ul>
<p>There is also a Coptic Orthodox church – St. Mary and St. Shenouda – in <a href="/wiki/Coulsdon" title="Coulsdon">Coulsdon</a>, England.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Works_and_Translations">Works and Translations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Shenoute&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Works and Translations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>There has been difficulty in accessing and reconstructing the works of Shenoute for a variety of readings. The number of legible manuscripts his works survive in are not many, and he wrote in Coptic, which is a language that ceased in use early in the history of Christianity, which added to the obscurity of his writings. For a long time, his works stopped being transmitted, and the remaining manuscripts were subject to decay from then one. Shenoute was responsible for collecting his own literary works into two collections, which were then transmitted. These were the <i>Canons</i> and the <i>Discourses</i> (or <i>Logoi</i>).<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Johannes Leipoldt published several volumes of his works, although he did so without access to all the manuscripts and so did not produce a critical edition.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> The transmission and structure of these two collections were first reconstructed in a dissertation by Stephen Emmel.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Dwight Young has published some Coptic editions of this work with English translation.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Bentley Layton has additionally published new Coptic texts of his rule and their translations.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
