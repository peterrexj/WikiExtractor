					<div id="contentSub"><div id="mw-content-subtitle"></div></div>
					
					
					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Bishop in Laodicea, Syria, during the early 4th century CE</div>
<p><b>Theodotus</b> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: Θεόδοτος; c. 260 – c. 335) was the bishop of <a href="/wiki/Laodicea_in_Syria" title="Laodicea in Syria">Laodicea in Syria</a> from the early 300s. He replaced Stephen,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> who apostasized during the <a href="/wiki/Great_Persecution" class="mw-redirect" title="Great Persecution">Great Persecution</a> (303–313). The exact year of his consecration cannot be fixed more precisely.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008254_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008254-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> He attended at least four church councils.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008261_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008261-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea" class="mw-redirect" title="Eusebius of Caesarea">Eusebius of Caesarea</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Church_History_(Eusebius)" title="Church History (Eusebius)">Historia ecclesiastica</a></i>, Theodotus "proved his personal name ... true" and was a gift from God to the diocese of Laodicea.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> Eusebius also praises him for his knowledge of "the science of bodily healing" and for being a "dedicated student of divine teachings", implying that he was trained in both medicine and theology prior to becoming bishop. He used his medical training to treat the faithful during the Great Persecution. Eusebius and Theodotus were probably born around the same time. They were close friends and ecclesiastical allies throughout their episcopal careers. Eusebius dedicated two apologetic works, <i><a href="/wiki/Praeparatio_evangelica" title="Praeparatio evangelica">Praeparatio evangelica</a></i> and <i>Demonstratio evangelica</i>, both written between about 312 and 318, to Theodotus.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008256–257_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008256–257-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the <a href="/wiki/Arian_controversy" title="Arian controversy">Trinitarian controversy</a> of the early fourth century, Theodotus sided with <a href="/wiki/Arius_of_Alexandria" class="mw-redirect" title="Arius of Alexandria">Arius of Alexandria</a>. In a letter to <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Nicomedia" title="Eusebius of Nicomedia">Eusebius of Nicomedia</a> around 321, Arius named Theodotus as one of his eastern supporters along with Eusebius of Caesarea, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Paulinus_of_Tyre&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Paulinus of Tyre (page does not exist)">Paulinus of Tyre</a>, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Athanasius_of_Anazarbus&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Athanasius of Anazarbus (page does not exist)">Athanasius of Anazarbus</a>, <a href="/w/index.php?title=Gregory_of_Berytus&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Gregory of Berytus (page does not exist)">Gregory of Berytus</a> and <a href="/w/index.php?title=A%C3%ABtius_of_Lydda&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Aëtius of Lydda (page does not exist)">Aëtius of Lydda</a>. According to Arius' chief rival, <a href="/wiki/Athanasius_of_Alexandria" title="Athanasius of Alexandria">Athanasius of Alexandria</a>, Theodotus wrote in support of Arius, but he does not quote any of his writings.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008257_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008257-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Theodotus attended the provincial <a href="/wiki/Synods_of_Antioch" title="Synods of Antioch">council of Antioch</a> held in late 324 or early 325. He was one of only three bishops<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup> who refused to subscribe to the council's statement of faith, which was based on the theology of the bishop of Alexandria, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Alexander_I_of_Alexandria" title="Pope Alexander I of Alexandria">Alexander I</a>. They were excommunicated pending their recantation at the <a href="/wiki/First_Council_of_Nicaea" title="First Council of Nicaea">"great and holy synod"</a> scheduled for June 325. Theodotus attended the synod, which took place in <a href="/wiki/Nicaea" title="Nicaea">Nicaea</a>, and subscribed to the <a href="/wiki/Nicene_Creed" title="Nicene Creed">Nicene Creed</a>, probably on the basis of the explanation of terminology given by the Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantine_I" class="mw-redirect" title="Constantine I">Constantine I</a>. Three months after Nicaea, Constantine banished Eusebius of Nicomedia and <a href="/wiki/Theognis_of_Nicaea" title="Theognis of Nicaea">Theognis of Nicaea</a> for communicating with some of Arius' deposed supporters. Shortly after, he wrote to Theodotus warning him from committing the same error.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008257–259_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008257–259-10">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Theodotus attended the council in Antioch in late 327 that deposed Bishop <a href="/wiki/Eustathius_of_Antioch" title="Eustathius of Antioch">Eustathius of Antioch</a> on charges of <a href="/wiki/Sabellianism" title="Sabellianism">Sabellianism</a>. He may have presided over the council of bishops that met in 328 to elect a new bishop of Antioch. His last known act was the excommunication of <a href="/wiki/Apollinaris_(the_Elder)" title="Apollinaris (the Elder)">Apollinarius the Elder</a> and his son, <a href="/wiki/Apollinaris_of_Laodicea" title="Apollinaris of Laodicea">Apollinarius the Younger</a>. They had been part of a group of Christians attending a lecture by the sophist <a href="/wiki/Epiphanius_of_Syria" class="mw-redirect" title="Epiphanius of Syria">Epiphanius of Syria</a>. According to custom, Epiphanius dismissed the "uninitiated and profane" (a cue to Christians to leave) before reciting a hymn to the pagan god <a href="/wiki/Dionysus" title="Dionysus">Dionysus</a>. The Apollinarii and some other Christians stayed throughout. Theodotus reprimanded them and excommunicated the Apollinarii father and son for setting a bad example, since they were a <a href="/wiki/Presbyter" title="Presbyter">presbyter</a> and <a href="/wiki/Lector" title="Lector">lector</a> in the church, respectively. They were readmitted to communion after repentance. This took place sometime between about 328 and 335. By 335, <a href="/wiki/George_of_Laodicea" title="George of Laodicea">George of Alexandria</a> had replaced Theodotus as bishop of Laodicea.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008259–260_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008259–260-11">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Constantine's letter implies that Theodotus was a leading figure among those supporters of Arius who signed the Nicene Creed.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008257–259_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008257–259-10">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Theodoret" title="Theodoret">Theodoret</a>, writing over a century later, considered him one of the leaders of <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008260n_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008260n-12">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Because none of his writing survives, it is impossible to know Theodotus' theology with any exactitude.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;d&#93;</a></sup> He may have defended Arius more out of loyalty to the Alexandrian church, with which Laodicea had strong connections, than out of strong theological agreement.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008261_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelCogliano2008261-3">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He is sometimes called a <a href="/wiki/Eusebians" class="mw-redirect" title="Eusebians">Eusebian</a>, that is, a follower of Eusebius of Nicomedia.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBlumell2012278_n6_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlumell2012278_n6-14">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
