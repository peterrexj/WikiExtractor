<p><b>Thecla</b> (<a href="/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Greek language">Ancient Greek</a>: <span lang="grc">Θέκλα</span>, <span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn">Thékla</i></span>; <a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a>: <span lang="el">Θέκλα</span>; <a href="/wiki/Turkish_language" title="Turkish language">Turkish</a>: <i lang="tr">Tekla</i>) was a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Early_Christian" class="mw-redirect" title="Early Christian">early Christian</a> Church, and a reported follower of <a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle" title="Paul the Apostle">Paul the Apostle</a>. The earliest record of her life comes from the ancient <a href="/wiki/New_Testament_apocrypha" title="New Testament apocrypha">apocryphal</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Acts_of_Paul_and_Thecla" title="Acts of Paul and Thecla">Acts of Paul and Thecla</a></i>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Church_tradition">Church tradition</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Church tradition">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main articles: <a href="/wiki/Acts_of_Paul_and_Thecla" title="Acts of Paul and Thecla">Acts of Paul and Thecla</a> and <a href="/wiki/Life_and_Miracles_of_Saint_Thecla" title="Life and Miracles of Saint Thecla">Life and Miracles of Saint Thecla</a></div>
<p>The <i>Acts of Paul and Thecla</i> is a 2nd-century text (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;AD&#160;180</span>) which forms part of the <i><a href="/wiki/Acts_of_Paul" title="Acts of Paul">Acts of Paul</a></i>, but was also circulated separately. According to the text, Thecla was a young noble virgin from <a href="/wiki/Konya" title="Konya">Iconium</a> who chose to leave her fiance so she could convert to Christianity and follow Paul.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the text, it is said that Thecla spent three days sitting by her window, listening to Paul speak about the Christian God and the importance of living in chastity. Thecla's mother, Theoclia, and fiance, Thamyris, became concerned that Thecla was going to follow Paul's teachings. They turned to local authorities to punish Paul for being a Christian and "mak[ing] virgins averse to marriage." Paul was sent to prison, where Thecla visited him, kissed his bonds, and refused to leave him and return to her mother and fiance. Paul was made to leave the city and Thecla was condemned to be burned.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>However, Thecla was miraculously saved from <a href="/wiki/Death_by_burning" title="Death by burning">burning at the stake</a> by the onset of a storm. She then encountered Paul outside of <a href="/wiki/Konya" title="Konya">Iconium</a>, where she told him, "I will cut my hair off and I shall follow you wherever you go."<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> She then traveled with Paul to <a href="/wiki/Antioch_of_Pisidia" title="Antioch of Pisidia">Antioch of Pisidia</a>. There, a nobleman named Alexander desired Thecla and attempted to rape her. Thecla fought him off, tore his cloak, and knocked his coronet off his head, which caused her to be put on trial for assault. She was sentenced to be <a href="/wiki/Damnatio_ad_bestias" title="Damnatio ad bestias">eaten by wild beasts</a>, but was again saved by a series of miracles. In one scene, female beasts, particularly lionesses, protected her against her male aggressors. While in the arena, she baptized herself by throwing herself into a nearby lake full of aggressive seals, who were all killed by lightning before they could devour her.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Thecla rejoined Paul in <a href="/wiki/Myra" title="Myra">Myra</a>, "wearing a mantle that she had altered so as to make a man's cloak."<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> As she traveled, she preached the word of God and encouraged women to imitate her by living a life of chastity.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> According to some versions of the <i>Acts</i>, Thecla lived in a cave in <a href="/wiki/Silifke" title="Silifke">Seleucia Cilicia</a> for 72 years, where she continued to spread Christianity.
</p><p>It is also said that Thecla spent the rest of her life in <a href="/wiki/Maaloula" title="Maaloula">Maaloula</a>, a village in Syria. There, she became a healer and performed many miracles, but remained constantly persecuted. In one instance, as her persecutors were about to get to her, she called out to God, a new passage was opened in the cave she was in, and the stones closed behind her. Before her death, she was able to go to Rome and lie down beside Paul's tomb.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Traditions_and_interpretations">Traditions and interpretations</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Traditions and interpretations">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Church Fathers recount a number of traditions about Thecla. <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Nyssa" title="Gregory of Nyssa">Gregory of Nyssa</a> writes in the 4th century (Homily 14 <i>in Cant</i>) that she undertook the sacrifice of herself, by giving death to the flesh,<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> practicing great austerities, extinguishing in herself all earthly affections, so that nothing seemed to remain living in her but reason and spirit: the whole world seemed dead to her as she was to the world.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Macarius_Magnes" title="Macarius Magnes">Macarius Magnes</a>, shortly after AD 300, wrote how the message of Christianity was "the Sword, [Matt 10:34] which cuts relations from each other [Matt:10:35], as it cut Thecla from Theocleia."<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Around AD 280, Thecla features as one of the characters in <a href="/wiki/Methodius_of_Olympus" title="Methodius of Olympus">Methodius of Olympus</a>' <i>Symposium</i>, in which she displays considerable knowledge of profane philosophy, various branches of literature, and eloquent yet modest discourse. Methodius states that she received her instruction in divine and evangelical knowledge from Paul, and was eminent for her skill in sacred science ("Logos 8").
</p><p>The martyrdom of Thecla is frequently referred to in the earliest <i><a href="/wiki/Acts_of_the_Martyrs" title="Acts of the Martyrs">Acts of the Martyrs</a></i>. Eugenius, a martyr of Trebizond under <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a> (284–305), couples Thecla with David and Daniel in his prayers. The exordium of the <i><a href="/w/index.php?title=Acts_of_Polyeuctes&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Acts of Polyeuctes (page does not exist)">Acts of Polyeuctes</a></i> (died 259) refers to Thecla and <a href="/wiki/Perpetua" class="mw-redirect" title="Perpetua">Perpetua</a>, and there were certainly many virgin martyrs who drew their first inspiration from the same source. <a href="/wiki/Eugenia_of_Rome" title="Eugenia of Rome">Eugenia of Rome</a> in the reign of <a href="/wiki/Commodus" title="Commodus">Commodus</a> (180–192) is reported in the Acts of her martyrdom to have taken Thecla as her model.
</p><p>Paul is also an ambiguous figure in the <i>Acts of Paul and Thecla</i>. He is seen as a preacher of <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a>, but one with whom women are besotted. His teachings lead Thecla into trouble, and yet he is never there when the trouble comes. This presentation of Paul as ascetic preacher, discouraging marriage, appears to be very different from that of the <a href="/wiki/Pastoral_Epistles" class="mw-redirect" title="Pastoral Epistles">Pastoral Epistles</a>. For instance, <a href="/wiki/1_Timothy" class="mw-redirect" title="1 Timothy">1 Timothy</a> 4:1–3 has Paul explicitly condemning anyone who forbids marriage. However, <a href="/wiki/1_Corinthians" class="mw-redirect" title="1 Corinthians">1 Corinthians</a> 7 is more ambivalent about marriage, saying that "it is well for a man not to touch a woman" (7:1). This text has been interpreted as ideologically closer to <i>Paul and Thecla</i>. In any event, <i>Paul and Thecla</i> indicates one possible understanding of Paul's legacy in the second century.
</p><p>According to some scholars, Thecla's story inspired many later stories of women saints who dressed as men, including St. <a href="/wiki/Anastasia_the_Patrician" title="Anastasia the Patrician">Anastasia the Patrician</a>, St. Matrona of Perge, St. <a href="/wiki/Euphrosyne_of_Alexandria" title="Euphrosyne of Alexandria">Euphrosyne of Alexandria</a>, St. <a href="/wiki/Life_of_Saint_Apolinaria" title="Life of Saint Apolinaria">Apolinaria</a>, St. <a href="/wiki/Eugenia_of_Rome" title="Eugenia of Rome">Eugenia of Rome</a>, St. <a href="/wiki/Marina_the_Monk" title="Marina the Monk">Marina the Monk</a>, and St. <a href="/wiki/Theodora_of_Alexandria" title="Theodora of Alexandria">Theodora of Alexandria</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Written three or four centuries after the <a href="/wiki/Acts_of_Paul_and_Thecla" title="Acts of Paul and Thecla">Acts of Paul and Thecla</a>, these stories reference Thecla's story through thematic connections, and in the case of <i>The Life of Eugenia</i>, explicitly.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:StTheclaShrine-Latakia.png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/StTheclaShrine-Latakia.png/220px-StTheclaShrine-Latakia.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="151" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/StTheclaShrine-Latakia.png/330px-StTheclaShrine-Latakia.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/StTheclaShrine-Latakia.png/440px-StTheclaShrine-Latakia.png 2x" data-file-width="817" data-file-height="560" /></a><figcaption>St. Thecla Shrine. Latakia, Syria</figcaption></figure>
<p>In the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a>, the wide circulation of the <i>Acts of Paul and Thecla</i> is evidence of her veneration. She was called "apostle and protomartyr among women" and "<a href="/wiki/Equal-to-apostles" title="Equal-to-apostles">equal-to-apostles</a> in sanctity". She was widely cited as an <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetic</a> role model for women. During the fourth and fifth centuries, Thecla was lauded in literature as an exemplary virgin and martyr by ascetic writers and theologians such as <a href="/wiki/Methodius_of_Olympus" title="Methodius of Olympus">Methodius of Olympus</a>, <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Nyssa" title="Gregory of Nyssa">Gregory of Nyssa</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Nazianzus" title="Gregory of Nazianzus">Gregory of Nazianzus</a>. The Eastern Rites of the Eastern Orthodox Church commemorate her on 24 September in churches following the new Calendar and 7 October for those using the old or Julian Calendar.
</p><p>Her veneration flourished particularly at <a href="/wiki/Silifke" title="Silifke">Seleucia Cilicia</a> (where she was said to have lived to old age and be buried), Iconium (present day <a href="/wiki/Konya" title="Konya">Konya</a>), and <a href="/wiki/Nicomedia" title="Nicomedia">Nicomedia</a>. The society<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Avoid_weasel_words" class="mw-redirect" title="Wikipedia:Avoid weasel words"><span title="The material near this tag possibly uses too vague attribution or weasel words. (July 2022)">which?</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> also appeared at least as early as the 4th century in Western Europe. <a href="/wiki/Chamali%C3%A8res" title="Chamalières">Chamalières</a> in France was believed to hold relics. The obscure saints, Tecla of <a href="/wiki/Aquileia" title="Aquileia">Aquileia</a> and of <a href="/wiki/Trieste" title="Trieste">Trieste</a> are modeled after her.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> In <a href="/wiki/Bede#Hagiography" title="Bede">Bede's martyrology</a>, Thecla is celebrated on 23 September, which was her <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> in the West, though in 1969 the Roman Catholic Church removed Thecla's feast day from the Calendar of Saints for lack of historic evidence.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> The Western Rite Parishes of the Orthodox Churches continue to celebrate her on 23 September (new Calendar Parishes) and 6 October (old Calendar Churches). The Western Rite Monastic <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Saint_Paul_the_First_Hermit" title="Order of Saint Paul the First Hermit">Order of Saint Paul the First Hermit</a> celebrates her feast day on 24 September.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A local <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a> tradition of Thecla may have inspired an episode connected to <a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle" title="Paul the Apostle">Paul the Apostle</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> "It is otherwise difficult to account for the very great popularity of the cult of St. Thecla, which spread over East and West, and made her the most famous of virgin martyrs," wrote <a href="/wiki/M._R._James" title="M. R. James">M.&#160;R. James</a>, the editor of this <i>Acta</i> (James 1924).
</p>
<h3><span id="Tomb_of_Saint_Thecla.2C_Silifke"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Tomb_of_Saint_Thecla,_Silifke">Tomb of Saint Thecla, Silifke</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Tomb of Saint Thecla, Silifke">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Aya_Tekla_yeralt%C4%B1_s%C4%B1%C4%9F%C4%B1na%C4%9F%C4%B1_g%C3%B6r%C3%BCn%C3%BCm%C3%BC.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/Aya_Tekla_yeralt%C4%B1_s%C4%B1%C4%9F%C4%B1na%C4%9F%C4%B1_g%C3%B6r%C3%BCn%C3%BCm%C3%BC.jpg/220px-Aya_Tekla_yeralt%C4%B1_s%C4%B1%C4%9F%C4%B1na%C4%9F%C4%B1_g%C3%B6r%C3%BCn%C3%BCm%C3%BC.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/Aya_Tekla_yeralt%C4%B1_s%C4%B1%C4%9F%C4%B1na%C4%9F%C4%B1_g%C3%B6r%C3%BCn%C3%BCm%C3%BC.jpg/330px-Aya_Tekla_yeralt%C4%B1_s%C4%B1%C4%9F%C4%B1na%C4%9F%C4%B1_g%C3%B6r%C3%BCn%C3%BCm%C3%BC.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/Aya_Tekla_yeralt%C4%B1_s%C4%B1%C4%9F%C4%B1na%C4%9F%C4%B1_g%C3%B6r%C3%BCn%C3%BCm%C3%BC.jpg/440px-Aya_Tekla_yeralt%C4%B1_s%C4%B1%C4%9F%C4%B1na%C4%9F%C4%B1_g%C3%B6r%C3%BCn%C3%BCm%C3%BC.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3264" data-file-height="2448" /></a><figcaption>Aya Thekla in Seleucia</figcaption></figure>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Aya_Tekla_Church" title="Aya Tekla Church">Aya Tekla Church</a></div>
<p>The cave-tomb in <a href="/wiki/Silifke" title="Silifke">Seleucia</a> was one of the most celebrated in the Christian world. Gregory of Nazianzus withdrew to the shrine of "the highly praised young maid Thecla"<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> for three years. The site was described by <a href="/wiki/Egeria_(pilgrim)" title="Egeria (pilgrim)">Egeria</a> in the mid-380s. It was restored several times, among others by the Emperor <a href="/wiki/Zeno_(emperor)" title="Zeno (emperor)">Zeno</a> in the 5th century, and today the ruins of the tomb and sanctuary are called <a href="/wiki/Aya_Tekla_Church" title="Aya Tekla Church">Aya Tekla Church</a> or <i>Meriamlik</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> A 5th-century anonymous work, <i>The Life and Miracles of Thecla</i>, concentrates on the town.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Tomb_of_Saint_Thecla.2C_Maaloula"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Tomb_of_Saint_Thecla,_Maaloula">Tomb of Saint Thecla, Maaloula</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Tomb of Saint Thecla, Maaloula">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Mar_Takla_monastery_01.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Mar_Takla_monastery_01.jpg/220px-Mar_Takla_monastery_01.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Mar_Takla_monastery_01.jpg/330px-Mar_Takla_monastery_01.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Mar_Takla_monastery_01.jpg/440px-Mar_Takla_monastery_01.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3456" data-file-height="2592" /></a><figcaption>The <a href="/wiki/Convent_of_St._Thecla_(Maaloula)" class="mw-redirect" title="Convent of St. Thecla (Maaloula)">Convent of St. Thecla</a> in <a href="/wiki/Maaloula" title="Maaloula">Maaloula</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>In <a href="/wiki/Maaloula" title="Maaloula">Maaloula</a>, Syria, a <a href="/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church" title="Greek Orthodox Church">Greek Orthodox</a> <a href="/wiki/Nunnery" class="mw-redirect" title="Nunnery">nunnery</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Convent_of_Saint_Thecla_(Maaloula)" title="Convent of Saint Thecla (Maaloula)">Convent of Saint Thecla</a>, was built near her cave tomb, reached by steps in the mountainside, a pilgrimage site with a holy well. The Church tradition is that the mountain opened miraculously to protect Thecla from her persecutors.
</p><p>On Monday, December 2, 2013, during the <a href="/wiki/Syrian_civil_war" title="Syrian civil war">Syrian civil war</a>, twelve nuns there were seized by <a href="/wiki/Al-Qaeda" title="Al-Qaeda">Al-Qaeda</a> radicals of <a href="/wiki/Al-Nusra_Front" title="Al-Nusra Front">Al-Nusra Front</a> during the bombardment of her shrine<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>. Three months later the nuns were exchanged for relatives of terrorists. In April 2014, the town was liberated by Syrian governmental troops. On May 30, 2018, the rector of the Church, Ilias Ades, announced that the monastery would be entirely restored in a month by the <a href="/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church_of_Antioch" class="mw-redirect" title="Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch">Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch</a> with help from the <a href="/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church" title="Russian Orthodox Church">Russian Orthodox Church</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> The Monastery is a popular destination for Eastern Orthodox Christians from around the world, including Russia.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Monastery_of_Saint_Tecla.2C_Larnaca"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Monastery_of_Saint_Tecla,_Larnaca">Monastery of Saint Tecla, Larnaca</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Monastery of Saint Tecla, Larnaca">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>According to tradition, the Roman empress <a href="/wiki/Helena,_mother_of_Constantine_I" title="Helena, mother of Constantine I">Helena</a> founded the monastery of Saint Tecla that is located in Mosfiloti near <a href="/wiki/Larnaca" title="Larnaca">Larnaca</a>. After the <a href="/wiki/Mamluk" title="Mamluk">Mamluks</a> had taken control of <a href="/wiki/Armenian_Kingdom_of_Cilicia" title="Armenian Kingdom of Cilicia">Armenian Kingdom of Cilicia</a>, part of the hallows of Saint Thecla were carried to Cyprus by <a href="/wiki/Christians" title="Christians">Christians</a>. The hallows are in this monastery now.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="St._Menas_in_Cyprus">St. Menas in Cyprus</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: St. Menas in Cyprus">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>An inscription in remembrance "of the martyr Thecla" in the church of St. Menas in Cyprus, and dated to the second half of the 1st century, was interpreted in the early twentieth century as evidence for her historical existence.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> At this pilgrimage site near the Church of St. Menas in Cyprus, women had the option to buy a flask which they could fill with holy water, oil, or even dirt from that stop which many women visited during their pilgrimage. These flasks depict the image of Menas on one side and Thecla on the other side.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="Catacomb_of_Saint_Tecla.2C_Rome"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Catacomb_of_Saint_Tecla,_Rome">Catacomb of Saint Tecla, Rome</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Catacomb of Saint Tecla, Rome">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Catacomb_of_Saint_Thecla" title="Catacomb of Saint Thecla">Catacomb of Saint Thecla</a></div>
<p>In June 2010, on a wall of the <i>Catacomba di Santa Tecla</i> in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, <a href="/wiki/Roman_Curia" title="Roman Curia">Vatican</a> archaeologists of the Pontifical Commission for Sacred Archaeology, using laser technology to remove layers of clay and lime rind, discovered a frescoed portrait of St <a href="/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle" title="Paul the Apostle">Paul the Apostle</a>, "recognizable by his thin face and dark pointed beard... with small eyes and furrowed brow",<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> which they believe is the oldest image in existence of Paul, dating from the late 4th century.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Movement_of_St._Thecla">The Movement of St. Thecla</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: The Movement of St. Thecla">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>St. Thecla, with her dedication and image of a chosen saint, started a following of masses of women across Asia Minor and Egypt. St. Thecla was praised among these women as a sort of patron of <a href="/wiki/Women%27s_empowerment" title="Women&#39;s empowerment">empowerment for women</a>: in the <i>Acts of Paul and Thecla</i>, St. Thecla preached to men, and baptized herself, all things that were normally supposed to have only been done by men.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>St. Thecla created a culture of imitation in these women. Several of them would live as virgins in households, in tombs (as she was rumored to do), and sometimes in monasteries. These women would travel together as bands of empowered virgins telling stories of Thecla and her grace. Other women in the Movement of St. Thecla would name their daughters after her and engrave her face on their tombs and on their oil lamps. All of these women were empowered by Thecla, a woman who did things that not many women would ever dare to do, and they built a strong community in which they empowered each other.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage">Patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Thecla&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Thecla is sometimes counted as the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Tarragona" title="Tarragona">Tarragona</a> in <a href="/wiki/Catalonia" title="Catalonia">Catalonia</a> (<a href="/wiki/Spain" title="Spain">Spain</a>), where the cathedral has a <a href="/wiki/St_Tecla%27s_Chapel" class="mw-redirect" title="St Tecla&#39;s Chapel">chapel dedicated to her</a>. Her feast day remains the town's major local holiday.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (February 2015)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> In Spanish-speaking countries, she is also facetiously counted as the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Computer" title="Computer">computers</a> and <a href="/wiki/Internet" title="Internet">Internet</a>, from the <a href="/wiki/Homophony" title="Homophony">homophony</a> with the Spanish and <a href="/wiki/Catalan_language" title="Catalan language">Catalan</a> word <i>tecla</i> ("key").<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (March 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> The <a href="/wiki/St_Thecla%27s_Cathedral" class="mw-redirect" title="St Thecla&#39;s Cathedral">earliest cathedral</a> in <a href="/wiki/Milan" title="Milan">Milan</a> was also dedicated to her; its baptistry and remnants of its structure are still accessible below the <a href="/wiki/Milan_Cathedral" title="Milan Cathedral">present structure</a>. The <i>duomo</i> of the town of <a href="/wiki/Este,_Veneto" title="Este, Veneto">Este, Veneto</a>, is dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Santa_Tecla,_Este" title="Santa Tecla, Este">Santa Tecla</a>. <a href="/wiki/Lebanon" title="Lebanon">Lebanon</a> has 42 churches dedicated to St.&#160;Takla or Taqla. One of the oldest is the St.&#160;Taqla Church in <a href="/w/index.php?title=Masqa&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Masqa (page does not exist)">Masqa</a>, <a href="/wiki/Matn_District" title="Matn District">Matn District</a>, built in 1695. The church boasts an 1870 painting of Thecla by the Italian artist <a href="/w/index.php?title=Vincento_Lampodico&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Vincento Lampodico (page does not exist)">Vincento Lampodico</a>.
</p><p>In Syria, there is a Greek Orthodox church of St. Thecla in <a href="/wiki/Darayya" title="Darayya">Darayya</a>. In 1849, some people found a cave in <a href="/wiki/Latakia" title="Latakia">Latakia</a> which later became St. Taqla's Shrine.
</p><p>In the United States there are three Roman Catholic parishes named for Saint Thecla: in <a href="/wiki/Clinton_Township,_Macomb_County,_Michigan" title="Clinton Township, Macomb County, Michigan">Clinton, Michigan</a>; in <a href="/wiki/Pembroke,_Massachusetts" title="Pembroke, Massachusetts">Pembroke, Massachusetts</a>; and in <a href="/wiki/Chicago" title="Chicago">Chicago</a>, <a href="/wiki/Illinois" title="Illinois">Illinois</a>.
</p><p>Several cities and towns are named for her:
</p>
