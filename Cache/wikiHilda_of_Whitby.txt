<p><b>Hilda</b> (or <b>Hild</b>) of <b>Whitby</b> (c. 614 – 680) was a <a href="/wiki/Christianity" title="Christianity">Christian</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> and the founding <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">abbess</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> at <a href="/wiki/Whitby" title="Whitby">Whitby</a>, which was chosen as the venue for the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Whitby" title="Synod of Whitby">Synod of Whitby</a> in 664. An important figure in the <a href="/wiki/Christianisation_of_Anglo-Saxon_England" title="Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England">Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England</a>, she was abbess at several monasteries and recognised for the wisdom that drew kings to her for advice.
</p><p>The source of information about Hilda is the <i><a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People" title="Ecclesiastical History of the English People">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a> in 731, who was born approximately eight years before her death. He documented much of the Christian conversion of the <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxons</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to Bede, Hilda was born in 614 into the <a href="/wiki/Deira" title="Deira">Deiran</a> royal household. She was the second daughter of Hereric, nephew of <a href="/wiki/Edwin_of_Northumbria" title="Edwin of Northumbria">Edwin, King of Deira</a> and his wife, Breguswīþ.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKiefer1999_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKiefer1999-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> When Hilda was still an infant, her father was poisoned while in exile at the court of the <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Britons" title="Celtic Britons">Brittonic</a> king of <a href="/wiki/Elmet" title="Elmet">Elmet</a> in what is now <a href="/wiki/West_Yorkshire" title="West Yorkshire">West Yorkshire</a>. In 616, Edwin killed <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelfrith" title="Æthelfrith">Æthelfrith</a>, the son of <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelric_of_Bernicia" title="Æthelric of Bernicia">Æthelric of Bernicia</a>, in battle. He created the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of Northumbria">Kingdom of Northumbria</a> and took its throne. Hilda was brought up at King Edwin's court.
</p><p>In 625, the widowed Edwin married the Christian princess <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelburh_of_Kent" title="Æthelburh of Kent">Æthelburh of Kent</a>, daughter of King <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelberht_of_Kent" title="Æthelberht of Kent">Æthelberht of Kent</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Merovingian_dynasty" title="Merovingian dynasty">Merovingian</a> princess <a href="/wiki/Bertha_of_Kent" title="Bertha of Kent">Bertha of Kent</a>. As part of the marriage contract, Aethelburh was allowed to continue her Roman Christian worship and was accompanied to Northumbria with her chaplain, <a href="/wiki/Paulinus_of_York" title="Paulinus of York">Paulinus of York</a>, a Roman monk sent to England in 601 to assist <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury" title="Augustine of Canterbury">Augustine of Canterbury</a>. Augustine's mission in England was based in Kent, and is referred to as the <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_mission" title="Gregorian mission">Gregorian mission</a> after the pope who sent him.<sup id="cite_ref-Bognor_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bognor-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> As queen, Æthelburh continued to practice her Christianity and no doubt influenced her husband's thinking as her mother Bertha had influenced her father.
</p><p>In 627, King Edwin was <a href="/wiki/Baptism" title="Baptism">baptised</a> on <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Easter</a> Day, 12 April, along with his entire <a href="/wiki/Courtier" title="Courtier">court</a>, which included the 13-year-old Hilda,<sup id="cite_ref-Bognor_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bognor-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> in a small wooden church hastily constructed for the occasion near the site of the present <a href="/wiki/York_Minster" title="York Minster">York Minster</a>.
</p><p>In 633, Northumbria was overrun by the neighbouring pagan King of Mercia, at which time King Edwin fell in battle. Paulinus accompanied Hilda and Queen Æthelburh and her companions to the Queen's home in Kent. Queen Æthelburh founded a convent at <a href="/wiki/Lyminge" title="Lyminge">Lyminge</a> and it is assumed that Hilda remained with the Queen-Abbess.<sup id="cite_ref-Katoomba_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Katoomba-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Hilda's elder sister, <a href="/wiki/Hereswith" title="Hereswith">Hereswith</a>, married Ethelric, brother of King <a href="/wiki/Anna_of_East_Anglia" title="Anna of East Anglia">Anna of East Anglia</a>, who with all of his daughters became renowned for their Christian virtues. Later, Hereswith became a nun at <a href="/wiki/Chelles_Abbey" title="Chelles Abbey">Chelles Abbey</a> in <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a> (modern <a href="/wiki/France" title="France">France</a>). Bede resumes Hilda's story at a point when she was about to join her widowed sister at Chelles Abbey. At the age of 33, Hilda decided instead to answer the call of Bishop <a href="/wiki/Aidan_of_Lindisfarne" title="Aidan of Lindisfarne">Aidan of Lindisfarne</a> and returned to Northumbria to live as a <a href="/wiki/Nun" title="Nun">nun</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELindemann2014_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELindemann2014-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Abbess">Abbess</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Abbess">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Detail_from_St._Hilda_at_Hartlepool_by_James_Clark_(Oil_Painting).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/Detail_from_St._Hilda_at_Hartlepool_by_James_Clark_%28Oil_Painting%29.jpg/220px-Detail_from_St._Hilda_at_Hartlepool_by_James_Clark_%28Oil_Painting%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="165" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/Detail_from_St._Hilda_at_Hartlepool_by_James_Clark_%28Oil_Painting%29.jpg/330px-Detail_from_St._Hilda_at_Hartlepool_by_James_Clark_%28Oil_Painting%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/Detail_from_St._Hilda_at_Hartlepool_by_James_Clark_%28Oil_Painting%29.jpg/440px-Detail_from_St._Hilda_at_Hartlepool_by_James_Clark_%28Oil_Painting%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2592" data-file-height="1944" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Detail_from_St._Hilda_at_Hartlepool_by_James_Clark_(Oil_Painting).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Saint  Hilda at Hartlepool by <a href="/wiki/James_Clark_(artist)" title="James Clark (artist)">James Clark</a> (Oil Painting)</div></div></div>
<p>Hilda's original convent is not known except that it was on the north bank of the <a href="/wiki/River_Wear" title="River Wear">River Wear</a>. Here, with a few companions, she learned the traditions of <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">Celtic</a> <a href="/wiki/Monasticism" title="Monasticism">monasticism</a>, which Bishop Aidan brought from <a href="/wiki/Iona" title="Iona">Iona</a>. After a year Aidan appointed Hilda as the second <a href="/wiki/Abbess" title="Abbess">Abbess</a> of <a href="/wiki/Hartlepool_Abbey" title="Hartlepool Abbey">Hartlepool Abbey</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThurston1910_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThurston1910-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> No trace remains of this abbey, but its monastic cemetery has been found near the present <a href="/wiki/St_Hilda%27s_Church,_Hartlepool" title="St Hilda&#39;s Church, Hartlepool">St Hilda's Church</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hartlepool" title="Hartlepool">Hartlepool</a>.
</p><p>In 657 Hilda became the founding abbess of <a href="/wiki/Whitby_Abbey" title="Whitby Abbey">Whitby Abbey</a>, then known as <i>Streoneshalh</i>; she remained there until her death.<sup id="cite_ref-ohp_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ohp-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> Archaeological evidence shows that her monastery was in the Celtic style, with its members living in small houses, each for two or three people. The tradition in double monasteries, such as Hartlepool and Whitby, was that men and women lived separately but worshipped together in church. The exact location and size of the church associated with this monastery is unknown.
</p><p>Bede states that the original ideals of monasticism were maintained strictly in Hilda's abbey. All property and goods were held in common; Christian virtues were exercised, especially <a href="/wiki/Peace" title="Peace">peace</a> and <a href="/wiki/Charity_(virtue)" class="mw-redirect" title="Charity (virtue)">charity</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-northern_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-northern-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Everyone had to study the <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">Bible</a> and do good works.
</p><p>Five men from this monastery later became <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishops</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThurston1910_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThurston1910-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Two, <a href="/wiki/John_of_Beverley" title="John of Beverley">John of Beverley</a>, Bishop of Hexham and <a href="/wiki/Wilfrid_II_(bishop_of_York)" title="Wilfrid II (bishop of York)">Wilfrid</a>, Bishop of York, were <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> for their service to the Christian church at a critical period in its fight against paganism.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Character">Character</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Character">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Aidan_visits_St_Hilda.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/St_Aidan_visits_St_Hilda.JPG/220px-St_Aidan_visits_St_Hilda.JPG" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/St_Aidan_visits_St_Hilda.JPG/330px-St_Aidan_visits_St_Hilda.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/St_Aidan_visits_St_Hilda.JPG/440px-St_Aidan_visits_St_Hilda.JPG 2x" data-file-width="600" data-file-height="800" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Aidan_visits_St_Hilda.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><a href="/wiki/Aidan_of_Lindisfarne" title="Aidan of Lindisfarne">Aidan of Lindisfarne</a> visits Hild. <a href="/wiki/Gloucester_Cathedral" title="Gloucester Cathedral">Gloucester Cathedral</a>, by <a href="/wiki/Christopher_Whall" title="Christopher Whall">Christopher Whall</a>.</div></div></div>
<p>Bede describes Hilda as a woman of great energy, who was a skilled administrator and teacher. As a landowner she had many in her employ to care for sheep and cattle, farming, and woodcutting. She gained such a reputation for wisdom that kings and princes sought her advice.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThurston1910_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThurston1910-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> However, she also had a concern for ordinary folk such as <a href="/wiki/C%C3%A6dmon" title="Cædmon">Cædmon</a>. He was a herder at the monastery, who was inspired in a dream to sing verses in praise of God. Hilda recognized his gift and encouraged him to develop it. Bede writes, "All who knew her called her mother because of her outstanding devotion and grace".<sup id="cite_ref-ohp_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ohp-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Synod_of_Whitby">Synod of Whitby</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Synod of Whitby">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The prestige of Whitby is reflected in the fact that King <a href="/wiki/Oswiu" title="Oswiu">Oswiu</a> of Northumberland chose Hilda's monastery as the venue for the <a href="/wiki/Synod_of_Whitby" title="Synod of Whitby">Synod of Whitby</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Bognor_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bognor-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> the first <a href="/wiki/Synod" title="Synod">synod</a> of the Church in his kingdom. He invited churchmen from as far away as <a href="/wiki/Wessex" title="Wessex">Wessex</a> to attend the synod. Most of those present, including Hilda, accepted the King's decision to adopt the method of calculating <a href="/wiki/Easter" title="Easter">Easter</a> currently used in <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a>, establishing Roman practice as the norm in Northumbria. The monks from <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a>, who would not accept this, withdrew to <a href="/wiki/Iona" title="Iona">Iona</a>, and later to <a href="/wiki/Ireland" title="Ireland">Ireland</a>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Illness_and_death">Illness and death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Illness and death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Hilda suffered from a fever for the last seven years of her life, but she continued to work until her death on 17 November 680&#160;AD, at what was then the advanced age of sixty-six. In her last year she set up another monastery, fourteen miles from Whitby, at <a href="/wiki/Hackness" title="Hackness">Hackness</a>. She died after receiving <a href="/wiki/Viaticum" title="Viaticum">viaticum</a>, and her legend holds that at the moment of her death the bells of the monastery of Hackness tolled. A nun there named <a href="/wiki/Begu_(nun)" title="Begu (nun)">Begu</a> claimed to have witnessed Hilda's soul being borne to heaven by angels.<sup id="cite_ref-acc_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-acc-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legends">Legends</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Legends">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Hilda_memorial_ammonites.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/St_Hilda_memorial_ammonites.jpg/220px-St_Hilda_memorial_ammonites.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="472" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/St_Hilda_memorial_ammonites.jpg/330px-St_Hilda_memorial_ammonites.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/St_Hilda_memorial_ammonites.jpg/440px-St_Hilda_memorial_ammonites.jpg 2x" data-file-width="880" data-file-height="1888" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:St_Hilda_memorial_ammonites.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>St. Hilda monument detail in Whitby. Note ammonites at feet.</div></div></div>
<p>A local legend says that when sea birds fly over the abbey they dip their wings in honour of Saint Hilda. Another legend tells of a plague of <a href="/wiki/Snake" title="Snake">snakes</a> which Hilda turned to stone, supposedly explaining the presence of <a href="/wiki/Ammonite" class="mw-redirect" title="Ammonite">ammonite</a> <a href="/wiki/Fossils" class="mw-redirect" title="Fossils">fossils</a> on the shore. It was not unknown for local "artisans" to carve snakes' heads onto ammonites, and sell these "relics" as proof of her miracle.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELovett1905333–334_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELovett1905333–334-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>In fact, the ammonite genus <i><a href="/wiki/Hildoceras" title="Hildoceras">Hildoceras</a></i> takes its scientific name from St. Hilda. The coat of arms of nearby Whitby includes three such 'snakestones', and depictions of ammonites appear in the shield of the <a href="/wiki/University_of_Durham" class="mw-redirect" title="University of Durham">University of Durham</a>'s <a href="/wiki/College_of_St_Hild_and_St_Bede" class="mw-redirect" title="College of St Hild and St Bede">College of St Hild and St Bede</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-dur.ac.uk_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dur.ac.uk-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> 
</p><p>A carved ammonite stone is set into the wall by the entrance to the former chapel of St Hild's College, Durham, which later became part of the College of St Hild and St Bede. The coat of arms of <a href="/wiki/St_Hilda%27s_College,_Oxford" title="St Hilda&#39;s College, Oxford">St. Hilda's College, Oxford</a>, includes a curled snake, and the ammonite is used by the college as a symbol.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The veneration of Hilda from an early period is attested by the inclusion of her name in the calendar of Saint Willibrord, written at the beginning of the 8th century. According to one tradition, her relics were translated to Glastonbury by King Edmund; another tradition holds that Saint Edmund brought her relics to Gloucester.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a>, the feast day of Saint Hilda is 17 November. In some parts of the <a href="/wiki/Anglican_Communion" title="Anglican Communion">Anglican Communion</a>, her <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Church_of_England)" title="Calendar of saints (Church of England)">feast</a> (<a href="/wiki/Lesser_Festival_(Anglicanism)" title="Lesser Festival (Anglicanism)">Lesser Festival</a>) is on 18 November, while in some others, such as the Anglican Church of Australia, it is on 17 November. 
</p><p>In the <a href="/wiki/Church_of_England" title="Church of England">Church of England</a> however, it is kept on <a href="/wiki/November_19" title="November 19">19 November</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> In the calendar approved for formerly Anglican <a href="/wiki/Personal_Ordinariate" class="mw-redirect" title="Personal Ordinariate">Personal Ordinariate</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pastoral_Provision" title="Pastoral Provision">Pastoral Provision</a> parishes in the Roman Catholic Church, the feast day of Saint Hilda is celebrated on 23 June, together with those of Saint <a href="/wiki/%C3%86thelthryth" title="Æthelthryth">Ethelreda</a>, Abbess of Ely (died 679), and Saint <a href="/wiki/Mildthryth" class="mw-redirect" title="Mildthryth">Mildred</a>, Abbess of Minster-in-Thanet (died ca. 700).
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Patronage">Patronage</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Patronage">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Hilda is considered one of the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saints</a> of learning and culture, including poetry, due to her patronage of Cædmon. Saint Hilda is the patron saint of the National Cathedral School for Girls in <a href="/wiki/Washington,_D.C." title="Washington, D.C.">Washington, D.C.</a>
</p><p>In addition, <a href="/wiki/St_Hilda%27s_College,_Oxford" title="St Hilda&#39;s College, Oxford">St Hilda's College, Oxford</a>, established in 1893 for female students, remained with that status for more than 100 years, before turning co-educational when it was deemed that the percentage of women studying at Oxford had risen to near 50 percent. The symbol of the college is the ammonite of St Hilda and during the centenary, 100 silver ammonites were created; now proudly owned by alumnae of the college in honour of St. Hilda's achievements - and those of the first 100 years of female students at the college.<sup id="cite_ref-oxfordcollege_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-oxfordcollege-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Iconography">Iconography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Iconography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>St Hilda is generally depicted with a pastoral staff and carrying an abbey church. Often, there are ammonites at her feet.<sup id="cite_ref-ohp_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ohp-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Whitby_Abbey">Whitby Abbey</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Whitby Abbey">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Whitby_Abbey" title="Whitby Abbey">Whitby Abbey</a></div>
<p>Hilda was succeeded as abbess by <a href="/wiki/Eanfl%C3%A6d" title="Eanflæd">Eanflæd</a>, widow of King Oswiu, and their daughter, <a href="/wiki/%C3%86lffl%C3%A6d_of_Whitby" title="Ælfflæd of Whitby">Ælfflæd</a>. From then onward we know nothing about the abbey at Whitby until it was destroyed by the Danish invaders in 867. After the <a href="/wiki/Norman_conquest_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Norman conquest of England">Norman conquest</a> that began in 1066 AD, monks from <a href="/wiki/Evesham,_Worcestershire" class="mw-redirect" title="Evesham, Worcestershire">Evesham</a> re-founded the <a href="/wiki/Whitby_Abbey" title="Whitby Abbey">abbey</a> as a <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> house for men. Thus it continued until the <a href="/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Monasteries" class="mw-redirect" title="Dissolution of the Monasteries">Dissolution of the Monasteries</a> by <a href="/wiki/Henry_VIII" title="Henry VIII">Henry VIII</a> in 1539.
</p><p>There is said to be the wraith of St Hilda, who appears in the ruins wrapped in a shroud, and the bells of the abbey can be heard ringing under the water, where they sank with the ship taking them to London after the abbey was dismantled.<sup id="cite_ref-melbourne_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-melbourne-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Churches">Churches</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Churches">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Two churches in Whitby (<a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a><sup id="cite_ref-RCParish_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RCParish-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> and <a href="/wiki/Anglican" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican">Anglican</a>), have been dedicated under her patronage and another, in <a href="/wiki/Bilsborrow" title="Bilsborrow">Bilsborrow</a>, <a href="/wiki/Lancashire" title="Lancashire">Lancashire</a>.
</p><p>Other Anglican Churches dedicated to St Hilda in Northern England include <a href="/wiki/St_Hilda%27s_Church,_South_Shields" title="St Hilda&#39;s Church, South Shields">St Hilda's Church, South Shields</a>, one in the Cross Green area of Leeds, and another in Jesmond, Newcastle upon Tyne. It was opened in September 1882. There is a statue of St Hilda in the nave, depicting her as the Mother of her Abbey at Whitby. She also appears in a stained glass window at the east end of the church. The church still is active and a sung mass is held there every Sunday. Several small streets in the immediate area are named after the church -- St Hilda's Mount, St Hilda's Road, among them.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Religious_communities">Religious communities</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Religious communities">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>A community of <a href="/wiki/Anglican" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican">Anglican</a> sisters, the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_the_Holy_Paraclete" title="Order of the Holy Paraclete">Order of the Holy Paraclete</a> was founded in 1915 at St Hilda's Priory,<sup id="cite_ref-sneatoncastle.co.uk_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sneatoncastle.co.uk-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> on the western edge of Whitby town. More recently, the Community of St Aidan and St Hilda has been founded on <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a>.
</p><p>A group of <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Catholic">Anglo-Catholic</a> deaconesses founded in 1910 by Fr Frederick Burgess lived on the grounds of Christ Church, New Haven, Connecticut, in a house they called St Hilda's House. The deaconesses of St Hilda's House served the church, the children of the church school, and the poor and orphaned of New Haven until the early 1970s operating a free medicine clinic, soup kitchen and many other ministries. Today, St Hilda's House-<a href="/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)" title="Episcopal Church (United States)">Episcopal</a> Service Corps program at Christ Church New Haven has retained the name, and part of the legacy of the St Hilda's deaconesses.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Educational_institutions">Educational institutions</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: Educational institutions">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>St Hilda has become the patron of many schools and colleges all over the world. The <a href="/wiki/College_of_St_Hild_and_St_Bede" class="mw-redirect" title="College of St Hild and St Bede">College of St Hild and St Bede</a>, Durham, <a href="/wiki/St_Hild%27s_Church_of_England_School" title="St Hild&#39;s Church of England School">St Hild's Church of England School</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hartlepool" title="Hartlepool">Hartlepool</a>, <a href="/wiki/St_Hilda%27s_College,_Oxford" title="St Hilda&#39;s College, Oxford">St Hilda's College, Oxford</a> and <a href="/wiki/St_Hilda%27s_Collegiate_School" title="St Hilda&#39;s Collegiate School">St Hilda's Collegiate School</a>, <a href="/wiki/Dunedin" title="Dunedin">Dunedin</a> are named after Saint Hilda. A stone from the 13th century ruins of Whitby Abbey where St Hilda founded the Monastery of Streoneshalh c. 657AD was donated to <a href="/wiki/St_Hilda%27s_College_(University_of_Melbourne)" class="mw-redirect" title="St Hilda&#39;s College (University of Melbourne)">St Hilda's College (University of Melbourne)</a> by the Whitby Urban Council. This stone is located at the college entrance.<sup id="cite_ref-melbourne_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-melbourne-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/St._Hilda%27s_College,_University_of_Toronto" title="St. Hilda&#39;s College, University of Toronto">St. Hilda's College, University of Toronto</a> is the women's college of <a href="/wiki/University_of_Trinity_College" class="mw-redirect" title="University of Trinity College">University of Trinity College</a>. Saint Hilda is honoured as co-patron (with <a href="/wiki/Blessed_Virgin_Mary" class="mw-redirect" title="Blessed Virgin Mary">Our Lady</a>). Daily services are held in the Lady Chapel by Trinity's Faculty of Divinity.
</p><p>St Hilda's Diocesan High School is an <a href="/wiki/Anglican" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglican">Anglican</a> boarding school for girls in Brown's Town, St. Ann, <a href="/wiki/Jamaica" title="Jamaica">Jamaica</a>, founded by Canon James Philip Hall, Rector of St. Mark's Anglican Church in Brown's Town 1906-07. St. Hilda's College in Buenos Aires, Argentina was founded in 1912.
</p><p>There are two schools situated in Australia in recognition of St. Hilda. They are both 'St. Hilda's Anglican School for Girls' which is an independent, girls' school in <a href="/wiki/Southport" title="Southport">Southport</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Queensland and <a href="/wiki/Mosman_Park" class="mw-redirect" title="Mosman Park">Mosman Park</a>, <a href="/wiki/Western_Australia" title="Western Australia">Western Australia</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On the upper west side of <a href="/wiki/Manhattan" title="Manhattan">Manhattan</a> in New York City is St Hilda's and St Hugh's School, an independent Episcopal day school that opened its doors in 1950. The school is coeducational and includes toddlers to grade eight.
</p><p>St Hilda is honoured in <a href="/wiki/Singapore" title="Singapore">Singapore</a> in the St. Hilda's primary and secondary schools.
</p><p>During the <a href="/wiki/British_Empire" title="British Empire">British Empire</a> in <a href="/wiki/India" title="India">India</a>, Anglican missionaries have built "St Hilda Boarding School" at <a href="/wiki/Miri-Maka" class="mw-redirect" title="Miri-Maka">Miri-Maka</a>.
</p><p>St Hilda's School, Ootacamund, southern India was established by Church of England sisters in 1895.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Fiction">Fiction</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Hilda_of_Whitby&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: Fiction">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Hilda appears as a main character in the 1994 novel <i>Absolution for Murder</i>, the first book in <a href="/wiki/Peter_Tremayne" class="mw-redirect" title="Peter Tremayne">Peter Tremayne</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Sister_Fidelma_mysteries" title="Sister Fidelma mysteries">Sister Fidelma mysteries</a>.
</p><p>Hilda appears as a main character in <a href="/wiki/Melvyn_Bragg" title="Melvyn Bragg">Melvyn Bragg</a>'s 1996 novel, <i><a href="/wiki/Credo_(novel)" title="Credo (novel)">Credo</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBragg1996_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBragg1996-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The 2013 novel <i><a href="/wiki/Hild_(novel)" title="Hild (novel)">Hild</a></i> by <a href="/wiki/Nicola_Griffith" title="Nicola Griffith">Nicola Griffith</a> is based on the life of Hilda.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEBond2013_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBond2013-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/w/index.php?title=Jill_Dalladay&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Jill Dalladay (page does not exist)">Jill Dalladay</a>'s 2015 novel <i>The Abbess of Whitby</i> combines historical record and fiction to imagine Hilda's life before she became a nun.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Maureen_Duffy" title="Maureen Duffy">Maureen Duffy</a>'s play <i>The Choice</i> premiered at the <a href="/wiki/Jermyn_Street_Theatre" title="Jermyn Street Theatre">Jermyn Street Theatre</a> in 2018. This monologue sees Hilda recount two episodes of her life that are recorded by <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>: the 'miracle' of the poet-cowherd <a href="/wiki/C%C3%A6dmon" title="Cædmon">Caedmon</a>, and the role Hilda played in the transition from Irish to Roman Catholicism in the English church.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Vibeke_Vasbo" title="Vibeke Vasbo">Vibeke Vasbo</a>'s Danish novel <i>Hildas sang</i> (Gyldendal, 1991) was translated into English by Gaye Kynoch and published in 2018 as <i>The Song of Hild</i> by <a href="/w/index.php?title=Sacristy_Press&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Sacristy Press (page does not exist)">Sacristy Press</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
