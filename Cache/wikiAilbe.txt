<p><b>Saint Ailbe</b> (<small>Irish:&#160;</small><span title="Representation in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)" class="IPA" lang="ga-Latn-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/Irish" title="Help:IPA/Irish">[ˈalʲvʲə]</a></span>; <a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Albeus, Alibeus</i>), usually known in English as <b>St Elvis</b> (<a href="/wiki/British_English" title="British English">British</a>/<a href="/wiki/Welsh_language" title="Welsh language">Welsh</a>), <b>Eilfyw</b> or <b>Eilfw</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> was regarded as the chief 'pre-Patrician' saint of <a href="/wiki/Ireland" title="Ireland">Ireland</a> (although his death was recorded in the early 6th-century). He was a <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">bishop</a> and later <a href="/wiki/List_of_Irish_saints" class="mw-redirect" title="List of Irish saints">saint</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-thurston_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-thurston-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Little that can be regarded as reliable is known about Ailbe: in Irish sources from the 8th century he is regarded as the first bishop, and later patron saint of Emly in Munster. Later Welsh sources (from the 11th c.) associate him with <a href="/wiki/Saint_David" title="Saint David">Saint David</a> whom he was credited with baptizing and very late sources (16th c.) even give him a local Welsh genealogy making him an <i><a href="/wiki/Ancient_Briton" class="mw-redirect" title="Ancient Briton">Ancient Briton</a></i>.
</p><p>Saint Ailbe is venerated as one of the four great patrons of Ireland. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is 12 September. He is the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Archdiocese_of_Cashel_and_Emly" class="mw-redirect" title="Archdiocese of Cashel and Emly">Archdiocese of Cashel and Emly</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-emly_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-emly-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Sources">Sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ailbe_of_Emly&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/Hagiography" title="Hagiography">life</a> of Ailbe is included in the <i>Vitae Sanctorum Hiberniae</i> (VSH), a Latin collection of medieval Irish saints' lives compiled in the 14th century. There are three major manuscript versions of the VSH: the Dublin, Oxford, and <a href="/wiki/Codex_Salmanticensis" title="Codex Salmanticensis">Salamanca</a>. Charles Plummer compiled an edition of the VSH based on the two surviving Dublin manuscripts in 1910.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Professor William W. Heist of the University of Michigan compiled an edition of the <a href="/wiki/Codex_Salmanticensis" title="Codex Salmanticensis">single Salamanca manuscript</a> in 1965<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Oxford professor <a href="/wiki/Richard_Sharpe_(academic)" class="mw-redirect" title="Richard Sharpe (academic)">Richard Sharpe</a> suggests that the Salamanca manuscript is the closest to the original text from which all three versions derive. Sharpe's analysis of the Irish name-forms in the <i>Codex Salamanticensis</i> showed similarities between it and the <i>Life of Saint Brigid</i>, a verifiably 7th-century text, leading him to posit that nine (and possibly ten) of the lives were written much earlier, <span title="circa">c.</span><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;750</span>&#8211;850.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He further proposed that this earlier Life of Ailbe in the Codex Salmanticensis was originally composed to further the cause of the <a href="/wiki/%C3%89oganacht" class="mw-redirect" title="Éoganacht">Éoganacht</a> Church of <a href="/wiki/Emly" title="Emly">Emly</a>. <i>The Law of Ailbe</i> (784) was issued, possibly in response to the <a href="/wiki/Early_Irish_law" title="Early Irish law">Law of Patrick</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The later lives of the Dublin collection go further and make Ailbe the principal 'pre-Patrican' Saint of Ireland (the others are <a href="/wiki/Ciar%C3%A1n_of_Saighir" class="mw-redirect" title="Ciarán of Saighir">Ciarán of Saighir</a>, Declan of Ardmore, <a href="/wiki/Abb%C3%A1n" title="Abbán">Abbán</a> of Moyarney and <a href="/wiki/Ibar_of_Beggerin" title="Ibar of Beggerin">Ibar of Beggerin</a> or Beggery Island)<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> The Dublin Life of Ailbe asserts that Munster was entrusted to him by Saint Patrick, while to similar effect, Ailbe is called a "second Patrick and patron of Munster" (<i>secundus Patricius et patronus Mumenie</i>) in the Life of Saint <a href="/wiki/Decl%C3%A1n_of_Ardmore" title="Declán of Ardmore">Declán of Ardmore</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Further material is provided by the lives of related saints such as <a href="/wiki/Saint_Patrick" title="Saint Patrick">Patrick</a>. All include numerous miraculous events and obvious inconsistencies and anachronisms.<sup id="cite_ref-sabi_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sabi-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> In fact the earliest mention of the name <i>Ailbeus</i> would seem to be in <a href="/wiki/Tirechan" class="mw-redirect" title="Tirechan">Tirechan</a>'s late 7th century Life of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Patrick" title="Saint Patrick">Saint Patrick</a><sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> although this seems to be in reference to a different 'Ailbe', a priest associated with the <i>Ui Aillello</i>, in Connaught, latterly known as 'Saint Ailbe of Sencua (Shancoe in County Sligo)'. Other early mentions of Ailbe are in the 8th century <i>Navigatio Brendani</i> ("Voyage of Saint <a href="/wiki/Brendan_the_Navigator" title="Brendan the Navigator">Brendan</a>")<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> and in the <a href="/wiki/Martyrology_of_Tallaght" title="Martyrology of Tallaght">Martyrology of Tallaght</a> and <a href="/wiki/Martyrology_of_Oengus" class="mw-redirect" title="Martyrology of Oengus">Martyrology of Oengus</a> from the early years of the 9th century.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legendary_life">Legendary life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ailbe_of_Emly&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Legendary life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In a legend that goes back to the <i>Vita</i>, or 'Saint's Life', Ailbhe's father fled King Cronan before the child's birth and his mother's servants—ordered by the king to put the baby to death—instead placed him on a rock in the wilderness where he was found and nursed by a she-wolf.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> Long afterwards, when Ailbe was bishop, an old she-wolf being pursued by a hunting party ran to the bishop and laid her head upon his breast. Ailbe protected the wolf and thereafter fed her and her cubs every day from his hall.<sup id="cite_ref-thurston_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-thurston-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> Ailbe was discovered in the forest by visiting Britons: these British foster-parents were said to have planned to leave him in Ireland when they returned home but were constantly and miraculously unable to make the passage until they consented to take him with them.<sup id="cite_ref-answer_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-answer-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> They then took Ailbe with them when they returned to Wales (<i>Vita Albei</i> 2).
</p><p>A tradition also going back to the earliest <i>Vita</i> (<i>Vita Albei</i> 9) held that he went to Rome and was ordained as a bishop by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Hilary" class="mw-redirect" title="Pope Hilary">Saint Hilary</a> who was then <a href="/wiki/List_of_popes" title="List of popes">pope</a>. Upon being ordained in Rome, he was said to have fed the people of the city for three days before returning home.<sup id="cite_ref-answer_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-answer-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> At the end of his life, a supernatural ship came and he boarded to learn the secret of his death. Returning from the faerie world, he went back to Emly to die and be buried.<sup id="cite_ref-answer_19-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-answer-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The earliest <i>Vita</i> states that Saint Ailbe was baptised by Palladius (<i>Vita Albei</i> 2), something that might be compatible with the tradition that made him a 'pre-Patrician' evangelizer of Ireland (since Palladius was recorded as having been sent to Ireland in 431, most likely before Patrick's time). The year of his death – 528 - that is recorded in the  'Annals of Innisfallen' (compiled at Emly probably in 1092),<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> is not, however, compatible with a 'pre-Patrician' career. It may well be, though, a reflection of the fact that many such <i>obits</i> (records of the date of death) of Irish saints were retrospectively added to the annals.
</p><p>Ailbhe was said to have founded the <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> and <a href="/wiki/Archdiocese_of_Cashel_and_Emly" class="mw-redirect" title="Archdiocese of Cashel and Emly">diocese</a> of <a href="/wiki/Emly" title="Emly">Emly</a> (<a href="/wiki/Irish_language" title="Irish language">Irish</a>: <i lang="ga">Imlech</i><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>), which became very important in <a href="/wiki/Munster" title="Munster">Munster</a>. He was said to have been responsible for King <a href="/wiki/Aengus_of_Munster" class="mw-redirect" title="Aengus of Munster">Aengus</a>'s donation of island lands for <a href="/wiki/Enda_of_Aran" title="Enda of Aran">Saint Enda</a>'s monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-ballingarry_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ballingarry-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> He is also associated with the 6th-century foundation of <a href="/wiki/Clane_Friary" title="Clane Friary">Clane Friary</a>, in modern County Kildare.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Connections_with_Wales">Connections with Wales</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ailbe_of_Emly&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Connections with Wales">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Life of Saint David, written by Rhigyfarch in the late 11th century,<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> states that Ailbe baptized <a href="/wiki/Saint_David" title="Saint David">Saint David</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> the <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Wales" title="Wales">Wales</a>. In Welsh traditions, he then <a href="/wiki/Fosterage" title="Fosterage">fostered</a> the boy<sup id="cite_ref-elvislives_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-elvislives-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> while serving as <a href="/wiki/Bishop_of_St_David%27s" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishop of St David&#39;s">bishop of Menevia</a> (present-day <a href="/wiki/St_David%27s" class="mw-redirect" title="St David&#39;s">St David's</a>) before leaving to <a href="/wiki/Mission_(Christianity)" class="mw-redirect" title="Mission (Christianity)">missionize</a> southern Ireland.. He was also regarded as the founder of  <i>Llanailfyw</i>  or <a href="/wiki/St_Elvis,_Pembrokeshire" title="St Elvis, Pembrokeshire">St Elvis</a> in <a href="/wiki/Pembrokeshire" title="Pembrokeshire">Pembrokeshire</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-sabi_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-sabi-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Late Welsh sources<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> give him a British ancestry. Thus the 16th c. <i>Achau’r Saint</i> records "<i>Eilvyw a Dirdan Saint Breudan</i>" (variant&#160;: "<i>Breudain</i>") while a 16thc. Manuscript of  <i>Bonedd y Saint</i>  records "<i>Ailvyw vab Dirdan</i>". This would make him a descendant of <a href="/wiki/Vortimer_the_Blessed" class="mw-redirect" title="Vortimer the Blessed">Guorthemir</a> (Modern Welsh: <i>Gwerthefyr</i>; English: Vortimer the Blessed), and a cousin of <a href="/wiki/List_of_Welsh_saints" title="List of Welsh saints">saints</a> <a href="/wiki/Saint_David" title="Saint David">David</a>, <a href="/wiki/Saint_Cybi" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Cybi">Cybi</a>, and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Saint_Sadyrnin&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Saint Sadyrnin (page does not exist)">Sadyrnin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-elvislives_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-elvislives-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Possible_pre-Christian_origins">Possible pre-Christian origins</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ailbe_of_Emly&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Possible pre-Christian origins">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Professor <a href="/wiki/P%C3%A1draig_%C3%93_Riain" title="Pádraig Ó Riain">Pádraig Ó Riain</a> suggests the cult of Saint Ailbe may have pre-Christian origins.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> The name <i>Ailbe</i> figures quite extensively in a context of Irish folk tale, with its likely origins mainly in pre-Christian pagan mythology. For instance <i>Ailbe</i> was the name of the 'divine hound'  in "<a href="/wiki/The_Tale_of_Mac_Da_Th%C3%B3%27s_Pig" title="The Tale of Mac Da Thó&#39;s Pig">The Tale of Mac Da Thó's Pig</a>"<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> associated with the <i>Mag Ailbe</i> or 'plain of Ailbe',<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> where stood a  <i>Lia Ailbe</i>, or 'stone of Ailbe'.  The 'divine hound' <i>Ailbe</i> defended <a href="/wiki/Leinster" title="Leinster">Leinster</a>, the chief centre of which was <i>Aillen</i>, whose female eponym, <i><a href="/wiki/Aillen" title="Aillen">Aillen</a></i>, owned a marvellous lap dog <i>Ailbe</i>, according to the  'Metrical <a href="/wiki/Dindsenchas" title="Dindsenchas">Dindsenchas</a>'.To these 'canine' associations one might compare the tradition which identified Ailbe's father as ‘<i>Ol-chu</i>’ (‘<i>Olcnais</i>’ in <i>Vita Albei</i> 1), 'great-hound', as well as the (likely related) story of the infant Ailbe being cared for by a she-wolf.
</p><p>An ‘<i>Ailbe Grúadbrecc</i>’, meanwhile, was the daughter of <i><a href="/wiki/Cormac_mac_Airt" title="Cormac mac Airt">Cormac mac Airt</a></i> (premier mythical Irish king) and a wife (as her sister <i><a href="/wiki/Gr%C3%A1inne" title="Gráinne">Gráinne</a></i>) of <i>Finn</i> (= literally, 'white') or <a href="/wiki/Fionn_mac_Cumhaill" title="Fionn mac Cumhaill">Fionn mac Cumhaill</a> in the <i>Tochmarc Ailbe</i>,<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> <i>Echtrae Cormaic maic Airt</i><sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup> and "The Burning of Finn's House".<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> Ailbe was also the name of several of Finn's <i><a href="/wiki/Fianna" title="Fianna">fianna</a></i> (comrades in his band), and their women in <i><a href="/wiki/Acallam_na_Sen%C3%B3rach" title="Acallam na Senórach">Acallam na Senórach</a></i><sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> and <i>Duanaire Finn</i>. An <i>Ailbe</i> was also daughter of <i><a href="/wiki/Mider" class="mw-redirect" title="Mider">Mider</a></i>, son of the <i><a href="/wiki/Dagda" class="mw-redirect" title="Dagda">Dagda</a>.</i><sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="The_name_.22Ailbe.22"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="The_name_&quot;Ailbe&quot;">The name "Ailbe"</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ailbe_of_Emly&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: The name &quot;Ailbe&quot;">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Further information: <a href="/wiki/Elvis_(name)" title="Elvis (name)">Elvis (name)</a></div>
<p>The name <i>Ailbe</i> was explained in the <i>Vita Albei</i> as derivative of <i>ail</i> 'a rock' and <i>beo</i>, 'living'. In the words of Baring Gould and Fisher<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> this is "a very doubtful etymology". It is clearly related to the story of his being exposed behind a rock after his birth, before being cared for by a wolf (<i>Vita Albei</i> 2)<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> and looks very much like a folk-etymology. 
Nevertheless, we can note a sporadic association of Ailbe (as a saint or mythological figure) with ' rocks' (Irish <i>ail</i>). The <i>Lia Ailbe</i> (stone of Ailbe) on the <i>Magh Ailbe</i> (plain of Ailbe) may be in origin tautological, while a  <i>Sliabh Ailbe</i> was associated with a legendary figure <i>Ailbe</i> in <i>Duanaire Finn</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> The <i>Inbher Ailbhine</i>   mentioned in Tirechan's <i>Vita Patricii</i> (<i>Tirechan</i> 5.2)<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> may contain <i>ail</i>, 'a rock', according to Watson.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> It is at a "marvellous stone altar ( = prominent rock with religious associations ) on the mountain of the <i>Ui Ailello</i>" where Patrick was said to have installed the second St Ailbe (of <i>Sencua</i>)  - probably at the old site of the church of Shancoe, County Sligo, where a large rock overlooks a well:.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup> This might all be best explained by a typical process of sound assimilation of <i>ail</i> 'a rock' to the name <i>ail-be</i>.
</p><p>The root <i>albho-</i> 'white, bright' as in Latin <i>albus</i>, 'white' appears to figure in the names of various deities or semi-deities, or names with likely mythological associations, hence the <i>Mons Albanus</i>. <i>Albula</i> as an old name for the Tiber and the legendary <i>Alba Longa</i> in Latium; the Germanic deities <i>Albiahenae</i><sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> the semi-divine prophetess, <i>Albruna</i>  mentioned by Tacitus (Vulgar Latin <i>Aurinia</i>: <i>Germania</i> 8) or the spiritual or demonic beings from the Germanic world, which are represented in modern English by the word, 'elf';<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> the <i>Alphito</i> which was recorded as the name of an 'ogress' or 'nursery bugbear' and might well have been appropriate to an earlier strata of Greek gods;<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup> and possibly the  ‘<i>R̥bhus</i>’ of Indian mythology and the Rhig Veda.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> This root may also be found in the names of Celtic deities such as <i>Albarinus</i>, <i>Albocelo</i><sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup> (if they do not contain Latin <i>Albus</i>) and possibly the deity <i>Albius</i> recorded in a single inscription from Aignay-le Duc,.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;47&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>However the root <i>albho-</i> 'white, bright' does not figure in Irish or in fact in any of the extant Celtic languages. It may figure in the Celtic language of ancient Gaul (as in the names above) but there it may in fact have been borrowed from the ancient Ligurian language (the root is very common in place names from ancient Liguria). There does, however, appear the root  <i>albi(i̭)o-</i>, 'world' in the Brittonic Celtic languages: as seen for instance in Wesh <i>elfydd</i>, 'world, land'. In fact this root has convincingly been argued to be related to the root <i>albho-</i> 'white, bright'<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;48&#93;</a></sup> and it certainly appears in the Gaulish divine name <i>albio-rix</i> ("king of the world", parallel to <i>Dumno-rix</i> and <i>Bitu-rix</i> of similar meaning) .<sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;49&#93;</a></sup> However it does not appear in Irish, with one sole exception: the Irish name for 'Britain', that is the Irish version of the name <i>Albion</i> found in ancient sources as the oldest recorded name for Britain. This appears in Irish as <i>Albe-</i>, <i>Alpe-</i> and <i>Albu</i>, <i>Alpu</i>.  There is, however, no obvious explanation for this name to appear in the form <i>ailbe</i> and the root  <i>albi(i̭)o-</i> would not take that form in Irish, according to the way that language normally developed.  The <i>i</i>, in the  <i>ai</i> of <i>Ailbe</i>, is not a full vowel but represents an audible 'glide' before a palatalised <i>l</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">&#91;50&#93;</a></sup> This palatalised <i>l</i>, with <i>i</i>-glide is not found in Irish <i>Albu</i>, 'Britain'.
</p><p>All of this renders the precise form of the name <i>Ailbe</i>, in Irish, arguably, somewhat mysterious.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Ailbe_of_Emly&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:202px;"><a href="/wiki/File:StAilbe%27sCrossEmly.JPG" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/StAilbe%27sCrossEmly.JPG/200px-StAilbe%27sCrossEmly.JPG" decoding="async" width="200" height="268" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/StAilbe%27sCrossEmly.JPG/300px-StAilbe%27sCrossEmly.JPG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/StAilbe%27sCrossEmly.JPG/400px-StAilbe%27sCrossEmly.JPG 2x" data-file-width="3440" data-file-height="4608" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:StAilbe%27sCrossEmly.JPG" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>St Ailbe's Cross in Emly.</div></div></div>
<p>In <a href="/wiki/Emly" title="Emly">Emly</a>, there is a <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Ireland" title="Catholic Church in Ireland">Catholic</a> church dedicated to St Ailbe which dates to the late nineteenth century. An ancient and weathered <a href="/wiki/Celtic_cross" title="Celtic cross">Celtic cross</a> in its churchyard is known as "St Ailbe's Cross". The early nineteenth-century church of St Ailbe is now used as the village hall. A ninth-century monastic rule, written in <a href="/wiki/Old_Irish" title="Old Irish">Old Irish</a>, bears his name.<sup id="cite_ref-Duffy_51-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Duffy-51">&#91;51&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-52" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-52">&#91;52&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Although <a href="/wiki/St_Elvis,_Pembrokeshire" title="St Elvis, Pembrokeshire">St Elvis</a> in Wales is now in ruins,<sup id="cite_ref-53" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-53">&#91;53&#93;</a></sup> there is still a shrine to the parish's namesake at <style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1073938472">.mw-parser-output .geo-default,.mw-parser-output .geo-dms,.mw-parser-output .geo-dec{display:inline}.mw-parser-output .geo-nondefault,.mw-parser-output .geo-multi-punct{display:none}.mw-parser-output .longitude,.mw-parser-output .latitude{white-space:nowrap}</style><span class="plainlinks nourlexpansion"><a class="external text" href="//geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Ailbe_of_Emly&amp;params=51_52_12.7_N_5_10_43.2_W_"><span class="geo-default"><span class="geo-dms" title="Maps, aerial photos, and other data for this location"><span class="latitude">51°52′12.7″N</span> <span class="longitude">5°10′43.2″W</span></span></span><span class="geo-multi-punct">&#xfeff; / &#xfeff;</span><span class="geo-nondefault"><span class="geo-dec" title="Maps, aerial photos, and other data for this location">51.870194°N 5.178667°W</span><span style="display:none">&#xfeff; / <span class="geo">51.870194; -5.178667</span></span></span></a></span>, which bears an inscription concerning his name and connection to St David.<sup id="cite_ref-54" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-54">&#91;54&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
