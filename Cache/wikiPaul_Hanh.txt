<p><b>Vietnamese Martyrs</b> (<a href="/wiki/Vietnamese_language" title="Vietnamese language">Vietnamese</a>: <i lang="vi">Các Thánh Tử đạo Việt Nam</i>; <a href="/wiki/French_language" title="French language">French</a>: <i lang="fr">Martyrs du Viêt Nam</i>) or <b>Saint Andrew Dung-Lac and Companions</b> (<a href="/wiki/Vietnamese_language" title="Vietnamese language">Vietnamese</a>: <i lang="vi">Anrê Dũng-Lạc và các bạn tử đạo</i>), also known as the <b>Martyrs of Annam</b>, <b>Martyrs of <a href="/wiki/Tonkin" title="Tonkin">Tonkin</a> and <a href="/wiki/Cochinchina" title="Cochinchina">Cochinchina</a></b>, <b>Martyrs of Indochina</b>, are <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saints</a> on the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a> who were <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a>.  On June 19, 1988, thousands of <a href="/wiki/Overseas_Vietnamese" title="Overseas Vietnamese">Overseas Vietnamese</a> worldwide gathered at the Vatican for the Celebration of the Canonization of 117 Vietnamese Martyrs, an event chaired by Monsignor <a href="/wiki/Tr%E1%BA%A7n_V%C4%83n_Ho%C3%A0i" class="mw-redirect" title="Trần Văn Hoài">Trần Văn Hoài</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>  Their <a href="/wiki/Memorial_(liturgy)" title="Memorial (liturgy)">memorial</a> is on November 24 (although several of these saints have another memorial, having been beatified and on the calendar prior to the canonization of the group).
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="History">History</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Vietnamese_Martyrs&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Vatican estimates the number of Vietnamese martyrs at between 130,000 and 300,000.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> John Paul II decided to canonize both those whose names are known and unknown, giving them a single feast day.
</p><p>The Vietnamese Martyrs fall into several groupings, those of the <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican</a> and <a href="/wiki/Jesuit" class="mw-redirect" title="Jesuit">Jesuit</a> missionary era of the 18th century and those killed in the politically inspired persecutions of the 19th century. A representative sample of only 117 martyrs—including 96 Vietnamese, 11 Spanish Dominicans, and ten French members of the <a href="/wiki/Paris_Foreign_Missions_Society" title="Paris Foreign Missions Society">Paris Foreign Missions Society</a> (Missions Etrangères de Paris (MEP))—were beatified on four separate occasions: 64 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIII" title="Pope Leo XIII">Pope Leo XIII</a> on May 27, 1900; eight by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_X" title="Pope Pius X">Pope Pius X</a> on May 20, 1906; 20 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_X" title="Pope Pius X">Pope Pius X</a> on May 2, 1909; and 25 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII" title="Pope Pius XII">Pope Pius XII</a> on April 29, 1951.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (December 2009)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> All 117 of these Vietnamese Martyrs were canonized on June 19, 1988.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> A young Vietnamese Martyr, <a href="/wiki/Andrew_of_Ph%C3%BA_Y%C3%AAn" title="Andrew of Phú Yên">Andrew of Phú Yên</a>, was beatified in March 2000, by Pope John Paul II.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Vietnam_martyrs_Paul_Mi_Pierre_Duong_Pierre_Truat_18_December_1838.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/Vietnam_martyrs_Paul_Mi_Pierre_Duong_Pierre_Truat_18_December_1838.jpg/220px-Vietnam_martyrs_Paul_Mi_Pierre_Duong_Pierre_Truat_18_December_1838.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="321" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/Vietnam_martyrs_Paul_Mi_Pierre_Duong_Pierre_Truat_18_December_1838.jpg/330px-Vietnam_martyrs_Paul_Mi_Pierre_Duong_Pierre_Truat_18_December_1838.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/Vietnam_martyrs_Paul_Mi_Pierre_Duong_Pierre_Truat_18_December_1838.jpg/440px-Vietnam_martyrs_Paul_Mi_Pierre_Duong_Pierre_Truat_18_December_1838.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1840" data-file-height="2686" /></a><figcaption>Vietnamese martyrs Paul Mi, Pierre Duong, Pierre Truat, martyred on 18 December 1838</figcaption></figure>
<p>The tortures these individuals underwent are considered by the Vatican to be among the worst in the history of Christian martyrdom. The torturers hacked off limbs joint by joint, tore flesh with red hot tongs, and used drugs to enslave the minds of the victims. Christians at the time were branded on the face with the words "tả đạo" (<a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%B7%A6" class="extiw" title="wikt:左">左</a><a href="https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E9%81%93" class="extiw" title="wikt:道">道</a>, lit. "Left (Sinister) religion")<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> and families and villages which subscribed to Christianity were obliterated.<sup id="cite_ref-Attwater_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Attwater-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The letters and example of <a href="/wiki/Th%C3%A9ophane_V%C3%A9nard" title="Théophane Vénard">Théophane Vénard</a> inspired the young Saint <a href="/wiki/Th%C3%A9r%C3%A8se_of_Lisieux" title="Thérèse of Lisieux">Thérèse of Lisieux</a> to volunteer for the <a href="/wiki/Carmelite" class="mw-redirect" title="Carmelite">Carmelite</a> nunnery at Hanoi, though she ultimately contracted <a href="/wiki/Tuberculosis" title="Tuberculosis">tuberculosis</a> and could not go. In 1865 Vénard's body was transferred to his Congregation's church in Paris, but his head remains in Vietnam.<sup id="cite_ref-KatNo_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-KatNo-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There are several Catholic <a href="/wiki/Parish" title="Parish">parishes</a> in the United States, Canada, and elsewhere dedicated to the Martyrs of Vietnam (Holy Martyrs of Vietnam Parishes), one of which is located in Arlington, Texas, in the Dallas-Fort Worth area.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> Others can be found in <a href="/wiki/Houston" title="Houston">Houston</a> and <a href="/wiki/Austin,_Texas" title="Austin, Texas">Austin, Texas</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Denver" title="Denver">Denver</a>, <a href="/wiki/Seattle" title="Seattle">Seattle</a>, <a href="/wiki/San_Antonio" title="San Antonio">San Antonio</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> Arlington, Virginia; Richmond, Virginia; and Norcross, Georgia. There are also churches named after individual saints, such as St. Philippe Minh Church in <a href="/wiki/Saint_Boniface,_Manitoba" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Boniface, Manitoba">Saint Boniface, Manitoba</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span id="The_Nguy.E1.BB.85n_Campaign_against_Catholicism_in_the_19th_century"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Nguyễn_Campaign_against_Catholicism_in_the_19th_century">The Nguyễn Campaign against Catholicism in the 19th century</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Vietnamese_Martyrs&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: The Nguyễn Campaign against Catholicism in the 19th century">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Catholic Church in Vietnam was devastated during the <a href="/wiki/T%C3%A2y_S%C6%A1n_rebellion" class="mw-redirect" title="Tây Sơn rebellion">Tây Sơn rebellion</a> in the late 18th century. During the turmoil, the missions revived, however, as a result of cooperation between the French Vicar Apostolic Pigneaux de Behaine and Nguyen Anh. After Nguyen's victory in 1802, in gratitude to assistance received, he ensured protection to missionary activities. However, only a few years into the new emperor's reign, there was growing antipathy among officials against Catholicism and missionaries reported that it was purely for political reasons that their presence was tolerated.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Tolerance continued until the death of the emperor and the new emperor Minh Mang succeeding to the throne in 1820.
</p><p>Converts began to be harassed without official edicts in the late 1820s, by local governments. In 1831, the emperor passed new laws on regulations for religious groupings in Viet Nam, and Catholicism was then officially prohibited. In 1832, the first act occurred in a largely Catholic village near <a href="/wiki/Hue_(city)" class="mw-redirect" title="Hue (city)">Hue</a>, with the entire community being incarcerated and sent into exile in Cambodia. In January 1833, a new kingdom-wide edict was passed calling on Vietnamese subjects to reject the religion of Jesus and required suspected Catholics to demonstrate their renunciation by walking on a wooden cross. Actual violence against Catholics, however, did not occur until the <a href="/wiki/L%C3%AA_V%C4%83n_Kh%C3%B4i_revolt" title="Lê Văn Khôi revolt">Lê Văn Khôi revolt</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>During the rebellion, a young French missionary priest named <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Marchand" title="Joseph Marchand">Joseph Marchand</a> was living in sickness in the rebel Gia Dinh citadel. In October 1833, an officer of the emperor reported to the court that a foreign Christian religious leader was present in the citadel. This news was used to justify the edicts against Catholicism, and led to the first executions of missionaries in over 40 years. The first executed was named <a href="/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois-Isidore_Gagelin" title="François-Isidore Gagelin">Francois Gagelin</a>. Marchand was captured and executed as a "rebel leader" in 1835, he was put to death by "<a href="/wiki/Slow_slicing" class="mw-redirect" title="Slow slicing">slicing</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Further repressive measures were introduced in the wake of this episode in 1836. Prior to 1836, village heads had only to simply report to local mandarins about how their subjects had recanted Catholicism; after 1836, officials could visit villages and force all the villagers to line up one by one to trample on a cross and if a community was suspected of harbouring a missionary, militia could block off the village gates and perform a rigorous search; if a missionary was found, collective punishment could be meted out to the entire community.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Missionaries and Catholic communities were able to sometimes escape this through bribery of officials; they were also sometimes victims of extortion attempts by people who demanded money under the threat that they would report the villages and missionaries to the authorities.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> The missionary Father Pierre Duclos said:
</p>
<blockquote><p>with gold bars murder and theft blossom among honest people.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>The court became more aware of the problem of the failure to enforce the laws and applied greater pressure on its officials to act; officials that failed to act or those tho who were seen to be acting too slowly were demoted or removed from office (and sometimes were given severe corporal punishment), while those who attacked and killed the Christians could receive promotion or other rewards. Lower officials or younger family members of officials were sometimes tasked with secretly going through villages to report on hidden missionaries or Catholics that had not apostasized.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The first missionary arrested during this (and later executed) was the priest <a href="/wiki/Jean-Charles_Cornay" title="Jean-Charles Cornay">Jean-Charles Cornay</a> in 1837. A military campaign was conducted in Nam Dinh after letters were discovered in a shipwrecked vessel bound for Macao. Quang Tri and Quang Binh officials captured several priests along with the French missionary <a href="/wiki/Bishop" title="Bishop">Bishop</a> <a href="/wiki/Pierre_Dumoulin-Borie" title="Pierre Dumoulin-Borie">Pierre Dumoulin-Borie</a> in 1838 (who was executed). The court translator, Francois Jaccard, a Catholic who had been kept as a prisoner for years and was extremely valuable to the court, was executed in late 1838; the official who was tasked with this execution, however, was almost immediately dismissed.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A priest, Father Ignatius Delgado, was captured in the village of <a href="/wiki/Can_Lao" class="mw-redirect" title="Can Lao">Can Lao</a> (<a href="/wiki/Nam_%C4%90%E1%BB%8Bnh_Province" class="mw-redirect" title="Nam Định Province">Nam Định Province</a>), put in a cage on public display for ridicule and abuse, and died of hunger and exposure while waiting for execution; <a rel="nofollow" class="external autonumber" href="http://saints.sqpn.com/saint-clemente-ignacio-delgado-cebrian/">[1]</a> the officer and soldiers that captured him were greatly rewarded (about 3&#160;kg of silver was distributed out to all of them), as were the villagers that had helped to turn him over to the authorities.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> The bishop Dominic Henares was found in Giao Thuy district of Nam Dinh (later executed); the villagers and soldiers that participated in his arrest were also greatly rewarded (about 3&#160;kg of silver distributed). The priest, Father Joseph Fernandez, and a local priest, Nguyen Ba Tuan, were captured in Kim Song, Nam Dinh; the provincial officials were promoted, the peasants who turned them over were given about 3&#160;kg of silver and other rewards were distributed. In July 1838, a demoted governor attempting to win back his place did so successfully by capturing the priest Father Dang Dinh Vien in Yen Dung, Bac Ninh province. (Vien was executed). In 1839, the same official captured two more priests: Father Dinh Viet Du and Father Nguyen Van Xuyen (also both executed).<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In Nhu Ly near Hue, an elderly Catholic doctor named Simon Hoa was captured and executed. He had been sheltering a missionary named Charles Delamotte, whom the villagers had pleaded with him to send away. The village was also supposed to erect a shrine for the state-cult, which the doctor also opposed. His status and age protected him from being arrested until 1840, when he was put on trial and the judge pleaded (due to his status in Vietnamese society as both an elder and a doctor) with him to publicly recant; when he refused he was publicly executed.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>A peculiar episode occurred in late 1839, when a village in Quang Ngai province called Phuoc Lam was victimized by four men who extorted cash from the villagers under threat of reporting the Christian presence to the authorities. The governor of the province had a Catholic nephew who told him about what happened, and the governor then found the four men (caught smoking opium) and had two executed as well as two exiled. When a Catholic lay leader then came to the governor to offer their gratitude (thus perhaps exposing what the governor had done), the governor told him that those who had come to die for their religion should now prepare themselves and leave something for their wives and children; when news of the whole episode came out, the governor was removed from office for incompetence.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Many officials preferred to avoid execution because of the threat to social order and harmony it represented, and resorted to use of threats or torture in order to force Catholics to recant. Many villagers were executed alongside priests according to mission reports. The emperor died in 1841, and this offered respite for Catholics. However, some persecution still continued after the new emperor took office. Catholic villages were forced to build shrines to the state cult. The missionary Father Pierre Duclos (quoted above) died in prison in after being captured on the Saigon river in June 1846. The boat he was traveling in, unfortunately contained the money that was set for the annual bribes of various officials (up to 1/3 of the annual donated French mission budget for Cochinchina was officially allocated to 'special needs') in order to prevent more arrests and persecutions of the converts; therefore, after his arrest, the officials then began wide searches and cracked down on the Catholic communities in their jurisdictions. The amount of money that the French mission societies were able to raise made the missionaries a lucrative target for officials that wanted cash, which could even surpass what the imperial court was offering in rewards. This created a cycle of extortion and bribery which lasted for years.<sup id="cite_ref-ramsay_12-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ramsay-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
