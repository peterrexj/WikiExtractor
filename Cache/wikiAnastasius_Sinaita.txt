<p><b>Anastasius Sinaita</b> (died after 700), also called <b>Anastasius of Sinai</b> or <b>Anastasius the Sinaite</b>, was a <a href="/wiki/Greeks" title="Greeks">Greek</a> writer, priest and abbot of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Catherine%27s_Monastery" title="Saint Catherine&#39;s Monastery">Saint Catherine's Monastery</a> on <a href="/wiki/Mount_Sinai" title="Mount Sinai">Mount Sinai</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anastasius_Sinaita&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>What little is known about his life is gathered from his own works.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> In Antiquity, he was often confused with the bishop and writer <a href="/wiki/Anastasius_I_of_Antioch" title="Anastasius I of Antioch">Anastasius I of Antioch</a> (559–598),<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and the authorship of various works attributed to Anastasius of Sinai is still vigorously disputed. A canon has been tentatively accepted by modern scholars, but even among these Anastasian works there are spurious sections.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> His writings concern questions and answers about issues of Christian dogma, ritual, and lifestyle (catechism); sermons; and <a href="/wiki/Exegesis" title="Exegesis">exegesis</a>. He was fond of tracing the <a href="/wiki/Etymologies" class="mw-redirect" title="Etymologies">etymologies</a> of key Christian terms; he was erudite in the <a href="/wiki/Bible" title="Bible">Bible</a> and early <a href="/wiki/Patristic" class="mw-redirect" title="Patristic">Patristic</a> literature; and he had a pervasive interest in the nature of God and man, especially in the <a href="/wiki/Person_of_Christ" class="mw-redirect" title="Person of Christ">person of Christ</a> (<a href="/wiki/Christology" title="Christology">Christology</a>).<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> He was not reluctant to develop and express his own theories about key ecclesiastical issues, which led to later commentaries, emendations, and perhaps even <a href="/wiki/Censorship" title="Censorship">censorship</a> of parts of his works.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Major_works">Major works</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Anastasius_Sinaita&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Major works">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The principal works transmitted under Anastasius' name include the <i>Viae Dux</i>, <i>Quaestiones et Responsiones</i>,  <i>Hexaemeron</i>, <i>Homilia i, ii, iii de creatione hominis</i>, and the <i>Narrationes</i>. The <i>Viae Dux</i> - also called the <i>Hodegos</i> (Greek transliteration) and "Guide Along the Right Path" (English translation) - was written in defense of the <a href="/wiki/Chalcedonian_Creed" class="mw-redirect" title="Chalcedonian Creed">Chalcedonian Creed</a>. A collection of works by Anastasius, the <i>Viae Dux</i> served to support the true faith and to counter the attacks of heretics, in particular <a href="/wiki/Monophysites" class="mw-redirect" title="Monophysites">Monophysites</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His <i>Quaestiones et Responsiones</i> ("Questions and Responses") was a popular genre<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> and falls under the category of pastoral theology. It offers advice, largely to the lay community, on spiritual and sacramental matters, charitable donations, marriage, and other subjects. Here Anastasius reveals a distinctly personal tone and offers a window into the day-to-day existence of ordinary people.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> It is especially significant because it is an eyewitness account of the expansion of Islam into Sinai and Egypt, which were predominantly Christian, and of the effect that Moslem domination had on Christian life and beliefs.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Anastasius was probably the author of the <i>Hexaemeron</i>, a commentary in 12 books about the <a href="/wiki/Genesis_creation_narrative" title="Genesis creation narrative">Genesis creation narrative</a>. (<i>Hexaemeron</i>, sometimes spelled <a href="/wiki/Hexameron" title="Hexameron">Hexameron</a>, means “six-days”.) In the <i>Hexaemeron</i> Anastasius argues that Moses on Mount Sinai was inspired by the Holy Spirit to write not only the creation narrative, but also in the same text to prophesize the New Creation through Christ. Thus Adam represents Christ and Eve represents the Church. Anastasius' extensive exegesis of the beginning of Genesis draws upon commentaries written by many Fathers of the Church, including <a href="/wiki/Clement_of_Alexandria" title="Clement of Alexandria">Clement of Alexandria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origen</a>, <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Nyssa" title="Gregory of Nyssa">Gregory of Nyssa</a>, <a href="/wiki/Gregory_of_Nazianzus" title="Gregory of Nazianzus">Gregory of Nazianzus</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Pseudo-Dionysius_the_Areopagite" title="Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite">Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite</a>. One reason for some doubts about Anastasius’ authorship is the lack of any surviving manuscript copied before the end of the fifteenth century. The lack of earlier manuscripts, however, could be the result of <a href="/wiki/Censorship" title="Censorship">censorship</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Allegorical_interpretations_of_Genesis" title="Allegorical interpretations of Genesis">allegorical interpretations of Genesis</a> in the <i>Hexaemeron</i> by Anastasius are in many ways a counterpoint to the more literal <i>Hexaemeron</i> written by <a href="/wiki/Basil_the_Great" class="mw-redirect" title="Basil the Great">Basil the Great</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
