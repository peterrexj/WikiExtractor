<p><b>Eusebius of Vercelli</b> (c. March 2, 283 – August 1, 371) was a bishop from <a href="/wiki/Sardinia" title="Sardinia">Sardinia</a> and is counted a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a>. Along with <a href="/wiki/Athanasius" class="mw-redirect" title="Athanasius">Athanasius</a>, he affirmed the divinity of <a href="/wiki/Jesus" title="Jesus">Jesus</a> against <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a>.

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Eusebius_of_Vercelli&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Eusebius was born in <a href="/wiki/Sardinia" title="Sardinia">Sardinia</a>, in 283. After his father's martyrdom, he was taken to <a href="/wiki/Rome" title="Rome">Rome</a> by his mother, where he later became a <a href="/wiki/Lector" title="Lector">lector</a>. He became the first bishop in <a href="/wiki/Vercelli" title="Vercelli">Vercelli</a> (in northern Italy), probably sometime in the early- to mid-340s. According to a letter of <a href="/wiki/Ambrose" title="Ambrose">Ambrose</a> to the congregation in Vercelli two decades after Eusebius' death, the local leaders recognized his piety and thus elected him rather than local candidates (<i>Epistola lxiii, Ad Vercellenses</i>). Inspired by St Athanasius's <i>Life of St Anthony</i>, he founded a priestly community in Vercelli that resembled a monastic community. This <a href="/wiki/Cenobitic_monasticism" title="Cenobitic monasticism">coenobium</a>, in turn, inspired others such as <a href="/wiki/Gaudentius_of_Novara" title="Gaudentius of Novara">Gaudentius of Novara</a>, <a href="/wiki/Eustasius_of_Aosta" title="Eustasius of Aosta">Eustasius of Aosta</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Maximus_of_Turin" title="Maximus of Turin">Maximus of Turin</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Benedict_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Benedict-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> He was the first bishop to live in common with the clergy, devoting his best energies to form them in piety and zeal.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>  For this reason the <a href="/wiki/Canons_Regular" class="mw-redirect" title="Canons Regular">Canons Regular of St. Augustine</a> honor him along with <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine</a> as their founder.<sup id="cite_ref-Ott_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ott-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 354, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Liberius" title="Pope Liberius">Pope Liberius</a> asked Eusebius to join Bishop <a href="/wiki/Lucifer_of_Cagliari" title="Lucifer of Cagliari">Lucifer of Cagliari</a> in carrying a request to the Emperor <a href="/wiki/Constantius_II" title="Constantius II">Constantius II</a> at <a href="/wiki/Milan" title="Milan">Milan</a>, pleading for the emperor to convoke a council to end the dissensions over the status of Athanasius of Alexandria and the matter of <a href="/wiki/Arianism" title="Arianism">Arianism</a>. The synod was held in Milan in 355. Eusebius attended part of the council, but refused to condemn Athanasius and so was exiled, first to <a href="/wiki/Bet_She%27an" class="mw-redirect" title="Bet She&#39;an">Scythopolis</a> in Syria, under the watchful eye of the Arian bishop <a href="/wiki/Patrophilus_of_Scythopolis" title="Patrophilus of Scythopolis">Patrophilus</a>, whom Eusebius calls his jailer, then to <a href="/wiki/Cappadocia" title="Cappadocia">Cappadocia</a>, and lastly to the <a href="/wiki/Thebaid" title="Thebaid">Thebaid</a>, in Upper Egypt.<sup id="cite_ref-Ott_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ott-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Several letters surrounding the council written to or by Eusebius still survive, as do two letters written by him during his exile.
</p><p>In the latter place Eusebius was dragged through the streets and persecuted in many ways, but never gave up the <a href="/wiki/Catholicism" class="mw-redirect" title="Catholicism">Catholic</a> faith.<sup id="cite_ref-SHMI_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SHMI-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Upon the accession of <a href="/wiki/Julian_the_Apostate" class="mw-redirect" title="Julian the Apostate">Julian</a>, the exiled bishops were free to return to their sees. Eusebius passed through Alexandria and there attended Athanasius' synod of 362 which confirmed the divinity of the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Spirit" title="Holy Spirit">Holy Ghost</a> and the orthodox doctrine concerning the <a href="/wiki/Incarnation" title="Incarnation">Incarnation</a>. The synod also agreed both to deal mildly with the repentant bishops who had signed Arianizing creeds under pressure and to impose severe penalties upon the leaders of several of the Arianizing factions.<sup id="cite_ref-Ott_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ott-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While still on his way home, Eusebius took the synod's decisions to <a href="/wiki/Antioch" title="Antioch">Antioch</a> and hoped to reconcile the schism there.  The church was divided between adherents of <a href="/wiki/Eustathius_of_Antioch" title="Eustathius of Antioch">Eustathius of Antioch</a>, who had been deposed and exiled by the Arians in 331, and those of the <a href="/wiki/Meletius_of_Antioch" title="Meletius of Antioch">Meletians</a>. Since Meletius' election in 361 was brought about chiefly by the Arians, the Eustathians would not recognize him, although he solemnly proclaimed his orthodox faith after his episcopal consecration. The Alexandrian synod had desired that Eusebius should reconcile the Eustathians with Bishop Meletius, by purging his election of whatever might have been irregular in it, but Eusebius found that Lucifer of Cagliari had also passed that way, and had unilaterally consecrated <a href="/wiki/Paulinus,_Bishop_of_Antioch" class="mw-redirect" title="Paulinus, Bishop of Antioch">Paulinus</a>, the leader of the Eustathians, as Bishop of Antioch.
</p><p>Unable to reconcile the factions, he continued towards home, visiting other churches along the way in the interest of promulgating and enforcing the orthodox faith. Once back in Vercelli in 363, he continued to be a leader with <a href="/wiki/Hilary_of_Poitiers" title="Hilary of Poitiers">Hilary of Poitiers</a> in defeating Arianism in the Western Church, and was one of the chief opponents of the Arian bishop <a href="/wiki/Auxentius_of_Milan" title="Auxentius of Milan">Auxentius of Milan</a>. He died in 370 or 371.
</p><p>Although in the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">Middle Ages</a> he was sometimes referred to as a martyr, this was more to honor the sufferings he endured in standing up for his faith. Later legends of his martyrdom have no historical basis. The Roman Catholic Church celebrates his feast on August 2.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>  His former feast day of December 16 roughly coincided with his elevation as bishop.  His current feast day roughly coincides with the anniversary of his death. <a href="/wiki/Vercelli_Cathedral" title="Vercelli Cathedral">Vercelli Cathedral</a> is dedicated to him.
</p><p>
In a General Audience in October 2007, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a> observed, </p><blockquote><p>Therefore, Pastors, Eusebius said, must urge the faithful not to consider the cities of the world as their permanent dwelling place but to seek the future city, the definitive heavenly Jerusalem. This "eschatological reserve" enables Pastors and faithful to preserve the proper scale of values without ever submitting to the fashions of the moment and the unjust claims of the current political power. The authentic scale of values – Eusebius' whole life seems to say – does not come from emperors of the past or of today but from Jesus Christ,...<sup id="cite_ref-Benedict_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Benedict-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>

<ul><li>Three short letters of Eusebius are printed in <a href="/wiki/Jacques_Paul_Migne" title="Jacques Paul Migne">Migne</a>, <i>Pat.Lat.</i>, XII, 947-54 and X, 713-14.<sup id="cite_ref-Benedict_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Benedict-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li><a href="/wiki/Jerome" title="Jerome">Jerome</a> (<i>Of Famous Men</i>, c. lvi, and Epstle lxi, n. 2) ascribes to him a Latin translation of a commentary on the Psalms, written originally in Greek by <a href="/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea" class="mw-redirect" title="Eusebius of Caesarea">Eusebius of Caesarea</a>; but this work has been lost.</li>
<li>In the cathedral at Vercelli is preserved the <i><a href="/wiki/Codex_Vercellensis" title="Codex Vercellensis">Codex Vercellensis</a></i>, the earliest manuscript of the old Latin Gospels ("Codex a"), which was believed to have been written by Eusebius, though now scholars tend to doubt it.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup></li>
<li>Krüger (<i>Lucifer, Bischof von Calaris</i>, Leipzig, 1886, 118–30) ascribes to Eusebius a baptismal oration by Caspari (<i>Quellen sur Geschichte des Taufsymbols</i>, Christiania, 1869, II, 132-40).</li>
<li>The confession of faith "Des. Trinitate confessio", <i>P.L.</i>, XII, 959–968, sometimes ascribed to Eusebius, is spurious.<sup id="cite_ref-Ott_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ott-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup></li></ul>
