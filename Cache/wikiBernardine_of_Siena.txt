<p><b>Bernardino of Siena</b>, <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Friars_Minor" title="Order of Friars Minor">OFM</a> (8 September 1380&#160;&#8211;&#32;20 May 1444), also known as <b>Bernardine</b>, was an Italian <a href="/wiki/Priesthood_in_the_Catholic_Church" title="Priesthood in the Catholic Church">priest</a> and <a href="/wiki/Franciscan" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan">Franciscan</a> missionary preacher in <a href="/wiki/Italy" title="Italy">Italy</a>. He was a systematizer of <a href="/wiki/Scholasticism" title="Scholasticism">Scholastic</a> economics. His preaching, his book burnings, and his "<a href="/wiki/Bonfire_of_the_vanities" title="Bonfire of the vanities">bonfires of the vanities</a>" made him famous/infamous during his own lifetime because they were frequently directed against sorcery, <a href="/wiki/Gambling" title="Gambling">gambling</a>, <a href="/wiki/Infanticide" title="Infanticide">infanticide</a>, <a href="/wiki/Witchcraft" title="Witchcraft">witchcraft</a>, homosexuals, Jews, Romani "Gypsies", <a href="/wiki/Usury" title="Usury">usury</a>, etc. Bernardino was later <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonised</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> – where he is also referred to as "the Apostle of Italy" – for his efforts to revive the country's Catholicism during the 15th century.<sup id="cite_ref-cna_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-cna-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Sources">Sources</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Sources">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Two <a href="/wiki/Hagiographies" class="mw-redirect" title="Hagiographies">hagiographies</a> of Bernardino of Siena were written by two of his friends; the one the same year in which he died, by Barnaba of Siena; the other by the humanist <a href="/wiki/Maffeo_Vegio" title="Maffeo Vegio">Maffeo Vegio</a>. Another important contemporary biographical source is that written by the Sienese diplomat Leonardo Benvoglienti, who was another personal acquaintance of Bernardino's.<sup id="cite_ref-butler_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-butler-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> The historian <a href="/wiki/Franco_Mormando" title="Franco Mormando">Franco Mormando</a> notes that "[t]he first works to be produced about Bernardine right after his death [in 1444] were biographical: by the year 1480, there were already over a dozen written accounts of the preacher's life".<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Bernardino_-_Della_confessione_regole_12.,_Adi_ultimo_di_gennaio_MCCCCLXXXXIIII_-_2473056.tif" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Bernardino_-_Della_confessione_regole_12.%2C_Adi_ultimo_di_gennaio_MCCCCLXXXXIIII_-_2473056.tif/lossy-page1-220px-Bernardino_-_Della_confessione_regole_12.%2C_Adi_ultimo_di_gennaio_MCCCCLXXXXIIII_-_2473056.tif.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="303" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Bernardino_-_Della_confessione_regole_12.%2C_Adi_ultimo_di_gennaio_MCCCCLXXXXIIII_-_2473056.tif/lossy-page1-330px-Bernardino_-_Della_confessione_regole_12.%2C_Adi_ultimo_di_gennaio_MCCCCLXXXXIIII_-_2473056.tif.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Bernardino_-_Della_confessione_regole_12.%2C_Adi_ultimo_di_gennaio_MCCCCLXXXXIIII_-_2473056.tif/lossy-page1-440px-Bernardino_-_Della_confessione_regole_12.%2C_Adi_ultimo_di_gennaio_MCCCCLXXXXIIII_-_2473056.tif.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2438" data-file-height="3357" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Bernardino_-_Della_confessione_regole_12.,_Adi_ultimo_di_gennaio_MCCCCLXXXXIIII_-_2473056.tif" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Della confessione regole 12.</i>, 1494</div></div></div>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Bernardino was born in 1380 to the noble Albizzeschi family in <a href="/wiki/Massa_Marittima" title="Massa Marittima">Massa Marittima</a>, <a href="/wiki/Tuscany" title="Tuscany">Tuscany</a>, a town in the <i><a href="/wiki/Contado" class="mw-redirect" title="Contado">contado</a></i> of <a href="/wiki/Siena" title="Siena">Siena</a> of which his father, Albertollo degli Albizzeschi, was then governor.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMormando199925–45_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMormando199925–45-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Left orphaned at six, he was raised by a pious aunt. In 1397, after a course of civil and canon law, he joined the Confraternity of Our Lady attached to the hospital of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_della_Scala,_Siena" title="Santa Maria della Scala, Siena">Santa Maria della Scala</a>. Three years later, when the plague visited Siena, he ministered to the plague-stricken, and, assisted by ten companions, took upon himself for four months entire charge of this hospital.<sup id="cite_ref-robinson_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-robinson-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> He escaped the plague but was so exhausted that a fever confined him for several months.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> In 1403 he joined the Observant branch of the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Friars_Minor" title="Order of Friars Minor">Order of Friars Minor</a> (the <a href="/wiki/Franciscan_Order" class="mw-redirect" title="Franciscan Order">Franciscan Order</a>), with strict observance of <a href="/wiki/Francis_of_Assisi" title="Francis of Assisi">Saint Francis</a>'s Rule. Bernardino was ordained a priest in 1404 and was commissioned as a preacher the next year.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> In about 1406 Saint <a href="/wiki/Vincent_Ferrer" title="Vincent Ferrer">Vincent Ferrer</a>, a <a href="/wiki/Dominican_Order" title="Dominican Order">Dominican</a> friar and missionary, while preaching at <a href="/wiki/Alessandria" title="Alessandria">Alessandria</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Piedmont" title="Piedmont">Piedmont</a> region of Italy, allegedly foretold that his mantle should descend upon one who was then listening to him, and said that he would return to France and Spain, leaving to Bernardino the task of evangelizing the remaining peoples of Italy.<sup id="cite_ref-robinson_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-robinson-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Bernardino_as_preacher">Bernardino as preacher</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Bernardino as preacher">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Preaching_style">Preaching style</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Preaching style">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Before Bernardino, most preachers either read a prepared speech or recited a rhetorical oration. Instead of remaining cloistered and preaching only during the liturgy, Bernardino preached directly to the public. For more than 30 years, he preached all over Italy and played a great part in the religious revival of the early fifteenth century. Although he had a weak and hoarse voice, he is said to have been one of the greatest preachers of his time.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> His style was simple, familiar, and abounding in imagery. Cynthia Polecritti, in her biography of Bernardino, notes that the texts of his sermons "are acknowledged masterpieces of colloquial Italian". He was an elegant and captivating preacher, and his use of popular imagery and creative language drew large crowds to hear his reflections. Invitations were often extended by the civil authorities rather than the ecclesiastical, as sometimes the towns would make money from the crowds that came to hear him.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMormando19995_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMormando19995-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Unlike many of his contemporaries, Bernardino chose his themes not from the daily liturgy, but from the ordinary lives of the people of Siena. He selected biblical themes to focus on the immediate interests of his audience. This proved effective in drawing their attention.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Women comprised the majority of listeners, and the size of the crowd varied according to the day, time, and topic of the sermon. Polecritti notes that the subject matter of his sermons reveals much about the contemporary context of 15th-century Italy.<sup id="cite_ref-horan_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-horan-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bernardino travelled from place to place, remaining nowhere more than a few weeks. These journeys were all made on foot. In the towns, the crowds assembled to hear him were at times so great that it became necessary to erect a pulpit on the marketplace.<sup id="cite_ref-thureau_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-thureau-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> Like Vincent Ferrer, he usually preached at dawn. His hearers, so as to ensure themselves standing room, would arrive beforehand, many coming from far-distant villages. The sermons often lasted three or four hours. He was invited to <a href="/wiki/Ferrara" title="Ferrara">Ferrara</a> in 1424, where he preached against the excess of luxury and immodest apparel. In <a href="/wiki/Bologna" title="Bologna">Bologna</a>, he spoke out against gambling, much to the dissatisfaction of the card manufacturers and sellers. Returning to <a href="/wiki/Siena" title="Siena">Siena</a> in April 1425, he preached there for 50 consecutive days.<sup id="cite_ref-thureau_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-thureau-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> His success was said to be remarkable. "<a href="/wiki/Bonfire_of_the_Vanities" class="mw-redirect" title="Bonfire of the Vanities">Bonfires of the Vanities</a>" were held at his sermon sites,<sup id="cite_ref-robinson_5-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-robinson-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup> where people threw mirrors, high-heeled shoes, perfumes, locks of false hair, cards, dice, <a href="/wiki/Chess" title="Chess">chessmen</a>, and other frivolities to be burned. Bernardino enjoined his listeners to abstain from blasphemy, indecent conversation, and games of hazard, and to observe feast days.<sup id="cite_ref-thureau_11-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-thureau-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bernardino was not universally popular. In L'Aquila, someone once sawed the legs of the pulpit, causing him to fall into the crowd.
</p><p>In 1427, Bernardino was called to Siena to allay factional strife; however, he preferred preaching to being an arbitrator. When he spoke against factions and vendettas, he was uncharacteristically tactful. To Policretti, this suggests that the situation could have become violent at the smallest provocation.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Both while he was alive and after his death (the first edition of his works, for the most part elaborate sermons, was printed at <a href="/wiki/Lyon" title="Lyon">Lyon</a> in 1501), the legacy of Bernardino was far from tame: of a strict, moral temper, he preached fiery sermons against many classes of people and these were riddled with denunciations. He characterized some women as "witches", and called for sodomites to be ostracized or otherwise removed from the human community. He thus was a champion of what historian Robert Moore called "the persecuting society" of late medieval Christian Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="On_women">On women</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: On women">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>The historian <a href="/wiki/Franco_Mormando" title="Franco Mormando">Franco Mormando</a> notes that in the twenty large volumes of Bernardino's extant printed works, the saint has much to say about women and to women, "married, unmarried, widowed, and those enclosed in nunneries". However, at the end of his study, "Bernardino of Siena: 'Great Defender' or 'Merciless Betrayer' of Women?", Mormando concludes: "...despite his sincere moments of greater empathy with women, Bernardino proves to be very much in the mainstream of writings on women of the Trecento and Quattrocento ... Though, in his compassion for her, Bernardine would have the woman's domestic and social 'cage' be as comfortable and as humane as possible, it is there in the same traditional cage that Bernardino nonetheless wants her to remain, under the guard of father, brother, husband, or parish priest."<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bernardino presented the Virgin Mary as an example for women. He advised girls never to talk to a man unless one of their parents was present.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> In one sermon Bernardino cautions women about marrying men who care more for their dowries than for them.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> On one occasion he asked mothers to come to church with their daughters alone so that he speak to them frankly about sexual abuse in marriage. He also spoke against the confinement of unwilling girls to convents.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> He presented Saint Joseph as an example for men, while emphasizing Mary's obedience to her husband.
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Against_sodomy_and_homosexuality">Against sodomy and homosexuality</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Against sodomy and homosexuality">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:San_Bernardino_(El_Greco).jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/San_Bernardino_%28El_Greco%29.jpg/220px-San_Bernardino_%28El_Greco%29.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="416" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/San_Bernardino_%28El_Greco%29.jpg/330px-San_Bernardino_%28El_Greco%29.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/San_Bernardino_%28El_Greco%29.jpg/440px-San_Bernardino_%28El_Greco%29.jpg 2x" data-file-width="1545" data-file-height="2920" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:San_Bernardino_(El_Greco).jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Saint Bernardino</i>, painting by <a href="/wiki/El_Greco" title="El Greco">El Greco</a>, Museo del Prado, Madrid</div></div></div>
<p>On <a href="/wiki/Sodomy" title="Sodomy">sodomy</a> (predominantly directed towards <a href="/wiki/Homosexual" class="mw-redirect" title="Homosexual">homosexual</a> men), Bernardino keenly pointed out the reputation of the Italians beyond their own borders.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup> In the same work is a detailed analysis of Bernardino's preaching against witchcraft and Jews.&lt;/ref&gt; He particularly decried Florentine lenience towards men having sex with men; in <a href="/wiki/Verona" title="Verona">Verona</a>, he approvingly reminded listeners that a man was quartered and his limbs hung from the city gates for homosexual intercourse; in <a href="/wiki/Genoa" title="Genoa">Genoa</a>, men were regularly burned if found guilty of sodomy; and in <a href="/wiki/Venice" title="Venice">Venice</a> a sodomite had been tied to a column along with a barrel of pitch and brushwood and set to fire. He advised the people of Siena to do the same. In 1424 he dedicated three consecutive sermons in Florence to the subject, in the course of a Lenten sermon preached in Santa Croce, he admonished his hearers:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>Whenever you hear sodomy mentioned, each and every one of you spit on the ground and clean your mouth out as well. If they don't want to change their ways by any other means, maybe they will change when they're made fools of. Spit hard! Maybe the water of your spit will extinguish their fire.<sup id="cite_ref-Crompton2003_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Crompton2003-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>In Siena Bernardino preached a full sermon against sodomy, including homosexuality, in 1425 and then 1427; linking it directly with fears about the depopulation as it did not lead to children and was therefore unproductive: "You don't understand that [sodomy] is the reason you have lost half your population over the last twenty-five years".<sup id="cite_ref-Naphy2004_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Naphy2004-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> Over time his teachings helped mould public sentiment and dispel indifference over controlling sodomy and homosexual conduct more vigorously. Everything unpredictable or calamitous in human experience he attributed to sodomy, including floods and the plague, as well as linking the practice to local population decline.<sup id="cite_ref-Rocke1998_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Rocke1998-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> After one of his sermons in Siena, four "irate sodomites" attempted to beat him with clubs.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMormando19996_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMormando19996-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Trial_in_Rome">Trial in Rome</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Trial in Rome">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Siena.Duomo.CappVoto.StBernardine.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/10/Siena.Duomo.CappVoto.StBernardine.jpg/220px-Siena.Duomo.CappVoto.StBernardine.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/10/Siena.Duomo.CappVoto.StBernardine.jpg/330px-Siena.Duomo.CappVoto.StBernardine.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/10/Siena.Duomo.CappVoto.StBernardine.jpg/440px-Siena.Duomo.CappVoto.StBernardine.jpg 2x" data-file-width="960" data-file-height="1280" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Siena.Duomo.CappVoto.StBernardine.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Saint Bernardino, Capella del Voto, Siena</div></div></div>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Unknown_artist_Bernardino_of_Siena_XV_c_,_National_Museum_of_Serbia.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6a/Unknown_artist_Bernardino_of_Siena_XV_c_%2C_National_Museum_of_Serbia.jpg/220px-Unknown_artist_Bernardino_of_Siena_XV_c_%2C_National_Museum_of_Serbia.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="421" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6a/Unknown_artist_Bernardino_of_Siena_XV_c_%2C_National_Museum_of_Serbia.jpg/330px-Unknown_artist_Bernardino_of_Siena_XV_c_%2C_National_Museum_of_Serbia.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6a/Unknown_artist_Bernardino_of_Siena_XV_c_%2C_National_Museum_of_Serbia.jpg/440px-Unknown_artist_Bernardino_of_Siena_XV_c_%2C_National_Museum_of_Serbia.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2088" data-file-height="3996" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Unknown_artist_Bernardino_of_Siena_XV_c_,_National_Museum_of_Serbia.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Unknown artist: <i>Bernardino of Siena</i>, 15th century, <a href="/wiki/National_Museum_of_Serbia" title="National Museum of Serbia">National Museum of Serbia</a></div></div></div>
<p>Especially known for his devotion to the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Name_of_Jesus" title="Holy Name of Jesus">Holy Name of Jesus</a>, which was previously associated with <a href="/wiki/John_of_Vercelli" title="John of Vercelli">John of Vercelli</a> and the Dominican order, Bernardino devised a symbol—IHS—the first three letters of the name of Jesus in Greek, in Gothic letters on a blazing sun. This was to displace the insignia of factions (for example, <a href="/wiki/Guelphs_and_Ghibellines" title="Guelphs and Ghibellines">Guelphs and Ghibellines</a>). The devotion spread, and the symbol began to appear in churches, homes and public buildings. Opponents thought it a dangerous innovation.<sup id="cite_ref-foley_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-foley-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1426 Bernardino was summoned to Rome to stand trial on charges of <a href="/wiki/Heresy" title="Heresy">heresy</a> himself for his promotion of this devotion to the Holy Name of Jesus<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> Theologians including <a href="/wiki/Paul_of_Venice" title="Paul of Venice">Paul of Venice</a> gave their opinions. Bernardino was found innocent of heresy, and he impressed <a href="/wiki/Pope_Martin_V" title="Pope Martin V">Pope Martin V</a> sufficiently that Martin requested he preach in Rome. He thereupon preached every day for 80 days. Bernardino's zeal was such that he would prepare up to four drafts of a sermon before starting to speak. That same year, he was offered the <a href="/wiki/Bishopric_of_Siena" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishopric of Siena">bishopric of Siena</a>, but declined in order to maintain his monastic and evangelical activities. In 1431, he toured Tuscany, <a href="/wiki/Lombardy" title="Lombardy">Lombardy</a>, <a href="/wiki/Romagna" title="Romagna">Romagna</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Ancona" title="Ancona">Ancona</a> before returning to Siena to prevent a war against Florence. Also in 1431, he declined the <a href="/wiki/Bishopric_of_Ferrara" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishopric of Ferrara">bishopric of Ferrara</a>, and in 1435 he declined the <a href="/wiki/Bishopric_of_Urbino" class="mw-redirect" title="Bishopric of Urbino">bishopric of Urbino</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-acton_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-acton-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/John_of_Capistrano" title="John of Capistrano">John of Capistrano</a> was Bernardino's friend, and <a href="/wiki/James_of_the_Marches" title="James of the Marches">James of the Marches</a> was his disciple during these years. Cardinals urged both Pope Martin V and <a href="/wiki/Pope_Eugene_IV" title="Pope Eugene IV">Pope Eugene IV</a> to condemn Bernardino, but both almost instantly acquitted him. A trial at the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Florence" title="Council of Florence">Council of Florence</a> also ended with an acquittal. The <a href="/wiki/Holy_Roman_Emperor" title="Holy Roman Emperor">Holy Roman Emperor</a> <a href="/wiki/Sigismund" title="Sigismund">Sigismund</a> sought Bernardino's counsel and intercession and Bernardino accompanied him to Rome in 1433 for his coronation.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="On_Contracts_and_Usury"><i>On Contracts and Usury</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: On Contracts and Usury">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Economic_theory">Economic theory</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Economic theory">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Bernardino was the first theologian after <a href="/wiki/Peter_John_Olivi" title="Peter John Olivi">Peter John Olivi</a> to write an entire work systematically devoted to Scholastic economics. His greatest contribution to economics was a discussion and defence of the entrepreneur. His book, <i>On Contracts and Usury</i>, written during the years 1431–1433, dealt with the justification of private property, the ethics of trade, the determination of value and price, and the usury question.<sup id="cite_ref-acton_24-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-acton-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>One of his contributions was a discussion on the functions of the business entrepreneur, whom Bernardino saw as performing the useful social function of transporting, distributing, or manufacturing goods. According to <a href="/wiki/Murray_Rothbard" title="Murray Rothbard">Murray Rothbard</a>, Bernardino's insight in determining just value prefigured "...the Jevons/Austrian analysis of supply and cost over five centuries later."<sup id="cite_ref-mnr_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mnr-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> He extended this to a theory of the "just wage", which would be determined by the demand for labour and the available supply. Wage inequality is a function of differences in skill, ability and training. Skilled workers are scarcer than unskilled so that the former will command a higher wage.<sup id="cite_ref-mnr_25-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mnr-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Usury">Usury</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Usury">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Usury was one of the principal objects of Bernardino's attacks, and he did much to prepare the way for the establishment of the beneficial loan societies, known as <a href="/wiki/Mount_of_piety" title="Mount of piety">Monti di Pietà</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-robinson_5-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-robinson-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bernardino followed the earlier scholastic philosophers <a href="/wiki/Anselm_of_Canterbury" title="Anselm of Canterbury">Anselm of Canterbury</a> and <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas" title="Thomas Aquinas">Thomas Aquinas</a> (who quotes <a href="/wiki/Aristotle" title="Aristotle">Aristotle</a>) in condemning the practice of usury, which they defined as charging interest on a loan. Scholastic analysis of usury, based in part on Roman law, was both complex and, in some instances, seemingly contradictory. Interest on delayed payment was deemed valid as it was considered compensation for damages suffered by the creditor being deprived of his property.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Bernardino saw usury as concentrating all the money of the city into a few hands. However, he did accept the theory of <i>lucrum cessans</i>, interest charged to compensate for profits foregone in lieu of capital investment.<sup id="cite_ref-mnr_25-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mnr-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> A distinction was also drawn for joint ventures, in which the creditor assumed some portion of the risk.
</p><p>In Milan, he was visited by a merchant who urged him to inveigh strenuously against usury, only to find that his visitor was himself a prominent usurer, whose activities were prompted by a wish to lessen competition.<sup id="cite_ref-thureau_11-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-thureau-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h4><span class="mw-headline" id="Anti-Semitism">Anti-Semitism</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Anti-Semitism">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h4>
<p>Bernardino is particularly regarded today as being a "major protagonist of Christian anti-semitism".<sup id="cite_ref-Franco_Mormando_1999_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Franco_Mormando_1999-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
In January 1427 he was in <a href="/wiki/Orvieto" title="Orvieto">Orvieto</a>, where he preached on the topic of <a href="/wiki/Usury" title="Usury">usury</a>, urging the executive to take stringent steps against all such as were addicted to this business, many of whom were Jews. Blaming the poverty of local Christians on Jewish usury, his call for Jews to be banished and isolated from their wider communities led to segregation.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> His audiences often used his words to reinforce actions against Jews, and his preaching left a legacy of resentment on the part of Jews.<sup id="cite_ref-Franco_Mormando_1999_27-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Franco_Mormando_1999-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Franciscan_Vicar_General">Franciscan Vicar General</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Franciscan Vicar General">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Soon thereafter, he withdrew again to <a href="/wiki/Serracapriola" title="Serracapriola">Serracapriola</a> to compose a further series of sermons. He resumed his missionary labours in 1436 but was forced to abandon them when he became vicar-general of the Observant branch of the Franciscans in Italy in 1438.
</p><p>Bernardino had worked to grow the Observants from the outset of his religious life: although he was not in fact its founder (the origins of the Observants, or <i><a href="/wiki/Zelanti" title="Zelanti">Zelanti</a></i>, can be traced back to the middle of the fourteenth century). Nevertheless, Bernardino became to the Observants what <a href="/wiki/Bernard_of_Clairvaux" title="Bernard of Clairvaux">Saint Bernard</a> had been to the <a href="/wiki/Cistercians" title="Cistercians">Cistercians</a>, their principal support and indefatigable propagator. Instead of one hundred and thirty Friars constituting the Observance in Italy at Bernardino's reception into the order, it counted over four thousand shortly before his death. Bernardino also founded, or reformed, at least three hundred convents of Friars. He sent missionaries to different parts of Asia, and it was largely through his efforts that many ambassadors from different schismatical nations attended the <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Florence" title="Council of Florence">Council of Florence</a>, in which we find him addressing the assembled Fathers in Greek.<sup id="cite_ref-robinson_5-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-robinson-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Being Vicar General inevitably cut back his opportunities to preach, but he continued to speak to the public when he could. Having in 1442 persuaded the Pope to finally accept his resignation as Vicar-General so that he might give himself more undividedly to preaching, Bernardino again resumed his missionary work. Despite the <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">papal bull</a> issued by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Eugene_IV" title="Pope Eugene IV">Pope Eugene IV</a> in 1443, "<a href="/wiki/Illius_qui_se_pro_divini" title="Illius qui se pro divini">Illius qui se pro divini</a>", which charged Bernardino to preach the indulgence for the <a href="/wiki/Crusade" class="mw-redirect" title="Crusade">Crusade</a> against the Turks, there is no record of his having done so. In 1444, notwithstanding his increasing infirmities, Bernardino, desirous that there should be no part of Italy which had not heard his voice, set out to the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Naples" title="Kingdom of Naples">Kingdom of Naples</a>.
</p><p>Bernardino died that year at <a href="/wiki/L%27Aquila" title="L&#39;Aquila">L'Aquila</a> in <a href="/wiki/Abruzzo" title="Abruzzo">Abruzzo</a> and is buried in the <a href="/wiki/Basilica_of_San_Bernardino" title="Basilica of San Bernardino">Basilica of San Bernardino</a>. According to tradition, his grave continued to leak blood until two factions of the city achieved reconciliation.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Canonisation_and_iconography">Canonisation and iconography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=14" title="Edit section: Canonisation and iconography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tleft"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Pietro_di_Giovanni_d%27Ambrogio._St_Bernardino_196x89cm._ca.1444._Pinacoteca,_Siena.jpeg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0e/Pietro_di_Giovanni_d%27Ambrogio._St_Bernardino_196x89cm._ca.1444._Pinacoteca%2C_Siena.jpeg/220px-Pietro_di_Giovanni_d%27Ambrogio._St_Bernardino_196x89cm._ca.1444._Pinacoteca%2C_Siena.jpeg" decoding="async" width="220" height="452" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0e/Pietro_di_Giovanni_d%27Ambrogio._St_Bernardino_196x89cm._ca.1444._Pinacoteca%2C_Siena.jpeg/330px-Pietro_di_Giovanni_d%27Ambrogio._St_Bernardino_196x89cm._ca.1444._Pinacoteca%2C_Siena.jpeg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0e/Pietro_di_Giovanni_d%27Ambrogio._St_Bernardino_196x89cm._ca.1444._Pinacoteca%2C_Siena.jpeg/440px-Pietro_di_Giovanni_d%27Ambrogio._St_Bernardino_196x89cm._ca.1444._Pinacoteca%2C_Siena.jpeg 2x" data-file-width="626" data-file-height="1287" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Pietro_di_Giovanni_d%27Ambrogio._St_Bernardino_196x89cm._ca.1444._Pinacoteca,_Siena.jpeg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>An early painting of Saint Bernardino, c. 1444, by Pietro di Giovanni d'Ambrogio</div></div></div><p>.
</p><p>Reports of miracles attributed to Bernardino multiplied rapidly after his death, and he was <a href="/wiki/Canonized" class="mw-redirect" title="Canonized">canonized</a> in 1450, only six years after his death, by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Nicholas_V" title="Pope Nicholas V">Pope Nicholas V</a>. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a> is on 20 May, the day of his death.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Antonio_Marescotti,_Saint_Bernardino_of_Siena,_1380-1444,_Canonized_1450_(obverse),_c._1444-1462,_NGA_44372.jpg" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Antonio_Marescotti%2C_Saint_Bernardino_of_Siena%2C_1380-1444%2C_Canonized_1450_%28obverse%29%2C_c._1444-1462%2C_NGA_44372.jpg/220px-Antonio_Marescotti%2C_Saint_Bernardino_of_Siena%2C_1380-1444%2C_Canonized_1450_%28obverse%29%2C_c._1444-1462%2C_NGA_44372.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="220" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Antonio_Marescotti%2C_Saint_Bernardino_of_Siena%2C_1380-1444%2C_Canonized_1450_%28obverse%29%2C_c._1444-1462%2C_NGA_44372.jpg/330px-Antonio_Marescotti%2C_Saint_Bernardino_of_Siena%2C_1380-1444%2C_Canonized_1450_%28obverse%29%2C_c._1444-1462%2C_NGA_44372.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Antonio_Marescotti%2C_Saint_Bernardino_of_Siena%2C_1380-1444%2C_Canonized_1450_%28obverse%29%2C_c._1444-1462%2C_NGA_44372.jpg/440px-Antonio_Marescotti%2C_Saint_Bernardino_of_Siena%2C_1380-1444%2C_Canonized_1450_%28obverse%29%2C_c._1444-1462%2C_NGA_44372.jpg 2x" data-file-width="3093" data-file-height="3092" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Antonio_Marescotti,_Saint_Bernardino_of_Siena,_1380-1444,_Canonized_1450_(obverse),_c._1444-1462,_NGA_44372.jpg" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Antonio Marescotti, <i>Saint Bernardino of Siena</i> (obverse), c. 1444–1462. Made shortly after Bernardino's death.</div></div></div>
<p>Mormando observes that "Bernardino's lifetime coincided with the blossoming not only of art in general but also of full-fledged, realistic portraiture as a distinct genre. This may explain why Bernardino, it seems, has the distinction of being the first saint in Church history for whom we have an indisputably authentic portrait in art. Many of the earliest portraits were presumably based on his death mask..."<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> Bernardino also lived into the early days of the <a href="/wiki/Old_master_print" title="Old master print">print</a> and was the subject of portraits in his lifetime, as well as a death-mask, which were copied to make prints, so that he is one of the earliest saints to have a fairly consistent appearance in art; though many <a href="/wiki/Baroque" title="Baroque">Baroque</a> images, such as that by <a href="/wiki/El_Greco" title="El Greco">El Greco</a>, are idealized compared to the realistic ones made in the decades after his death.
</p>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Sancti_Bernardini_Senensis_Opera_omnia.tif" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2b/Sancti_Bernardini_Senensis_Opera_omnia.tif/lossy-page1-220px-Sancti_Bernardini_Senensis_Opera_omnia.tif.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="309" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2b/Sancti_Bernardini_Senensis_Opera_omnia.tif/lossy-page1-330px-Sancti_Bernardini_Senensis_Opera_omnia.tif.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2b/Sancti_Bernardini_Senensis_Opera_omnia.tif/lossy-page1-440px-Sancti_Bernardini_Senensis_Opera_omnia.tif.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4676" data-file-height="6569" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Sancti_Bernardini_Senensis_Opera_omnia.tif" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div>Bernardino's portrait, in <i>Sancti Bernardini Senensis Opera omnia</i> (1745)</div></div></div>
<p>After his death, the Franciscans promoted an iconographical program of diffusion of images of Bernardino, which was second only to that of the founder of the order. As such, he is one of the earliest saints whose appearance was given a distinct and readily recognisable iconography. Artists of the late medieval and Renaissance periods often represented him as small and emaciated,<sup id="cite_ref-dictionary_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dictionary-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> with three mitres at his feet (representing the three bishoprics which he had rejected) and holding in his hand the <i><a href="/wiki/Christogram" title="Christogram">IHS</a></i> monogram with rays emanating from it (representing his devotion to the "Holy Name of Jesus"), which was his main attribute.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> He appears to have been a favourite in the works of <a href="/wiki/Luca_della_Robbia" title="Luca della Robbia">Luca della Robbia</a>, and one of the finest examples of Renaissance art includes relief carvings of the saint, which can be seen in the oratory of <a href="/wiki/Perugia_Cathedral" title="Perugia Cathedral">Perugia Cathedral</a>.
</p><p>A portrait is known to have circulated in Siena just after the death of Bernardino which, on the basis of physiognomic similarities with his death mask at L'Aquila, is believed to have been a good likeness. It is thought probable that many subsequent depictions of the saint derive from this portrait.
</p><p>The most famous depictions of Bernardino are found in the cycle of frescoes of his life, which were executed towards the end of the fifteenth century by <a href="/wiki/Pinturicchio" title="Pinturicchio">Pinturicchio</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Bufalini_Chapel" title="Bufalini Chapel">Bufalini Chapel</a> of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_in_Aracoeli" class="mw-redirect" title="Santa Maria in Aracoeli">Santa Maria in Aracoeli</a>, Rome.
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Bernardino_of_Siena&amp;action=edit&amp;section=15" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Saint Bernardino is the Roman Catholic <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of <a href="/wiki/Advertising" title="Advertising">advertising</a>, <a href="/wiki/Communication" title="Communication">communications</a>, <a href="/wiki/Compulsive_gambling" class="mw-redirect" title="Compulsive gambling">compulsive gambling</a>, <a href="/wiki/Respiratory_system" title="Respiratory system">respiratory</a> problems, as well as any problems involving the chest area. He is the patron saint of <a href="/wiki/Carpi,_Emilia-Romagna" title="Carpi, Emilia-Romagna">Carpi</a>, Italy; the Philippine <a href="/wiki/Barangay" title="Barangay">barangays</a> of <a href="/wiki/Kay-Anlog" class="mw-redirect" title="Kay-Anlog">Kay-Anlog</a> in <a href="/wiki/Calamba,_Laguna" title="Calamba, Laguna">Calamba, Laguna</a> and Tuna in <a href="/wiki/Cardona,_Rizal" title="Cardona, Rizal">Cardona, Rizal</a>; and the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_San_Bernardino" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of San Bernardino">diocese</a> of <a href="/wiki/San_Bernardino,_California" title="San Bernardino, California">San Bernardino, California</a>, USA. <a href="/wiki/Siena_College" title="Siena College">Siena College</a>, a Franciscan Catholic liberal arts college in <a href="/wiki/New_York_(state)" title="New York (state)">New York</a>, USA, was named after him and placed under his spiritual patronage. The mountain pass in the Alps, <i>Il Passo di San Bernardino</i>, is named in honour of the saint, whose fame reached well into northern Italy and southern Switzerland.
</p><p>His cult also spread to England at an early period, and was particularly promulgated by the <a href="/wiki/Franciscans" title="Franciscans">Observant Friars</a>, who first established themselves in the country in <a href="/wiki/Greenwich" title="Greenwich">Greenwich</a>, in 1482, not forty years after his death, but who were later suppressed.<sup id="cite_ref-dictionary_30-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-dictionary-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
