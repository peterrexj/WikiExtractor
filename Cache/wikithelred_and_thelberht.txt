					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content mw-content-ltr" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="mw-parser-output"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Princes of the Kingdom of Kent</div>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1066479718">.mw-parser-output .infobox-subbox{padding:0;border:none;margin:-3px;width:auto;min-width:100%;font-size:100%;clear:none;float:none;background-color:transparent}.mw-parser-output .infobox-3cols-child{margin:auto}.mw-parser-output .infobox .navbar{font-size:100%}body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-header,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-subheader,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-above,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-title,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-image,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-full-data,body.skin-minerva .mw-parser-output .infobox-below{text-align:center}</style><table class="infobox vcard"><tbody><tr><th colspan="2" class="infobox-above n" style="background-color:gold;"><div style="display:inline;" class="fn">Saints Aethelred  and Aethelberht of Kent (of Eastry)</div></th></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Born</th><td class="infobox-data">seventh century</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Died</th><td class="infobox-data">c. 669</td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label">Venerated&#160;in</th><td class="infobox-data"><a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Christianity" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon Christianity">Anglo-Saxon Christianity</a></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><span class="nowrap">Major <a href="/wiki/Shrine" title="Shrine">shrine</a></span></th><td class="infobox-data">Ramsey Abbey in <a href="/wiki/Huntingdonshire" title="Huntingdonshire">Huntingdonshire</a>; <a href="/wiki/Canterbury" title="Canterbury">Canterbury</a><sup id="cite_ref-patrick_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patrick-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints" title="Calendar of saints">Feast</a></th><td class="infobox-data">17 October (translation of relics)<sup id="cite_ref-patrick_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patrick-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></td></tr><tr><th scope="row" class="infobox-label"><a href="/wiki/Saint_symbolism" title="Saint symbolism">Attributes</a></th><td class="infobox-data">with Aethelberht, as royal brothers, sometimes with <a href="/wiki/Sword" title="Sword">swords</a><sup id="cite_ref-patrick_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-patrick-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Saints <b>Æthelred</b> and <b>Æthelberht</b> (also <i>Ethelred</i>, <i>Ethelbert</i>)  according to the <a href="/wiki/Kentish_royal_legend" class="mw-redirect" title="Kentish royal legend">Kentish royal legend</a> (attested in the 11th century) were princes of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent" title="Kingdom of Kent">Kingdom of Kent</a> who were murdered in around AD 669, and later commemorated as <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saints</a> and <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyrs</a>. Their story forms an important element in the legend of Saint <a href="/wiki/Mildrith" title="Mildrith">Mildrith</a>, because the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Minster-in-Thanet" title="Minster-in-Thanet">Minster</a> in <a href="/wiki/Thanet_District" title="Thanet District">Thanet</a> is said to have been founded in atonement for the crime.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Historical_context">Historical context</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelred_and_%C3%86thelberht&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Historical context">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>King <a href="/wiki/Eorcenberht_of_Kent" title="Eorcenberht of Kent">Eorcenberht of Kent</a> seized the rule of Kent in 640  in precedence to his elder brother <a href="/wiki/Eormenred_of_Kent" title="Eormenred of Kent">Eormenred</a>. Both were sons of Eadbald of Kent (r. c. 616–640). The legend, contained in a Latin <i>Passio</i>, tells that Eormenred and his wife Oslafa had several children including the two sons Aethelred and Aethelberht, and a daughter Eormenbeorg, also known as <a href="/wiki/Domne_Eafe" title="Domne Eafe">Domne Eafe</a>. Eafe married <a href="/wiki/Merewalh" title="Merewalh">Merewalh</a>, ruler of the <a href="/wiki/Magons%C3%A6te" title="Magonsæte">Maegonsaetan</a>, a people situated in the west Midlands in the <a href="/wiki/Shropshire" title="Shropshire">Shropshire</a> area. King Eorcenberht married <a href="/wiki/Seaxburh_of_Ely" title="Seaxburh of Ely">Seaxburh</a>, daughter of King <a href="/wiki/Anna_of_East_Anglia" title="Anna of East Anglia">Anna of East Anglia</a>, and ruled as a Christian king: he was the first ruler to order the abandonment and destruction of idols throughout his kingdom, and to establish the forty days' fast of <a href="/wiki/Lent" title="Lent">Lent</a> to be observed by royal authority (<a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>, <i><a href="/wiki/Ecclesiastical_History_of_the_English_People" title="Ecclesiastical History of the English People">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i>,  iii,8). He had two sons, <a href="/wiki/Ecgberht_of_Kent" title="Ecgberht of Kent">Ecgberht</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hlothhere" class="mw-redirect" title="Hlothhere">Hlothhere</a>, and two daughters, Eormenhild and Eorcongota. On Eorconberht's death of the plague in 664, Ecgberht succeeded him as King of Kent.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="The_legend">The legend</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=%C3%86thelred_and_%C3%86thelberht&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: The legend">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Kentish_Royal_Legend" title="Kentish Royal Legend">Kentish Royal Legend</a></div> <link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1033289096"/><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: <a href="/wiki/Domne_Eafe#The_legend" title="Domne Eafe">Domne Eafe §&#160;The legend</a></div>
<p>According to the  legend, the princes were very pious Christian youths and lived at <a href="/wiki/Eastry" title="Eastry">Eastry</a>, Kent, at a royal dwelling belonging to their cousin King Egberht. (It is likely that such a residence existed, for Sir <a href="/wiki/Frank_Stenton" title="Frank Stenton">Frank Stenton</a> pointed out that the placename Eastry, comparable to <a href="/wiki/Surrey" title="Surrey">Surrey</a> in formation, represented an early administrative centre.) A royal retainer named Thunor wished to secure the succession of King Ecgberht from a possible rival claim by these youths. He therefore had them secretly murdered, and their bodies hidden beneath the royal seat in the Hall at Eastry. After they were missed, but nowhere found, the crime was revealed by a column of light which appeared shining above the place of concealment.
</p><p>When King Egberht learned of the crime he was filled with sorrow and remorse at the act which had been done in his name, and planned to have the bodies buried at <a href="/wiki/Canterbury" title="Canterbury">Canterbury</a>. However, the people charged with the task of taking the bodies there found it impossible to move them. After these efforts the king took advice from his religious leaders, who recommended that he have them taken to Wakering in the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Essex" title="Kingdom of Essex">Kingdom of Essex</a> for burial, where a monastery already existed. The site was probably <a href="/wiki/Great_Wakering" title="Great Wakering">Great Wakering</a>, not many miles away from the possibly royal burial-site of <a href="/wiki/Prittlewell" title="Prittlewell">Prittlewell</a>, Essex. With this new destination the bodies consented to be moved, and were venerated in their final resting-place as royal Christian <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyrs</a>.
</p><p>At about this time Egberht's mother Queen Seaxburh founded her own double monastery at <a href="/wiki/Minster,_Swale" title="Minster, Swale">Minster</a> in <a href="/wiki/Isle_of_Sheppey" title="Isle of Sheppey">Sheppey</a>, on the south bank of the <a href="/wiki/Thames_Estuary" title="Thames Estuary">Thames Estuary</a> nearly opposite Wakering. The monastery at <a href="/wiki/St_Mary%27s_Church,_Reculver" title="St Mary&#39;s Church, Reculver">Reculver</a> was founded in 669. Ecgberht then founded the monastery of Minster in Thanet, to be ruled over by the sister of the murdered princes. She was the mother of Saint Mildrith, who afterwards succeeded her as abbess. Some sources also claim that another monastery was established at Eastry for the same reason, over which a sister of <a href="/wiki/Mildrith" title="Mildrith">Mildrith</a>'s ruled as abbess. Another sister, <a href="/wiki/Mildburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Mildburg">Mildburg</a>, remained among the Magonsaetan and governed the monastery of <a href="/wiki/Much_Wenlock" title="Much Wenlock">Much Wenlock</a> in Shropshire.
</p><p>In addition to the Latin Passio (edited by David Rollason) a version of the story appears in <a href="/wiki/Roger_of_Wendover" title="Roger of Wendover">Roger of Wendover</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Flores_Historiarum" title="Flores Historiarum">Flores Historiarum</a></i> (<i>Flowers of History</i>), compiled in the early thirteenth century. Excavations at Great Wakering have recently uncovered a site of Middle Saxon occupation including a fragment of ornamented stone-sculpture, which may derive from the place named in the legend.
</p>
