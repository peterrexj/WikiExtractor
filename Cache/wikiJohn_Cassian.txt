<p><b>John Cassian</b>, also known as <b>John the Ascetic</b> and <b>John Cassian the Roman</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Ioannes Eremita Cassianus</i>, <i>Ioannus Cassianus</i>, or <i>Ioannes Massiliensis</i>;<sup id="cite_ref-ccel_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ccel-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;AD&#160;360</span> – <abbr title="circa">c.</abbr><span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#8201;435</span>), was a <a href="/wiki/Christian_monk" class="mw-redirect" title="Christian monk">Christian monk</a> and <a href="/wiki/Theologian" class="mw-redirect" title="Theologian">theologian</a> celebrated in both the Western and Eastern churches for his mystical writings.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911458-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Cassian is noted for his role in bringing the ideas and practices of early <a href="/wiki/Christian_monasticism" title="Christian monasticism">Christian monasticism</a> to the <a href="/wiki/Middle_Ages" title="Middle Ages">medieval</a> West.
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biography">Biography</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Biography">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Cassian was born around 360, most likely in the region of <a href="/wiki/Scythia_Minor_(Roman_province)" class="mw-redirect" title="Scythia Minor (Roman province)">Scythia Minor</a> (now <a href="/wiki/Dobruja" title="Dobruja">Dobruja</a>, a historical region shared today by <a href="/wiki/Romania" title="Romania">Romania</a> and <a href="/wiki/Bulgaria" title="Bulgaria">Bulgaria</a>),<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKurianSmith2010241_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKurianSmith2010241-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELamm2012220_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELamm2012220-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHinson2010263_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHinson2010263-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEEgan201033_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEgan201033-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStone201043_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStone201043-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECassian20003_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECassian20003-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> although some scholars assume a Gallic origin.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHassett1908_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHassett1908-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELake200427_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELake200427-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStewart199827_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStewart199827-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> The son of wealthy parents, he received a good education: his writings show the influence of <a href="/wiki/Cicero" title="Cicero">Cicero</a> and <a href="/wiki/Persius" title="Persius">Persius</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-duffy_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-duffy-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> He was bilingual in Latin and Greek.<sup id="cite_ref-feiss_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-feiss-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cassian mentions having a sister in his first work, the <i>Institutes</i>, with whom he corresponded in his monastic life; she may have ended up with him in Marseilles.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStewart19984_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStewart19984-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>As a young adult he traveled to <a href="/wiki/Palestine_(region)" title="Palestine (region)">Palestine</a> with an older friend Germanus, with whom he would spend much of the next twenty-five years. There they entered a <a href="/wiki/Hermitage_(religious_retreat)" title="Hermitage (religious retreat)">hermitage</a> near <a href="/wiki/Bethlehem" title="Bethlehem">Bethlehem</a>. After remaining in that community for about three years,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELake200427_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELake200427-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> they journeyed to the <a href="/wiki/Wadi_El_Natrun" title="Wadi El Natrun">desert of Scete</a> in <a href="/wiki/Roman_Egypt" title="Roman Egypt">Egypt</a>, which was rent by Christian struggles. There they visited a number of <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastic</a> foundations.
</p><p>Approximately fifteen years later, about 399, Cassian and Germanus faced the <a href="/wiki/Origenist_Crises" class="mw-redirect" title="Origenist Crises">Anthropomorphic controversy</a> provoked in letter form by <a href="/wiki/Theophilus_I_of_Alexandria" title="Theophilus I of Alexandria">Theophilus, Archbishop of Alexandria</a>. Cassian noted that the majority of the monks received the message of their patriarch "with bitterness", and charged Theophilus with heresy for impugning the plain teaching of scripture.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEShepherd1938263–273_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEShepherd1938263–273-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Following an unsuccessful journey to Alexandria to protest the matter, Cassian and Germanus fled with about 300 other <a href="/wiki/Origen" title="Origen">Origenist</a> monks. Cassian and Germanus went to <a href="/wiki/Constantinople" title="Constantinople">Constantinople</a>, where they appealed to the <a href="/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_of_Constantinople" title="Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople">Patriarch of Constantinople</a>, <a href="/wiki/John_Chrysostom" title="John Chrysostom">John Chrysostom</a>, for protection. Cassian was ordained a <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a> and became a member of the clergy attached to the patriarch while the struggles with the imperial family ensued. When the patriarch was forced into <a href="/wiki/Exile" title="Exile">exile</a> from Constantinople in 404, the Latin-speaking Cassian was sent to Rome to plead his cause before <a href="/wiki/Pope_Innocent_I" title="Pope Innocent I">Pope Innocent I</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-duffy_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-duffy-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>While he was in Rome, Cassian accepted the invitation to found an Egyptian-style <a href="/wiki/Monastery" title="Monastery">monastery</a> in southern <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Marseilles" class="mw-redirect" title="Marseilles">Marseilles</a>. He may also have spent time as a priest in <a href="/wiki/Antioch" title="Antioch">Antioch</a> between 404 and 415. In any case, he arrived in Marseilles around 415. His foundation, the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Victor,_Marseille" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of Saint Victor, Marseille">Abbey of St Victor</a>, was a complex of monasteries for both men and women, one of the first such institutes in the West, and served as a model for later monastic development.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHassett1908_10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHassett1908-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cassian's achievements and writings influenced <a href="/wiki/Benedict_of_Nursia" title="Benedict of Nursia">Benedict of Nursia</a>, who incorporated many of the principles into his <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict" title="Rule of Saint Benedict">monastic rule</a>, and recommended to his own monks that they read the works of Cassian. Since Benedict's rule is still followed by <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a>, <a href="/wiki/Cistercian" class="mw-redirect" title="Cistercian">Cistercian</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Trappist" class="mw-redirect" title="Trappist">Trappist</a> monks, John Cassian's thought still exercises influence over the spiritual lives of thousands of men and women in the <a href="/wiki/Latin_Church" title="Latin Church">Latin Church</a>.
</p><p>Cassian died in 435 at Marseille.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Writings">Writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Cassian came very late into writing and did so only when a request was made by one or more important persons.  His sources were the same as those of <a href="/wiki/Evagrius_Ponticus" title="Evagrius Ponticus">Evagrius Ponticus</a>, but he added his own ideas, which were arranged in extensive collections. Evagrius was, however, the single most important influence on Cassian's ideas, due to his reverence for the "Origenist" monks (who also relied predominantly on Evagrius) of <a href="/wiki/Nitria_(monastic_site)" title="Nitria (monastic site)">Nitria</a>, <a href="/wiki/Kellia" title="Kellia">Kellia</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Wadi_El_Natrun" title="Wadi El Natrun">Scetis</a>, three early monastic centres in the desert of the northwestern <a href="/wiki/Nile_Delta" title="Nile Delta">Nile Delta</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-feiss_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-feiss-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDriver20024_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDriver20024-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Around 420, at the request of Bishop <a href="/wiki/Castor_of_Apt" title="Castor of Apt">Castor of Apt</a> in Gallia Narbonensis, Cassian wrote two major spiritual works, the <i>De institutis coenobiorum</i> (<i>Institutes of the Coenobia</i>) and the <i>Conlationes</i> or <i>Collationes patrum in scetica eremo</i> (<i>Conferences of the Desert Fathers</i>). In these, he codified and transmitted the wisdom of the <a href="/wiki/Desert_Fathers" title="Desert Fathers">Desert Fathers</a> of Egypt. The <i>Institutes</i> deal with the external organization of monastic communities, while the <i>Conferences</i> deal with "the training of the inner man and the perfection of the heart".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHassett1908_10-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHassett1908-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His books were written in <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a>, in a simple, direct style. They were swiftly translated into Greek, which indicates the Eastern monks recognized him as one of their own.<sup id="cite_ref-feiss_14-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-feiss-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> Some of his works had been translated into <a href="/wiki/Georgian_language" title="Georgian language">Georgian</a> by the 11th century.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChadwick1950172_18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChadwick1950172-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Institutes">The <i>Institutes</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: The Institutes">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <i>Institutes</i> were meant to help Castor to establish a <a href="/wiki/Cenobitic_monasticism" title="Cenobitic monasticism">coenobium</a> following the model of Egypt, in contrast to the existing monastic life in Gaul, which included the work of <a href="/wiki/Martin_of_Tours" title="Martin of Tours">Martin of Tours</a>. According to Hugh Feiss the <i>Institutes</i> are a counterweight to Sulpicius Severus’ <i>Life of Martin</i> and <i>Dialogues</i>, and are an attempt to put order into a movement Cassian regarded as chaotic. Cassian, who insists on manual work, had a higher opinion of and close ties with the monastery on the <a href="/wiki/L%C3%A9rins_Abbey" title="Lérins Abbey">Island of Lerins</a>, founded by <a href="/wiki/Honoratus" title="Honoratus">Honoratus</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-feiss_14-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-feiss-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In Books 1–4 of <i>Institutions</i>, Cassian discusses clothing, prayer and rules of monastic life. Books 5–12 are rules on morality, specifically addressing the eight <a href="/wiki/Vices" class="mw-redirect" title="Vices">vices</a>&#160;– <a href="/wiki/Gluttony" title="Gluttony">gluttony</a>, <a href="/wiki/Lust" title="Lust">lust</a>, <a href="/wiki/Avarice" class="mw-redirect" title="Avarice">greed</a>, <a href="/wiki/Hubris" title="Hubris">hubris</a>, <a href="/wiki/Wrath" class="mw-redirect" title="Wrath">wrath</a>, <a href="/wiki/Envy" title="Envy">envy</a>, <a href="/wiki/Acedia" title="Acedia">listlessness</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Boasting" title="Boasting">boasting</a>&#160;– and what to do to cure these vices. In the <i>Institutions</i>, Cassian discusses a will that is more complex than the will at the heart of the Pelagian message. Willful monks are a contentious problem, and Cassian paid considerable attention to analyzing the will, treating the corrupt will, and chiefly subordinating even the good will for the good of the community and ultimately, the will of God.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECasiday200796_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECasiday200796-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Outline of Cassian's <i>Institutes</i>:<sup id="cite_ref-Harmless_2004_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Harmless_2004-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>

<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="The_Conferences">The <i>Conferences</i></span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: The Conferences">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The <i>Conferences</i>, dedicated to <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_I" title="Pope Leo I">Pope Leo</a>, to the bishop of <a href="/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus" title="Fréjus">Fréjus</a>, and to the monk Helladius, summarize important conversations that Cassian had with elders from the monastery at <a href="/wiki/Wadi_El_Natrun" title="Wadi El Natrun">Scetis</a> about principles of the spiritual and ascetic life.  This book addresses specific problems of spiritual theology and the ascetic life.  It was later read in <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> communities after the evening meal,<sup id="cite_ref-Ben_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ben-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup> and from the Latin title, <i>Collationes</i>, comes the word <i>collation</i> in the sense of "light meal".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEFunk200778-79_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFunk200778-79-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcKean2005333_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcKean2005333-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Spirituality">Spirituality</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Spirituality">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The desert ascetics of Egypt followed a three-step path to mysticism: <i>Purgatio</i>, <i>Illuminatio</i>, and <i>Unitio</i>. These stages correspond to the <a href="/wiki/Purgative_way" class="mw-redirect" title="Purgative way">three ways</a> of later Catholic theology. During the first level, <i>Purgatio</i> (in Greek, <i><a href="/wiki/Catharsis" title="Catharsis">Catharsis</a></i>), young monks struggled through prayer and <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">ascetic practices</a> to gain control of "the flesh"—specifically by purging their gluttony, lust and desire for possessions. This period of purgation, which often took many years, was intended to teach young monks that whatever strength they had to resist these desires (<a href="/wiki/Divine_grace" title="Divine grace">grace</a>) came directly from the <a href="/wiki/Holy_Spirit" title="Holy Spirit">Holy Spirit</a>.
</p><p>At this point, the <i>Illuminatio</i> (<i><a href="/wiki/Theoria" class="mw-redirect" title="Theoria">theoria</a></i> in Greek) commenced. During this period the monks practiced the paths to holiness as revealed in the Gospel, identifying strongly with the Christ who taught the <a href="/wiki/Sermon_on_the_Mount" title="Sermon on the Mount">Sermon on the Mount</a> (found in Matthew 5–7). Many monks took in visitors and students and tended the poor as much as their resources allowed. Many monks died never having moved past this period. The final stage was the <i>Unitio</i> (<i><a href="/wiki/Theosis_(Eastern_Orthodox_theology)" class="mw-redirect" title="Theosis (Eastern Orthodox theology)">theosis</a></i> in Greek), a period in which the soul of the monk was meant to bond with the Spirit of God in a union often described as the marriage of the <i><a href="/wiki/Song_of_Solomon" class="mw-redirect" title="Song of Solomon">Song of Solomon</a></i> (also called the "Song of Songs" or the "Canticle of Canticles"). To find the solitude and peace that this level of mystical awareness demanded, elderly monks often fled into the deep desert or into remote forests.
</p><p>His asceticism, while rigorous, was tempered by common sense. Cassian says hospitality should override ascetical routine. Even the most contemplative of anchorites should entertain visitors. Both asceticism and ministry are aspects of the practical life.<sup id="cite_ref-feiss_14-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-feiss-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In his <i>Conferences</i>, Cassian recommended as "absolutely necessary for possessing the perpetual awareness of God" the formula in <a href="/wiki/Psalm_70" title="Psalm 70">Psalm 70</a> (69) v. 2, <i>Deus, in adiutorium meum intende. Domine, ad adiuvandum me festina</i> ("God, incline unto my aid; O Lord, make haste to help me"),<sup id="cite_ref-Ps70_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ps70-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> He says of it:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>&gt;Not without reason has this verse been selected out of the whole body of Scripture. For it takes up all the emotions that can be applied to human nature and with great correctness and accuracy it adjusts itself to every condition and every attack. It contains an invocation of God in the face of any crisis, the humility of a devout confession, the watchfulness of concern and of constant fear, a consciousness of one's own frailty, the assurance being heard, and confidence in a protection that is always present and at hand, for whoever calls unceasingly on his protector is sure that he is always present. It contains a burning love and charity, an awareness of traps, and a fear of enemies. Seeing oneself surrounded by these day and night, one confesses that one cannot be set free without the help of one's defender. This verse is an unassailable wall, an impenetrable breastplate, and a very strong shield for those who labour under the attack of demons.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite><a href="#CITEREFCassian1997">Cassian 1997</a>, p.&#160;381, Tenth Conference, X, 2-4</cite></div></blockquote>
<p><a href="/wiki/Benedict_of_Nursia" title="Benedict of Nursia">Benedict of Nursia</a> praises Cassian's <i>Conferences</i> in his <a href="/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict" title="Rule of Saint Benedict">rule</a><sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup> and use of this formula became part of the <a href="/wiki/Liturgy_of_the_Hours" title="Liturgy of the Hours">Liturgy of the Hours</a> in the Western Church, in which all the <a href="/wiki/Canonical_hours" title="Canonical hours">canonical hours</a>, including the minor hours, start with this <a href="/wiki/Versicle" class="mw-redirect" title="Versicle">versicle</a>, which is omitted only if the hour begins with the <a href="/wiki/Invitatory" title="Invitatory">Invitatory</a>, the introduction to the first hour said in the day, whether it be the Office of Readings or Morning Prayer. <a href="/wiki/Alphonsus_Liguori" title="Alphonsus Liguori">Alphonsus Liguori</a> also cites Cassian's recommendation to use this short prayer continually.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELiguori200939_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELiguori200939-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In the West, Cassian's proposition that "the slightest glimmer of goodwill" could be attributed to the human drive was widely regarded as unacceptable in relation to the prosperity of the Augustinianism of the period (Conf. 13.7.1; cf Prosper of Aquitaine <i>Contra Collatorem;</i> Cassiodorus, <i>Institutiones</i> 1.29; <i>Decretum</i> <i>Gelasianum</i> V.7<i>)</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMcGuckin2011533_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGuckin2011533-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> In his <i>Thirteenth Conference</i> and in writings to the Monks of Lerins, Cassian qualifies this by saying the good will is "stirred" by God: 
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>For when God sees us inclined to will what is good, He meets, guides, and strengthens us: for "At the voice of thy cry, as soon as He shall hear, He will answer thee;" and:  "Call upon Me," He says, "in the day of tribulation and I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify Me." And again, if He finds that we are unwilling or have grown cold, He stirs our hearts with salutary exhortations, by which a good will is either renewed or formed in us.</p><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite><a href="#CITEREFSchaff2009">Schaff 2009</a>, p.&#160;428</cite></div></blockquote>
<p>Cassian finds the will to be insufficient for spiritual progress, and traces this back to the initial sin of pride. Cassian illustrates advanced cases of the will's pathology in the <i>Institutions</i>, saying these problems began when man "believed himself capable of attaining the glory of the Godhead by his <i>freedom of will</i> and hard work." To this end, Cassian believes the renunciate must conquer his will, overcome it, and even kill it.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECasiday200797_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECasiday200797-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In regards to demons, Cassian noted that the earliest coenobites would ensure one monk was reciting a prayer, psalmody, or reading at all times, due to their belief that demons were especially prevalent at night. Cassian promotes David's evil spirit repulsing prayer at Ps. 35: 1–3, for demons actively oppose the virtuous life, and could be warded off with prayer.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECasiday2007172–173_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECasiday2007172–173-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Accusations_of_Semipelagianism">Accusations of Semipelagianism</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Accusations of Semipelagianism">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="As_viewed_by_the_Roman_Catholic_Church">As viewed by the Roman Catholic Church</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: As viewed by the Roman Catholic Church">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>His third book, <i>On the Incarnation of the Lord</i>, was a defense of orthodox doctrine against the views of <a href="/wiki/Nestorius" title="Nestorius">Nestorius</a>, and was written at the request of the Archdeacon of Rome, later <a href="/wiki/Pope_Leo_I" title="Pope Leo I">Pope Leo I</a>. In this book Cassian points out a link between Nestorianism which stresses the humanity of Jesus and Pelagianism which stresses human effort. Later theologians, however, labeled Cassian as "<a href="/wiki/Semipelagianism" class="mw-redirect" title="Semipelagianism">Semipelagian</a>" because he stressed the role of the <a href="/wiki/Free_will_in_theology" title="Free will in theology">human will</a>, as opposed to Augustine's stress on the totality of grace, in moving towards salvation.<sup id="cite_ref-duffy_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-duffy-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The ideas expressed by Cassian to which critics have pointed as examples of his alleged Semipelagianism are found in his <i>Conferences</i>, in book 3, the Conference of Abbot Paphnutius; book 5, the Conference of Abbot Serapion; and most especially in book 13, the Third Conference of Abbot Chaeremon.
</p><p>The view that Cassian propounded Semipelagianism has been disputed. Lauren Pristas, writes: "For Cassian, salvation is, from beginning to end, the effect of God's grace. It is fully divine. Salvation, however, is salvation of a rational creature who has sinned through free choice. Therefore, salvation necessarily includes both free human consent in grace and the gradual rehabilitation in grace of the faculty of free choice. Thus Cassian insists salvation is also fully human. His thought, however, is not Semi-Pelagian, nor do readers who submit to the whole corpus emerge Semi-Pelagians."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPristas1993_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPristas1993-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup> And Augustine Casiday states that "for Cassian ... although sparks of goodwill may exist (which are not directly caused by God), they are totally inadequate and only direct divine intervention can ensure our spiritual progress".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEMoss20094_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMoss20094-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Latin_Church" title="Latin Church">Latin Church</a> condemned Semipelagianism in the local <a href="/wiki/Council_of_Orange_(529)" title="Council of Orange (529)">Council of Orange (529)</a>, but recognizes Cassian himself as a saint.<sup id="cite_ref-Martyrologium_Romanum_p._385_33-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Martyrologium_Romanum_p._385-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> It did not endorse Augustine entirely<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEZackrison200473_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEZackrison200473-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> and, while later Catholic theologians accepted Augustine's authority, they interpreted his views in the light of writers such as Cassian.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGonzalez201058_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGonzalez201058-35">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="As_viewed_by_the_Eastern_Orthodox_Church">As viewed by the Eastern Orthodox Church</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: As viewed by the Eastern Orthodox Church">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Augustine Casiday states that Cassian "baldly asserts that God's grace, not human <a href="/wiki/Free_will" title="Free will">free will</a>, is responsible for 'everything which pertains to salvation' - even faith."<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECasiday2007103_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECasiday2007103-36">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> Some other Orthodox, who do not apply the term "Semi-Pelagian" to their theology, criticize the Roman Catholics for allegedly rejecting Cassian, whom they accept as fully orthodox,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTELossky1976198_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTELossky1976198-37">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> and for holding, as, in Casiday's interpretation, that <i>everything</i> which pertains to salvation comes from God's grace, and so that even the human consent to God's justifying action is itself an effect of grace,<sup id="cite_ref-stpaulsirvine.org_38-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-stpaulsirvine.org-38">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> This position of the Roman Catholic Church and of Cassian as interpreted by Casiday is attributed by Eastern Orthodox theologian <a href="/wiki/Georges_Florovsky" title="Georges Florovsky">Georges Florovsky</a> also to the Eastern Orthodox Church, which, he says, "always understood that God initiates, accompanies, and completes everything in the process of salvation", rejecting instead the <a href="/wiki/Calvinism" title="Calvinism">Calvinist</a> idea of <a href="/wiki/Irresistible_grace" title="Irresistible grace">irresistible grace</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Florovsky_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Florovsky-39">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup> Neither Cassian nor any of his teachings have ever been directly or indirectly called into question or condemned by Eastern Orthodox, as they are considered a witness to the Orthodox position.<sup id="cite_ref-AugWrit_40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AugWrit-40">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span id="In_Cassian.27s_writings"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="In_Cassian's_writings">In Cassian's writings</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: In Cassian&#039;s writings">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>In <i>The Book of Mystical Chapters</i>, a compilation of sayings of the <a href="/wiki/Church_Fathers" title="Church Fathers">Church Fathers</a> by renowned theologian and early church historian<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/John_Anthony_McGuckin" class="mw-redirect" title="John Anthony McGuckin">John Anthony McGuckin</a>, Cassian is quoted as saying the following: 
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"><blockquote class="templatequote"><div class="poem">
<p>The thief on the cross certainly did not receive<br />
the Kingdom of Heaven as a reward for his virtues<br />
but as a grace and a mercy from God.<br />
He can serve as an authentic witness<br />
that our salvation is given to us<br />
only by God's mercy and grace.<br />
All the holy masters knew this<br />
and unanimously taught that perfection in holiness<br />
can be achieved only through humility.
</p>
</div><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8202;<cite><a href="#CITEREFMcGuckin2003">McGuckin 2003</a>, p.&#160;24</cite></div></blockquote>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Other_views">Other views</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Other views">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>According to some scholars, Cassian is a prominent representative of a monastic movement in southern Gaul which,  ca. 425, gave expression to the <a href="/wiki/Soteriology" title="Soteriology">soteriological</a> view that much later was called Semipelagianism.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone2005701_42-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone2005701-42">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> This emphasized the role of <a href="/wiki/Free_will" title="Free will">free will</a> in that the first steps of <a href="/wiki/Salvation" title="Salvation">salvation</a> are in the power of the individual, without the need for <a href="/wiki/Divine_grace" title="Divine grace">divine grace</a>. His thought has been described as a "middle way" between <a href="/wiki/Pelagianism" title="Pelagianism">Pelagianism</a>, which taught that the will alone was sufficient to live a sinless life, and the view of <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</a>, which emphasizes <a href="/wiki/Original_sin" title="Original sin">original sin</a> and the absolute need for grace.
</p><p>For instance, Anglican priest and historian <a href="/wiki/Owen_Chadwick" title="Owen Chadwick">Owen Chadwick</a> stated that Cassian held that man can come to God without the intervention of divine grace first;<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECassian198527_43-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECassian198527-43">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup> and the Presbyterian theologian <a href="/wiki/B._B._Warfield" title="B. B. Warfield">B. B. Warfield</a> called Cassian the leader of the monastics in southern Gaul who asserted that men begin their turning to God and that God assists that beginning.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESchaff200721_44-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESchaff200721-44">&#91;41&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Influence">Influence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Influence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The spiritual traditions of Cassian had an immeasurable effect on Western Europe. Many different western spiritualities, from that of St <a href="/wiki/Benedict_of_Nursia" title="Benedict of Nursia">Benedict of Nursia</a> to that of St <a href="/wiki/Ignatius_of_Loyola" title="Ignatius of Loyola">Ignatius of Loyola</a>, owe their basic ideas to Cassian.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I" title="Pope Gregory I">Pope Gregory I</a>'s teaching on the seven deadly sins comes from Cassian, as does much of his teaching on compunction and prayer. <a href="/wiki/Philip_Neri" title="Philip Neri">Philip Neri</a> used to read Cassian to the laity and would frequently use his work as the starting point for his own addresses.<sup id="cite_ref-herald_45-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-herald-45">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup> He also influenced <a href="/wiki/John_Climacus" title="John Climacus">John Climacus</a> and <a href="/wiki/John_of_Damascus" title="John of Damascus">John of Damascus</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-feiss_14-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-feiss-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> as well as <a href="/wiki/Saint_Dominic" title="Saint Dominic">Saint Dominic</a>, <a href="/wiki/Francis_de_Sales" title="Francis de Sales">Francis de Sales</a>, and <a href="/wiki/John_Henry_Newman" title="John Henry Newman">John Henry Newman</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-herald_45-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-herald-45">&#91;42&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cassian's writings stress the role of prayer and personal asceticism in attaining salvation by contrast with Augustine's writings which stress the role of God's justice and grace (predestination) and take a more negative view of human effort. His teaching on overcoming the eight evil tendencies (See Books 5 to 12 of The Institutes) were the inspiration behind the way the Irish monks practised asceticism, as shown in the Irish Penitentials.<sup id="cite_ref-duffy_13-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-duffy-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <i>Institutes</i> had a direct influence on organization of monasteries described in the <i><a href="/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict" title="Rule of Saint Benedict">Rule of Saint Benedict</a></i>; Benedict also recommended that ordered selections of the <i>Conferences</i> be read to monks under his Rule. Moreover, the monastic institutions Cassian inspired kept learning and culture alive during the <a href="/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages" title="Early Middle Ages">Early Middle Ages</a>, and were often the only institutions that cared for the sick and poor.
</p><p>His works are excerpted in the <i><a href="/wiki/Philokalia" title="Philokalia">Philokalia</a></i> (<a href="/wiki/Greek_language" title="Greek language">Greek</a> for "love of the beautiful"), the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox</a> compendium on mystical Christian prayer.
</p><p>Even modern thinkers have been influenced by Cassian's thinking. <a href="/wiki/Michel_Foucault" title="Michel Foucault">Michel Foucault</a> was fascinated by the rigorous way Cassian defined and struggled against the "flesh".<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">&#91;43&#93;</a></sup> Perhaps because of investigations like these, Cassian's thought and writings are enjoying a recent popularity even in non-religious circles.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=John_Cassian&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Abbaye_de_St_Victor_-_1818.PNG" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Abbaye_de_St_Victor_-_1818.PNG/170px-Abbaye_de_St_Victor_-_1818.PNG" decoding="async" width="170" height="129" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Abbaye_de_St_Victor_-_1818.PNG/255px-Abbaye_de_St_Victor_-_1818.PNG 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Abbaye_de_St_Victor_-_1818.PNG/340px-Abbaye_de_St_Victor_-_1818.PNG 2x" data-file-width="1709" data-file-height="1293" /></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Victor,_Marseille" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of Saint Victor, Marseille">Abbey of Saint Victor, Marseille</a>, where his relics are placed</figcaption></figure>
<p>He is a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Churches</a>, with a <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> on 29 February. Because this day occurs only once every four years on <a href="/wiki/Leap_year" title="Leap year">leap years</a>, official church calendars often transfer his feast to another date (usually 28 February).<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">&#91;44&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic Church">Roman Catholic Church</a> also ranks him as a saint, with a feast day on 23 July (<a href="/wiki/Death_anniversary" title="Death anniversary">death anniversary</a>). Like his contemporaries <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo" title="Augustine of Hippo">Augustine of Hippo</a> and <a href="/wiki/John_Chrysostom" title="John Chrysostom">John Chrysostom</a>, he was never formally canonized, a process that came into use several centuries after his death.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHassett1908_10-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHassett1908-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Pope_Urban_V" title="Pope Urban V">Pope Urban V</a> referred to him as <i>sanctus</i> (a saint) and he was included in the Gallican Martyrology<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">&#91;45&#93;</a></sup> He is included also in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a> with a feast-day on 23 July.<sup id="cite_ref-Martyrologium_Romanum_p._385_33-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Martyrologium_Romanum_p._385-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>  In the Irish church, at the beginning of the ninth century, Cassian was commemorated on 25 November, as indicated in the Martyrology of Óengus: "Lasin nEoin Cassian assa érchain corann" (With John Cassian whose crown is very fair).  Like the great majority of recognized saints of the church, he is not one of the saints in the <a href="/wiki/General_Roman_Calendar" title="General Roman Calendar">General Roman Calendar</a>, but the <a href="/wiki/Archdiocese_of_Marseilles" class="mw-redirect" title="Archdiocese of Marseilles">Archdiocese of Marseilles</a> and some <a href="/wiki/Monastic_order" class="mw-redirect" title="Monastic order">monastic orders</a> celebrate his <a href="/wiki/Memorial" title="Memorial">memorial</a> on his feast day.
</p><p>In 2022, John Cassian was officially added to the <a href="/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_(Episcopal_Church)" title="Calendar of saints (Episcopal Church)">Episcopal Church liturgical calendar</a> with a feast day on 23 July.<sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">&#91;46&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Cassian's <a href="/wiki/Relic" title="Relic">relics</a> are kept in an underground chapel in the <a href="/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint_Victor,_Marseille" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbey of Saint Victor, Marseille">Monastery of Saint Victor</a> in <a href="/wiki/Marseille" title="Marseille">Marseilles</a>. His head and right hand are in the main church there.
</p>

<li id="cite_note-Florovsky-39"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-Florovsky_39-0">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">The existential and ontological meaning of man's created existence is precisely that God did not have to create, that it was a free act of Divine freedom. But— and here is the great difficulty created by an unbalanced Christianity on the doctrine of grace and freedom— in freely creating man God willed to give man an inner spiritual freedom. In no sense is this a Pelagian or Semi-Pelagian position. The balanced synergistic doctrine of the early and Eastern Church, a doctrine misunderstood and undermined by Latin Christianity in general from Saint Augustine on— although there was always opposition to this in the Latin Church— always understood that God initiates, accompanies, and completes everything in the process of salvation. What it always rejected— both spontaneously and intellectually— is the idea of irresistible grace, the idea that man has no participating role in his salvation. <li id="cite_note-AugWrit-40"><span class="mw-cite-backlink"><b><a href="#cite_ref-AugWrit_40-0">^</a></b></span> <span class="reference-text">Augustine's writings found their way to parts of the West Roman provinces. Saint John Cassian (circa 360-433), former ascetic in the deserts of Egypt and then deacon of the Patriarch of Constantinople Saint John Chrysostom, challenged Augustine's teaching about original sin and pre-destination without mentioning him. The teachings of Augustine on these points were condemned by the Council of Orange in 529.[ 18 ] Augustine's writings completely captured the 8th century Carolingian tradition which knew basically only Augustine until the 12th century. At that time the Franks acquired a translation of Saint John of Damascus' "Book on the Orthodox Faith" which they simply understood within their own Augustinian categories. By the 11th century the Franks had taken over all of Western Europe, except Spain, by either conquest or diplomacy. The Spanish Romans under Arab rule were still under the direct surveillance of the Roman Emperor of Constantinople New Rome. The Umayad Arabs of Spain and the Abbasid Arabs of Damascus and then Baghdad called their Roman Orthodox subjects Melkites, i.e. those who belong to the religion of the Roman Emperor in New Rome Constantinople. (<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1133582631"><cite id="CITEREFRomanides" class="citation web cs1">Romanides, John S. <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="http://www.romanity.org/htm/rom.02.en.the_cure_of_the_neurobiological_sickness_of_rel.01.htm">"The Cure of the Neurobiological Sickness of Religion"</a>.</cite><span title="ctx_ver=Z39.88-2004&amp;rft_val_fmt=info%3Aofi%2Ffmt%3Akev%3Amtx%3Abook&amp;rft.genre=unknown&amp;rft.btitle=The+Cure+of+the+Neurobiological+Sickness+of+Religion&amp;rft.aulast=Romanides&amp;rft.aufirst=John+S.&amp;rft_id=http%3A%2F%2Fwww.romanity.org%2Fhtm%2From.02.en.the_cure_of_the_neurobiological_sickness_of_rel.01.htm&amp;rfr_id=info%3Asid%2Fen.wikipedia.org%3AJohn+Cassian" class="Z3988"></span>) </span>
