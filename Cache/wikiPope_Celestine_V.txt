<p><b>Pope Celestine V</b> (<a href="/wiki/Latin_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Latin language">Latin</a>: <i lang="la">Caelestinus V</i>; 1215&#160;– 19 May 1296), born <b>Pietro Angelerio</b> (according to some sources <i>Angelario</i>, <i>Angelieri</i>, <i>Angelliero</i>, or <i>Angeleri</i>), also known as <b>Pietro da Morrone</b>, <b>Peter of Morrone</b>, and <b>Peter Celestine</b>, was head of the <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a> and ruler of the <a href="/wiki/Papal_States" title="Papal States">Papal States</a> for five months from 5 July to 13 December 1294, when he resigned. He was also a <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> and <a href="/wiki/Hermit" title="Hermit">hermit</a> who founded the order of the <a href="/wiki/Celestines" title="Celestines">Celestines</a> as a branch of the <a href="/wiki/Order_of_Saint_Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="Order of Saint Benedict">Benedictine order</a>.
</p><p>He was elected <a href="/wiki/Pope" title="Pope">pope</a> in the Catholic Church's <a href="/wiki/Papal_election,_1292%E2%80%9394" class="mw-redirect" title="Papal election, 1292–94">last non-conclave papal election</a>, ending a two-year impasse. Among the few edicts of his to remain in force was the confirmation of the right of the pope to resign; nearly all of his other official acts were annulled by his successor, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_VIII" title="Pope Boniface VIII">Boniface VIII</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Loughlin1908_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Loughlin1908-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> On 13 December 1294, a week after issuing the decree, Celestine resigned, stating his desire to return to his humble, pre-papal life. He was subsequently imprisoned by Boniface in the castle of <a href="/wiki/Fumone" title="Fumone">Fumone</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Lazio" title="Lazio">Lazio</a> region, in order to prevent his potential installation as <a href="/wiki/Antipope" title="Antipope">antipope</a>. He died in prison on 19 May 1296 at the age of 81.<sup id="cite_ref-Loughlin1908_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Loughlin1908-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Celestine was <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonized</a> on 5 May 1313 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_V" title="Pope Clement V">Pope Clement V</a>. No subsequent pope has taken the name Celestine.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Celestine_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Pietro Angelerio was born to parents Angelo Angelerio and Maria Leone in a town called Sant'Angelo Limosano, in the <a href="/wiki/Sicily" title="Sicily">Kingdom of Sicilia</a> (Sicily). Sant'Angelo Limosano is now part of <a href="/wiki/Provincia_di_Campobasso" class="mw-redirect" title="Provincia di Campobasso">Provincia di Campobasso</a>, in <a href="/wiki/Molise" title="Molise">Molise</a>, <a href="/wiki/Italy" title="Italy">Italy</a>.
</p><p>After his father's death he began working in the fields. His mother Maria was a key figure in Pietro's spiritual development: she imagined a different future for her deeply beloved son than becoming just a <a href="/wiki/Farmer" title="Farmer">farmer</a> or a <a href="/wiki/Shepherd" title="Shepherd">shepherd</a>. From the time he was a child, he showed great intelligence and love for others. He became a <a href="/wiki/Benedictine" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedictine">Benedictine</a> <a href="/wiki/Monk" title="Monk">monk</a> at <a href="/w/index.php?title=Faifoli&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Faifoli (page does not exist)">Faifoli</a><span class="noprint" style="font-size:85%; font-style: normal;">&#160;&#91;<a href="https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbazia_di_Santa_Maria_di_Faifoli" class="extiw" title="it:Abbazia di Santa Maria di Faifoli">it</a>&#93;</span> in the <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Benevento" class="mw-redirect" title="Diocese of Benevento">Diocese of Benevento</a> when he was 17. He showed an extraordinary disposition toward <a href="/wiki/Asceticism" title="Asceticism">asceticism</a> and solitude, and in 1239 retired to a solitary cavern on the <a href="/wiki/Montagne_del_Morrone" title="Montagne del Morrone">Montagne del Morrone</a>, hence his name (Peter of Morrone). Five years later he left this retreat, and went with two companions to a similar cave on the even more remote <a href="/wiki/Maiella" title="Maiella">Maiella</a> mountain in the <a href="/wiki/Abruzzi" class="mw-redirect" title="Abruzzi">Abruzzi</a> region of central Italy, where he lived as strictly as possible according to the example of <a href="/wiki/John_the_Baptist" title="John the Baptist">John the Baptist</a>. Accounts exist of the severity of his <a href="/wiki/Penance" title="Penance">penitential</a> practices.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (March 2021)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Founding_of_the_Celestines">Founding of the Celestines</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Celestine_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Founding of the Celestines">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>

<p>While living like this, in 1244, he founded the order subsequently named after him, the <a href="/wiki/Celestines" title="Celestines">Celestines</a>. A new religious community was formed, and Pietro gave them a rule formulated in accordance with his own practices. In 1264 the new institution was approved by <a href="/wiki/Urban_IV" class="mw-redirect" title="Urban IV">Urban IV</a>. Having heard that it was probable that <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_X" title="Pope Gregory X">Pope Gregory X</a>, then holding <a href="/wiki/Second_Council_of_Lyon" title="Second Council of Lyon">a council at Lyon</a>, would suppress all such new orders as had been founded since the <a href="/wiki/Lateran_Council" class="mw-redirect" title="Lateran Council">Lateran Council</a> had commanded that such institutions should not be further multiplied, Pietro went to Lyon. There he succeeded in persuading Gregory to approve his new order, making it a branch of the <a href="/wiki/Benedictines" title="Benedictines">Benedictines</a> and following the rule of <a href="/wiki/Saint_Benedict" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Benedict">Saint Benedict</a>, but adding to it additional severities and privations. Gregory took it under papal protection, assured to it the possession of all property it might acquire, and endowed it with exemption from the authority of the ordinary. Nothing more was needed to ensure the rapid spread of the new association and Pietro lived to see himself as "superior-general" to thirty-six monasteries and more than six hundred monks. Pietro, however, cannot be accused of ambition or the lust of power when a monastic superior, any more than when he insisted on divesting himself of the papacy, to which he was subsequently raised.
</p><p>As soon as he had seen his new order thus consolidated he gave up the government of it to a certain Robert, and retired once again to a still more remote solitude to give himself up more entirely to solitary penance and prayer. Shortly afterwards, in a chapter of the order held in 1293, the original monastery of <a href="/wiki/Majella" class="mw-redirect" title="Majella">Majella</a> being judged to be too desolate and exposed to too rigorous a climate, it was decided that <a href="/wiki/Abbazia_Morronese" class="mw-redirect" title="Abbazia Morronese">Abbazia Morronese</a> in the plains of <a href="/wiki/Sulmona" title="Sulmona">Sulmona</a> should be the headquarters of the order and the residence of the general-superior, as it continued to be until the order was extinguished in the 19th century.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Election_as_pope">Election as pope</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Celestine_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Election as pope">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1033289096">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Papal_election,_1292%E2%80%931294" class="mw-redirect" title="Papal election, 1292–1294">Papal election, 1292–1294</a></div>
<p>The cardinals assembled at <a href="/wiki/Perugia" title="Perugia">Perugia</a> after the death of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Nicholas_IV" title="Pope Nicholas IV">Pope Nicholas IV</a> in April 1292. After more than two years, a consensus had still not been reached. Pietro, well known to the cardinals as a Benedictine hermit, sent the cardinals a letter warning them that divine vengeance would fall upon them if they did not quickly elect a pope. Latino Malabranca, the aged and ill <a href="/wiki/Dean_of_the_College_of_Cardinals" title="Dean of the College of Cardinals">Dean</a> of the <a href="/wiki/College_of_Cardinals" title="College of Cardinals">College of Cardinals</a> cried out, "In the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, I elect brother Pietro di Morrone!" The cardinals promptly ratified Malabranca's desperate decision. When sent for, Pietro obstinately refused to accept the papacy, and even, as <a href="/wiki/Petrarch" title="Petrarch">Petrarch</a> says, tried to flee, until he was finally persuaded by a deputation of cardinals accompanied by the king of <a href="/wiki/Charles_II_of_Naples" title="Charles II of Naples">Naples</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Charles_Martel_of_Anjou" title="Charles Martel of Anjou">pretender to the throne of Hungary</a>. Elected on 5 July 1294,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> at age 79, he was crowned at <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_di_Collemaggio" title="Santa Maria di Collemaggio">Santa Maria di Collemaggio</a> in the city of <a href="/wiki/L%27Aquila" title="L&#39;Aquila">Aquila</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Abruzzo" title="Abruzzo">Abruzzo</a> on 29 August, taking the name Celestine V.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Papacy">Papacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Celestine_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Papacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Shortly after assuming office, Celestine issued a papal bull granting a rare <a href="/wiki/Plenary_indulgence" class="mw-redirect" title="Plenary indulgence">plenary indulgence</a> to all pilgrims visiting <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_di_Collemaggio" title="Santa Maria di Collemaggio">Santa Maria di Collemaggio</a> through its holy door on the anniversary of his papal coronation.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Celestinian_forgiveness" class="mw-redirect" title="Celestinian forgiveness">Celestinian forgiveness</a> (<i>Perdonanza Celestiniana</i>) festival is celebrated in L'Aquila every 28–29 August in commemoration of this event.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>With no political experience, Celestine proved to be an especially weak and ineffectual pope.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup> He held his office in the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Naples" title="Kingdom of Naples">Kingdom of Naples</a>, out of contact with the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Curia" title="Roman Curia">Roman Curia</a> and under the complete power of King <a href="/wiki/Charles_II_of_Naples" title="Charles II of Naples">Charles II</a>. He appointed the king's favorites to Church offices, sometimes several to the same office. One of these was <a href="/wiki/Louis_of_Toulouse" title="Louis of Toulouse">Louis of Toulouse</a>, whom Celestine ordered given <a href="/wiki/Clerical_tonsure" class="mw-redirect" title="Clerical tonsure">clerical tonsure</a> and <a href="/wiki/Minor_orders" title="Minor orders">minor orders</a>, although this was not carried out. He renewed a decree of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_X" title="Pope Gregory X">Pope Gregory X</a> that had established stringent rules for <a href="/wiki/Papal_conclave" title="Papal conclave">papal conclaves</a> after a similarly prolonged election. In one decree, he appointed three cardinals to govern the Church during <a href="/wiki/Advent" title="Advent">Advent</a> while he fasted, which was again refused.<sup id="cite_ref-mcbrien_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcbrien-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Realizing his lack of authority and personal incompatibility with papal duties, he consulted with Cardinal <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_VIII" title="Pope Boniface VIII">Benedetto Caetani</a> (his eventual successor) about the possibility of resignation.<sup id="cite_ref-mcbrien_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-mcbrien-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> This resulted in one final decree declaring the right of resignation. He promptly exercised this right, resigning on 13 December 1294, after five months and eight days as pope.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> In the formal instrument of renunciation, he recited as the causes moving him to the step: "The desire for humility, for a purer life, for a stainless conscience, the deficiencies of his own physical strength, his ignorance, the perverseness of the people, his longing for the tranquility of his former life".<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup> Having divested himself of every outward symbol of papal dignity, he slipped away from Naples and attempted to retire to his old life of solitude.
</p><p>The next pope to resign of his own accord was <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_XII" title="Pope Gregory XII">Gregory XII</a> in 1415 (to help end the <a href="/wiki/Western_Schism" title="Western Schism">Western Schism</a>), followed by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Benedict XVI</a> in 2013, 719 years later.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<p>The former Celestine, now reverted to Pietro Angelerio, was not allowed to become a hermit once again. Various parties had opposed his resignation and the new <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_VIII" title="Pope Boniface VIII">Pope Boniface VIII</a> had reason to worry that one of them might install him as an <a href="/wiki/Antipope" title="Antipope">antipope</a>. To prevent this he ordered Pietro to accompany him to Rome. Pietro escaped and hid in the woods before attempting to return to Sulmona to resume monastic life. This proved impossible, and Pietro was captured after an attempt to flee to <a href="/wiki/Dalmatia" title="Dalmatia">Dalmatia</a> was thwarted when a tempest forced his ship to return to port. Boniface imprisoned him in the castle of Fumone near <a href="/wiki/Ferentino" title="Ferentino">Ferentino</a> in <a href="/wiki/Lazio" title="Lazio">Lazio</a>, attended by two monks of his order, where Pietro died after 10 months at about the age of 81. His supporters spread the allegation that Boniface had treated him harshly and ultimately executed Pietro, but there is no clear historical evidence of this.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Pietro was buried at Ferentino, but his body was subsequently moved to the Basilica of <a href="/wiki/Santa_Maria_di_Collemaggio" title="Santa Maria di Collemaggio">Santa Maria di Collemaggio</a> in <a href="/wiki/L%27Aquila" title="L&#39;Aquila">L'Aquila</a>.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Philip_IV_of_France" title="Philip IV of France">Philip IV of France</a>, who had supported Celestine and bitterly opposed Boniface, nominated Celestine for sainthood following the election of <a href="/wiki/Pope_Clement_V" title="Pope Clement V">Pope Clement V</a>. The latter signed a decree of dispensation on 13 May 1306 to investigate the nomination.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> He was canonized on 5 May 1313 after a <a href="/wiki/Papal_consistory" title="Papal consistory">consistory</a> in which Boniface's <a href="/wiki/Caetani" title="Caetani">Caetani</a> family was outvoted by members of the rival <a href="/wiki/Colonna" class="mw-redirect" title="Colonna">Colonna</a> family.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Celestine_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Most modern interest in Celestine V has focused on his resignation.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> He was the first pope to formalize the resignation process and is often said to have been the first to resign; in fact he was preceded in this by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Pontian" title="Pope Pontian">Pontian</a> (235), <a href="/wiki/John_XVIII" class="mw-redirect" title="John XVIII">John XVIII</a> (1009), <a href="/wiki/Benedict_IX" class="mw-redirect" title="Benedict IX">Benedict IX</a> (1045), and <a href="/wiki/Pope_Gregory_VI" title="Pope Gregory VI">Gregory VI</a> (1046).<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> As noted above, Celestine's own decision was brought about by mild pressure from the Church establishment. His reinstitution of Gregory X's conclave system established by the papal bull <i><a href="/wiki/Ubi_periculum" title="Ubi periculum">Ubi periculum</a></i> has been respected ever since.
</p><p>A 1966 visit by <a href="/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI" title="Pope Paul VI">Pope Paul VI</a> to Celestine's place of death in Ferentino along with his speech in homage of Celestine prompted speculation that the Pontiff was considering retirement.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Celestine's remains survived the <a href="/wiki/2009_L%27Aquila_earthquake" title="2009 L&#39;Aquila earthquake">2009 L'Aquila earthquake</a> with one Italian spokesman saying it was "another great miracle by the pope".<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> They were then recovered from the basilica shortly after the earthquake.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup> While inspecting the earthquake damage during a 28 April 2009 visit to the Aquila, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI" title="Pope Benedict XVI">Pope Benedict XVI</a> visited Celestine's remains in the badly damaged Santa Maria di Collemaggio and left the woolen <a href="/wiki/Pallium" title="Pallium">pallium</a> he wore during his papal inauguration in April 2005 on his glass casket as a gift.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> Benedict XVI would go on to become the first pope since Celestine to voluntarily resign of his own initiative.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>To mark the 800th anniversary of Celestine's birth, Pope Benedict XVI proclaimed the Celestine year from 28 August 2009 through 29 August 2010.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> Benedict XVI visited the <a href="/wiki/Sulmona_Cathedral" title="Sulmona Cathedral">Sulmona Cathedral</a>, near Aquila, on 4 July 2010<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> as part of his observance of the Celestine year and prayed before the altar consecrated by Celestine containing his relics on 10 October 1294.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>His entry in the <i><a href="/wiki/Martyrologium_Romanum" class="mw-redirect" title="Martyrologium Romanum">Martyrologium Romanum</a></i> for 19 May reads as follows:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p><i>Ad Castrum Fumorense prop Alatrium in Latio, natalis sancti Petri Caelestini, qui, cum vitam eremeticam in Aprutio ageret, fama sanctitatis et miraculorum clarus, octogenarius Romanus Pontifex electus est, assumpto nomine Caelestini Quinti, sed eodem anno munere se abdicavit et solitudinem recedere maluit.</i></p></blockquote>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"/><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>At <i>Castrum Fumorense</i> near <a href="/wiki/Alatri" title="Alatri">Alatri</a> in <a href="/wiki/Lazio" title="Lazio">Lazio</a>, the birth of Saint Peter Celestine, who, when leading the life of a hermit in Abruzzo, being famous for his sanctity and miracles, was elected Roman Pontiff as an octogenarian, assumed the name Celestine V, but abandoned his office that same year and preferred to return to solitude.</p></blockquote>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="In_literature">In literature</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Pope_Celestine_V&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: In literature">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<div class="thumb tright"><div class="thumbinner" style="width:222px;"><a href="/wiki/File:Caelestinus_V_-_Opuscula_omnia,_1640_-_BEIC_9744840.tiff" class="image"><img alt="" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/Caelestinus_V_-_Opuscula_omnia%2C_1640_-_BEIC_9744840.tiff/lossy-page1-220px-Caelestinus_V_-_Opuscula_omnia%2C_1640_-_BEIC_9744840.tiff.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="335" class="thumbimage" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/Caelestinus_V_-_Opuscula_omnia%2C_1640_-_BEIC_9744840.tiff/lossy-page1-330px-Caelestinus_V_-_Opuscula_omnia%2C_1640_-_BEIC_9744840.tiff.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/Caelestinus_V_-_Opuscula_omnia%2C_1640_-_BEIC_9744840.tiff/lossy-page1-440px-Caelestinus_V_-_Opuscula_omnia%2C_1640_-_BEIC_9744840.tiff.jpg 2x" data-file-width="2300" data-file-height="3500" /></a>  <div class="thumbcaption"><div class="magnify"><a href="/wiki/File:Caelestinus_V_-_Opuscula_omnia,_1640_-_BEIC_9744840.tiff" class="internal" title="Enlarge"></a></div><i>Opuscula omnia</i>, 1640</div></div></div>
<p>A persistent tradition identifies Celestine V as the nameless figure <a href="/wiki/Dante_Alighieri" title="Dante Alighieri">Dante Alighieri</a> sees among those in the antechamber of Hell, in the enigmatic verses:
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"/><blockquote class="templatequote"><div class="poem">
<p><i>vidi e conobbi l'ombra di colui</i><br />
<i>che fece per viltade il gran rifiuto.</i><br />
("I saw and recognized the shade of him<br />
who due to cowardice made the <a href="/wiki/Great_refusal" title="Great refusal">great refusal</a>.")
</p>
</div><div class="templatequotecite">—&#8201;<cite><i>Inferno</i> III, 59–60</cite></div></blockquote>
<p>The first commentators to make this identification included Dante's son <a href="/wiki/Jacopo_Alighieri" title="Jacopo Alighieri">Jacopo Alighieri</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> followed by Graziolo Bambaglioli in 1324. The identification is also considered probable by recent scholars (e.g., Hollander, <a href="/wiki/Barbara_Reynolds" title="Barbara Reynolds">Barbara Reynolds</a>, Simonelli, Padoan). <a href="/wiki/Petrarch" title="Petrarch">Petrarch</a> was moved to defend Celestine vigorously against the accusation of cowardice and some modern scholars (e.g., Mark Musa) have suggested Dante may have meant someone else (<a href="/wiki/Esau" title="Esau">Esau</a>, <a href="/wiki/Diocletian" title="Diocletian">Diocletian</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pontius_Pilate" title="Pontius Pilate">Pontius Pilate</a> have been variously suggested).
</p><p>In 1346, Petrarch declared in his <i>De vita solitaria</i> that Celestine's refusal was a virtuous example of solitary life.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Pope Celestine V is referenced in Chapter 88 of <a href="/wiki/Dan_Brown" title="Dan Brown">Dan Brown</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Angels_%26_Demons" title="Angels &amp; Demons">Angels &amp; Demons</a></i>, where he is controversially referenced as an example of a <a href="/wiki/List_of_murdered_Popes" class="mw-redirect" title="List of murdered Popes">murdered pope</a>. Brown writes that an X-ray of his tomb "revealed a ten-inch nail driven into the Pope's skull."<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> While the reference to the X-ray is fictitious (no X-ray has ever been conducted on Celestine's tomb), it has been indeed alleged that Celestine was murdered, possibly by order of his successor, <a href="/wiki/Pope_Boniface_VIII" title="Pope Boniface VIII">Pope Boniface VIII</a>; however, there is no historical evidence of this.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The life of Pope Celestine V is dramatised in the plays <i><a href="/wiki/L%27avventura_di_un_povero_cristiano" class="mw-redirect" title="L&#39;avventura di un povero cristiano">L'avventura di un povero cristiano</a></i> (<i>The <a href="/wiki/Story_of_a_Humble_Christian" title="Story of a Humble Christian">Story of a Humble Christian</a></i>) by <a href="/wiki/Ignazio_Silone" title="Ignazio Silone">Ignazio Silone</a> in 1968 and <i>Sunsets and Glories</i> by <a href="/wiki/Peter_Barnes_(playwright)" title="Peter Barnes (playwright)">Peter Barnes</a> in 1990.
</p><p>His life is the subject of the short story <i>Brother of the Holy Ghost</i> in Brendan Connell's short story collection <i>The Life of Polycrates and Other Stories for Antiquated Children</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Celestine V is the subject of <a href="/w/index.php?title=Stefania_Del_Monte&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Stefania Del Monte (page does not exist)">Stefania Del Monte</a>'s book <i>Celestino V. Papa Templare o Povero Cristiano?</i>, published in 2009 and translated into English under the title <i>The Story and Legacy of Celestine V</i> in 2010.<sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>He is the subject of a popular history by author <a href="/wiki/Jon_M._Sweeney" title="Jon M. Sweeney">Jon M. Sweeney</a>, <i>The Pope Who Quit: A True Medieval Tale of Mystery, Death, and Salvation</i>, published by Image Books/Random House in 2012.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup> In 2013, HBO optioned the film rights.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Celestine V is the subject of the poem "Che Fece...Il Gran Rifiuto" by the modern Greek poet <a href="/wiki/Constantine_P._Cavafy" title="Constantine P. Cavafy">Constantine P. Cavafy</a>.
</p>
