<p><b>Karl Leisner</b> (28 February 1915 in <a href="/wiki/Rees,_Germany" title="Rees, Germany">Rees</a> &#8211; 12 August 1945 in <a href="/wiki/Planegg" title="Planegg">Planegg</a>, Germany) was a <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman Catholic">Roman Catholic</a> <a href="/wiki/Priest" title="Priest">priest</a> interned in the <a href="/wiki/Dachau_concentration_camp" title="Dachau concentration camp">Dachau concentration camp</a>. He died of <a href="/wiki/Tuberculosis" title="Tuberculosis">tuberculosis</a> shortly after being liberated by the <a href="/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II" title="Allies of World War II">Allied forces</a>. He has been declared a <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a> and was <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a> on 23 June 1996.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Life">Life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Karl_Leisner&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Leisner was born on 28 February 1915, the oldest of five children. When he was six years old, the family moved to <a href="/wiki/Kleve" title="Kleve">Kleve</a>, a city on the lower Rhine, where his father worked as a civil servant. He attended school and completed his <a href="/wiki/Gymnasium_(school)" title="Gymnasium (school)">gymnasium</a> studies in 1934. During his youth, he became an <a href="/wiki/Altar_boy" class="mw-redirect" title="Altar boy">altar boy</a> and, at the suggestion of the high school chaplain, formed a Catholic youth group, the Saint Werner Group. These youth groups combined prayer with outdoor activities, such as camping and cycling. Leisner turned out to be a natural leader and became a youth leader in the 1930s, during the era in which the Nazis were beginning to take control of all youth organizations. In order to avoid Nazi interference, Leisner would often take his group on camping trips to Holland or Belgium.<sup id="cite_ref-curricula_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-curricula-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>In 1934, when he was nineteen, Leisner entered the seminary in <a href="/wiki/Munich" title="Munich">Munich</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Phillips_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Phillips-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> and was named Diocesan Youth Leader by <a href="/wiki/Clemens_August_von_Galen" class="mw-redirect" title="Clemens August von Galen">Clemens August von Galen</a>, <a href="/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_M%C3%BCnster" title="Roman Catholic Diocese of Münster">Bishop of Münster</a>. He spent six months in compulsory agricultural work during which, despite Nazi opposition, he organized Sunday Mass for his fellow workers. His home was raided by the Gestapo, who seized his diaries and papers. These meticulously preserved documents tell how the spiritual young man became a religious leader.
</p><p>On 25 March 1939, Galen ordained Leisner a <a href="/wiki/Deacon" title="Deacon">deacon</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Phillips_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Phillips-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> Shortly after his ordination, during a medical examination, the doctor told the new deacon that he had contracted <a href="/wiki/Tuberculosis" title="Tuberculosis">tuberculosis</a>. In those days, the sole treatment available for the disease was good food and fresh air. These were to be found at a <a href="/wiki/Sanitorium" class="mw-redirect" title="Sanitorium">sanitorium</a> in <a href="/wiki/St._Blasien" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Blasien">St. Blasien</a>, in the <a href="/wiki/Black_Forest" title="Black Forest">Black Forest</a>, where he was sent and began to recover. It was during his recovery that a fellow patient heard him criticize <a href="/wiki/Adolf_Hitler" title="Adolf Hitler">Adolf Hitler</a>. The Gestapo arrested him as a political prisoner on 9 November 1939, when he was twenty-five. He was interned first in <a href="/wiki/Freiburg" class="mw-redirect" title="Freiburg">Freiburg</a> and later in <a href="/wiki/Mannheim" title="Mannheim">Mannheim</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-romano_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-romano-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> Leisner was initially imprisoned in the <a href="/wiki/Sachsenhausen_concentration_camp" title="Sachsenhausen concentration camp">Sachsenhausen concentration camp</a>, but was moved to the <a href="/wiki/Dachau_concentration_camp" title="Dachau concentration camp">Dachau concentration camp</a> on 14 December 1940, where he became prisoner No. 22,356. Since he was a deacon, Leisner was assigned to the priests' block.<sup id="cite_ref-curricula_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-curricula-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Prisoners often had to work outside in snow or rain and then had to sleep in their wet prison clothes. Cold weather, poor rations and harsh treatment proved a dangerous combination for someone already suffering to tuberculosis. Such conditions
caused Leisner's condition to become active. Then, during an inspection, two <a href="/wiki/Gestapo" title="Gestapo">Gestapo</a> guards beat him unconscious and he spent several hours on the floor of his hut. By March 1942, he was spitting blood and was forced to report to the infirmary where medical experiments were known to be performed. He was put in a room that was crowded with over 100 tuberculosis patients. In Dachau, there was never any attempt to cure a disease and very little care was given to the sick. During inspections of the infirmary, any patient
thought to be incurable was executed.<sup id="cite_ref-curricula_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-curricula-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On 17 December 1944, <a href="/wiki/Gaudete_Sunday" title="Gaudete Sunday">Gaudete Sunday</a>, a fellow prisoner, French Bishop <a href="/wiki/Gabriel_Piguet" title="Gabriel Piguet">Gabriel Piguet</a>, secretly ordained him a <a href="/wiki/Priesthood_(Catholic_Church)" class="mw-redirect" title="Priesthood (Catholic Church)">priest</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-romano_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-romano-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The necessary paperwork with the authorization for the ordination, as well as other necessary items, were smuggled into the camp by "<i>Mädi</i>", the "Angel of Dachau", a young woman named Josefa Mack. (Mack went on to become a <a href="/wiki/School_Sisters_of_Notre_Dame" title="School Sisters of Notre Dame">School Sister of Notre Dame</a> in Munich, called Sister Maria Imma.) Some imprisoned Protestant pastors helped organize the event and a Jewish violinist played music near the barracks to create a diversion.<sup id="cite_ref-Phillips_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Phillips-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The newly ordained priest only celebrated a single Mass and was so ill that he had to postpone his first Mass for over a week.<sup id="cite_ref-romano_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-romano-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> When Dachau was liberated on 4 May 1945, Leisner was taken to a sanatarium in <a href="/wiki/Planegg" title="Planegg">Planegg</a>, near Munich. He died there a few months later, on 12 August 1945.
</p><p>Leisner's body was taken to Kleve and buried in a local cemetery on 20 August 1945. His remains were exhumed and re-interred in the crypt of the <a href="/wiki/Xanten_Cathedral" title="Xanten Cathedral">Cathedral of Xanten</a> in 1966.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Beatification">Beatification</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Karl_Leisner&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Beatification">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>On a visit to <a href="/wiki/Berlin" title="Berlin">Berlin</a> in 1996, Pope John Paul II recognized Leisner as a martyr for the Catholic faith and beatified him, together with <a href="/wiki/Bernhard_Lichtenberg" title="Bernhard Lichtenberg">Bernhard Lichtenberg</a>, another Nazi resister. His <a href="/wiki/Feast_day" class="mw-redirect" title="Feast day">feast day</a> is 12 August.
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Canonization" title="Canonization">canonization</a> process for Leisner has not yet been completed.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Media">Media</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Karl_Leisner&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Media">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Some works have been published in English about Leisner. One is <i>The Victory of Father Karl</i> by Otto Pies, published in 1957. It was a translation of <i>Stephanus heute; Karl Leisner, Priester und Opfer</i>. A radio drama adaptation was produced for "The Hour of St. Francis" with the same title. A half-hour docudrama on videotape was released by the <a href="/wiki/Daughters_of_St._Paul" title="Daughters of St. Paul">Daughters of St. Paul</a> in 1984, also with the same title.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Leisner_and_Schoenstatt">Leisner and Schoenstatt</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Karl_Leisner&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Leisner and Schoenstatt">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Karl Leisner got to know the <a href="/wiki/Schoenstatt_Apostolic_Movement" title="Schoenstatt Apostolic Movement">Schoenstatt movement</a> as a teenager and belonged to a Schoenstatt group - which included the future Bishop of Münster, Heinrich Tenhumberg - up till his final days. Throughout his life, the few but intense times of personal encounter with <a href="/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus" title="Mary, mother of Jesus">Our Lady</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Schoenstatt_Shrine" title="Schoenstatt Shrine">Original Shrine</a> in Schoenstatt remained the decisive milestones for Karl Leisner on his path of calling. "Christ, my passion" - led by this <a href="/wiki/Personal_goal_setting" class="mw-redirect" title="Personal goal setting">ideal</a>, he worked in the diocesan youth and wrestled his way to a decision for a celibate life as a priest. In the <a href="/wiki/Dachau_concentration_camp" title="Dachau concentration camp">Dachau concentration camp</a> he founded, together with Josef Fischer, the first Schoenstatt group in Dachau, which had to end its meetings in the 1942 starvation year. From 1943 Karl Leisner belonged to the group "Victor in vinculis Mariae" (victor in the shackles of Mary) and thus to the circle of Schoenstaetters around the founder <a href="/wiki/Joseph_Kentenich" title="Joseph Kentenich">Josef Kentenich</a>. From the ideal and the fraternity of this group, Karl Leisner drew the strength to accept his fate, which was burdened by the tuberculosis of the lung as well as the difficult conditions in the concentration camp, as the will of God and to offer his life as a martyr.
</p>
