<p><b>Gideon</b> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap"><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a href="/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="/ɡ/: &#39;g&#39; in &#39;guy&#39;">ɡ</span><span title="/ɪ/: &#39;i&#39; in &#39;kit&#39;">ɪ</span><span title="&#39;d&#39; in &#39;dye&#39;">d</span><span title="/i/: &#39;y&#39; in &#39;happy&#39;">i</span><span title="/ə/: &#39;a&#39; in &#39;about&#39;">ə</span><span title="&#39;n&#39; in &#39;nigh&#39;">n</span></span>/</a></span></span>; <a href="/wiki/Hebrew_language" title="Hebrew language">Hebrew</a>: <span style="white-space:nowrap;" lang="he" dir="rtl"><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1020748213"><span class="script-hebrew" style="font-size: 115%;" dir="rtl">גִּדְעוֹן</span>&#8206;</span>, <small><a href="/wiki/Modern_Hebrew" title="Modern Hebrew">Modern</a>:</small>&#160;<i><span title="Hebrew-language romanization"><i lang="he-Latn">Gīdʿōn</i></span></i>, <small><a href="/wiki/Tiberian_vocalization" title="Tiberian vocalization">Tiberian</a>:</small>&#160;<i><span title="Hebrew-language romanization"><i lang="he-Latn">Gīḏəʿōn</i></span></i>) also named <b>Jerubbaal</b><sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">&#91;a&#93;</a></sup> and <b>Jerubbesheth</b>,<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">&#91;b&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECheyne1901_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECheyne1901-3">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> was a <a href="/wiki/Military_rank" title="Military rank">military leader</a>, <a href="/wiki/Biblical_judges" class="mw-redirect" title="Biblical judges">judge</a> and prophet whose calling and victory over the <a href="/wiki/Midianites" class="mw-redirect" title="Midianites">Midianites</a> are recounted in <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt0706.htm#1">Judges 6–8</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Judges" title="Book of Judges">Book of Judges</a> in the <a href="/wiki/Hebrew_Bible" title="Hebrew Bible">Hebrew Bible</a>.
</p><p>Gideon was the son of <a href="/wiki/Joash_the_Abiezrite" class="mw-redirect" title="Joash the Abiezrite">Joash</a>, from the <a href="/wiki/Abiezrite" title="Abiezrite">Abiezrite</a> clan in the <a href="/wiki/Tribe_of_Manasseh" title="Tribe of Manasseh">tribe of Manasseh</a> and lived in <a href="/wiki/Ophrah" title="Ophrah">Ephra (Ophrah)</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGigot1909_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGigot1909-4">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> As a leader of the <a href="/wiki/Israelites" title="Israelites">Israelites</a>, he won a decisive victory over a Midianite army despite a vast numerical disadvantage, leading a troop of 300 "valiant" men.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup>
Archaeologists in southern Israel have found a 3,100-year-old fragment of a jug with five letters written in ink that appear to represent the name Jerubbaal, or Yeruba'al.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTERollstonGarfinkelKeimerDavis2021_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTERollstonGarfinkelKeimerDavis2021-6">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup>
</p>

<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Names">Names</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Names">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The nineteenth-century <a href="/wiki/Strong%27s_Concordance" title="Strong&#39;s Concordance">Strong's Concordance</a> derives the name "Jerubbaal" from "Baal will contend", in accordance with the folk etymology, given in <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://bible.oremus.org/?passage=Judges%206:32&amp;version=nrsv">Judges 6:32</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-StrongH3378_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-StrongH3378-7">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Mud_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mud-8">&#91;c&#93;</a></sup> According to biblical scholar <a href="/wiki/Lester_L._Grabbe" title="Lester L. Grabbe">Lester Grabbe</a> (2007), "[Judges] 6.32 gives a nonsensical etymology of his name; it means something like 'Let Baal be great.<span style="padding-right:.15em;">'</span>"<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEGrabbe200799_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrabbe200799-9">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Likewise, where Strong gave the meaning "<a href="/wiki/Hewer" title="Hewer">hewer</a>" to the name Gideon,<sup id="cite_ref-StrongH1438_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-StrongH1438-10">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Biblical scholar Simon John DeVries (1975) suggests the etymology "driver".<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDeVries197579_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeVries197579-11">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The "besheth" part of the name "Jerubbesheth" (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt08b11.htm#21">2 Samuel 11:21</a>) means "shame". This a pious editorialization of "Baal", as is also found in the names of Saul's son <a href="/wiki/Ish-bosheth" title="Ish-bosheth">Ish-bosheth</a> and grandson <a href="/wiki/Mephibosheth" title="Mephibosheth">Mephibosheth</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Mandelkern1937_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Mandelkern1937-12">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Biblical_narrative">Biblical narrative</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Biblical narrative">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Maarten_van_Heemskerck_024.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/Maarten_van_Heemskerck_024.jpg/170px-Maarten_van_Heemskerck_024.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="620" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/Maarten_van_Heemskerck_024.jpg/255px-Maarten_van_Heemskerck_024.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/Maarten_van_Heemskerck_024.jpg/340px-Maarten_van_Heemskerck_024.jpg 2x" data-file-width="839" data-file-height="3061" /></a><figcaption>"<a href="/wiki/Adam_and_Eve/Gideon_and_the_Fleece" title="Adam and Eve/Gideon and the Fleece">Gideon thanks God for the Miracle of the Dew</a>", painting by <a href="/wiki/Maarten_van_Heemskerck" title="Maarten van Heemskerck">Maarten van Heemskerck</a> (<a href="/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_des_Beaux-Arts_de_Strasbourg" title="Musée des Beaux-Arts de Strasbourg">Musée des Beaux-Arts de Strasbourg</a>)</figcaption></figure>
<p>As is the pattern throughout the <a href="/wiki/Book_of_Judges" title="Book of Judges">Book of Judges</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Israelites" title="Israelites">Israelites</a> again turned away from <a href="/wiki/Yahweh" title="Yahweh">Yahweh</a> after 40 years of peace brought by <a href="/wiki/Deborah" title="Deborah">Deborah</a>'s victory over <a href="/wiki/Canaan" title="Canaan">Canaan</a>; as punishment, the <a href="/wiki/Midian" title="Midian">Midianites</a>, <a href="/wiki/Amalek" title="Amalek">Amalekites</a> and other <a href="/wiki/Bedouin" title="Bedouin">Bedouin</a> peoples harried Israel for seven years.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Calling">Calling</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Calling">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>God chose Gideon, a young man from the <a href="/wiki/Tribe_of_Manasseh" title="Tribe of Manasseh">tribe of Manasseh</a>, to free the people of Israel and to condemn their <a href="/wiki/Idolatry" title="Idolatry">idolatry</a>. The <a href="/wiki/Angel_of_the_Lord#Hebrew_Bible" title="Angel of the Lord">Angel of the Lord</a>, or "the Lord’s angelic messenger"<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> came "in the character ... of a traveller who sat down in the shade [of the terebinth tree] to enjoy a little refreshment and repose"<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> and entered into conversation with Gideon. The narrative has echoes of the meeting between <a href="/wiki/Abraham" title="Abraham">Abraham</a> and the visitors who came to him in the <a href="/wiki/Terebinth" class="mw-redirect" title="Terebinth">terebinths</a> of <a href="/wiki/Mamre" title="Mamre">Mamre</a> and promised Abraham and <a href="/wiki/Sarah" title="Sarah">Sarah</a>, in their old age, that they would have a son (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt0118.htm#1">Genesis 18:1–15</a>).<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (February 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> The angel greeted Gideon: "The Lord is with you, you mighty man of valor!"<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gideon requested proof of God's will by three miracles: firstly a sign from the angel, in which the angel appeared to Gideon and caused fire to shoot up out of a rock,<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup> and then two signs involving a <a href="/wiki/Wool" title="Wool">fleece</a>, performed on consecutive nights and the exact opposite of each other. First Gideon woke up to his fleece covered in dew, but the surrounding ground dry; then the next morning, his fleece was dry but the surrounding ground covered in dew.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>On God's instruction, Gideon destroyed the town's altar to <a href="/wiki/Baal" title="Baal">Baal</a> and the symbol of the goddess <a href="/wiki/Asherah" title="Asherah">Asherah</a> beside it, receiving the byname of <i>Jerubbaal</i> from his father:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r996844942">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 40px}.mw-parser-output .templatequote .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;padding-left:1.6em;margin-top:0}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>Therefore on that day he (Joash) called him Jerubbaal, saying, Let Baal plead against him, because he hath thrown down his altar.<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Gathering_an_army">Gathering an army</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Gathering an army">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Gideon then sent out messengers to gather men from the tribes of <a href="/wiki/Tribe_of_Asher" title="Tribe of Asher">Asher</a>, <a href="/wiki/Tribe_of_Zebulun" title="Tribe of Zebulun">Zebulun</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Tribe_of_Naphtali" title="Tribe of Naphtali">Naphtali</a>, as well as his own tribe of Manasseh, in order to meet an armed force of Midian and Amalek that had crossed the <a href="/wiki/Jordan_River" title="Jordan River">Jordan River</a>. Gideon's force encamped at <a href="/wiki/En_Harod" title="En Harod">En Harod</a>, while the Midianites camped near the "Hill of Moreh".<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>God informed Gideon that the men he had gathered were too many: with so many men, the Israelites might claim the victory as their own, rather than crediting God. God first instructed Gideon to send home those men who were afraid. Gideon invited any man who wanted to leave, to do so; 22,000 men returned home and 10,000 remained. God then told Gideon that this number was still too many, and instructed Gideon to bring the men to the water to drink. God commanded to send home those who had bowed down to drink, as well as those who drank directly from the water "like a dog". Only 300 men, who had cupped the water with their hands to drink it, were allowed to remain.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Night_attack">Night attack</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Night attack">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Schnorr_von_Carolsfeld_Bibel_in_Bildern_1860_075.png" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/Schnorr_von_Carolsfeld_Bibel_in_Bildern_1860_075.png/220px-Schnorr_von_Carolsfeld_Bibel_in_Bildern_1860_075.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="177" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/Schnorr_von_Carolsfeld_Bibel_in_Bildern_1860_075.png/330px-Schnorr_von_Carolsfeld_Bibel_in_Bildern_1860_075.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/Schnorr_von_Carolsfeld_Bibel_in_Bildern_1860_075.png/440px-Schnorr_von_Carolsfeld_Bibel_in_Bildern_1860_075.png 2x" data-file-width="1119" data-file-height="902" /></a><figcaption><i>Gideon's Call</i>, 1860 woodcut by <a href="/wiki/Julius_Schnorr_von_Karolsfeld" class="mw-redirect" title="Julius Schnorr von Karolsfeld">Julius Schnorr von Karolsfeld</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>During the night, God instructed Gideon to approach the Midianite camp. There, Gideon overheard a Midianite man tell a friend of a dream in which "a loaf of <a href="/wiki/Barley_bread" title="Barley bread">barley bread</a> tumbled into the camp of Midian",<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> causing their tent or camp to collapse. This was interpreted as meaning that God had given the Midianites over to Gideon. Gideon returned to the Israelite camp and gave each of his men a <a href="/wiki/Shofar" title="Shofar">shofar</a> and a clay jar with a torch hidden inside. Divided into three companies, Gideon and his 300 men marched on the enemy camp. He instructed them to blow the trumpet, give a battle cry and light torches, simulating an attack by a large force. As they did so, the Midianite army fled.<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gideon sent messengers ahead into Israel calling for the <a href="/wiki/Tribe_of_Ephraim" title="Tribe of Ephraim">Ephraimites</a> to pursue the retreating Midianites and two of their leaders, <a href="/wiki/Oreb_and_Zeeb" title="Oreb and Zeeb">Oreb and Zeeb</a>. Gideon and the three hundred pursued <a href="/wiki/Zebah_and_Zalmunna" title="Zebah and Zalmunna">Zebah and Zalmunna</a>, the two Midianite kings. When he had asked for provisions in his pursuit, the men of <a href="/wiki/Sukkot_(place)" title="Sukkot (place)">Succoth</a> and <a href="/wiki/Peniel_(biblical)" class="mw-redirect" title="Peniel (biblical)">Peniel</a> refused and taunted Gideon. After capturing the two kings, Gideon punished the men of Succoth, and pulled down the tower of Peniel killing all the men there.  Gideon invited his eldest son, <a href="/wiki/Jether" title="Jether">Jether</a>, to slay Zebah and Zalmunna, but being still young at the time, he did not have the confidence to carry out his father's request, so Zebah and Zalmunna called on Gideon to perform the deed himself. Gideon then killed Zebah and Zalmunna as justice for the death of his brothers (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt0708.htm#19">Judges 8:19–21</a>). The place where Gideon slew Oreb after the defeat of the Midianites was called the <i>Rock of Oreb</i>. It was probably the place now called Orbo, on the east of Jordan, near <a href="/wiki/Bethshean" class="mw-redirect" title="Bethshean">Bethshean</a>. Zeeb was killed at "the wine press of Zeeb".
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Subsequent_events">Subsequent events</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Subsequent events">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The Israelites invited Gideon to become their king and to found a <a href="/wiki/Dynasty" title="Dynasty">dynasty</a>, but he refused, telling them that only God was their ruler.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Gideon went on to make an <i><a href="/wiki/Ephod" title="Ephod">ephod</a></i> out of the gold won in battle. This action eventually caused the whole of Israel to turn away from God yet again and the ruin of Gideon and his family.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> Gideon had 70 sons from the many women he took as wives. He also had a <a href="/wiki/Shechem" title="Shechem">Shechemite</a> concubine who bore him a son whom he named <a href="/wiki/Abimelech_(Judges)" title="Abimelech (Judges)">Abimelech</a>, which means "my father is king".<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>There was peace in Israel for 40 years during the life of Gideon. As soon as Gideon died of old age, the Israelites again turned to worship the false god <a href="/wiki/Baal_Berith" title="Baal Berith">Baal Berith</a> and ignored the family of Gideon (<a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt0708.htm#33">Judges 8:33</a>). Gideon was succeeded for a brief time as ruler of Shechem by his son Abimelech.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Rabbinic_commentary">Rabbinic commentary</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Rabbinic commentary">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>According to <a href="/wiki/Louis_Ginzberg" title="Louis Ginzberg">Louis Ginzberg</a>'s <a href="/wiki/Midrash" title="Midrash">Midrash</a> <a href="/wiki/Anthology" title="Anthology">anthology</a> <i><a href="/wiki/The_Legends_of_the_Jews" class="mw-redirect" title="The Legends of the Jews">The Legends of the Jews</a></i>: "Elated by the victory over Sisera, Israel sang a hymn of praise, the song of Deborah, and God, to reward them for their pious sentiments, pardoned the transgression of the people. But they soon slipped back into the old ways, and the old troubles harassed them. Their back-sliding was due to the witchcraft of a Midianite priest named Aud. He made the sun shine at midnight, and so convinced the Israelites that the idols of Midian were mightier than God, and God chastised them by delivering them into the hands of the Midianites. They worshipped their own images reflected in the water, and they were stricken with dire poverty. They could not bring so much as a meal offering, the offering of the poor. On the eve of one Passover, Gideon uttered the complaint: "Where are all the wondrous works which God did for our fathers in this night, when he slew the first-born of the Egyptians, and Israel went forth from slavery with joyous hearts?" God appeared unto him, and said: "Thou who art courageous enough to champion Israel, thou art worthy that Israel should be saved for thy sake."<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>To explain why the 300 men who brought water to their mouths with their hands were chosen to fight, while those who lapped the water directly or bowed down were not chosen, some rabbis explain that lapping directly is disgraceful and similar to a dog, while bowing down resembles idolatry.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to <i>The Legends of the Jews</i>: "..In the high priest's breastplate, Joseph was represented among the twelve tribes by Ephraim alone, not by Manasseh, too. To wipe out this slight upon his own tribe, Gideon made an ephod bearing the name of Manasseh. He consecrated it to God, but after his death homage was paid to it as an idol. In those days the Israelites were so addicted to the worship of Beelzebub that they constantly carried small images of this god with them in their pockets, and every now and then they were in the habit of bringing the image forth and kissing it fervently."<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Textual_history">Textual history</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Textual history">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>In the early twentieth century, the text of <a href="/wiki/Judges_6" title="Judges 6">Judges 6</a>–<a href="/wiki/Judges_8" title="Judges 8">8</a> was regarded by the "critical school" as a composite narrative, combining <a href="/wiki/Jahwist" title="Jahwist">Jahwist</a>, <a href="/wiki/Elohist" title="Elohist">Elohist</a> and <a href="/wiki/Deuteronomic" class="mw-redirect" title="Deuteronomic">Deuteronomic</a> sources, with further interpolations and editorial comments of the <a href="/wiki/Second_Temple_period" title="Second Temple period">Second Temple period</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHirsch1906_31-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHirsch1906-31">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup> According to this approach, the D source or (D) material has an overwhelming presence in Judges 6–8. The earlier source material used is present. However, the message and theological view has the style of the Deuteronomistic school of Authors.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTENiditch200810_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTENiditch200810-32">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> The core (Jahwist) narrative consists of Gideon wishing to avenge the death of his brothers, gathering 300 men of his own clan and pursuing the Midianite chiefs Zebah and Zalmunna, slaying them and consecrating an idol (<i>ephod</i>) made from the spoils of war, which makes his city of Ophrah the seat of an oracle and giving Gideon himself the status of a rich chief with a large harem (Judges 8:4–10a, 11–21, 24–27a, 29–32).<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHirsch1906_31-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHirsch1906-31">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>However, other scholars see the story as a single narrative, whose author made allusions to various Pentateuchal passages that he was familiar with.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> The story also contains consistent thematic elements which suggest a unified composition.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Emil_G._Hirsch" title="Emil G. Hirsch">Emil G. Hirsch</a> alleged a historical nucleus in the narrative, reflecting the struggle of the <a href="/wiki/Tribe_of_Manasseh" title="Tribe of Manasseh">tribe of Manasseh</a> with hostile Bedouins across the Jordan, along with "reminiscences of tribal jealousies on the part of Ephraim" in the early period of Hebrew settlement, later conflated with the religious context of connecting Yahweh with the shrine at Ophrah.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHirsch1906_31-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHirsch1906-31">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/G._A._Cooke" class="mw-redirect" title="G. A. Cooke">G. A. Cooke</a>, writing in the <a href="/wiki/Cambridge_Bible_for_Schools_and_Colleges" title="Cambridge Bible for Schools and Colleges">Cambridge Bible for Schools and Colleges</a> notes the discontinuity between Ephraimite anger towards Gideon shown in <a rel="nofollow" class="external text" href="https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt0708.htm#1">Judges 8:1–3</a> and the proposition of kingship over [all] Israel, and therefore concludes that "these verses appear to come from a [secondary] source".<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>According to <a href="/wiki/Yairah_Amit" title="Yairah Amit">Yairah Amit</a>, <a href="/wiki/Mark_S._Smith" title="Mark S. Smith">Mark S. Smith</a>, and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Simon_John_De_Vries&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Simon John De Vries (page does not exist)">Simon John De Vries</a>, the use of both names "Gideon" and "Jerubbaal" reflects two originally independent sets of stories combined by an editor who wishes them to be seen as referring to a single character.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTESmith200246_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith200246-36">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDeVries197579_11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeVries197579-11">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEAmit1999102_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAmit1999102-37">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup> The name Jerubbaal given to Gideon is originally a theophoric name meaning "Baal strives", but it was later given the interpretation of "let Baal strive against him" in order to avoid conflict with the more rigorous development of the religion of Yahweh in later centuries.<sup id="cite_ref-PurFra_38-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-PurFra-38">&#91;d&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Christian_reception">Christian reception</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Christian reception">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Unicorn_annunciation.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bc/Unicorn_annunciation.jpg/170px-Unicorn_annunciation.jpg" decoding="async" width="170" height="262" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bc/Unicorn_annunciation.jpg/255px-Unicorn_annunciation.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bc/Unicorn_annunciation.jpg/340px-Unicorn_annunciation.jpg 2x" data-file-width="390" data-file-height="600" /></a><figcaption>Gideon's fleece, as symbol of Mary, in a "Hunt of the Unicorn <a href="/wiki/Annunciation" title="Annunciation">Annunciation</a>" (ca. 1500) from a Netherlandish <a href="/wiki/Book_of_hours" title="Book of hours">book of hours</a>. For the complicated <a href="/wiki/Iconography" title="Iconography">iconography</a>, see <a href="/wiki/Hortus_Conclusus" class="mw-redirect" title="Hortus Conclusus">Hortus Conclusus</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<p>In the <a href="/wiki/New_Testament" title="New Testament">New Testament</a>, Gideon is mentioned in <a href="/wiki/Hebrews_11" title="Hebrews 11">chapter 11</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Epistle_to_the_Hebrews" title="Epistle to the Hebrews">Epistle to the Hebrews</a> as an example of a man of faith, one of several "heroes of faith" mentioned there:<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r996844942"><blockquote class="templatequote"><p> Time would fail me to tell of Gideon [and others] who through faith subdued kingdoms, worked righteousness, obtained promises, stopped the mouths of lions, quenched the violence of fire, escaped the edge of the sword, out of weakness were made strong, became valiant in battle, turned to flight the armies of the aliens.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Gideon is regarded as a <a href="/wiki/Saint" title="Saint">saint</a> by the <a href="/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church" title="Eastern Orthodox Church">Eastern Orthodox Church</a> and <a href="/wiki/Catholic_Church" title="Catholic Church">Catholic Church</a>. He is also commemorated, together with the other righteous figures of the Old Testament, on the Sunday before Christmas (Fourth Sunday of <a href="/wiki/Advent" title="Advent">Advent</a>). He is commemorated as one of the <a href="/wiki/Saints" class="mw-redirect" title="Saints">Saints</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church" title="Armenian Apostolic Church">Armenian Apostolic Church</a> on July 30, and in the <a href="/wiki/Roman_Martyrology" title="Roman Martyrology">Roman Martyrology</a> on September 26.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (January 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>In the <a href="/wiki/Protestant_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant Reformation">Protestant Reformation</a>, the Gideon narrative was employed in polemics against the Catholic clergy. <a href="/wiki/Hans_von_R%C3%BCte" title="Hans von Rüte">Hans von Rüte</a>'s <i>Gideon</i> (1540) compares the removal of saints' relics from churches to Gideon's destruction of Baal's altar.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Gideons_International" class="mw-redirect" title="Gideons International">Gideons International</a> is an American organization dedicated to Christian evangelism, founded in 1899, dedicated to the distribution of free Bibles. The organization's logo represents a two-handled pitcher and torch, symbolizing the implements used by Gideon to scare the Midianite army.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Cultural_references">Cultural references</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=10" title="Edit section: Cultural references">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span id="Origin_of_the_phrase_.22putting_out_a_fleece.22"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Origin_of_the_phrase_&quot;putting_out_a_fleece&quot;">Origin of the phrase "putting out a fleece"</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=11" title="Edit section: Origin of the phrase &quot;putting out a fleece&quot;">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The origin of the phrase "putting out a fleece" is a reference to the story of Gideon meaning to look for a sign from God before undertaking some action or carrying out some plan.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Military_references">Military references</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=12" title="Edit section: Military references">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Much like the 300 Spartans <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Thermopylae" title="Battle of Thermopylae">at Thermopylae</a>, Gideon has become symbolic of military success of a small elite force against overwhelming numerical odds. The 12th-century <i><a href="/wiki/Poem_of_Almer%C3%ADa" title="Poem of Almería">Poem of Almería</a></i> invokes "the strength of Samson and the sword of Gideon" in the context of the <a href="/wiki/Reconquista" title="Reconquista">Reconquista</a> of <a href="/wiki/Almer%C3%ADa" title="Almería">Almería</a> led by <a href="/wiki/Ponce_Giraldo_de_Cabrera" title="Ponce Giraldo de Cabrera">Ponce Giraldo de Cabrera</a> (1147). <a href="/wiki/Benedikt_Gletting" title="Benedikt Gletting">Benedikt Gletting</a> (16th century) invokes the "Sword of Gideon" in a call for a pious and confident defense of the <a href="/wiki/Old_Swiss_Confederacy" title="Old Swiss Confederacy">Old Swiss Confederacy</a> against the threat of the <a href="/wiki/Franco-Ottoman_alliance" title="Franco-Ottoman alliance">Franco-Ottoman alliance</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup>  The Gideon narrative was invoked by <a href="/wiki/Covenanter" class="mw-redirect" title="Covenanter">Covenanter</a> commander <a href="/wiki/Archibald_Strachan" title="Archibald Strachan">Archibald Strachan</a> prior to <a href="/wiki/Battle_of_Carbisdale" title="Battle of Carbisdale">Battle of Carbisdale</a> (1650).  The <a href="/wiki/Gideon_Force" title="Gideon Force">Gideon Force</a> was a small British-led  special force in the <a href="/wiki/East_African_Campaign_(World_War_II)" class="mw-redirect" title="East African Campaign (World War II)">East African Campaign</a> during World War II. <a href="/wiki/El_Junquito_raid" title="El Junquito raid">El Junquito raid</a> code-named Operation Gideon in 2018 and <a href="/wiki/Operation_Gideon_(2020)" title="Operation Gideon (2020)">Operation Gideon (2020)</a> were dissident military operations in <a href="/wiki/Venezuela" title="Venezuela">Venezuela</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Archaeological_evidence">Archaeological evidence</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Gideon&amp;action=edit&amp;section=13" title="Edit section: Archaeological evidence">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>A five-letter inscription appearing to represent the name Jerubbaal (another name used in the Hebrew Bible for Gideon) was discovered on a 3100-year old fragment of a jug in Israel.<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup> According to Smithsonian Magazine, "the artifact's age lines up with the time period depicted in the Book of Judges."<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
