<p><b>Sigeberht of East Anglia</b> (also known as <b>Saint Sigebert</b>), (<a href="/wiki/Old_English" title="Old English">Old English</a>: <i>Sigebryht</i>) was a saint and a king of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">East Anglia</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxon" class="mw-redirect" title="Anglo-Saxon">Anglo-Saxon</a> kingdom which today includes the English counties of <a href="/wiki/Norfolk" title="Norfolk">Norfolk</a> and <a href="/wiki/Suffolk" title="Suffolk">Suffolk</a>. He was the first English king to receive a Christian baptism and education before his succession and the first to abdicate in order to enter the monastic life. The principal source for Sigeberht is <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_ecclesiastica_gentis_Anglorum" class="mw-redirect" title="Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i>, which was completed in the 730s.
</p><p>Sigeberht was probably either a younger son of <a href="/wiki/R%C3%A6dwald_of_East_Anglia" title="Rædwald of East Anglia">Rædwald of East Anglia</a>, or his step-son from Rædwald's marriage to a pagan princess from the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Essex" title="Kingdom of Essex">kingdom of Essex</a>. Nothing is known of his life before he was exiled to <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a>, which was possibly done in order to ensure that Rædwald's own descendants ruled the kingdom. After his step-brother <a href="/wiki/Eorpwald_of_East_Anglia" title="Eorpwald of East Anglia">Eorpwald's</a> assassination in about 627, Sigeberht returned to East Anglia and (perhaps in the aftermath of a military campaign) became king, ruling jointly with <a href="/wiki/Ecgric_of_East_Anglia" title="Ecgric of East Anglia">Ecgric</a>, who may have been either a son of Rædwald's, or his nephew.
</p><p>During Sigeberht's reign the cause of Christianity in East Anglia was advanced greatly, even though his co-ruler Ecgric probably remained a pagan. Alliances were strengthened between the Christian kingdoms of <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent" title="Kingdom of Kent">Kent</a>, <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Northumbria" class="mw-redirect" title="Kingdom of Northumbria">Northumbria</a>  and East Anglia, with Sigeberht playing an important part in the establishment of the Christian faith in his kingdom: <a href="/wiki/Felix_of_Burgundy" title="Felix of Burgundy">Saint Felix</a> arrived in East Anglia to assist him in establishing his <a href="/wiki/Episcopal_see" title="Episcopal see">episcopal see</a> at <a href="/wiki/Dommoc" title="Dommoc">Dommoc</a>, he started a school for teaching <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a> and he granted the Irish monk <a href="/wiki/Saint_Fursey" title="Saint Fursey">Saint Fursey</a> a monastery site at Cnobheresburg (possibly <a href="/wiki/Burgh_Castle" title="Burgh Castle">Burgh Castle</a>). He eventually abdicated his power to Ecgric and retired to his monastery at Beodricesworth. At an unknown date, East Anglia was attacked by a Mercian army led by its king, <a href="/wiki/Penda_of_Mercia" title="Penda of Mercia">Penda</a>. Ecgric and the East Anglians appealed to Sigeberht to lead them in battle, but he refused and had to be dragged from his monastery to the battlefield. He refused to bear arms during the battle, during which both kings were slain and the East Anglian army was destroyed.
</p>

<h2><span id="Family_background.2C_exile.2C_conversion_and_education"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Family_background,_exile,_conversion_and_education">Family background, exile, conversion and education</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Family background, exile, conversion and education">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Sigeberht ruled the kingdom of East Anglia (<a href="/wiki/Old_English_language" class="mw-redirect" title="Old English language">Old English</a>&#58; <span title="Old English (ca. 450-1100)-language text"><i lang="ang">Ēast Engla Rīce</i></span>), a small independent <a href="/wiki/Anglo-Saxons" title="Anglo-Saxons">Anglo-Saxon</a> kingdom that comprised what are now the English counties of Norfolk and Suffolk and perhaps the eastern part of the Cambridgeshire <a href="/wiki/The_Fens" title="The Fens">Fens</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>It is not known when Sigeberht was born and nothing is known of his life before he was exiled from East Anglia prior to becoming king, as few records have survived from this period of English history. The most reliable source for Sigeberht's background and career is <a href="/wiki/Bede" title="Bede">Bede</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Historia_ecclesiastica_gentis_Anglorum" class="mw-redirect" title="Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum">Ecclesiastical History of the English People</a></i> (produced in 731), in which Bede stated that Sigeberht was the brother of <a href="/wiki/Eorpwald_of_East_Anglia" title="Eorpwald of East Anglia">Eorpwald</a><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;note 1&#93;</a></sup> and the son of <a href="/wiki/R%C3%A6dwald_of_East_Anglia" title="Rædwald of East Anglia">Rædwald</a>, who ruled the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_East_Anglia" title="Kingdom of East Anglia">kingdom of East Anglia</a> from about 599 to 624, but <a href="/wiki/William_of_Malmesbury" title="William of Malmesbury">William of Malmesbury</a> described him as Rædwald's stepson.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> The stepson theory is strengthened by the fact that the name <i>Sigeberht</i> is without comparison in the East Anglian <a href="/wiki/Wuffingas" title="Wuffingas">Wuffingas</a> dynasty, but closely resembles the naming fashions of the <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Essex" title="Kingdom of Essex">East Saxon royal house</a>. If this identification is correct, Rædwald's wife had previously been married to an East Saxon prince or ruler.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (June 2023)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup><sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Accuracy_dispute#Disputed_statement" title="Wikipedia:Accuracy dispute"><span title="Even the unreliable source this was originally referenced to considered this dubious (June 2023)">dubious</span></a>&#32;<span class="metadata"> &#8211; <a href="/wiki/Talk:Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia#Dubious" title="Talk:Sigeberht of East Anglia">discuss</a></span></i>&#93;</sup> Rædwald's own principal heir was Rægenhere (a youth of warrior age in 616, when he was slain in battle)<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200572_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200572-5">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> and his second heir was Eorpwald, slain by the heathen <a href="/wiki/Ricberht_of_East_Anglia" title="Ricberht of East Anglia">Ricberht</a> in about 627.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200599_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200599-7">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Rædwald was baptised before 616 and a Christian altar existed in his temple, but his son Eorpwald was not himself a convert when he succeeded Rædwald in about 624.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200597_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200597-8">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup> Since it is known that Rædwald's wife (who was Sigeberht's mother) did not become a Christian, Sigeberht must have received limited encouragement to convert to Christianity before being sent to <a href="/wiki/Gaul" title="Gaul">Gaul</a> and remaining there as an exile for many years during the lifetime of Eorpwald, "while fleeing from the enmity of Rædwald", as Bede reports.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">&#91;8&#93;</a></sup> His exile supports the stepson theory, if Rædwald was protecting Eorpwald's succession against a possible claim by a son who was not of the Wuffingas line.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200268_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200268-10">&#91;9&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Whilst living in Gaul as an exile, Sigeberht was converted and baptized and became a devout Christian and a man of learning. He was strongly impressed by the religious institutions and schools for the study of reading and writing which he found during his long exile.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005100–101_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005100–101-11">&#91;10&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="King_of_the_East_Angles">King of the East Angles</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: King of the East Angles">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Accession">Accession</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Accession">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg/220px-Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="273" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg/330px-Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg/440px-Britain_peoples_circa_600.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="994" data-file-height="1233" /></a><figcaption>A map showing the general locations of the Anglo-Saxon peoples around the year 600</figcaption></figure>
<p>After an interregnum prompted by Eorpwald's assassination, Sigeberht returned from Gaul to become ruler of the East Angles. It is likely that he gained the kingdom by military means, because his prowess as a commander was later remembered. During his reign, part of the kingdom was governed by <a href="/wiki/Ecgric_of_East_Anglia" title="Ecgric of East Anglia">Ecgric</a> his 'kinsman', a relationship described by the Latin term <i>cognatus</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">&#91;11&#93;</a></sup> This may mean that Ecgric was a son of Rædwald. However, the historian Steven Plunkett is amongst those that consider Ecgric to be the same person as Æthelric, named in the East Anglian tally (in the <i><a href="/wiki/Anglian_collection" title="Anglian collection">Anglian collection</a></i>) as a son of <a href="/wiki/Eni_of_East_Anglia" title="Eni of East Anglia">Eni</a>, Rædwald's brother.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005100_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005100-13">&#91;12&#93;</a></sup> Whoever the pagan Ecgric was, Sigeberht had equal or senior power while he ruled, because the influence of his religious patronage was felt throughout his kingdom.
</p><p>Sigeberht's Christian conversion may have been a decisive factor in his achieving royal power, since at that time <a href="/wiki/Edwin_of_Northumbria" title="Edwin of Northumbria">Edwin of Northumbria</a> (616–632 or 633) was the senior English king and he and <a href="/wiki/Eadbald_of_Kent" title="Eadbald of Kent">Eadbald</a>, who ruled Kent, were Christian. Eadbald certainly had contacts with the Frankish rulers. After <a href="/wiki/Dagobert_I" title="Dagobert I">Dagobert</a> succeeded <a href="/wiki/Clotaire_II" class="mw-redirect" title="Clotaire II">Clothar II</a> in Francia in 628, Sigeberht's emergence helped to strengthen the English conversion upon which Edwin's power rested.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200599–100_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200599–100-14">&#91;13&#93;</a></sup> Sigeberht is likely to have encouraged the conversion of Ecgric, if he was not already Christian. Edwin's encouragement took shape in the marriage of his grand-niece <a href="/wiki/Hereswitha" class="mw-redirect" title="Hereswitha">Hereswitha</a>, sister of <a href="/wiki/Hilda_of_Whitby" title="Hilda of Whitby">Hilda of Whitby</a>, to Æthelric, Rædwald's nephew. Hereswith and Hilda were under Edwin's protection and were baptised with him in 626.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200598_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200598-15">&#91;14&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Foundation_of_the_East_Anglian_bishopric">Foundation of the East Anglian bishopric</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Foundation of the East Anglian bishopric">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Williamson_p16_3.svg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Williamson_p16_3.svg/220px-Williamson_p16_3.svg.png" decoding="async" width="220" height="193" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Williamson_p16_3.svg/330px-Williamson_p16_3.svg.png 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Williamson_p16_3.svg/440px-Williamson_p16_3.svg.png 2x" data-file-width="691" data-file-height="607" /></a><figcaption>The kingdom of East Anglia during the early Saxon period</figcaption></figure>
<p>Bede relates that the East Anglian apostle <a href="/wiki/Felix_of_Burgundy" title="Felix of Burgundy">Felix of Burgundy</a> came to England from <a href="/wiki/Burgundy_(region)" class="mw-redirect" title="Burgundy (region)">Burgundy</a> as a missionary bishop and was sent by <a href="/wiki/Saint_Honorius" class="mw-redirect" title="Saint Honorius">Honorius</a>, <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury" title="Archbishop of Canterbury">Archbishop of Canterbury</a>, to assist in establishing Christianity in Sigeberht's kingdom.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWarner1996109_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWarner1996109-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> William of Malmesbury had the later story that Felix accompanied Sigeberht to East Anglia. In either case, this dates Sigeberht's accession to around 629–630, because Felix was bishop for seventeen years, his successor <a href="/wiki/Thomas_(bishop)" class="mw-redirect" title="Thomas (bishop)">Thomas</a> for five and Thomas' successor <a href="/wiki/Brigilsus" title="Brigilsus">Berhtgisl Boniface</a> for seventeen&#160;– and Berhtgisl died in around 669.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEKirby200066_17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKirby200066-17">&#91;16&#93;</a></sup> Sigeberht established the bishop's seat of his kingdom for Felix at Dommoc,<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">&#91;17&#93;</a></sup> claimed variously for <a href="/wiki/Dunwich" title="Dunwich">Dunwich</a> or <a href="/wiki/Walton,_Suffolk" title="Walton, Suffolk">Walton</a>, <a href="/wiki/Felixstowe" title="Felixstowe">Felixstowe</a> (both coastal sites in <a href="/wiki/Suffolk" title="Suffolk">Suffolk</a>). If the seat was at Walton (as Rochester claimed during the 13th century), the site of Dommoc may have been within the precinct of a <a href="/wiki/Ancient_Rome" title="Ancient Rome">Roman</a> fort which formerly stood there.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHaslam199241_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaslam199241-19">&#91;18&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Foundation_of_the_East_Anglian_school">Foundation of the East Anglian school</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Foundation of the East Anglian school">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>Sigeberht secured the future of the Church in East Anglia when he established a school in his kingdom so that boys could be taught reading and writing in Latin, on the model that he had witnessed in Gaul.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWarner1996109_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWarner1996109-16">&#91;15&#93;</a></sup> Felix assisted him by obtaining teachers of the kind who taught in <a href="/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent" title="Kingdom of Kent">Kent</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStenton1988116_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStenton1988116-20">&#91;19&#93;</a></sup> According to the <i>Life of Gregory the Great</i>, <a href="/wiki/Paulinus_of_York" title="Paulinus of York">Paulinus of York</a>, who from 633 to 644 was the Bishop of <a href="/wiki/Diocese_of_Rochester" title="Diocese of Rochester">Rochester</a> in northern Kent, had been connected with Rædwald's court during the exile of Edwin.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200260_21-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200260-21">&#91;20&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">&#91;21&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h3><span class="mw-headline" id="Foundation_of_Cnobheresburg">Foundation of Cnobheresburg</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Foundation of Cnobheresburg">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h3>
<p>The allegiance of Felix to <a href="/wiki/Province_of_Canterbury" title="Province of Canterbury">Canterbury</a> determined the Roman basis of the East Anglian Church, influenced along continental lines,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStenton1988117_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStenton1988117-23">&#91;22&#93;</a></sup> though Felix's training in Burgundy may have been coloured by the teaching of the Irish missionary <a href="/wiki/Columbanus" title="Columbanus">Saint Columbanus</a> in <a href="/wiki/Luxeuil-les-Bains" title="Luxeuil-les-Bains">Luxeuil</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200265_24-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200265-24">&#91;23&#93;</a></sup> In around 633, perhaps shortly before <a href="/wiki/Aidan_of_Lindisfarne" title="Aidan of Lindisfarne">Saint Aidan</a> was sent to <a href="/wiki/Lindisfarne" title="Lindisfarne">Lindisfarne</a> from <a href="/wiki/Iona" title="Iona">Iona</a>, the Irish royal hermit and missionary <a href="/wiki/Saint_Fursey" title="Saint Fursey">Saint Fursey</a> came to East Anglia from the <a href="/wiki/Athlone" title="Athlone">Athlone</a> area, along with his priests and brethren. Sigeberht granted him a monastery site in an old <a href="/wiki/Roman_fort" class="mw-redirect" title="Roman fort">Roman fort</a> called Cnobheresburg,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEWarner1996111–112_25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWarner1996111–112-25">&#91;24&#93;</a></sup> usually identified as <a href="/wiki/Burgh_Castle" title="Burgh Castle">Burgh Castle</a>, near <a href="/wiki/Great_Yarmouth" title="Great Yarmouth">Great Yarmouth</a>. Felix and Fursey both effected a large number of conversions and established many churches in Sigeberht's kingdom. Bede records that Archbishop Honorius and Bishop Felix much admired the work of Saint Aidan of Lindisfarne<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26">&#91;25&#93;</a></sup> and it is therefore likely that they also appreciated the tasks accomplished by Saint Fursey, whose community also lived according to the ascetic principles of <a href="/wiki/Celtic_Christianity" title="Celtic Christianity">Irish Christianity</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005105_27-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005105-27">&#91;26&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Abdication_and_martyrdom">Abdication and martyrdom</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Sigeberht_of_East_Anglia&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Abdication and martyrdom">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>At some point during his reign, Sigeberht abdicated his power to Ecgric and retired to lead a religious life within a monastery he had built for his own use. Bede does not name the location of Sigeberht's monastery, but later sources name it as Beodricesworth, afterwards called <a href="/wiki/Bury_St_Edmunds" title="Bury St Edmunds">Bury St Edmunds</a>. If that identification is accepted, the likely site was in the original precinct of the mediaeval abbey at Bury St Edmunds, probably the 'worth' or <a href="/wiki/Curtilage" title="Curtilage">curtilage</a> of Beodric after whom the place was originally named.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005106_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett2005106-28">&#91;27&#93;</a></sup> The site occupied a strong position on the upper reaches of the <a href="/wiki/River_Lark" title="River Lark">Lark valley</a>, which drains north-west into the Great Fen through important early settlements at <a href="/wiki/Icklingham" title="Icklingham">Icklingham</a>, <a href="/wiki/Culford" title="Culford">Culford</a>, <a href="/wiki/West_Stow" title="West Stow">West Stow</a> and others. This was a line of access towards <a href="/wiki/Ely,_Cambridgeshire" title="Ely, Cambridgeshire">Ely</a>, where a foundation of <a href="/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury" title="Augustine of Canterbury">Saint Augustine</a> may already have existed, and towards <a href="/wiki/Soham" title="Soham">Soham</a>, where Saint Felix is thought to have founded a monastery.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200573,_102_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPlunkett200573,_102-29">&#91;28&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>At an unknown date, which may have been in the early 640s,<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYorke200262_30-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYorke200262-30">&#91;29&#93;</a></sup> East Anglia was attacked by a <a href="/wiki/Mercia" title="Mercia">Mercian</a> army and Ecgric was obliged to defend it with a much smaller force, though one that was not negligible. The East Angles appealed to Sigeberht to leave his monastery and lead them in battle, hoping that his presence and the memory of his former military exploits would encourage the army and make them less likely to flee. Sigeberht refused, saying that he had renounced his worldly kingdom and now lived only for the heavenly kingdom. However, he was dragged from the monastery to the battlefield where, unwilling to bear arms, he went into battle carrying only a staff. The Mercians were victorious and Sigeberht, Ecgric and many of the East Angles were slain and their army was routed. In this way Sigeberht became a Christian martyr.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">&#91;30&#93;</a></sup> He is among the names of the kings who according to an ancient saying, were avenged by the slaying of Penda in 654.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHenry_of_Huntingdon1996121_32-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHenry_of_Huntingdon1996121-32">&#91;31&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">&#91;32&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The Church that Sigeberht had done so much to establish in East Anglia survived for two centuries, enduring 'evil times' (such as the period when the kingdom was under attack by the armies of <a href="/wiki/Penda_of_Mercia" title="Penda of Mercia">Penda of Mercia</a>). It lasted continuously under a succession of bishops until the Danish <a href="/wiki/Great_Heathen_Army" title="Great Heathen Army">Great Heathen Army</a> invaded East Anglia in the 860s.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEStenton1988117,_248_34-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStenton1988117,_248-34">&#91;33&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>The feast day of Sigeberht is commemorated on various dates, even within the same religious tradition. For example, different Catholic calendars of saints designate 16 January, 27 September, or 29 October, or note two dates as alternatives.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEThua2019_35-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThua2019-35">&#91;34&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEYoung2015_36-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEYoung2015-36">&#91;35&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTECeltic_Saints2021_37-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTECeltic_Saints2021-37">&#91;36&#93;</a></sup> <i>Lives of the English Saints</i>, written by <a href="/wiki/John_Henry_Newman" title="John Henry Newman">John Henry Newman</a> in 1843, is amongst the texts that gives Sigeberht's feast day as being observed on 29 October.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">&#91;37&#93;</a></sup> Eastern Orthodox sources also <a href="/wiki/January_16_(Eastern_Orthodox_liturgics)" title="January 16 (Eastern Orthodox liturgics)">give 16 January</a>, but in addition list 25 January and 27 September as the relevant feast day.<sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEOrthodox_Church_in_America2022_39-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOrthodox_Church_in_America2022-39">&#91;38&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEOrthodox_Englandn.d._40-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEOrthodox_Englandn.d.-40">&#91;39&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEHoly_Trinity_Orthodox_Church2012_41-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHoly_Trinity_Orthodox_Church2012-41">&#91;40&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
