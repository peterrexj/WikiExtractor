<p><b>Margaret Ball</b> (1515–1584) was a prominent member of 16th-century <a href="/wiki/Irish_people" title="Irish people">Irish</a> society, who, despite being the widow of a <a href="/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_Dublin" title="Lord Mayor of Dublin">Lord Mayor of Dublin</a>, was arrested for her adherence to the Catholic faith and died of deprivation in the dungeons of <a href="/wiki/Dublin_Castle" title="Dublin Castle">Dublin Castle</a>. She was declared a <a href="/wiki/Martyr" title="Martyr">martyr</a> for the faith by the Catholic Church and <a href="/wiki/Beatification" title="Beatification">beatified</a> in 1992, one of a group of 17 <a href="/wiki/Irish_Catholic_Martyrs" title="Irish Catholic Martyrs">Irish Catholic Martyrs</a>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Early_life">Early life</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Margaret_Ball&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Early life">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>She was born <b>Margaret Bermingham</b><sup id="cite_ref-chapel_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chapel-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup> in Corballis, a <a href="/wiki/Townland" title="Townland">townland</a> now part of the village of <a href="/wiki/Skryne" title="Skryne">Skryne</a> in <a href="/wiki/County_Meath" title="County Meath">County Meath</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-Ballygall_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ballygall-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup> where her father, Nicholas, had purchased a farm when he emigrated from England. The family later became politically active; her brother, William Bermingham, protested in London against <a href="/wiki/Earl" title="Earl">Earl</a> <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Radclyffe,_3rd_Earl_of_Sussex" title="Thomas Radclyffe, 3rd Earl of Sussex">Thomas Radclyffe</a>,<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">&#91;3&#93;</a></sup> when he imposed the <a href="/wiki/Protestant_Reformation" class="mw-redirect" title="Protestant Reformation">Protestant Reformation</a> on behalf of the boy-king <a href="/wiki/Edward_VI_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Edward VI of England">Edward VI</a>. When she was 16 years old, Margaret Bermingham married <a href="/wiki/Bartholomew_Ball" title="Bartholomew Ball">Bartholomew Ball</a>, an <a href="/wiki/Alderman" title="Alderman">alderman</a> of the <a href="/wiki/City_of_Dublin" class="mw-redirect" title="City of Dublin">City of Dublin</a>, whose wealthy family operated the bridge over the <a href="/wiki/River_Dodder" title="River Dodder">River Dodder</a>, which is still known as <a href="/wiki/Ballsbridge" title="Ballsbridge">Ballsbridge</a>. She then moved to the city, where the couple lived at <a href="/wiki/Ballygall" title="Ballygall">Ballygall</a> House in north county Dublin and had a townhouse on <a href="/wiki/Merchant%27s_Quay" class="mw-redirect" title="Merchant&#39;s Quay">Merchant's Quay</a>. They had ten children, though only five survived to adulthood. Bartholomew Ball was elected Lord Mayor of Dublin in <a href="/wiki/1553_in_Ireland" title="1553 in Ireland">1553</a>, making Margaret the <a href="/wiki/Lady_Mayoress" title="Lady Mayoress">Lady Mayoress</a> of the city. She had a comfortable life with a large household and many servants, and she was recognised for organising classes for the children of local families in her home.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Arrest_and_death">Arrest and death</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Margaret_Ball&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Arrest and death">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Margaret_Ball.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a7/Margaret_Ball.jpg/220px-Margaret_Ball.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="339" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a7/Margaret_Ball.jpg 1.5x" data-file-width="271" data-file-height="417" /></a><figcaption>Margaret Ball</figcaption></figure>
<p>In 1558 <a href="/wiki/Elizabeth_I_of_England" class="mw-redirect" title="Elizabeth I of England">Queen Elizabeth I</a> reversed the policy of her sister <a href="/wiki/Mary_I_of_England" title="Mary I of England">Queen Mary</a> and imposed her <a href="/wiki/Elizabethan_Religious_Settlement" title="Elizabethan Religious Settlement">Religious Settlement</a> upon all of her realms. In 1570 the <a href="/wiki/Holy_See" title="Holy See">Holy See</a> responded with the <a href="/wiki/Papal_bull" title="Papal bull">papal bull</a> <i><a href="/wiki/Regnans_in_Excelsis" title="Regnans in Excelsis">Regnans in Excelsis</a></i>, which declared Elizabeth to be an illegitimate usurper. During this time of religious persecution, it was well known that Ball provided "safe houses" for any bishops or priests who might be passing through Dublin.<sup id="cite_ref-chapel_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chapel-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Margaret Ball's eldest son, <a href="/wiki/Walter_Ball_(Alderman)" class="mw-redirect" title="Walter Ball (Alderman)">Walter</a>, who wanted to follow in his father's footsteps and advance his political career, embraced the "new religion" and was appointed Commissioner for Ecclesiastical Causes in <a href="/wiki/1577_in_Ireland" title="1577 in Ireland">1577</a>. Margaret was disappointed with her son's change of faith and tried to change his mind. On one occasion, she told him that she had a "special friend" for him to meet. Walter arrived early with a company of soldiers, and found that the "special friend" was <a href="/wiki/Dermot_O%27Hurley" title="Dermot O&#39;Hurley">Dermot O'Hurley</a>, <a href="/wiki/Archbishop_of_Cashel" title="Archbishop of Cashel">Archbishop of Cashel</a>. He was celebrating <a href="/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)" title="Mass (liturgy)">Mass</a> with the family.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Castletower.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/Castletower.jpg/220px-Castletower.jpg" decoding="async" width="220" height="293" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/Castletower.jpg/330px-Castletower.jpg 1.5x, //upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7a/Castletower.jpg 2x" data-file-width="384" data-file-height="512" /></a><figcaption>The Record Tower, dating from c.1228, the sole surviving part of <a href="/wiki/Dublin_Castle" title="Dublin Castle">Dublin Castle</a> that still appears as it did during the <a href="/wiki/Elizabethan_era" title="Elizabethan era">Elizabethan era</a>. To its left is the <a href="/wiki/Chapel_Royal_(Dublin_Castle)" class="mw-redirect" title="Chapel Royal (Dublin Castle)">Chapel Royal</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Immediately after his installation as Lord Mayor of Dublin in <a href="/wiki/1580_in_Ireland" title="1580 in Ireland">1580</a>, Walter had his mother and her personal <a href="/wiki/Chaplain" title="Chaplain">chaplain</a> arrested and taken to the dungeons of <a href="/wiki/Dublin_Castle" title="Dublin Castle">Dublin Castle</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">&#91;4&#93;</a></sup> Due to her advanced age and severe arthritis, she had to be transported there by a wooden pallet.<sup id="cite_ref-Ballygall_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ballygall-2">&#91;2&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>When the family protested, Walter declared that his mother should have been executed, but he had spared her. She would be allowed to go free if she "took the Oath", which probably referred to the <a href="/wiki/Oath_of_Supremacy" title="Oath of Supremacy">Oath of Supremacy</a>. Her second son, <a href="/wiki/Nicholas_Ball_(Alderman)" class="mw-redirect" title="Nicholas Ball (Alderman)">Nicholas</a>, who supported her, was elected Mayor of Dublin in 1582. However, Walter was still Commissioner for Ecclesiastical Causes, which was a royal appointment. He outranked Nicholas and kept him from securing their mother's release from prison. Nicholas visited her daily, bringing her food, clothing and candles.
</p><p>Ball died in 1584 at the age of sixty-nine, which was an advanced age at the time. She was crippled with <a href="/wiki/Arthritis" title="Arthritis">arthritis</a> and had lived for three years in the cold, wet dungeon of Dublin Castle with no natural light. When she died she was buried in the cemetery at <a href="/wiki/St._Audoen%27s_Church,_Dublin_(Church_of_Ireland)" title="St. Audoen&#39;s Church, Dublin (Church of Ireland)">St. Audoen's Church</a> in Dublin. Although she could have altered her <a href="/wiki/Will_(law)" class="mw-redirect" title="Will (law)">will</a>, she still bequeathed her property to Walter upon her death.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Veneration">Veneration</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Margaret_Ball&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Veneration">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>Ball had remained in the dungeon when she could have returned to a life of comfort at any time by simply "taking the oath". Two generations later this pattern was repeated when Blessed <a href="/wiki/Francis_Taylor_(martyr)" title="Francis Taylor (martyr)">Francis Taylor</a>, who was Mayor of Dublin (1595–1596) and was married to Gennet Shelton, a granddaughter of Ball, was condemned to the dungeons after exposing fraud in the parliamentary elections to the <a href="/wiki/Irish_House_of_Commons" title="Irish House of Commons">Irish House of Commons</a>. He likewise refused to "take the oath" and died in Dublin Castle in 1621.
</p><p>Ball and Taylor could not have known each other, but they were beatified together, along with <a href="/wiki/Dermot_O%27Hurley" title="Dermot O&#39;Hurley">Dermot O'Hurley</a> and 14 other Catholic martyrs, on 27 September 1992 by <a href="/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II" title="Pope John Paul II">Pope John Paul II</a>.
</p>
<h2><span class="mw-headline" id="Legacy">Legacy</span><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Margaret_Ball&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Legacy">edit</a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></h2>
<p>The Blessed Margaret Ball Chapel in <a href="/wiki/Santry" title="Santry">Santry</a> is dedicated to her.<sup id="cite_ref-chapel_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chapel-1">&#91;1&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Ball, along with St. <a href="/wiki/Columbanus" title="Columbanus">Columbanus</a> and St. <a href="/wiki/Mary_MacKillop" title="Mary MacKillop">Mary MacKillop</a> <a href="/wiki/Sisters_of_St_Joseph_of_the_Sacred_Heart" title="Sisters of St Joseph of the Sacred Heart">RSJ</a>, was named a <a href="/wiki/Patron_saint" title="Patron saint">patron saint</a> of the 50th International Eucharistic Congress held in Ireland in June 2012.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">&#91;5&#93;</a></sup>
</p><p>Her statue stands outside <a href="/wiki/St._Mary%27s_Pro-Cathedral" class="mw-redirect" title="St. Mary&#39;s Pro-Cathedral">St. Mary's Pro-Cathedral</a>; she is one of only five historical women (excluding <a href="/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus" title="Mary, mother of Jesus">Mary, mother of Jesus</a>) to have a public statue in Dublin, the others being <a href="/wiki/Catherine_MacAuley" class="mw-redirect" title="Catherine MacAuley">Catherine MacAuley</a> (<a href="/wiki/Baggot_Street" title="Baggot Street">Baggot Street</a>), <a href="/wiki/Veronica_Guerin" title="Veronica Guerin">Veronica Guerin</a> (<a href="/wiki/Dublin_Castle" title="Dublin Castle">Dublin Castle</a>), <a href="/wiki/Constance_Lloyd" title="Constance Lloyd">Constance Lloyd</a> (<a href="/wiki/Merrion_Square" title="Merrion Square">Merrion Square</a>) and <a href="/wiki/Constance_Markiewicz" class="mw-redirect" title="Constance Markiewicz">Constance Markiewicz</a> (three locations).<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">&#91;6&#93;</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">&#91;7&#93;</a></sup>
</p>
